I work on agentic systems: programs that plan over long horizons and occasionally inspect their own draft reasoning before committing to a plan. My team has been treating the introspection layer as a pure win, but I have now watched it fail in two quite different ways. Sometimes it is just overhead, where every decision pays latency and compute for a second pass that contributes nothing. Sometimes it actively degrades behavior: the agent reopens decisions it had already made well, and in one case, after we turned on fine-grained logging of a long-tuned parsing routine, the routine performed worse while it was being watched, which reminded me of an athlete who misses a shot once she starts attending to the mechanics of her own stroke. Other people on the team draw the opposite conclusion, that the object-level process is the problem and that we should be reflecting more, not less. I no longer trust either slogan.

What I want is the specification a theory of when thinking about thinking helps or hurts would have to satisfy before I would let that theory decide whether to enable a meta-layer in a given deployment. Please give me exactly five desiderata as a numbered list, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should be one or two sentences: state the requirement and the case, distinction, or constraint that forces it. Within each item, flag whether the requirement is non-negotiable for any adequate theory or only a preference of one camp. If some subset of the five cannot be jointly satisfied, indicate that on the relevant numbered items, and if one requirement does most of the work in excluding naive positions like always reflect or never reflect, identify it. Skip scoring existing theories; the criteria themselves are what I am after.
