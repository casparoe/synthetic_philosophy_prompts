Ive been re-reading Jean-Paul Sartre's "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (the 1946 lecture, in Philip Mairet's translation) and I keep getting stuck on the place where he sums up the Christian objection:

From the Christian side, we are reproached as people who deny the reality and seriousness of human affairs. For since we ignore the commandments of God and all values prescribed as eternal, nothing remains but what is strictly voluntary. Everyone can do what he likes, and will be incapable, from such a point of view, of condemning either the point of view or the action of anyone else.

I want you to write a dialogue, somewhere around 1,000–1,200 words, between two characters who take this passage seriously. One is a committed Sartrean (call her Marta), and the other is a critic who thinks the objection basically goes through — a moral realist, maybe a lapsed Catholic, who thinks that if you throw out "all values prescribed as eternal" you lose any right to condemn or even strongly prefer one way of living over another. The critic should also press the internal tension: Sartre goes on to talk about authenticity, bad faith, good faith, and full responsibility, and those all sound like normative standards, so where do they come from if existence precedes essence?

Make both of them competent. The Sartrean should get the actual moves, not a strawman: freedom isnt one value among others but the condition of having any values at all; bad faith is self-undermining because its a project of fleeing a freedom that you're exercising in the very act of fleeing it; choosing for yourself means choosing for all of humanity, so you have to be able to will your choice universally; failing to choose is itself a choice made in bad faith. But the critic should land real punches on those moves, too: isnt the universalizability test just Kant sneaking in the back door? can Sartre really say the person who chooses domination is wrong, as opposed to just choosing something Sartre happens to find repellent? if the "human condition" (as opposed to a human nature) still has a fixed structure — being free, being in the world, being mortal — isnt that an essence by another name? Let them actually answer each other, make small concessions, maybe revise their positions once; don't let them talk past each other.

Then, after the dialogue, give me a short paragraph in your own voice, out of character, saying which side you think is left holding the stronger objection and where (if anywhere) I've mis-set the debate. If the whole "incoherence" charge rests on a misunderstanding of what Sartre means by a value or by bad faith, say that plainly and don't just write the dialogue anyway.
