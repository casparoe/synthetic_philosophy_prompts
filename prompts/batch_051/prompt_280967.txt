Here is a parallel I keep noticing and cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial. Aligning an AI to a group of people seems to involve two separable steps: representing each person's preferences as an ordering over possible outcomes, and then combining those individual orderings into a single objective function for the AI to optimize. The second step looks formally identical to the problem Arrow's impossibility theorem addresses — constructing a social welfare function from individual preference orderings. The conditions even seem to line up. The requirement that each person's preferences count maps to non-dictatorship. The requirement that the AI prefer X to Y whenever everyone does maps to the weak Pareto condition. And the requirement that the AI's ranking of X and Y not depend on some irrelevant alternative Z maps to independence of irrelevant alternatives. If the mapping is genuine and the background assumptions of the theorem have alignment analogues, then with at least three alternatives either no acceptable group objective exists or one of those alignment desiderata must be abandoned. Is this a real structural identity or just pattern-matching? If it is real, what actually transfers? For instance, which alignment choices play the role of Arrow's unrestricted-domain assumption, and do moves to cardinal utilities, interpersonal comparisons, or pairwise preference aggregation escape the impossibility or merely exchange one set of conditions for another? And where does the parallel predict something false — where do the two structures come apart?
