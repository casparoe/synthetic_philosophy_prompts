So I'm in this local democracy group and everyone's suddenly into lottocracy. Their one big argument is that a citizens' assembly chosen by lot is like a random sample in an opinion poll, and sometimes they dress it up as like randomization in a clinical trial: it's representative by construction, so whatever it decides is a trustworthy stand-in for what the whole public would think if everyone had the same time and briefing. They lean on this hard, mostly to wave away the objection that assemblies arent elected and nobody gave them permission. I want to audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table—what maps to what (the population, the sampling frame, the sample, the measurement, the estimate?)—then the places where it breaks, and especially the features of random sampling that get quietly imported when you say it's like a poll or a trial but dont actually hold for a year-long citizens' assembly. Does the conclusion—that the assembly inherits the trustworthiness of a properly drawn sample—survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? And if you'd swap in a better analogy, what would it be?
