Just got back from a long argument with a friend about vagueness, and I've ended up fairly convinced by supervaluationism — but he kept pushing the epistemicist line, and now I can't tell whether I actually hold the view or just find it easy to defend in the moment. His line was roughly: if you want to keep classical logic and still say there's a fact of the matter about "heap," just say the boundary is sharp and we're irremediably ignorant of where it is. I was defending the supervaluationist picture instead: vagueness is semantic indecision, there are lots of admissible ways of making "heap" precise, and a sentence is true simpliciter iff it's true on every admisable precisification. That gets you excluded middle without committing to a sharp cut-off in the world itself.

But I kept getting stuck on some things, and I want an honest map of what would count against supervaluationism as a position I've tentatively adopted. Not the lazy objection ("so there's no sharp boundary?" — that's not really an objection to this view). I want the actual pressure points, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular: (1) is the notion of an "admissible precisification" itself vague or just left unexplained, and if it is vague, doesn't the view relocate the problem rather than solve it; (2) higher-order vagueness — if "admissible" has borderline cases, the account seems to climb its own levels and maybe never bottoms out; (3) penumbral connections — the extensions of predicates like "red" and "orange" constrain each other, so you can't just precisify each term one by one, and then the whole-language precisification starts to look like an unexplained heavyweight. My friend also kept muttering about S5 — something like, on supervaluationism "definitely" behaves as a modal operator whose logic is S5, so whatever is borderline comes out definitely borderline, and higher-order vagueness gets flattened, supposedly by the view's own lights.

So lay out the defeaters for supervaluationism: which ones genuinely undermine it, which ones can a committed supervaluationist shrug off, and which would they themselves admit count against the view? If some of these panned out, would they push me specifically toward epistemicism, toward something else, or nowhere in particular? And has the view ended up qualified enough that it can't be caught out by anything — and if so, is that a worse problem for a theory of vagueness than it would be for a theory in metaphysics? Please write the answer in British English.
