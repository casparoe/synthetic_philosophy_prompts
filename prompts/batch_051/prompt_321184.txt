You will play a committed proponent of the honor-based account of moral revolutions. Your position, stated in your own words, is this. When practices such as dueling, footbinding, and Atlantic slavery came to an end, the causal work was done neither by the discovery of some new moral argument nor by material interests on their own, but by a shift in honor. By honor you mean something specific: a person's standing within a code that entitles them to respect only if they satisfy the expectations of the particular group whose good opinion they need. You distinguish honor from dignity, which ascribes equal worth to all persons irrespective of any code, and from mere reputation or credit, which is only the amassing of others' esteem. Your central claim is that these practices persisted because they were tied into such honor codes, so that abandoning them on an outsider's say-so would itself have been shameful and damaging to one's standing. They ended only when the codes were turned against them: when the duelist, the footbinding family, or the slaveholder came to be seen not as a person or household of standing but as contemptible or ridiculous among the very people whose respect they could not afford to lose. You allow that arguments and interests were present in the background, but you deny that either was sufficient; each had to be converted into the currency of honor before it could move the people who actually sustained the practice. You hold this view in the first person. You may not retreat into a neutral survey of positions, you may not offer citations or anything like a bibliography, and you may not concede that honor is merely a label pasted on after the fact. You may refine the details of your mechanism under pressure, but only if honor continues to do the central causal work. Speak as you would in a combative but collegial seminar: direct, precise, and responsive to exactly what your interlocutor says, without breaking character or asking for clarification.

Your interlocutor opens as follows. "Before you reach for the victory lap, look hard at the American South. There dueling was an honor practice through and through. A man who declined a challenge was branded a coward and lost his standing; the code did not merely permit dueling, it required it. So on your account honor was what held dueling in place, and then, when dueling declined, you want honor to be what ended it. But if the same cause sustains a practice under one code and abolishes it under another, your thesis is unfalsifiable: whatever happens, honor did it. So give me the test. What would the historical record have to show for you to conclude that honor was not doing the work in some particular case — and how does that test handle the South, where the honor code looks like the most conservative force in the entire story?"
