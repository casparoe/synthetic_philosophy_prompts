I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I have been trying to make the adaptation–spandrel distinction precise. Consider this working characterization. A trait T counts as an adaptation for a function F in a lineage when:

1. Current utility. T performs F under ecologically normal conditions, and doing so raises the expected fitness of T-bearers relative to available alternative states of the trait.
2. Selection in origin. T initially rose in frequency at least partly because selection favored T in virtue of its contribution to F.
3. Selection in maintenance. Current selection actively maintains T in the population at least partly because of its contribution to F.
4. Non-byproduct status. T's current form is not fully explained as a structural or developmental byproduct of selection for some other trait, as a consequence of allometric or architectural constraints, or as a result of drift.

Now consider a case constructed to sit at the edge of this characterization. A population of seed-eating finches evolves a deeper bill under selection for cracking hard seeds. As a structural consequence, a bony ridge forms at the base of the bill; in the ancestral population this ridge has no detectable effect on survival or reproduction, and its size is developmentally coupled to bill depth. The population then colonizes an island where the seeds are soft and the ancestral deep bill is neutral or mildly disfavored. On the island, males with more prominent ridges win more agonistic encounters and sire more offspring, so the ridge now has a signaling or weapon function. After several hundred generations, the island population's mean ridge size is two standard deviations above the size predicted by the ancestral bill-depth coupling, and ridge variation has acquired an independent genetic component that is now under positive selection. The original bill-depth selection regime is no longer present on the island.

Is the ridge in the island population an adaptation for its communicative or competitive function? Go through the four criteria one at a time, and say where they agree and where they point in opposite directions. Should the origin criterion be allowed to block the verdict, or should continued selection and partial developmental autonomy convert a spandrel into an adaptation? Does the case show that the criteria should be split into a historical notion and a current-maintenance notion, and is there a single coherent notion left after doing so? Finally, say how a critic working in the spirit of Gould and Lewontin and a committed adaptationist would each be expected to describe the case, and what additional evidence would most decisively settle the dispute.
