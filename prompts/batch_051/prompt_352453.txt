Okay so my decision-theory reading group has spent four months on the Ellsberg paradox and I'm starting to think we've been arguing past each other the whole time, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who's right.

The two camps, as I'd describe them. The 'bias' camp treats the Ellsberg two-urn pattern as a glitch in the same family as the Allais paradox and the certainty effect: people prefer the known-risk urn because the ambiguous one gives them a bad feeling, not because anything is actually worse about it. On their view ambiguity aversion is a bug to correct for, and if a regulator or doctor refuses to act until probabilities are crisp, that's bad reasoning wearing a virtue's clothes.

The 'caution' camp says no: preferring the known-proportion urn is exactly what a sensible agent should do when information is missing, and the entire ambiguity-model literature — maxmin expected utility and its relatives — is there to show the preference is coherent, axiomatizable, and a legitimate notion of rationality instead of a deviation from expected utility.

Everyone agrees on the behavioral pattern: most people prefer the 50/50 urn to the unknown urn and prefer betting on red in the known urn over red in the unknown urn. We all know the axioms and the representations. It isnt ignorance; the same five moves just keep cycling through.

My hypothesis is that the standoff is partly a disguised empirical disagreement — does the Ellsberg pattern survive incentives, feedback, repetition, being made to feel accountable, or being framed as a one-shot choice among whole distributions rather than a bet — and partly a normative disagreement about what 'rational' is doing when one side leans on the axioms and the other on an error theory.
