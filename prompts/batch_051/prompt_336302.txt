I recently read a general-audience book on philosophy of mind whose treatment of physicalism felt plausible while I was reading it and too clean immediately afterwards. The author summarizes the position and its central difficulty roughly as follows:

"Physicalism is the view that everything that exists is ultimately composed of the particles, fields, and forces described by physics, and that every real fact—including every mental fact—is fixed by the physical facts. Its central problem is Hempel's dilemma. If 'physical' means the entities and properties countenanced by current physics, then physicalism is very likely false, because current physics is known to be incomplete and will be revised; physics as it now stands has no place for consciousness. If instead 'physical' means the entities and properties of some future completed physics, then the thesis becomes empty: because we do not know what future physics will include, physicalism now amounts only to saying that whatever the true final science says exists, exists. That formulation is compatible with anything—even with a completed physics that countenances irreducible souls—so it cannot be the substantive doctrine that rules out dualism. Physicalism therefore either rests on a physics we know to be false or collapses into triviality. This is why some philosophers have concluded that the label is too broken to do serious work, and that the real debate should be reframed in terms of reduction rather than ontology."
