I am a PhD student in ancient philosophy, working on the epistemology of the *Phaedo*. Consider this passage (Plato, *Phaedo* 74a):

We say there is such a thing as equality. I do not mean one piece of wood equal to another, or one stone to another, or anything of that sort, but something beyond that—equality in the abstract. Shall we say there is such a thing, or not?

I want you to write the desiderata that a theory of the Forms must meet if it is to do the explanatory work this passage assigns to the Form of Equality—and, more generally, the work assigned to the Forms in the *Phaedo* and the *Republic*'s sun, line, and cave. Treat the passage as a primary datum, not as a place-holder for "Plato believed in abstract objects."

For each desideratum, give the textual or argumentative consideration behind it, and say whether it is non-negotiable for a recognizably Platonic view or a preference of a particular camp (e.g., Forms as paradigms, self-predication, participation as causation, recollection). I most want your sorting of genuine constraints from artifacts of a translation or of mid-century Anglophone readings.

Then identify any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—in particular, any pair that would force the Form of Equality to become a perfect particular equal thing, thereby inviting something like the Third Man. Say whether that is a defect of the list or a result the list itself delivers.

End by saying which single desideratum most sharply separates an "ordinary universals" reading of Forms from a reading on which a Form is a standard or measure that sensible things fall short of but cannot be. The passage does not count toward the limit. Max 900 words.
