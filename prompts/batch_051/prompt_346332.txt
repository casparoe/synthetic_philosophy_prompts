Since I retired from a career in physical chemistry and began reading legal philosophy, I have been worrying at a set of cases about what philosophers of law call naked statistical evidence, meaning evidence that does no more than report the frequency of a property in some class and then place a particular litigant inside that class. In the laboratory I was content to act on a posterior probability once the costs of the two possible errors were included, so I am genuinely puzzled by the law's suspicion of bare statistics, and even more puzzled that I seem to share the suspicion. I should be grateful if you would help me find what principle, if any, lies behind my reactions, or else tell me which of my reactions I ought to discard.

The cases are these. A pedestrian is struck and injured by a bus that drives away; on the route in question, four out of five buses are operated by the Blue Bus Company and the remainder by another line, and the pedestrian can say nothing about the color. In a civil action on the balance of probabilities, I am inclined to find the Blue Bus Company liable. A rodeo has a thousand spectators, of whom four hundred paid and six hundred entered without tickets; a spectator picked at random is sued for the unpaid admission, and I would not find against him, even though my own arithmetic says he is more likely than not to have entered without paying. In a prison yard, ninety-nine of a hundred prisoners take part in a serious assault on a guard while the hundredth stands apart, and no one can identify the man who stood apart; I would not convict any of them on a criminal standard, though for each the probability of guilt is 0.99. Yet I would find causation established in a civil suit against a factory that discharged a carcinogen into the town's water, where sound epidemiology says that seventy percent of the relevant cancers in the town during the relevant period are attributable to the discharge and nothing distinguishes the plaintiff's cancer from the others. Is there one principle that delivers all four verdicts, perhaps by distinguishing proof of what caused an outcome from proof that a particular person performed an act, or are my Blue Bus and rodeo verdicts in conflict no matter how that distinction is drawn?

Please also test whatever principle you propose on a fifth case I cannot decide. The plaintiff was injured by a defective pharmaceutical; the drug is made by several manufacturers to an identical formula and package, and the plaintiff can prove only that Manufacturer A supplied sixty percent of the market in the relevant region, while nothing about the pill identifies its maker. Should she recover against Manufacturer A, and if so should recovery be for the whole loss or only sixty percent of it? What does your principle say about whether the underlying problem here is one about knowledge, or only one about markets and how to apportion a loss? I would prefer the answer as continuous prose, as though you were writing me a long email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
