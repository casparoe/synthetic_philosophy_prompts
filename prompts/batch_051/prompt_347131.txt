I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so tell me if I'm forcing this. There's a view I've run into that the real medium of a game is agency: what you appreciate in a game isn't the board or the graphics or the story, but the shape of the choices it puts inside you — the constraints, the stakes, the way each move closes others off. Chess looks like the cleanest case. But in The Queen's Gambit (the Netflix series, or the Tevis novel — either works) Beth Harmon's relationship to chess reads as compulsion and self-medication, not free play; her agency inside the game feels like a hole she keeps falling into. Give me a reading of the work through that agency-as-art lens. Does it show chess as art in that agency sense, or does it quietly show the view misses what it's like to be the person inside the game? Where does the lens light up a scene or a choice, and where does the work resist it enough that an honest critic has to say the lens is wrong? I keep getting stuck on the final Moscow match: is it an aesthetic achievement or just the addiction wearing her? And as a novelist I want to know whether a spectator watching Beth — the way a reader watches a character — can appreciate game-agency that Beth herself doesn't quite have; maybe watching a game is a different aesthetic act than playing it, and the show is about that gap. Dont cite anything. Do say what would change your mind: which consideration about Beth's agency, or about what games do, would flip your verdict, and where your reading is most likely to come apart.
