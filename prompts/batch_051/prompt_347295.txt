For some time I have found the conciliationist view in the epistemology of peer disagreement convincing. The thought is this: if I learn that someone who is my epistemic peer—as intelligent as I am, as familiar with the same body of evidence, and as careful in evaluating it—has reached the opposite conclusion, then I have no non-question-begging way to prefer my own judgment to theirs, so I ought to move substantially in their direction. The steadfast view denies this: I experience my own reasoning from the inside, but I have only their report of theirs, and that first-person access is enough to license my retaining my view.

Now suppose a specific development. Argument-mapping and machine-verified reasoning systems become cheap and ubiquitous enough that any disputant can hand the other a complete log of their reasoning—every premise, the weight attached to it, every inferential step, every point at which a different judgment could have been made. The logs are not summaries or after-the-fact rationalizations; they are the full path by which each side reached its conclusion, and both sides can verify that the other's log is internally coherent. Two peers inspect each other's logs, agree about what the logs contain, and yet each still concludes that the other took a wrong turn at an identifiable step, and that this step is precisely where the error entered.

Does conciliationism survive this development? If I can now locate exactly where my peer's reasoning departed from mine, am I still required to split the difference with them, or has the transparency of their reasoning broken the symmetry that grounded conciliationism in the first place?
