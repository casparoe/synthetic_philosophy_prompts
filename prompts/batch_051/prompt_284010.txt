I've been reading blogs and watching talks about life extension for a couple years now, and I've noticed that almost everyone I argue with is what I call a deathist. They don't just think the treatments won't arrive; they defend the idea that dying is good. If I bring up slowing biological aging, they give reasons: life would get boring, death is what gives our choices meaning, it's natural, society needs old people to step aside, you'd have to watch everyone you love age and die anyway. I've developed a rule for these conversations. I don't treat these reasons as arguments I need to answer. I treat them as rationalizations. My thinking is that a mortal creature who has to get up every morning and live with the fact that it will die is going to generate defenses of dying, because the alternative—really wanting something it can never have—would be a permanent low-grade misery. So the reasons people give are just the visible part of a coping mechanism, and engaging with them is like arguing with a symptom. I apply the rule especially to people over forty, where I figure the rationalization has had time to harden.

Is this actualy defensible? My sister says I'm doing exactly the motivated reasoning I accuse them of: I've already decided life extension is good, so I explain away every argument against it. But the difference, I think, is that I have an account of why their arguments would be systematically untrustworthy, not just a hunch. Is there a version of my rule that keeps what's useful without being unfair—maybe discounting their reasons by some amount instead of ignoring them? Or does the boredom argument have to be met on its own terms no matter who says it, and why?
