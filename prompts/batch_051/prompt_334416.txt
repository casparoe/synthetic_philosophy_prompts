I run a once-a-month discussion group of about fifteen people, mostly engineers and data analysts who have spent years reading LessWrong and similar forums. They are comfortable with simple formal notation and short arguments, but none of them has worked through social choice theory beyond a rough memory of Arrow's theorem. In two weeks I am leading one forty-five-minute session on Sen's liberal paradox, and I want you to design the teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

I want the session to make the impossibility feel forced rather than clever or ideological. I have seen this topic go wrong in two predictable ways: people come away thinking Sen showed that liberalism is self-contradictory, or they think the example is rigged by letting people care about what others do.

Please structure your response in the following named sections, in this order, with the rough lengths given:

1. Prerequisites and notation (about 120 words): what the audience must already have in view before the paradox is stated, the minimal notation to put on the board first, and which pieces of Arrow's framework I can skip because they are not load-bearing for Sen's result.

2. Order of ideas (about 300 words): the sequence in which the components must be introduced — individual preference orderings, a social decision function, the Pareto criterion, minimal liberalism as local decisiveness over a protected pair, and the resulting cycle — and what depends on what. I want the dependency structure, not a prose summary of the theorem.

3. The carrying example (about 250 words): the one worked case that carries the session. Tell me what structural features it must have and must avoid. In particular, tell me whether to use the standard Lady Chatterley's Lover example or a desexualized substitute with identical logical features, and lay out the two preference orderings and the two protected pairs so that the cycle can be read directly off the board.

4. Misconception map (about 300 words): the predictable confusions this crowd will form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off before it solidifies. At minimum, address: treating rights as especially intense preferences; confusing the result with Arrow's theorem; thinking the example is rigged by the nosy-preferences assumption; and thinking the problem disappears if one weakens transitivity to acyclicity or removes only one person's protected pair.

5. Check for understanding (about 100 words): one question or one-minute exercise I can run at the end that would distinguish someone who has understood the impossibility from someone who has merely memorized the conclusion.

6. What to leave out (about 100 words): the material I should explicitly decline to cover at this level — for example, whether rights should be modeled as protected spheres rather than as decisiveness over pairs — and one sentence I can use when someone tries to pull the discussion there.

Do not include a reading list or ask me to fetch any source; the plan should be runnable entirely from the page.
