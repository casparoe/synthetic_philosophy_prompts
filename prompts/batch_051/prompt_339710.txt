For some time I have been unable to settle whether languages, traditions, and art forms carry a value of their own, the kind of value that survives when the people who bear them become few, or no longer exist, or no longer want them in the old way. Here are my verdicts on specific cases, and I would like the principle behind them if one exists; if it does not, I want to know which of my verdicts I should revise.

1. A language has eleven remaining native speakers, all over seventy. The community asks for a grant to record it and to run after-school classes so grandchildren can learn greetings, songs, and some everyday speech. My verdict: fund it, without much hesitation.

2. A coastal town's wooden-boat building craft is no longer commercially viable; four elderly builders know it. A grant would pay two apprentices a year to learn from them. My verdict: fund.

3. A midsummer festival is still widely attended and matters to the town, but its original ritual meaning has largely been forgotten; locals describe it as just what they do. The town asks for public money to keep it going. My verdict: fund, though I suspect my reasons here are not the same as in 1 and 2.

4. A folk opera survives only in fragmentary manuscripts and early recordings; the last performers died decades ago, and there is no distinct descendant community. The only person asking for its revival is a scholar who wants it reconstructed for performance. My verdict: I lean no, but I cannot say exactly why this should be no when 1, 5, and 8 are yes.

5. A small museum holds a damaged, minor sixteenth-century altarpiece by an unknown painter, the only surviving work from a local workshop. Restoration would be expensive, and the piece is neither famous nor heavily visited. My verdict: restore. I feel this strongly and would not first ask whether visitor numbers justify it.

6. An annual public commemoration marks a battle at which one group's ancestors massacred another's. The descendants of the victims object; the commemorating group says the event is their tradition. My verdict: not as a public festival. A museum or historical exhibition might be fine.

7. A diaspora community wants to establish a festival for a tradition assembled about thirty years ago, partly from old-country elements and partly from new ones invented in the diaspora. Its members identify strongly with it. My verdict: fund it on the same terms as any older tradition; I do not care whether it is authentic in some historical sense.

8. The last fluent speaker of a small language died a year ago. Her niece half-remembers songs and set phrases, and asks for a small grant to record what she can before they vanish. No teaching program, no community use, just an archive. My verdict: yes, though I would want it done cheaply and without ceremony.

Is there a single principle, a criterion for when a language, tradition, or art form has cultural value of the kind that can justify public support, that delivers all eight of these verdicts? If one principle cannot do the work, give the smallest honest set of candidate principles that between them covers the most, state each precisely, and say what each clause is for. Then run the principle or principles against all eight cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated. Finally, tell me what your principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on:

9. A language has had no living speakers for over a century. A self-organized revival society, whose members have some ancestry from the group but no prior connection to its practices, asks for public funding for classes, signs, and ceremonies in the language. My instinct is no, but I cannot reconcile that with 1 and 7.
