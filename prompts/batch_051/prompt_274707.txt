ok so ive been staring at the way one of those big effective-altruism grantmaking orgs prioritizes causes, and the whole thing is a three-variable spreadsheet on the world. every candidate cause gets scored on importance (how much badness is at stake), tractability (how much progress you buy per dollar), and neglectedness (how little attention the problem already gets), then the three get mashed together into a single ranking and they fund down the list. i want to know what this practice presupposes, not whether the spreadsheet is "correct." like, does it presuppose all the goods involved — preventing blindness, ending factory farming, lowering the chance of human extinction — sit on one scale, or is it compatable with the idea that some values just cant be ranked against each other? does "tractability" quietly commit you to an engineering picture of social change, where problems are solved by interventions with measureable effects, rather than a politics picture with coalitions and legitimacy and all that? does "neglectedness" presuppose diminishing returns to attention, and is that an empirical assumption or a philosophical one? also, the three criteria are probly not independent — a cause might be tractable because its been neglected, or neglected because its intractable — so does combining them double-count something, or smuggle in the same consideration twice? reconstruct the implicit theory of value and of change the practice is committed to, distinguish what it strictly needs from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is underdetermined, so several rival theories would license it. then tell me what would actually have to change in the scoring if you swapped in a rights-based or relationship-based view of obligation, and whether the practice could survive without a single dimension along which causes get compared.
