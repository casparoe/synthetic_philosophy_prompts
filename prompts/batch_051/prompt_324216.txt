I'm drafting an essay for an alignment workshop, and I'd like comments on the current draft before I expand it. The essay argues that the standard question — whether iterated amplification and distillation preserves alignment as well as competence — is subtly misframed. I'd be grateful for comments in four areas: (1) whether my description of amplification and distillation is fair to how those ideas are usually understood; (2) whether the central argument is interesting and distinct enough to justify developing into a full paper; (3) which step in the argument is weakest, and how you would either patch it or attack it more forcefully; and (4) whether the coined term "interface amnesia" earns its keep or obscures more than it clarifies.

Draft: "Decomposition as an implicit theory of the task"

Consider a value-laden base task of a familiar kind: draft a fair promotions policy for a department. In iterated amplification, a human overseer decomposes this task into subtasks — collecting legal constraints, summarizing past promotion decisions, listing affected stakeholders and their interests, drafting candidate policies, and checking drafts against fairness criteria. The overseer can call on an AI assistant, including copies of it, to speed up subtasks, and each subtask can be decomposed recursively. The result is an amplified policy: a slow, expensive procedure that combines human judgment with machine assistance and, in the ideal case, handles the base task better than either a human or a machine alone.

The target of my essay is a step usually treated as an implementation detail: every decomposition must specify interfaces between subtasks. Each subtask has an input contract and an output contract, and these contracts fix what information is passed forward, what counts as acceptable output, and what is declared out of scope. In the promotions example, most of the fairness content is not in any subtask's output taken alone. It lives in the interface decisions: which stakeholders get listed at all, which fairness criteria the checking subtask applies, whether past practice is entered as a neutral baseline or as a systematically suspect pattern, and which trade-offs are allowed to surface for the overseer rather than being suppressed by the assistant. Decomposition, then, is not a neutral way of making a large task tractable. It is the point at which the decomposer implicitly commits to a theory of what the task really is.

Distillation does something that is not fully captured by saying it compresses the amplified policy into a fast model. The distilled model is trained to imitate the amplified policy's input-output behavior on training examples. The interfaces themselves — the contracts, the scope exclusions, the criteria — need not be represented in the trained model as revisable decisions; they are baked into whatever statistical regularities the model learns. On the training distribution, the model may be perfectly competent and may match the amplified policy's outputs exactly. Off distribution, it loses something the amplified system had: the ability to notice that an interface decision no longer fits the case and to renegotiate it. I call this interface amnesia. A fairness theory that was explicit in the amplified procedure becomes a set of unexamined habits in the distilled model.

If this is right, the familiar question gets the dependence backward. Decomposition does not preserve an alignment that already exists in the base task; it creates an alignment-relevant position, a placed bet about which considerations count and who may contest them. Distillation is the mechanism by which that bet is converted from an explicit, challengeable feature of a procedure into an implicit disposition of a model. The real question is not whether amplification followed by distillation preserves alignment, but where alignment resides in the amplified system, and whether distillation can be made to transfer that locus rather than only the input-output mapping.

One might object that nothing prevents us from training the distilled model to output the interface specifications as well, or to flag cases in which its assumptions may not fit. But that reply only moves the problem up a level: the meta-task of renegotiating interfaces will itself require interfaces between meta-level considerations and object-level policy, and those interfaces carry normative commitments of their own. The regress does not prove that distillation must fail; it suggests that the competence/alignment split is the wrong lens for the problem.
