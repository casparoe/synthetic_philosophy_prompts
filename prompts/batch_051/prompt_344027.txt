I write for a general-audience science-and-ideas outlet, and I'm trying to turn a messy reporting file into something like a theory of why some moral norms flip and others don't. I've got verdicts on a pile of cases, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them—and where you'd force me to revise.

Case 1: dueling. Arguments against it (it's absurd, it's illegal, it's unchristian, it gets smart people killed over nothing) had been around for centuries and did close to nothing. It ends, as far as I can tell, when honor flips: being a gentleman stops meaning "I'll meet any insult with pistols" and starts meaning "I'm too serious a person for that theater." Verdict: honor kills it; argument doesn't.

Case 2: footbinding in China. Same shape. Missionaries an scholars denounced it for centuries with no mass movement. Then roughly 1890–1920 it ends in a generation. I read the change as national shame: it became the marker of a backward China that elites didn't want to be seen defending. Verdict: honor/shame again, not argument.

Case 3: chattel slavery in the Atlantic world. Here's the wrench. Abolitionist arguments clearly did something—they built mass politics and made slavery a public moral problem, and I can't write that off as mere overflow from economics. But it also ends partly because the economies shifted and enslaved people made the system costly through revolt and flight, Haiti especially. Verdict: argument, interests, and coercion all matter. I won't reduce it to one.

Case 4: meat-eating. To my own ear the arguments against factory farming are strong and have been made for fifty years by people much smarter than me. In rich countries vegetarians are still maybe 5 percent. My verdict: bare argument is nearly inert until cheap, convincing substitutes show up—so that's interests doing the work.

Case 5: honor killings. This one sits next to Case 1 and won't fit. It's also an honor practice, but argument, legal penalties, urbanization, rising incomes don't end it; in fact the honor system that made dueling ridiculous in London seems to sustain this in parts of South Asia and the Middle East. So why does honor flip in one case and not the other?

So my puzzle is this: I believe argument alone was inert in cases 1, 2, and 4, but mattered in case 3; I believe honor explains 1 and 2, but honor also explains why 5 persists. Is there one principle of norm change that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up? And whichever principle you land on, tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed—say, the decline of public executions in Europe.
