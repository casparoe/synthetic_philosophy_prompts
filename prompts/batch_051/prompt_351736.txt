I'm teaching Spinoza's Political Treatise again and keep coming back to a passage in chapter 11 that reads almost like a constitutional voting clause, and I want to test whether the actual wording does the work Spinoza thought it did. Heres the passage (Elwes translation, ch. 11, sec. 3):

"But my intention is not to treat of every kind, but of that only, 'wherein all, without exception, who owe allegiance to the laws of the country only, and are further independent and of respectable life, have the right of voting in the supreme council and of filling the offices of the dominion.'"

Right after this he glosses the terms: "who owe allegiance to the laws of the country only" excludes foreigners, because they are "under another's dominion"; "who are independent" excludes women and slaves, because they are under the authority of men and masters, and also children and wards under their parents and guardians; "and of respectable life" excludes those "infamous from crime, or some disgraceful means of livelihood."

Suppose a small modern polity copies that sentence verbatim into its fundamental law. Three cases land before its court:

(a) a permanent resident who has renounced her original citizenship and says she owes allegiance only to this polity's laws, is self-employed, and has an unblemished reputation;
(b) a full-time employee whose contract gives his employer authority to direct his work during working hours;
(c) an unmarried woman of independent means who is under no one's legal authority, manages her own household, and is otherwise within the stated terms.

The words, read plainly, seem to include (a) and (c), and maybe exclude (b) if "independent" means "not under anybody's authority." But Spinoza's own gloss says "independent" is there to exclude women, and "who owe allegiance to the laws of the country only" is there to exclude foreigners. If the gloss and the words pull apart in cases he didn't have in mind, which governs? Argue it from the words, the purpose of the clause, what a reasonable reader takes the clause to mean, and what Spinoza would most plausibly have said if asked about (c) in 1677. Then tell me which approach should win, and why. I care less about the practical answer than about whether Spinoza's criteron is really a criterion, or just a list of the people he already wanted in and out with a principle painted over it.
