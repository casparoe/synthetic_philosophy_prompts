I'm drafting a teaching note for an undergraduate course on political ideas, and I want to pair Brave New World with Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis. Read Huxley's World State as a worked example of the post-historical condition: a global order in which the great ideological contests have been settled and what remains is the smooth administration of comfortable, satisfied life. Fukuyama's worry is that such a world produces the Last Man — a person who no longer has the appetite for recognition through struggle, for staking something on being taken seriously. Brave New World reaches a similar destination by a different route: not the victory of liberal democracy, but Fordist mass production, behaviorist conditioning, a caste system, and soma. Where does the novel anticipate Fukuyama's argument, and where does the different route change the conclusion? Is the World State an instance of the end of history, or the opposite — a society frozen because it has eliminated the desire for recognition that, on Fukuyama's account, drives history? Work through two scenes closely. First, Bernard Marx's resentment and timidity: he wants distinction but has no arena in which that drive can become anything. Second, the long exchange between Mustapha Mond and John the Savage, in which the Controller explains why high art, religion, and heroism must be suppressed while John defends poetry, danger, freedom, and even unhappiness. Then be honest about where the reading strains: Huxley may be diagnosing industrial technique, mass consumption, and behaviorist psychology rather than exhausted ideology, and his stable world is anti-democratic in ways that Fukuyama's hoped-for liberal peace is not. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the thesis leaves abstract — what it shows from inside about what a world without the struggle for recognition would feel like, and whether that price is one we should refuse to pay.
