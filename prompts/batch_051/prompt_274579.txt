So I co-run a reading group on the history of science with two people who have been at each other's throats for months about anachronism. One is a contextualist: she thinks that when we translate an old argument into modern concepts—saying Ptolemy's epicycles were basically a Fourier decomposition, or that Euclid was doing early number theory—we end up describing a text the original author would not have recognized, and we fool ourselves into a story of smooth progress where the genuinely hard conceptual shifts dissapear. The other is a rational reconstructionist: he thinks refusing modern language is just antiquarianism, and that the only way to see what a piece of old mathematics or science actually managed to do is to say, in our terms, what structure it was groping toward, even when the author had no access to those terms.

They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I got stuck producing the document they would both put their names on. Draft it. I want: (a) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign—about what it even means to call a reading anachronistic, about the difference between borrowing modern notation and importing modern commitments, about when presentism is and is not a sin; (b) a disagreements table with the remaining differences, each stated so its own side would accept the wording rather than feeling strawmanned; (c) the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them; and (d) for each crux, at least one concrete case or test both agree would bear on it—say, a close comparison of an original proof with its modernized rewrite, or tracing whether a term like 'force' keeps a stable referent across two traditions—with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Try to make it precise enough that a historian of science would recognize both positions, and spend some of the work seperating disagreements about substance from disagreements that are really just about vocabulary. Please write it in American English.
