I want a case built to order, on Woodward's interventionist theory of causation. Roughly the view: X is a direct cause of Y relative to a set of variables just in case there's a possible intervention on X that changes Y while all the other variables in the set are held fixed, where an intervention is a surgical manipulation—it has to cause X, it has to override X's other causes, any influence it has on Y has to go through X, and it has to be independent of anything else that causes Y. Now construct a single-paragraph medical or epidemiological scenario where two researchers agree on every observable statistic—the whole joint distribution over a treatment, an outcome, a biomarker, and how the treatment got assigned—but they disagree about whether a particular manipulation of the treatment counts as an intervention, and the whole disagreement traces to one background causal commitment: one of them reads the biomarker as a common cause of the treatment and the outcome, the other reads it as a downstream effect of the treatment. The point of the case is to pressure the circularity worry: surgicalness is supposed to be what we use to test what causes what, but judging whether a manipulation is surgical already seems to require settling a causal question. Make sure both researchers are being reasonable, not sloppy, and that the numbers themselves don't decide between them. Then walk the case through each of the intervention conditions and show how the two researchers get opposite verdicts from the same manipulation, say precisely which underlying commitment is doing the work, what happens if you remove that feature, and finish by saying how a Woodwards-style defender would respond to the charge of circularity here—do they have to reformulate the view, or can they just accept that the account is relativized to a variable set?
