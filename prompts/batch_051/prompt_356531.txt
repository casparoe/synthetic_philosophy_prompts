For an advanced metaethics seminar, write the specification of what a theory of the right-kind/wrong-kind reason distinction must do to count as adequate. The distinction is motivated by cases like this: a powerful demon credibly threatens to harm you unless you admire him, which gives you a reason to admire him without giving any reason to think he is admirable. The problem is acute for fitting-attitude (or buck-passing) accounts of value, which analyze a thing's being good roughly in terms of there being reason to have some favorable attitude toward it; unless the relevant reasons can be restricted to the right kind, the demon case seems to trivialize the analysis.

Produce the list of desiderata such a theory must satisfy. For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a commitment of a particular camp. The cases and distinctions I most want the list to engage include: the demon's threat and structurally similar cases (for example, being offered payment to admire someone); the contrast between object-given and state-given reasons and whether that contrast lines up with the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction; whether wrong-kind reasons are genuine normative reasons or only reasons in some attenuated sense; and the constraint that the theory not beg the question when it is imported into a fitting-attitude account of value. Make sure to identify which desiderata are in tension with one another and whether any natural subset is jointly unsatisfiable; that is the part I most want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on a survey of existing views.
