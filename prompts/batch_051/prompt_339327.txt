Build a case in which a two-intension semantic framework and an austere one-intension framework are forced to deliver different descriptions of the same identity sentence. The sentence should have the form "P is Q," with P and Q two scientific terms. The point of the case is that the one-intension framework cannot represent a difference that the two-intension framework can; the difference must fall out of how P and Q are introduced, not be stipulated.

Backdrop. The two-intension framework assigns each expression a primary intension and a secondary intension. The primary intension takes a centered world—a possible world considered as the world of the utterance, with a marked center—to an extension, by evaluating the expression as it would be interpreted at that world. The secondary intension takes a counterfactual world to an extension in the usual way. The one-intension framework assigns only the secondary intension and treats primary-intension talk as the history of how an expression acquired its reference, not as part of its meaning.

The specifications:

1. P and Q are two scientific or technical terms. Each is introduced into the language by an explicit reference-fixing description—call them D(P) and D(Q)—a description that fixes the extension of the term without being synonymous with it. Each term is a rigid designator, referring to the same thing at every counterfactual world.

2. In the actual world, D(P) and D(Q) single out the same kind (for example, a substance, a biological species, or a structural type). That they coincide is a genuine empirical discovery, so "P is Q" is a posteriori.

3. At every counterfactual world, P and Q refer to the kind that the actual world's D(P) and D(Q) select, so "P is Q" is necessary: its secondary intension is the function true at every world.

4. At some centered world considered as actual, D(P) selects one thing and D(Q) selects another, so "P is Q" is false at that centered world. Its primary intension is contingent.

5. The verdict divergence is therefore this: P and Q have the same secondary intension but different primary intensions. The one-intension framework must say that P and Q are semantically indistinguishable, since they share their only semantic value; the two-intension framework locates the semantic difference in their primary intensions.

Present the scenario in one paragraph, in a realistic scientific setting. Do not use Twin Earth, water/H2O, Hesperus/Phosphorus, or any other case standard in the literature, and do not generate the failure in (4) by the Twin Earth device of duplicating all superficial appearances while swapping one familiar kind for another. Avoid overt indexicals and demonstratives.

After the scenario, deliver the following in order. (i) State P, Q, D(P), and D(Q) explicitly. (ii) Give the primary and secondary intensions of P, of Q, and of "P is Q." (iii) Fill in the two-dimensional matrix for "P is Q": the diagonal and the relevant off-diagonal cells. (iv) Demonstrate that the case satisfies (1)–(5). (v) Identify the single structural feature doing the work, and say what happens if it is removed: in particular, what changes if the two descriptions are made synonymous with the terms rather than merely reference-fixing, and what changes if D(P) and D(Q) are made necessarily coextensive at every centered world. (vi) Close with a paragraph in which a defender of the one-intension framework attacks the case, arguing that the divergent primary intensions are not part of the meaning of P and Q. Nothing may be stipulated into place; each of the five specifications must be generated by the descriptions and the manner in which the terms are introduced.
