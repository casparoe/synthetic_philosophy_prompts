I have been using a distinction I would like examined before I build anything further on it. I call it the Rung Above Principle, and it concerns our reliance on AI systems for cognitive work.

Think of a cognitive task — calculating, translating, forming a diagnosis, drafting an argument — as arranged into levels of control. Level 0 is the production of the output. Level 1 is the capacity to recognise whether a level-0 output is right, good, or plausible: the sense that 20 × 0.4 = 80 must be mistaken, that a translation has dropped the register, that a diagnosis has ignored a symptom. In general, level n+1 is the capacity to evaluate level-n performance.

The principle is this: a person may delegate level n of a task to an AI system without net loss of understanding just when they retain a live, exercisable competence at level n+1. Where the delegated level is the highest level they actually command, delegation ceases to be a tool and becomes a replacement; the apparent competence is borrowed, and it disappears the moment the system fails or is withdrawn.

Two consequences follow, and I find the second disturbing. First, it captures what is right in familiar worries about calculators without condemning calculator use wholesale. An adult who already has number sense can outsource arithmetic while the level-1 judgement — that such a result cannot be right — stays in the user, and the tool remains subordinate. A child who lacks that judgement is outsourcing at their highest existing level, and the machine becomes, in the relevant respect, authoritative. Second, the push towards explainable AI may, on this principle, deepen the very erosion it is meant to prevent. If a system not only produces an answer but also supplies the level-1 rationale — the explanation that tells the user why the answer is acceptable — then it is offering to occupy the very rung the user needed to keep. The better the explanation, the more thoroughly the audit itself is outsourced.

I can see that the image of cleanly ordered levels may be doing too much work, and that 'net loss of understanding' is carrying a lot of weight. Before I commit to the principle, I would like an honest assessment. Is it a genuine, testable claim or a dressed-up restatement of something obvious? Has this already been worked out under another name — in discussions of scaffolding, metacognition, or de-skilling? Where does it break down, and what kind of case does it misdescribe? Please give the strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Finally, I would be grateful for your answer in British English.
