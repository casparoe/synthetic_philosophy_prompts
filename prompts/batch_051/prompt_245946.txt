I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep bumping into the old Plutarchan claim that biography is moral technology: that you become better by studying a life closely, and that the small stuff — a throwaway joke, a nervous habit, the way someone looses their temper — reveals character more reliably than the famous battles and trials do. Psychology has a friendlier, more modern cousin of this idea, namely that we imitate salient models and that narrative moves us more than precepts, but I'm not sure the Plutarchan version is even falsifiable. So: how would you test the claim that reading exemplary lives, as against hagiographies written mainly to make the subject look saintly, actually improves moral character and not just moral vocabulary? I want a concrete design — the conditions you'd compare, what you'd measure and when — and the result that would count for the Plutarchan claim versus the rival explanation that vivid narrative alone, whatever its content, makes people momentarily nicer. Then say what a positive result would and wouldn't settle; for instance, would it show the exemplar did the work, or just that intense stories move people? And be explicit: how confident are you in the design, and what's the one point you're least sure of?
