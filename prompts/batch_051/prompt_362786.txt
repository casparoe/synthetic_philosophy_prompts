I have been trying to get clear about a puzzle in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I would like you to tell me whether the following argument goes through and, if not, exactly which step fails.

The puzzle is usually called the problem of old evidence. Consider the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion: astronomers had measured this anomaly for decades before 1915, and when Einstein's general theory of relativity was found to predict essentially the observed value, this was widely taken to be strong confirmation of the theory. But a simple Bayesian argument seems to show that evidence already known before a theory is proposed can never confirm it:

(1) On the standard Bayesian account, evidence e confirms a hypothesis h just in case P(h | e) > P(h).

(2) If e is known before h is proposed, then at the moment h is proposed the relevant agent assigns e probability one, so P(e) = 1.

(3) If P(e) = 1, then for any h with P(h) > 0, we have P(e | h) = 1: a proposition of which one is certain remains certain when one conditions on any hypothesis one has not already ruled out.

(4) Bayes's theorem then gives P(h | e) = P(e | h)P(h)/P(e) = 1 × P(h)/1 = P(h).

(5) Hence, by the criterion in (1), old evidence cannot raise the probability of a newly proposed theory, and so cannot confirm it.

(6) But Mercury's perihelion looks like exactly such a case: it was old evidence, and it did confirm general relativity.

So either one of these steps fails, or the Bayesian must give a different account of what happened when general relativity was proposed. Which is it? If a step fails, identify it and explain why; if none fails, say how a Bayesian should retell the Mercury case so that the apparent confirmation is not written off as an illusion.
