Here is an argument I have been unable to diagnose. It begins from familiar facts about classical electromagnetism and ends seeming to contradict the claim that gauge-dependent potentials are unreal. Tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and why.

(1) In classical electromagnetism, the electric and magnetic fields E and B can be derived from a scalar potential φ and a vector potential A.
(2) The potentials are not unique: certain changes to them, called gauge transformations, leave E, B, and every observational prediction unchanged.
(3) If two representations differ only by a gauge transformation, no possible observation can distinguish them.
(4) If no possible observation can distinguish two representations of a system, any difference between them is surplus structure of the representation, not a real difference in the world.
(5) Therefore the gauge-dependent features of the potentials are not real; only gauge-invariant quantities, such as E, B, or loop integrals of A, correspond to real facts.
(6) But in the Aharonov-Bohm effect, a charged particle's interference pattern is shifted by the magnetic flux inside a solenoid, even though the particle travels only through a region where the magnetic field B is zero.
(7) The standard account attributes this shift to the vector potential A in that region, a quantity defined only up to gauge, rather than to any field at the particle's location.
(8) So if (1)–(5) are sound, the very quantity that explains a real, measured effect is unreal; therefore the inference from (1)–(5) must go wrong somewhere.

Is the argument valid? If it is sound, does the contradiction in (8) really follow? If not, which step fails, and is the problem a false premise or an ambiguity? If a term shifts meaning, give the strongest reading on which the premises are true and check whether the contradiction still follows.
