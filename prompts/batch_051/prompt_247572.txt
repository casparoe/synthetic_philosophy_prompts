I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in which I lay out what I take to be my settled views on loss aversion and the endowment effect, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate it to my colleagues. For present purposes, by "loss aversion" I mean the claim that, relative to a reference point, losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains in people's experienced and evaluated well-being—not merely in their choice behavior or in how they talk about options. Here is the set of commitments I want to defend together:

(1) A broadly preference-satisfaction or informed-desire account of welfare is correct.  
(2) Loss aversion is a genuine feature of human well-being, not a cognitive bias that should be debiased away.  
(3) The endowment effect is a rational consequence of reference-dependent loss aversion, once ownership and entitlement help set the reference point; it is not a mere artifact of experimental demand, unfamiliarity, or transaction costs.  
(4) In public policy, it is legitimate to respect loss aversion—for example, through default rules and through reluctance to impose losses on existing entitlements—but this legitimacy does not by itself justify preserving the status quo.  
(5) Loss aversion is rationally permissible rather than rationally required; people can reasonably be expected to re-reference when their circumstances change, and sometimes they ought to try to.  
(6) The willingness-to-accept/willingness-to-pay gap should not be read directly as a measure of welfare change, because income effects and entitlement effects contaminate it independently of genuine loss aversion.  
(7) Hedonic adaptation is pervasive and shows that our occurrent preferences are unstable guides to our own welfare across time.

I have deliberately written these as I would defend them in print, so please do not soften them for me. I suspect that some of these pull against one another, but I am genuinely unsure where the real tensions are: some pairs may conflict only given an unstated assumption I would reject, and some apparent conflicts may dissolve entirely. Please adopt the role of a senior moral philosopher with a working knowledge of behavioral economics and welfare theory, and think through the commitments painstakingly rather than quickly.

I want the deliverable to be an audit, not a lecture. Go through the commitments pairwise and say which pairs are in genuine tension, which are only apparently in tension, and why. Then rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, specify what would have to give and what the cost would be. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether that revision is worth making. If my whole framing is confused—if, for example, the distinction between a "bias" and a "legitimate preference" does less work than I am assuming, or if my use of "welfare" is doing multiple incompatible jobs—say so plainly instead of answering within the framing.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order and with roughly these proportions:

**Clarifications and definitions** (about 15%)  
**Genuine tensions, ranked by severity** (about 35%)  
**Apparent tensions that dissolve** (about 20%)
