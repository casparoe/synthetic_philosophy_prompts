In a seminar on the ethics of foreign aid, a classmate dismissed a proposal for unconditional cash transfers by quoting the proverb "Give a man a fish, and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish, and you feed him for a lifetime," and concluded that aid should finance skills training and institution-building rather than direct grants. I suspect the proverb is being made to do more work than it can, but I want to see what work it could be doing. Lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims this proverb might be expressing when used in aid debates — for example, claims about what causes poverty, about how poor households use one-off resources, about whose knowledge should guide interventions, about the difference between relieving symptoms and building capacity, about dependency and self-sufficiency, and about what donors owe as against what merely works. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but contestable, or likely false, and whether it would actually support favouring training over cash transfers. Then say which reading is really carrying the anti-cash-transfer conclusion in a move like my classmate's, and what empirical or moral question would decide whether that reading is true.
