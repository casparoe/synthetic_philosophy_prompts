Earlier this year the small construction-industry review I edit published its harshest assessment to date: a score of two out of five, a prediction that the firm would not be operating in eighteen months, and the dismissal that it was the worst contractor in the metro area. The firm is now threatening a defamation action, and for the first time I have had to turn the working assumptions behind our reviews into an explicit editorial policy. I find myself committed to the following five positions, and before I write them into our guidelines I want to know whether they actually hang together.

1. A statement should be classified as protected opinion or actionable fact by asking what a reasonable reader would understand it to assert about the world: if it is understood as the author's evaluation, it is protected; if it is understood as a claim about how things objectively are, it may support a defamation claim.

2. The deeper mark of a factual statement is verifiability. What mainly distinguishes fact from evaluation is that a factual assertion can be shown to be false, while a sheer evaluation cannot be true or false and should not be actionable.

3. A numerical rating is a recalcitrant case. A score of two out of five is not merely the reviewer's evaluation: it presents the firm as occupying a determinate place on a shared scale of quality, and when the firm's actual performance does not support that placement the score should be actionable even though it appears inside a review.

4. A forecast points in the other direction. To predict that a firm will fail within eighteen months is to assert nothing true or false at the time of publication, since the future has not happened; the prediction should therefore be treated as opinion even if events later vindicate or falsify it.

5. Hyperbole should remain protected. To call a firm the worst contractor in the metro area is loose rhetoric that no reasonable reader accepts literally, and the law should not force reviewers to speak with the precision of a surveyor.

Please audit this set rather than merely describe it. I want to know which pairs among these five are in genuine tension, and which pairs may look incompatible but actually sit comfortably together. For every genuine tension, rank it by severity, say which of the two commitments would have to give, and state the cheapest revision that would resolve it. Then identify the single change to this set that would remove the most strain overall.
