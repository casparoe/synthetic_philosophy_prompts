I keep coming back to superrationality, and I can't make it sit still with my gut.

The theory, as I understand it: in a symmetric one-shot prisoner's dilemma between two equally rational people, whatever move one of them settles on as "the rational move" is the move the other settles on too, because they're in identical positions with identical reasoning. So defecting isn't just a move, it's a policy — and it's a policy which, if both players adopt it, leaves each of them worse off than the policy of cooperating would. A superrational player therefore doesn't ask "given that the other player is fixed, what maximises my payoff?" but "which move can I endorse as the answer to this game?" And the only move that survives that is cooperate. I'm definately drawn to this. It's economical, it gives a clean answer in every symetric game without needing repetition or reputation, and it makes the prisoner's dilemma stop being a story about how rational people must screw each other.

But my gut says: in a genuinely one-shot game with a stranger, no communication and no future, defection is just rational. The other player's move is already determined by whatever is going on in their head. My deciding to cooperate doesn't cause them to cooperate, and it doesn't reach into their reasoning. If they defect, I'm better off defecting; if they cooperate, I'm also better off defecting. That's dominance, and it doesn't stop being dominance just because I'd prefer to live in the world where everyone cooperates. Superrationality seems to be sneaking in a linkage between my reasoning and theirs that the whole set-up rules out. The fact that a "rational twin" would mirror me is irrelevant if the actual person opposite me is a rational stranger who has simply concluded that defection is the dominant move.

I don't need a referee to declare a winner. I want to know what each side would have to show before I'm entitled to let the other side go. Is there a reading on which superrationality isn't claiming a causal link and the intuition isn't just refusing to think in terms of policies, so that both can be kept? And if not, which one is doing the better job of tracking what "rational" is supposed to mean here?
