You're an expert on research ethics and Bayesian reasoning, especially how equipoise is supposed to work. Think through this slowly — before you diagnose the argument, I'd like you to briefly brainstorm the different senses of "equipoise" people use, because I think part of the mess might be hiding there.

I was trying to defend clinical equipoise to my department's research ethics committee and ended up with an argument that seems to prove way too much. Now I can't find the exit. Here it is, numbered so we can pick on steps:

(1) A clinician may ethically enrol a patient in a two-arm randomized trial only if she is in genuine individual equipoise about the arms: she must not believe, on balance, that either arm is better for the patient in front of her.

(2) Individual equipoise is a state of balanced (or nearly balanced) belief, and it is fragile. As soon as a trial has any data at all — even an early trend that would never cross a stopping boundary — a rational Bayesian clinician's beliefs shift, and the arms no longer look exactly equal.

(3) So by (1), the clinician must stop enrolling (and arguably stop the trial) as soon as the first interim results come in, unless those results are perfectly symmetric.

(4) But a trial is informative precisely because its interim data gradually move belief away from balance. So the better the trial does its epistemic job, the less ethical it becomes to keep running it. A trial can be completed only if its data never swayed anyone, in which case it proved nothing.

(5) Therefore individual equipoise as an enrollment condition bans exactly the informative trials — the ones that change practice. That conclusion is absurd; nobody wants to say medicine learned most of what it knows from trials that were unethical to continue.

(6) The standard fix is to drop individual equipoise for clinical equipoise: the trial is ethical as long as the relevant community of experts remains genuinely divided about which arm is best. But this seems to just relocate the problem. Clinical equipoise holds exactly when competent experts disagree; that means there are real experts who believe arm A and real experts who believe arm B. Each of those experts, if she is honest, has an individual lean about the patient in front of her. So by (1), none of the people who know enough to be in the equipoised community is actually permitted to enrol patients. Clinical equipoise makes trials ethical precisely when nobody is allowed to run them.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I don't want to give up the idea that randomized trials can be ethical, and I'd rather not give up the idea that a doctor's personal belief matters for what she may do to a patient. Is the flaw in (1), in (2), in the move from (5) to (6), or somewhere subtler — maybe in what "better for the patient in front of her" means in a trial context? Whatever premise you reject, price the alternatives: what was that premise doing, and can a repaired standard still do it?
