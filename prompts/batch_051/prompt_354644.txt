I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I have been trying to think through a feature of actually existing democracy that is so familiar it is rarely examined for what it presupposes. The feature is party politics as it currently operates. Elections are contested by parties; legislatures are organized through party membership; a party platform bundles together dozens of positions that have little internal connection to one another; and party discipline can require a representative to vote against her own considered judgment about the question actually before her. Voters describe themselves as supporting or identifying with a party, and after an election the winning party claims a mandate for its entire platform.

The tension that interests me is that this institution seems to treat political preference as both discovered and manufactured. Ordinary political language says parties transmit a will that already exists in the electorate, but the functioning of parties plainly assumes that parties also shape that will: they draft the platforms, they enforce conformity, and they supply voters with ready-made positions on issues the voters may never have given independent thought. A sharper statement of the worry comes from Simone Weil, who held that a party is less a vehicle for pre-existing opinion than a machine for producing collective passion; that party discipline suppresses individual judgment at precisely the points where judgment is most needed; and that a party, because its true object is its own growth rather than truth or justice, carries a tendency toward totalitarianism.

I am not asking for a history of parties or a program of institutional reform. I want to know what the ordinary practice, with its platforms, whipped votes, party identification, and mandate claims, presupposes about collective will, political judgment, and representation. Does it presuppose that a collective will exists before party activity and is merely channeled by it? Or does it presuppose that such a will has to be constructed, and if so, what view of legitimacy permits an institution to construct the very will it then presents itself as representing? Does the practice of bundling unrelated commitments into a single platform presuppose something about the coherence of voters' political judgments? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, say where its various parts presuppose inconsistent things, and mark the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, tell me which features of the institution would have to change if Weil's diagnosis were accepted, and which standard justifications for party politics that diagnosis would foreclose.
