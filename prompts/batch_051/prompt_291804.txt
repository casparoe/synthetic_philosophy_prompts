Here's the thing I keep going back and forth on. I accept the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem—it's not a loose analogy, it's a proof. With at least three candidates, unrestricted strict preferences, a single winner, no ties, and unanimity respected, any rule where honest voting is always your best move is a dictatorship. I find that compelling. But I also have a conviction I can't shake: a group of people ought to be able to pool their preferences into a decision without the method being gameable and without just appointing a dictator. That's not a nice-to-have; it's the whole point of voting.

I don't want to be told "the theorem is right, get over it," and I don't want to be told "it's irrelevant because real elections use two-party primaries" or whatever. I want to know what each surrender costs. If I keep the theorem and drop the intuition, what does that actually commit me to about every voting reform—does it collapse into "all methods are strategic, full stop," and how bad is that in practice? If I keep the intuition and weaken the setup instead—allow ties, random tie-breaking, use cardinal ballots like approval or range, or only demand that strategy-resistance hold approximately rather than in principle—which of those loopholes does the least damage to the original idea, and which one secretly gives up the thing I cared about? And is there a reading of "fair" or "decision" on which the theorem and the intuition don't really collide, or is one of them just wrong?

Also be explicit about how confident you are in the tradeoffs you're describing, and single out the point you're least sure of.
