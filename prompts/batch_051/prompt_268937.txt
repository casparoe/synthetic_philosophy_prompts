I help run a small philosophy discussion group — mostly grad students, a few of them in effective altruism-adjacent circles, none of them entomologists — and next month we're doing insect welfare. The whole session is built around the scale argument: there are something like 10^18 or 10^19 insects alive at any time, and even if you think it's very unlikely any individual insect is sentient, the numbers might swamp the uncertainty, so insect suffering could dominate our priorities. People in the group already split hard on this, but I suspect they're splitting for different reasons and talking past each other.

What I'd like from you is a 'which position do you actually hold?' quiz/decision tree for the session — 8 or 9 questions, each a concrete case (a mosquito biting you; a pesticide that saves a crop but probably kills a huge number of aphids and caterpillars; a malaria gene drive that sterilizes mosquitoes; a donation choice between bednets and an insect welfare org; that kind of thing) with 2–4 answer options, none of which mentions a philosophical label. The scoring key should sort people into the positions the answer patterns actually reveal: expected-value fanatic about insects, low-probability threshold, moral-uncertainty discounter, sentience-capacity skeptic, precautionary pluralist (who cares more about avoiding egregious harm than maximizing expected welfare), and a responsibility-first view that counts only insects we farm or otherwise create — plus whatever else the logic forces you to add. Build the tree from the logical space first: which questions genuinely seperate people, which combinations of answers are empty, and for each empty combination say whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Flag any two positions your questions can't pull apart. Every node should be a case or a plain claim, usable by someone who hasn't read the insect welfare literature — no 'expected value' or 'moral uncertainty' phrasing in the questions themselves. If a leaf has no standard name, call it 'unnamed' and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Make the first question the one that seperates people who think the low probability basically settles it from people who think the probabilities and the numbers have to be traded off somehow.
