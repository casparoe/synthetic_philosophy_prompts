I am a high school junior and one of two co-leaders of my school's Political Philosophy Society. We are organizing a structured panel for a school-wide civics event on how people should think about shared resources. The two positions on the panel are a Hardinian position, which treats the tragedy of the commons as the default fate of resources open to many users, and an Ostromian position, which holds that ordinary communities sometimes govern shared resources sustainably without full privatization or centralized state control. Rather than let the two sides talk past each other in front of an audience, I want them to start from a single document both camps have signed.

Please draft that document: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed Hardinian and a committed Ostromian. In an adversarial collaboration, the two camps commit to stating what they can both sign, stating their remaining disagreement in wording each side accepts as fair to its own view, and specifying what evidence or argument would actually move at least one of them.

The statement should contain the following parts.

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. The claims should be specific, not safely empty. They should cover at least these points: the difference between pure public goods, which are non-excludable and non-rivalrous, and common-pool resources, which are subtractable but difficult or costly to exclude people from; the logic of free-riding and why voluntary provision of a pure public good tends to be undersupplied; what Hardin's overgrazed pasture establishes and does not establish, given that the example assumes a pasture open to all with no communication, no monitoring, and no enforceable rules, so that each herder gains the full benefit of adding an animal while the cost is spread over everyone; and what each side must concede to the other before the disagreement properly begins.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one proposition on which the camps divide, then state the Hardinian view and the Ostromian view in words each side would accept as accurate for itself. Include rows on the scope and robustness of community-based governance: whether it scales beyond small, stable communities with shared norms; whether it survives outside shocks, migration, or conflicting interests; whether the monitoring and sanctioning that sustain it are themselves public goods that raise a second-order free-rider problem, meaning people would like the rules enforced but would prefer not to pay the cost of enforcing them; and whether state regulation or well-defined private property is the more reliable fallback when community governance fails.

3. A section on cruxes. State the sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move one side or the other, and for each crux describe the kind of evidence or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it. Where feasible, record each side's prediction in advance, so this is a real preregistration rather than a list of things they politely agree to disagree about.

4. A brief analysis of which parts of the dispute are verbal or definitional and which parts remain substantive once the vocabulary is fixed. In particular, address whether the conflict is partly about using commons for two different setups — an open-access regime with no rules versus a common-property regime with enforceable community rules — and whether Hardin's idea of coercion that the users themselves accept and Ostrom's graduated sanctions may describe much the same practice.

As you draft, pay attention to the drafting itself: flag places where an agreed claim came out slanted toward one camp on a first attempt and had to be repaired, and say how it was repaired. Do not include citations or a reading list; the value of the document is in the precision of its wording. Please write the answer in American English.
