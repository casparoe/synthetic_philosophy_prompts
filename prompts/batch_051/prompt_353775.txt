I'm a software engineer building a text classifier that tags political speeches and journalism according to the stories of a nation's past that they draw on, and the category I keep reaching for is myth — as in the Lost Cause myth or the Dunkirk myth. I need a working definition of myth in that national sense, one that separates it from a bare falsehood, a deliberate lie, and a merely one-sided account of real events, so that several annotators can apply the label consistently to borderline material. The cases I need the definition to handle are these:

(a) the Confederate Lost Cause narrative, which remembers the Confederacy as a doomed defense of states' rights and Southern honor rather than slavery;
(b) the Dunkirk story as used in British public life, where a military collapse and evacuation are remembered as a triumph of civilian courage and national character;
(c) Masada, where a real Roman siege of Jewish rebels became a modern story about a people who would rather die than be enslaved;
(d) the claim that the 2020 U.S. presidential election was stolen;
(e) the Apollo missions as a story of American ingenuity and frontier spirit; and
(f) a narrowly factual, well-sourced claim such as that Allied troops were evacuated from Dunkirk in 1940.

I am confident that (a) is a myth and that (d) is not, and I have no settled view about (b), (c), and (e). I also suspect the word is doing two jobs at once — one about historical accuracy and one about what a story does for a community — so if the cases split, tell me whether the fix is two definitions rather than one. Propose a definition, run it through all six, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts.
