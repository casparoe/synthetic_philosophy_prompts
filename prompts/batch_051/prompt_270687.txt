I've been stuck on something in an epistemology class, and its making me realize how weird our ordinary reactions are. Take two doctors: one really understands why a treatment works, the other just read the answer somewhere and now believes it for no good reason. Both prescribe the same drug, the patient recovers, and the future course of action would be exactly the same because neither is ever asked to explain or defend there belief. Yet we'd obviously rather be treated by the first one — we'd trust them more, praise them more, and if something went wrong for unrelated reasons we'd blame the second guy way harder. We'd probably fire the lucky guesser even though the outcome was identical.

Plato basically notices this in the Meno: true belief and knowledge steer action equally well, since someone with a mere true belief about the road to Larissa can point you there as reliably as someone who actually knows it. So what explains the asymmetry? If the belief is true and the behavior in every future respect is the same, what extra good does knowledge add? The stock answers — stability under challenge, ability to give reasons, reliability in nearby counterfactual cases — all seem to smuggle in differences that might never materialize in the case at hand. So is our preference for knowledge justified, or is it just epistemic snobbery we've never managed to shake? I want a real answer, not a survey: find the strongest justification you can, test it against cases where the knower and the lucky true-believer are indistinguishable in every respect that will ever show up, and then say whether you'd keep the asymmetry or give it up.
