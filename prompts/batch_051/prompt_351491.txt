You are a philosopher of technology who works on the design of social institutions and knows Dennett's later writing on AI. Think carefully: begin by listing the relevant actors and their incentives, then walk through the scheme step by step.

A legislator, persuaded by Dennett's worry that the most dangerous AI is not an autonomous superintelligence but a flood of counterfeit people — artificial interactants that pass as human and exploit our trust — proposes the following scheme:

(1) Any AI system whose output is designed, or is routinely used, to be mistaken for a human interlocutor must be registered as a counterfeit person.
(2) All output of a registered system must carry an automatically detectable, tamper-evident provenance marker, and anyone who relays that output downstream must preserve the marker, on pain of strict liability.
(3) Any person or organization that deploys or relays an unregistered counterfeit person is strictly liable for the harm caused by the resulting deception, regardless of intent.

Walk through how this scheme would actually behave. Who registers and who evades; where the marking requirement gets gamed, chokepointed, or circumvented; what it does to the supply of and demand for counterfeit people and to the trustworthiness of online communication; and how it differently affects small developers, large platforms, and state actors. Identify the first and most damaging failure modes, and specify which, if any, could be fixed by changing one element of the scheme without gutting its purpose. End with a judgment about whether the scheme would protect the trust that Dennett thinks makes human communication and institutions work, or would make things worse.
