Over the past year my aesthetics reading group has been working through the literature on imaginative resistance, and we keep ending at the same impasse: everyone agrees there is a phenomenon, but nobody agrees about what would explain it. The standard starting point is a story that asks readers to imagine, say, a commander who orders the massacre of a village and is presented by the narration as having done the right thing. Readers who effortlessly imagine dragons, faster-than-light travel, and narrators who contradict the story's own facts find themselves balking here. The two familiar camps are the can't view, on which some feature of evaluative concepts or of the architecture of imagination blocks readers from imagining the deviant proposition as true, and the won't view, on which readers could do it but refuse to, much as they might refuse a loaded premise in a thought experiment. Around these poles sit a tangle of refinements: accounts on which the author, not the reader, has gone wrong; accounts on which the real issue is what it takes for a fiction to make something true in fiction; accounts on which the phenomenon splits into several; and accounts on which the whole problem is an artifact of asking readers to do two incompatible things at once. The group has also kept tripping over adjacent cases: fictions that treat a nothing-special object as ravishingly beautiful, or a tired pun as hilarious, produce something like the same balking, but only sometimes and with a different feel.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the parties disagree about which cases count as the phenomenon rather than about how to explain a shared set of cases. Some have in mind explicit local stipulations, such as a narrator announcing that a particular atrocity was right; others have in mind the wholesale uptake of a work's evaluative outlook, such as a novel that makes a murderer's perspective seductive; still others have in mind the felt inability to shift one's evaluative concepts for the duration of a fiction. If that is right, the can't/won't standoff is not one substantive disagreement but several different questions — one about cognitive architecture, one about motivation and refusal, one about the metaphysics of fiction — sharing a label. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: what kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it verbal, about the term imaginative resistance itself? A genuine empirical or psychological disagreement about what ordinary readers actually do? A disagreement about what should count as an explanation in aesthetics? Or a debate running on a hidden normative premise, such as a view about whether we should be able to imagine wicked things as right, that the participants never put on the table? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a taxonomy of cases, an experiment, a formal account of truth in fiction, retiring the can't/won't vocabulary, or something else? Be specific about which distinctions or findings would move the debate, and which would not.
