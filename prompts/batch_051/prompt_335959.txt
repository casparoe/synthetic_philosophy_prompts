I've spent to long in rationalist-adjacent forums where someone trots out Plato's Meno as a way to explain why LLMs seem to know things they weren't explicitly trained on. The argument goes like this: Socrates gets an enslaved boy with no geometry training to solve the doubled-square problem by asking him questions. Right before the demonstration, in the Perseus text of Plato's Meno (82b), Socrates tells Meno: "Now observe closely whether he strikes you as recollecting or as learning from me." The modern version says the boy's correct answers are latent and Socratic questioning is just the retrieval mechanism; therefore mathematical knowledge isn't learned, it's recollected. By analogy, a transformer's correct answers are latent in its weights and prompting is retrieval, not the model learning or reasoning on the fly. So we should be platonists about math, and maybe about LLM knowledge too. I'm suspicious, but this analogy keeps doing real work in arguments about whether a model that solves a novel math problem has "understood" it or is just eliciting what was already in there.

Audit the analogy. I want a table: what maps to what—Socrates, Meno, the slave, the diagram, the questioning, the boy's prior training, the theory of recollection, maybe even the immortal soul if that's carrying weight. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended inference from the ones that don't. The passage itself sets up an observational criterion: recollecting versus learning. What evidence would actually distinguish those in the Meno, and what evidence would distinguish retrieval from new inference in a transformer? Does the Meno argument for recollection support mathematical platonism, and does the LLM version inherit that support? Finally, say what would change your mind—which argument, experiment, or consideration would make you flip your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail.
