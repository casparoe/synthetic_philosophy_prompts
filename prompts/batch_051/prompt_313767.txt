In my philosophy of law course this term, the maxim "ignorance of the law is no excuse" has appeared in three quite different argumentative roles. In a classroom hypothetical involving a defendant who breached a newly enacted and barely publicized regulation, it was used to settle the question of criminal liability. In a discussion of the rule of law, it was offered as a structural principle — something about legal authority being independent of anyone's actual knowledge. And in a debate about whether it is fair to punish someone for breaking a rule they could not have known, it was repeated as if it were itself a moral argument. I suspect the phrase covers a family of distinct claims, and that the fairness debate turns on which member of the family is doing the work in each context.

What I would like is a menu of the distinct precise theses this maxim could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical. I am especially interested in readings that separate claims about the law's validity, liability doctrine, moral culpability, what citizens can reasonably be expected to know, and the legitimacy of a legal system that imposes liability without a genuine notice requirement — but please do not treat that list as exhaustive. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false or at least deeply questionable, and identify a case or test that would cleanly discriminate it from its neighbors. Then return to the fairness objection: which reading or readings of the maxim would actually need to be true for the saying to answer the charge that it is unfair to bind people to rules they could not have known, and do those readings survive once they are made explicit? A menu of candidate precisifications with verdicts would serve me better than a single settled interpretation.
