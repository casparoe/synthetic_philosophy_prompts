I am taking a course on Augustine's ethics, and I have to defend, in seminar, a reconstruction of his position on lying. The reconstruction I was given runs like this: a lie is (a) an assertion that is false, (b) made by a speaker who believes it to be false, (c) with the intention of deceiving the hearer; and (d) every such lie is a sin, though lies differ in gravity, and the prohibition admits of no exception — not even a falsehood told to save an innocent life. The standard test case is the person who, asked by a would-be murderer whether his intended victim is hiding in the house, answers that he is not. On the reconstruction, Augustine says he must not say that, whatever the consequences.

The case I keep turning over sits just outside the definition. Suppose I am sheltering a neighbor, and someone who plainly means to kill him asks me whether he is in my house. The neighbor is in fact in the cellar. I reply, truthfully, "I saw him heading toward the river half an hour ago." I believe every part of what I say, and it is true; I choose this sentence precisely because I know the man will infer that the neighbor is no longer here and will go search the river. Work through the criteria one by one and tell me whether, on the reconstruction, this is an Augustinian lie. Where the verdict turns on how "intention of deceiving the hearer" is to be read — whether it requires intending the hearer to accept the content of the assertion, or only intending to produce a false belief by some means — say so explicitly, and say which reading Augustine's overall position needs and why. Then tell me whether this case is a counterexample to the criteria as stated, or a reason to distinguish lying from the wider class of intentional deception; if the criteria need amending, propose the amendment and test it against the murderer-at-the-door case.
