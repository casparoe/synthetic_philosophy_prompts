So im in a moral psychology seminar and we've been doing sentimentalism, and I've basically landed on Adam Smith's idea that conscience is an internalized impartial spectator. The way I read him, you start by sympathizing with how the people around you actually react to your conduct, then you correct for their bias and self-interest until you've built up an imagined observer who sees your situation from the outside — and the voice that approves or condemns you from inside is just that spectator internalized. I want to stress-test this before I write my paper on it. What would genuinely count against the view? I'm not after the stock rationalist objection that feeling alone can't justify norms. I mean the more concrete stuff: what we see in moral development, psychopathy, shame, moral progress, or the way people's consciences mostly track whatever their group already thinks, that would be hard for a spectator-based account to handle. Which of those would a Smithian have to concede count as real damage, and which would they say the theory already predicts? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if the position has been protected so much that it doesn't risk anything anymore, and whether that's actually a problem for a psychological account of conscience. Personally I'd be moved more by a case where someone's impartial spectator sincerely endorses something awful than by the abstract worry that the whole thing is circular.
