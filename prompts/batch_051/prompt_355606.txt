there's a puzzle i keep chewing on after a forum argument about whether you can be praiseworthy for doing the right thing for the wrong reasons, and i think the law is secretly confused about it in an interesting way. criminal law cares enormously about what was going on in your head: A shoots B, and whether that's murder, manslaughter, or excused is mostly a story about A's mental state. but the textbook doctrine says motive is irrelevant to guilt — only mens rea, the intent or knowledge you acted with, counts — and yet provocation and sentencing both bring motive and emotional state right back in. tort law runs the other way entirely: if your negligent act causes an injury, it mostly doesn't matter whether you were indifferent or genuinely trying your best, and the eggshell-skull rule says you take your victim as you find them, full stop. ordinary moral evaluation does a third thing: we want to praise the person who returns the wallet simply because returning it is right more than the person who returns it because a security camera is pointed at them, even though the observable act is identical. and then there are the reversal cases like Huck Finn deciding not to turn Jim in while thinking he's doing wrong, where a lot of us still want to call the act morally worthy, which sits awkwardly with any simple requirement that the agent be motivated by duty.

so: what theory of moral worth, or of acting for the right reasons, is the criminal law implicitly commited to? is it one coherent theory, or does it lurch between a roughly Kantian picture where the quality of the will is what counts and a roughly consequentialist picture where what counts is how dangerous or defective the conduct was? if we actually took the Kantian line — that an action has moral worth only when you do it because it's right, not because it happens to serve your inclinations — what would have to change in how the law assigns culpability, and in how we hand out praise and blame more generally? i'd also like you to flag where the practice is undertermined, where several different theories would license the same legal rules, because my prior is that the law doesn't have a single hidden philosophy so much as a pile of patches that built up over time.
