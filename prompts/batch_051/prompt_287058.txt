I write about behavioral economics and the science of decision-making. I’m preparing an essay on the Ellsberg paradox and what ambiguity aversion shows about rationality, and in the course of reporting I’ve found that my own commitments have settled into a fairly specific package. Before I let those commitments organize the essay, I want them audited for coherence.

Here is what I find myself holding:

1. I take subjective expected utility theory to be the correct normative core for decision under uncertainty. Specifically, I accept Savage’s sure-thing principle — the requirement that a preference between two acts should not depend on what the acts deliver in outcomes where they agree — and I think rational preferences should be representable as maximizing expected utility relative to a single probability function and a single utility function.

2. In the two-urn version of the Ellsberg problem, where one urn has 50 red and 50 black balls and the other has 100 balls in an unknown red–black mix, I would bet on red from the known urn rather than on red from the unknown urn, with equal stakes. I don’t think this preference is a bias to be explained away.

3. In the three-color version, where an urn contains 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportions, I would take both cautious bets: red rather than black, and black-or-yellow rather than red-or-yellow, with equal stakes. I regard both choices as rational.

4. I also believe the pair of choices in (3) cannot be represented by any single assignment of probabilities to red, black, and yellow.

5. I’m attracted to robustness as a criterion of choice: when evidence leaves a range of probability models open, it can be rational to prefer an act that performs acceptably across that range to one that is optimal under a single model but fragile across the rest.

6. I think risk and ambiguity are different in kind. A risk attitude is a matter of how utility curves over known chances; an ambiguity attitude is a distinct, legitimate component of rational choice — not an error, and not reducible to the curvature of the utility function.

Please audit this set. Which commitments pull against one another, and which pairs are only apparently in tension? Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs. Finally, which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent, and what would I lose by making it?
