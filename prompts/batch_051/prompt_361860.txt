I have a policy about how I handle arguments for moral considerability, and I want to know whether I am entitled to it. In the abstract I accept that whichever capacity grounds moral status — sentience, interests, or the felt texture of experience — is graded. It does not plausibly switch on at a clean boundary between a thermostat, a honeybee, and a pig; there is likely a continuum of responsiveness, integration, and self-relevance. But when I actually encounter a claim that some class of beings matters morally — that farmed insects suffer, that wild invertebrates have a welfare worth tracking, or that a sufficiently integrated artificial system could be wronged — I do not let my beliefs track that continuum. I apply a two-bin evidential filter. If the proposed patient falls below a rough threshold of behavioral and neural complexity, I assign the claim near-zero weight and stop engaging with it; I treat it as noise rather than as an open question, even when I cannot refute it. If it falls above the threshold, I treat the claim as live and update in the ordinary way. The result is that my moral beliefs are binary while my background picture of the world is not.

I adopted the threshold because I distrust my own ability to weigh arguments about marginal forms of mind. I have noticed that intermediate judgments like "some but extremely little" are unstable: they expand or contract depending on what the conclusion would cost me. I also suspect that anyone who says a cricket's pain counts 0.003 as much as a pig's is manufacturing precision. But I can describe the same policy less charitably: I have drawn the cutoff at exactly the boundary that protects the way I already eat, buy, and plan, and I am using an evidential rule to launder a conclusion I settled in advance.

Is this policy rationally defensible, or is it motivated reasoning with a threshold painted over it? Under what conditions is it legitimate to round a graded quantity to a binary in belief-updating, and what would a better version of the rule look like — for instance, a separate low-weight channel for near-threshold claims, or an asymmetric cutoff that errs toward over-inclusion, or a rule keyed to the severity of the alleged harm rather than the complexity of the patient? And how can I tell, from the inside, whether my threshold tracks a genuine difference in the reliability of the arguments or merely the edge of what I am willing to give up?
