Fair warning, this came out of an argument with a friend who runs a critical-thinking workshop, and I've been turning it over for a week. He says any view on which critical thinking is a moral duty hangs itself. Formally: (1) If critical thinking is a duty, then to any belief you hold, standing or not, you owe it the same treatment—you treat the belief as open to doubt until it survives critical scrutiny. (2) "I am morally required to think critically" is itself one of your beliefs. (3) So the duty applies reflexively: you must treat that belief as open to doubt. (4) But you can't scrutinize that particular belief without using the very standards it commends, and anything you produce will either presuppose those standards or fall short of the duty's own bar. (5) The duty itself says that a belief which can't clear scrutiny without begging the question should be downgraded at minimum, maybe dropped. (6) So the duty requires you to doubt the duty. (7) And a person can't be bound by a duty they are rationally required to doubt, since acting on a duty presupposes taking yourself to have it. (8) So either there is no duty to think critically, or there is but no one is ever required to follow it. The conclusion feels like it would eat the premise of the whole workshop. I'm inclinced to say the slip is somewhere around (4) or (7), but I can't put my finger on it, and I'm worried the fix is going to force me to admit that critical thinking is just a value and not anything like a duty. Tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs; if the honest answer is "yes, it's merely a value, not a duty," say that straight. Keep it under 350 words.
