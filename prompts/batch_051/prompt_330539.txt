I'm writing a position paper for a cognitive science seminar on language and innateness, and I want an external consistency check before I circulate it. My settled convictions, more or less: (1) The poverty of the stimulus argument identifies a real epistemically underdetermined learning problem for human syntax, not merely an artifact of idealized training data. (2) The standard nativist response—innate, language-specific constraints on the class of humanly possible grammars—is broadly correct. (3) Recent neural network and Bayesian models are importantly better at extracting distributional structure than their 1980s predecessors, and they have shown that some classic textbook examples of underdetermination can be learned with the right biases. (4) The fact that such models learn does not, by itself, refute the poverty of the stimulus argument, because it simply relocates the explanatory burden to the model's inductive biases and training objective. (5) A complete account of language acquisition probably needs innate constraints, but they need not be encoded as an autonomous Universal Grammar module; they could be a byproduct of more general constraints on memory, categorization, or action planning.

Could you go through these the way a tough but fair reviewer would? I want an audit, not a lecture: which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which are mutually reinforcing? For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what it would force me to revise, and identify the least costly revision that preserves the overall position. If I had to keep four of the five and revise one, which should it be? Please also say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
