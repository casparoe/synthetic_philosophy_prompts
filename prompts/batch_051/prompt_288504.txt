I've somehow volunteered to run a seventy-five-minute session for a mixed undergrad reading group on Douglas Hofstadter's idea of tangled hierarchies and the inviolate level, with Escher's Drawing Hands as the carrying image. Half of them are CS students who know recursion but roll their eyes at philosophy, and the rest are art students who think it's a neat optical trick. Nobody's read Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I don't want to lecture at them from the book. I know the rough shape of the idea: a tangled hierarchy is one where levels that usually point one way end up looping back across each other — a hand drawing the hand that draws it, a mind studying the brain that produces it — and the inviolate level is whatever has to stay outside the loop so the loop is visible at all, instead of everything just bottoming out. But I don't trust my sense of what depends on what.

Plan how I should teach this, not the explanation itself. What idea has to be in place before Drawing Hands can do real work, and what exactly should the drawing be made to carry — and what should it not be asked to carry? Which misconceptions should I expect (say: that it's just an infinite regress; that it proves hierarchies are fake; that the inviolate level is a mystical self; that it's the same as plain recursion in a programming language) and exactly where in the sequence should I head each one off, rather than trying to handle them all at once? I also need a closing question that distinguishes a student who got the loop from one who just found the picture clever, and a clear statement of what I should openly refuse to explain at this level so the hour dosen't drown.

The one thing I'm most anxious about is that the inviolate level will come across as me smuggling in a deus ex machina at the end. Build the sequence so that by the time I name it, it feels like the thing everyone had already been standing on.
