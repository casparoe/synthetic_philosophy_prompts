I've been going round in circles with my flatmate about whether not doing something can be a cause in the strict sense, not just the loose everyday sense. She thinks absences can't do anything — a cause has to be an actual occurrence with some physical story linking it to the outcome — whereas I'm inclined to say an omission is a cause whenever the outcome counterfactually depends on the thing that wasn't done. The stock examples (the gardener who doesn't water the plant, the lifeguard who doesn't jump in) have stopped doing any work, because each of us can just re-describe them to fit our view, so I'd rather have a fresh case of our own to pick apart.

Construct a thought experiment to order. It should be a single realistic, ordinary setting (a workplace, a hospital, a home — no trolleys, no physics experiments) in which one person's specific omission counts as a cause of a bad outcome on a counterfactual/difference-making account — had the omitted act been performed, the outcome would not have occurred, given the background facts — but does not count as a cause on a mechanistic/physical-process account, on which causes must be actual events that transmit something or run through a mechanism, and absences transmit nothing. Don't stipulate the verdicts; they should fall out of the two theories. Constraints: the omitted act must be something the person could easily have done and would normally do; the would-be act must prevent the outcome cleanly and deterministically, so no world-similarity or miracle quibbles; the bad outcome should be produced by an independent background process that the act would have interrupted; and the case must not trade on moral blame — we're after causation itself, not negligance. After the scenario, walk through the reasoning each theory gives and why they split, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work of pulling the two verdicts apart, and say what would happen if you removed that feature (would the two theories then converge, and on what verdict?). Then give the strongest reply a mechanistic theorist could make against the case, and say whether that reply is best read as rejecting the datum, rejecting the counterfactual theory, or re-describing the omission without conceding it is a cause.
