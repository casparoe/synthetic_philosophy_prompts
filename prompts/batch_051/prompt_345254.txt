I'm writing a short essay—more of a think piece for a philosophy blog—about when collective memory and the historical record conflict, and I'd like detailed comments before I finish it. Please act like a philosopher who works on collective memory and social epistemology, preferably one who is impatient with mushy both-sides positions. My working view is that the question "when a community's memory and the record conflict, which should give way?" is badly posed. I want to argue that collective memory and history answer to different norms: history to evidence and accuracy, but collective memory to something like the constitution of a shared subject—it is how a group says "we" and binds itself to ideals. So when the record contradicts a community's founding story, the right response is not automatic deference to the record; we should first ask what the memory is doing. A nation that remembers its revolution as cleaner than it was may be using the memory to hold itself to the ideals its revolution betrayed on the ground. But I also want a limit clause, because I don't want to end up defending myths that justify domination: when the disputed memory underwrites present injustice—say, denying a massacre or erasing the counter-memory of victims—then the record gets priority and correction is not a negotiation.

I know this sounds suspiciously tidy, and I worry the limit clause does all the work and that I've just dressed up a banal "it depends" in fancier terms. I'd love comments that really stress-test the thing: does the accuracy-norms vs identity-norms distinction hold up under pressure, or does it collapse as soon as you press on it? Which premise is weakest, and should I patch it or drop it? Where would a sharp reviewer—say a historian who thinks truth-telling is the whole job—hit me hardest? Please think carefully about the structure of the argument, be concrete and a bit mean rather than encouraging: start by brainstorming the three or four strongest objections an unsypathetic reader would raise, then tell me exactly which parts of the sketch to change and, where you can, suggest better wording.
