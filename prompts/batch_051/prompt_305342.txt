I am a graduate student working on Victorian moral philosophy, and Henry Sidgwick has been a central figure for me—I have always taken him to be one of the most careful ethical thinkers in the tradition. Recently I learned that Sidgwick also helped establish the Society for Psychical Research and spent decades attending séances, collecting testimony about telepathy, and treating the possibility of postmortem survival as a question that careful empirical work might settle. By my own lights, that is a long and serious investment in a program that now looks pseudoscientific, and learning this has changed how I read him.

I have turned that reaction into a standing policy. The policy is: whenever I discover that a thinker I otherwise rely on devoted sustained effort to a project I regard as discredited, I impose a discount on everything else I take from them. I do not dismiss the work outright—I still engage with it—but I no longer give their arguments any benefit of the doubt, and I stop treating their reputation as a reason to take a subtle argument seriously until I can reconstruct the argument's force without leaning on the thinker. In practice, I now read Sidgwick's ethical arguments looking for places where the same cast of mind that kept him at séances might be doing quieter damage.

My question is whether this policy is defensible or whether it is just reverse halo effect: an ad hominem dressed up as epistemic hygiene. Does being badly wrong in one domain tell me anything about a thinker's reliability in another? I can see a cleaner version—for example, a rule about discounting testimony in domains with terrible base rates, or a rule about not deferring to anyone across the board—but I cannot tell whether that clean version has any real connection to what I am actually doing. I am especially uneasy because Sidgwick is dead, his arguments are on the page, and I could simply evaluate each one on its merits; my policy seems to be a way of managing my own prior, not a way of discovering whether any particular argument succeeds. Is that a legitimate thing for a bounded agent to do, or is it a bias I have rationalized? How would I tell the difference from the inside?
