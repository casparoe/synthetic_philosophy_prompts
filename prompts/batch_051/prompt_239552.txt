I teach a final-year PPE seminar at a UK university and next week I have two hours on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox. My students are comfortable with normal-form games, dominance, and Nash equilibrium, but this will be their first real encounter with sequential rationality, subgame perfection, and common knowledge of rationality. Do not explain the paradox to me. Plan how I should explain it to them. I need the explanatory arc: what has to be in place before the rollback argument can bite, and in what order the remaining pieces should be introduced so that the conclusion—take at the first node—feels forced rather than merely stipulated. I also need the one worked example that should carry the whole session, and what its payoff structure and finite length must do; which variants (ties, side-agreements, a very short game, an infinite horizon) would undermine the lesson and why. Then map the misconceptions they will bring or form along the way—I expect 'it is just a prisoner's dilemma with more moves', 'backward induction predicts how the class would actually play it', and 'the paradox would vanish in a longer game or with more trusting players'—and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Tell me what to set aside openly at this level, and how to set it aside without implying that the omitted material makes the paradox trivial. Finally, give me one end-of-seminar question or task that would show me whether they have grasped the genuine tension—that each player's local incentive to stop is compatible with both players' strong shared interest in continuing—rather than merely memorised the rollback algorithm.
