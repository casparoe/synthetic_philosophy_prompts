I work in moral psychology where it runs into normative ethics, and I keep finding that the same experimental result gets classified differently depending on an unexamined distinction between preferences and biases in moral judgment. Before I read further or design another study, I want to fix the standard an adequate account of that distinction would have to meet, for two purposes: interpreting experimental work without begging normative questions, and deciding when an attitude is correctly attributed to an agent as something they hold rather than something their cognitive system does to them.

Write the desiderata, then. By a moral preference I mean a valuing attitude defeasibly attributable to the agent as their own — something they want, endorse, or are committed to, such that it can figure in their self-conception and practical reasoning. By a moral bias I mean a systematic distortion in moral judgment attributable not to the agent's values but to features of the judgment process: framing, ordering, salience, heuristic shortcuts, motivated cognition, and the like. The hard part is drawing this line without equating the agent's own view with the truth, or with what the theorist happens to find reasonable.

For each desideratum, give a precise statement, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than an optional virtue, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of a particular camp. Then flag any subsets of the list you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single criterion does the most to exclude the currently dominant accounts.

The list should at least be constrained by the following cases, though I do not want them to limit you:

Framing and order sensitivity. The same dilemma yields systematically different verdicts under different wording, ordering, or irrelevant anchors. Nearly everyone calls this bias; my difficulty is saying why a stable taste for certain frames may not count as a preference, without appealing to a contested invariance norm.

Unsupported moral conviction. Agents hold a moral verdict while unable to produce supporting reasons, and sometimes rationalize afterward. If reason-responsiveness is the criterion, what is the status of the verdict before the rationalization arrives, and what if the rationalization is plainly confabulated?

Retributive satisfaction. A person's preference for punitive outcomes survives reflection and explanation. Some accounts treat it as a legitimate moral preference; others treat it as a bias toward intuitions shaped for small-scale reciprocity. What in the list settles this without presupposing the answer?

Ingroup partiality. Systematic discounting of outgroup interests is classified as bias when implicit but often as legitimate special obligation when avowed. What feature — consistency, impartial justifiability, genuineness of feeling, something else — flips the classification, and can it do so noncircularly?

Adaptive valuation. An agent in constrained circumstances comes to prefer, apparently sincerely, the very arrangement that subordinates them. The account must be able to say the preference is genuine yet deformed without importing an external standard it has not argued for.

Meta-attitudes. An agent disavows a verdict they cannot shake, or endorses a verdict they cannot feel. How should first-order disposition and second-order endorsement be weighted, and what happens when they conflict?

Reciprocal accusation. Two agents hold opposed convictions with equal subjective certainty, each regarding the other's attitude as biased. A purely first-person test and a purely statistical test both fail here; what does the theory have left?

Finally, give a brief indication of how each of these candidate views fares against the list: a revealed-preference view on which the real preference is whatever survives debiasing procedures; an endorsement view on which the real preference is whatever the agent would reflectively affirm as their own; and a rational-criticizability view on which the preference/bias line just is the line between attitudes defensible to others and attitudes that are not. Keep that section brief; the list is the part I need.
