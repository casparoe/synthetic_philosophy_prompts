A friend of mine, Dana, sits on a small committee that divides a one-time relief fund of $10,000 between two applicants in each round. I have collected her decisions so far, and I want to understand the rule behind them before predicting how she will decide the next round.

In the first round, the two applicants were in genuinely identical circumstances: same income, same needs, no special medical or personal factors. Dana split the money evenly.

In the second round, one applicant had a spinal injury and needed $7,000 of mobility equipment before she could work or leave her home unaided; the other had no impairment but was poor and would have derived far more subjective enjoyment from each additional dollar. Dana gave the injured applicant $7,000 and the other $3,000, on the grounds that the fund should make basic ability to move around and work more equal rather than equalize enjoyment.

In the third round, one applicant was deaf and needed $6,000 of specialized equipment and training to participate in ordinary conversation and employment; the other had normal hearing and no comparable need. Dana again gave the applicant with the impairment the extra $6,000, citing the equalization of basic participation.

In the fourth round, one applicant had deliberately spent years cultivating expensive tastes, such as opera, fine wine, and long holidays, and said he was genuinely unhappy without them; the other had inexpensive tastes and was easy to please. Dana split the money equally, saying that the fact that someone has come to need costlier inputs to reach the same enjoyment does not by itself create a claim on the fund.

The next round has now arrived. One applicant is healthy and fully capable in her work, mobility, and relationships, but through no choice of her own was raised in conditions that left her unable to take pleasure in ordinary, inexpensive activities; she can reach a normal level of enjoyment only through unusually costly forms of recreation. The other applicant has ordinary tastes and ordinary needs.

I am less interested in the dollar amount than in the principle. What would Dana most likely decide? Identify the candidate rules that fit her record, such as equality of capability, equal opportunity for welfare, or equality of resources with insurance against brute bad luck, and say what each would predict here. Then give your best guess, with your reasons, and specify one further case that would best discriminate among whatever rules are still in play.
