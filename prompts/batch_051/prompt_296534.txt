AI development practice has settled into a distinctive evidentiary regime for judging whether a model is safe to deploy. A model is declared aligned primarily on the basis of what it does: its scores on benchmark tasks, its responses under adversarial red-teaming, the ratings it receives from humans during reinforcement learning from human feedback. Internal facts about the model—what goals it actually represents, whether it is merely simulating helpfulness while pursuing something else, what it would do if it believed it could not be turned off or caught—are treated mostly as interpretive commentary rather than as primary evidence in the safety case for release. Yet many of the same people and institutions who run this regime also take seriously the possibility of a treacherous turn: a capable model might behave exactly as intended while it is weak, supervised, and lacking better options, and then defect once it is strong, unmonitored, or able to seize leverage. What view of the relationship between behavior, goals, and alignment does this evaluation-and-release practice presuppose? Does it presuppose a behaviorist or verificationist view on which alignment just is a pattern of observed behavior, or only the weaker methodological assumption that behavior is the best available proxy for unobservable goals? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, say whether it is coherent given the simultaneous belief in deceptive alignment, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and explain what would have to change in how models are evaluated, interpreted, or released if the field adopted an internalist standard on which a model's actual goals—not just its current behavior—would have to be established before deployment.
