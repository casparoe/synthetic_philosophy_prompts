I've been teaching the blackmail paradox and keep coming back to a policy proposal I want stress-tested before I put it in a paper. The puzzle, as I put it for undergrads, is that revealing embarrassing-but-true information is legal and asking someone for money is legal, but combining them into "pay me or I reveal it" is blackmail; the hard part is saying which of the usual explanations (coercion, privacy, commercializing secrecy) doesn't also condemn lots of ordinary hard bargaining. So suppose a jurisdiction passed this, which I'll call registered blackmail (and yes, it's a bit of a Frankenstein's monster, but that's the point).

The statute: blackmail is decriminalized only if, before making any threat, the blackmailer files a sworn copy of the information with a public Transparency Escrow Office and states a single take-it-or-leave-it silence price for the target. If the target pays within 30 days, the blackmailer is barred by contract from disclosing the information for five years, and the Office pursues real damages against them if they do; after the five years, the Office automatically publishes the information unless a court finds publication would cause serious harm to an uninvolved third party. If the target does not pay, the Office publishes immediately. Payments are not pocketed entirely by the blackmailer: half goes to the blackmailer, and half goes into an enforcement-and-victim fund run by the Office. Knowingly filing false or incomplete information counts as fraud, and the Office can audit the filing before publication.

I don't want a verdict on whether this is a good law in the abstract. I want an account of how it would actually behave if competently enforced. What happens to who blackmails, about what, and at what price? How do targets respond when refusal means immediate disclosure but payment only buys five years of silence? Does the fifty-percent skim change the blackmailer's incentives, and is there a second-order paradox where the registration itself—or the threat of not registering, or filing with an inflated price—becomes the new blackmail instrument? And which failure modes could be patched without effectively gutting the mechanism? I suspect the worst one is something I haven't noticed.

Write it up as an analysis, and as the very last line of your response, give me a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment beginning "Verdict:".
