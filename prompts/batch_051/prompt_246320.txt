In preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of pest control, I constructed the following argument. Each step seems to me compelling, but the position to which it leads is one I cannot accept, and I would value a careful diagnosis.

(1) Moral considerability is grounded in welfare: a being falls within the scope of moral concern in virtue of having a point of view whose states can be better or worse for it.

(2) The capacities ordinarily taken as evidence of such a point of view — nociception, aversive learning, and the state-dependent weighing of threats against other motivations — are distributed across living things without any sharp boundary. There is no non-arbitrary line to draw just below the bee and just above the soil microfauna.

(3) The behavioral evidence that at least some insects possess felt affect — conditioned suppression of noxious stimuli, escape and avoidance learning, and choices in which the motivational force of a threat varies with the insect's state — is no weaker than the behavioral evidence on which we routinely credit rodents and fish with pain.

(4) Therefore at least some insects are moral patients: their welfare gives us pro tanto reason not to harm them.

(5) The harms that ordinary insecticide use prevents — ants in the kitchen, aphids on the roses, a wasp nest on the porch — are matters of convenience, small aesthetic preference, or minor annoyance, not the protection of life or health.

(6) Therefore ordinary insecticide use is pro tanto wrong, and absent a justification stronger than convenience, it is wrong all things considered.

(7) But now extend the same reasoning downward. Bacteria and other microbes show directed movement away from chemical and thermal conditions that would destroy them; mites and other soil microfauna flee from desiccating environments. If (1) and (2) are correct, there is no non-arbitrary place short of the bacterium to stop. The argument thus appears to extend to disinfecting a wound, taking antibiotics, and washing one's hands after working in the garden, since each knowingly destroys organisms whose aversive behavior is of the same general kind as the behavior cited in (3).

(8) That conclusion is intolerable: a theory that condemns basic hygiene and medicine because they harm microbial life has, in effect, argued itself into absurdity.

For my part, I am disposed to trust the behavioral evidence in (3), and I find (8) untenable; beyond that I am open to instruction. Where does the argument first go wrong? Name the premise or the step at which the reasoning fails; explain why that step nevertheless looked correct; and say what a theory must give up in order to block the inference from (1)–(6) to (8).
