I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about how policy arguments draw their force from a single controlling image. The image I keep coming back to is Garrett Hardin's lifeboat. In "Lifeboat Ethics: The Case Against Helping the Poor" (Psychology Today, September 1974), Hardin asks readers to picture each rich nation as a lifeboat, with the world's poor swimming around it, and argues that a rich nation's remaining carrying capacity is not a surplus to distribute but a margin of safety. Here is the passage where he makes one of the load-bearing moves:

"Since the boat has an unused excess capacity of 10 more passengers, we could admit just 10 more to it. But which 10 do we let in? How do we choose? Do we pick the best 10, "first come, first served"? And what do we say to the 90 we exclude? If we do let an extra 10 into our lifeboat, we will have lost our "safety factor," an engineering principle of critical importance. For example, if we don't leave room for excess capacity as a safety factor in our country's agriculture, a new plant disease or a bad change in the weather could have disastrous consequences."

I want to test this passage as an argumentative move, not just restate the familiar essay. Take the role of a professional philosopher who works on applied ethics and on the logic of policy metaphors. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the correspondences Hardin needs the reader to accept: what plays the boat, the sea, the passengers, the swimmers, the unused seats, the safety factor, the boarding parties he mentions later? Then audit the analogy itself. Which correspondences are explicit in the text, and which are quietly imported from the source domain? In particular, how much weight is the "safety factor" carrying, and does that notion transfer from a lifeboat to a nation's agricultural and economic system the way Hardin needs it to? Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his conclusions about foreign aid and immigration from the disanalogies that are real but irrelevant, and say whether those conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If the lifeboat is doing more work than it can bear, say what a more honest master image for his argument would be.

Format: lead with your bottom line; give the correspondences as a compact table or list; then separate the disanalogies that matter from those that do not; then give your verdict. Keep the entire response to no more than 900 words, not counting the Hardin passage quoted above.
