I sit on my company's dependency review board. At Thursday's meeting we will vote on a motion to remove a third-party library, petrel-parse, from all production services. A routine audit flagged two commits in the project that look suspicious — obfuscated bytecode in a patch release, a test suite deleted around the same time, and an unexplained change to how the library handles outbound network requests — but no one has been able to tie them to an actual exploit. The library is deployed in two places, with the same version and the same configuration. One deployment is in our customer-facing payments ledger, where a compromise would be financially and reputationally severe. The other is in an internal log-parsing utility, where a compromise would be inconvenient but containable. The body of evidence available to us is identical for both; only the blast radius differs.

Our charter says we may remove a dependency only when the board can say it knows the dependency is compromised, and it does not say whether that standard may vary with what the dependency does. Several board members argue that we should not remove the library unless we know the commits are malicious, and they insist the bar cannot legitimately differ between the two deployments: either the code is compromised or it is not, and that fact does not change depending on what the code touches. Others reply that "safe enough to ship" simply means something stricter when more is at stake, and that for the payments path the appropriate bar is much higher even though the underlying facts are the same.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this library across the two contexts, with a time frame, and it should give an honest treatment of the strongest argument for holding the evidential bar fixed across stakes. It should also address whether it is coherent for a single body, considering a single body of evidence, to reach different conclusions for the two deployments.
