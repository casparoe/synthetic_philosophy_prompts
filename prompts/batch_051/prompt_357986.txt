Suppose a country coming out of a civil war sets up a commission with the following rule. For one year, anyone who personally committed or ordered killings, torture, or disappearances may receive amnesty from future prosecution and civil suits, provided they give a complete account of every incident they participated in or directly know about: names, dates, places, chains of command, and the whereabouts of remains. A verification panel checks each account against other testimony and records, and the final version is published in a public, searchable registry under the person's own name. Amnesty is conditional. The person pays a fifteen percent surtax on all income above subsistence for twenty years into a victims' fund, is barred for life from public office, the police, the military, and teaching, and loses the amnesty retroactively if later evidence shows they omitted or falsified anything; the published account can then be used against them in court.

I am not asking whether this is the right thing to do in the abstract. I want to know how the institution would actually behave: who would come forward and who would stay silent, what they would be careful to confess and what they would bury, and how victims, prosecutors, and former commanders would each use it to their ends. Where is it gamed first, and how do the second-order effects distort the written record and future behavior? Which of those failure modes could be fixed without giving up the amnesty-for-truth trade?
