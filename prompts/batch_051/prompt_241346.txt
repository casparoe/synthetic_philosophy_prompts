I've been working on a small theory about why historical analogies mislead, and I'd like an honest assessment of it. I call it the third-act fallacy. An ordinary analogy, as I understand it, moves from shared relevant causes or properties to shared effects: this situation is like that one in the respects that actually produce the outcome, so it should produce the same outcome. But when people call a standoff with an aggressive power "another Munich," or predict that an intervention will become "another Vietnam," they are usually not doing that. They are moving from a shared first act — the new situation starts out shaped like the beginning of the old story — straight to the old story's third act, its ending. Negotiation will end in capitulation; involvement will end in quagmire. The middle of the story, where the causal work would have to be shown, gets skipped. I think the Thucydides trap often works the same way: it identifies a shared opening configuration, a rising power facing a ruling power, and then imports the ending of the Athens–Sparta story. I know this overlaps with the availability and representativeness heuristics, and with the claim that analogies suggest hypotheses rather than prove conclusions. Is my distinction between arguing from shared causes and arguing from a shared plot a real distinction, or am I just relabeling something familiar? Could a historical analogy ever be justified through narrative similarity alone? And where does the theory break — what does it wrongly classify, and what is the strongest objection I am missing?
