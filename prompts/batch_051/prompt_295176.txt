Courts sometimes strike down criminal statutes as void for vagueness when the law does not give an ordinary person fair notice of what it forbids and leaves police, prosecutors, and juries to settle its content case by case. The same courts, however, routinely enforce criminal and quasi-criminal law whose key terms are far from mechanically applicable: criminal negligence standards that turn on what a reasonable person would do, prohibitions on disorderly conduct or indecency, and concepts whose boundaries are fixed only through a long line of decisions. The system thus insists that rules be precise enough to guide conduct and simultaneously punishes people under rules it admits are open-textured.

What does this practice presuppose about language, rules, and legitimate punishment? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the void-for-vagueness doctrine as precisely as you can. In particular, does the doctrine presuppose that a valid criminal rule must be statable in advance in terms an ordinary speaker could follow, and what view of meaning or rule-following does that commitment carry with it? Does it presuppose that vagueness is a removable defect of some badly drafted rules rather than a standing feature of language, or is it compatible with the view that every term is vague at its edges and that precision is only ever local and practical? Does the doctrine treat law's guidance function and its authorization function differently, and can that difference be defended?

Then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say which rival theories of language the doctrine quietly rules out. Finally, tell me what would have to change if a legal system consistently adopted one of these views: that no rule determines its own application in advance, so the demand for precision can be met only retrospectively through the settlement of cases; or that a person may justly be punished under a standard whose content could not have been stated to her before she acted. Is void for vagueness, on its best reconstruction, a coherent requirement, or an unstable compromise between two rival pictures of what a legal rule is?
