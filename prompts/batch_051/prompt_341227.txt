I'm preparing a handout for a political ethics seminar, and I need a sorting instrument on the problem of dirty hands: a decision tree (or, if easier, a short quiz with a scoring key) that locates where someone actually stands on whether a political actor can do wrong by doing right, and on the main variants of that thought. I want the instrument to sort people by their answers to concrete cases and claim-based branching questions, not by which label they prefer.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of established names: first work out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the live positions within that space, mark unoccupied combinations, and note which positions cannot be separated by the questions you have chosen.

The first split should concern whether a correctly made all-things-considered decision can still leave a genuine moral remainder — guilt, regret, owed reparation, a wrong still done to someone — or whether any such remainder is only a sign that the calculation is unfinished or that the agent is irrational to feel it. After that, please include branches that separate at least these distinctions: (a) whether some act-types are absolutely prohibited even when violating the prohibition would prevent far more violations of the same type, or whether prohibitions have thresholds past which they give way; (b) whether the 'wrong' in 'do wrong by doing right' is merely an outweighed pro tanto reason rather than all-things-considered wrongdoing, or whether the required act can be genuinely wrong and still required; (c) whether the remainder attaches to the office rather than to the person who holds it; and (d) whether the phenomenon is specific to political action or is one instance of tragic choice in ordinary life. Also keep the problem of dirty hands distinct from the question of tragic dilemmas: the tree should reveal whether the respondent thinks there is no right thing to do, or that there is a right thing to do and it is also in some sense wrong.

Use at least the following cases among the decision points, phrased concretely so that someone can answer without knowing the technical vocabulary: a mayor who orders the torture of a captured plotter to locate a bomb; a minister who approves a strike expected to kill several civilians as collateral while eliminating the planner of an imminent attack; a negotiator who makes and then breaks a solemn promise to a rebel commander to prevent a massacre; and a leader who can avert an atrocity only by publicly lying about his own side's conduct.

The leaves should include, each plainly named and followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: strict absolutism; simple act-consequentialism; the unhappy remainder view, on which an outweighed reason leaves a residue without making the act wrong; dirty hands proper, understood as the view that a required act can still be a genuine wrong; role-relative dirty hands; and at least one view on which the whole problem signals a mistake in how we theorize about political action rather than a real feature of it. Where the logic of your tree yields a combination with no live occupant, label it 'unnamed' or 'unoccupied' and say briefly whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive as a package, or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions the chosen questions cannot separate, and say what further question would split them.

Aim for roughly six to eight branch questions and no more than nine leaves. Each branch label should be a full declarative claim rather than a name or slogan.
