EXAM QUESTION

Explain the distinction between moral realism and moral anti-realism. Distinguish two forms of anti-realism, one cognitivist and one non-cognitivist. Then assess the following claim: because ordinary moral discourse treats moral judgments as truth-apt, non-cognitivism is refuted, and the only remaining dispute is between moral realism and error theory.

STUDENT ANSWER

Moral realism is the view that there are mind-independent moral facts: moral judgments are truth-apt, some of them are true, and their truth does not depend on the attitudes of any individual or culture. Anti-realism is the family of views that reject this picture. Two important anti-realist positions are non-cognitivism and error theory. Non-cognitivism, associated with Ayer, holds that moral judgments are not truth-apt at all; moral utterances express approval, disapproval, or other non-cognitive states rather than beliefs, and so are neither true nor false. Error theory, associated with Mackie, is cognitivist: it holds that moral judgments are truth-apt and aim to state facts, but that there are no moral facts. According to the error theory, moral judgments are systematically false because they ascribe objectively prescriptive properties that nothing instantiates.

Turning to the target claim: since ordinary moral discourse treats moral judgments as truth-apt, non-cognitivism is refuted. I think this is roughly correct. In ordinary discussion we say things like "it is true that lying is wrong," we assert, deny, and offer reasons for moral conclusions, and we treat moral disagreements as conflicts of belief. This shows that moral judgments have truth-apt semantics. But if a sentence is truth-apt, it cannot merely express an emotion; stating a fact and expressing an attitude are mutually exclusive. Therefore non-cognitivism cannot account for ordinary moral discourse. Error theory, by contrast, is entirely compatible with this discourse: it already accepts that moral judgments are truth-apt and cognitive. However, the semantic point does not by itself establish realism. Realism requires not only that we are committed to mind-independent moral facts but that such facts actually obtain. Since ordinary discourse is evidence only for the first, the target claim supports cognitivism about moral language rather than moral realism proper. Thus the target claim is correct: the truth-aptness of ordinary moral discourse rules out non-cognitivism, leaving only the choice between realism and error theory.
