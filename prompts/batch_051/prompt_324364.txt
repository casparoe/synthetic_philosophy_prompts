I have been trying to think through an old logical puzzle in the doctrine of the Trinity, and I want to approach it through my own reactions to ordinary cases rather than through quotations or theology. The puzzle is this. The doctrine says there is exactly one God, that the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, and that the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Holy Spirit, and the Holy Spirit is not the Father. Taken at face value, that seems to have the shape of a contradiction: if the Father is identical with God and the Son is identical with God, then by the symmetry and transitivity of identity the Father is identical with the Son, which the doctrine expressly denies. Someone once replied to me that the word same is incomplete until one says same what, and that identity and distinctness are always relative to a count noun such as God, person, river, water, word, or occurrence. I do not yet know whether that is a real insight or a verbal sleight of hand, so I want to test it against cases where my instincts feel stable.

Here are my verdicts. I swam in a river at dawn and again at dusk. I say it was the same river but not the same water, and I see no contradiction. I typed the word cat four times on a page. I say there is one word written four times: the four occurrences are not identical with one another, but each occurrence is the same word as every other. A hiring committee met in June and then met again in July after one member had been replaced. I say it was the same committee but not the same collection of five people, and I do not think the committee was a sixth entity in the room alongside the five members. A bronze statue of Athena stood on my desk and I later melted it down. I say the statue was destroyed and the bronze was not, so the statue and the bronze were not identical; yet before the melting I would have counted one material object on the desk, not two. This last case bothers me, because if the statue and the bronze were not identical, I do not see how there were not two things; the same what slogan does not immediately tell me what to say here.

The principle I am testing says that there is no absolute identity or distinctness at all: identity and counting always come attached to a count noun. Is there one precise principle in that vicinity that delivers all or nearly all of my verdicts? State it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause of it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to revise, especially the Athena case. Then apply whatever principle you settle on to the Trinitarian claims I started from. In particular, would it license saying that the Father and the Son are the same God but not the same person, and does that genuinely preserve the claim that there is one God, or does it merely hide the problem inside the grammar of the words God and person? Finally, tell me what your principle predicts about a case I have not settled myself: a festival is cancelled one year and then returns under the same name and the same legal registration, with a new venue, new organizers, and only two of the old performers. Is it the same festival, and by what count noun does your answer go?
