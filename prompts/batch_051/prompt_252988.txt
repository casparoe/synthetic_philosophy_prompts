People in my corner of the rationalist blogosphere used to motivate the eliciting-latent-knowledge problem (ELK) with a setup I still think is underrated: an AI has access to a camera feed only it can see, and the training signal comes from a human who, later, sees whatever evidence the AI chooses to point her to. The old argument was that an honest reporter and a human-simulator policy—one that just tells the human what she'd conclude from that evidence anyway—get exactly the same reward, so behavioral training alone can't select for honesty. That framing implicitly assumed a fresh model trained from scratch against a behavioral reward. But the systems we actually worry about now are LLMs pretrained on huge chunks of human text, and by the time anyone asks one what happened on the camera, it already has human-ish concepts of the difference between what actually happened and what someone would likely think happened. Does the core ELK argument survive that shift? Is the right response that the old tie still holds and the pretrained prior merely breaks it in practice, or is one of the original premises actually under pressure?
