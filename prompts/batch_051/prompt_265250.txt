I have been trying to sort out what indicative conditionals are, and I find myself caught between a theory that seems to fit my actual reasoning perfectly and a result that seems to show the theory cannot be right. I want to know which side should yield, and what it would take to justify either surrender.

The theory, often called the suppositional or Ramsey-style view, is that the degree to which I should believe an indicative conditional is just my conditional probability in the consequent given the antecedent. Formally, P(if A, then C) = P(C | A). This has enormous systematic appeal. It explains why I accept a conditional when my conditional probability is high, why I reject it when that probability is low, and why my confidence in the conditional changes in step with my confidence in C on the supposition of A. It avoids the paradoxes of reading if as material implication, and it gives a unified account of what I am doing when I assess a conditional: I am not checking truth conditions but adjusting my beliefs under a supposition. It feels like the one view that takes seriously how I actually reason with conditionals.

But then there are the triviality results. As I understand them, if conditionals express genuine propositions in the usual sense, and if belief in any proposition obeys the standard probability axioms, then the equality above cannot hold across the board. The argument shows that for any ordinary propositions A and C with positive probability for both A-and-C and A-and-not-C, the thesis forces P(if A, then C) to equal P(C); further iterations force the probability function to become degenerate in ways no real agent's degrees of belief could be. So the suppositional view seems to face a stark choice: either conditionals do not express propositions with truth conditions after all, or the equation that makes the view so attractive must be restricted or rejected, or the standard probability framework must be revised.

My firm intuition is that conditionals do express propositions with truth conditions. When I say that if it rains, the match will be cancelled, I seem to be asserting something that could be correct or incorrect. I can deny it, conjoin it with other claims, and ask how likely it is, all of which seem to treat it as truth-evaluable. I cannot shake the sense that a conditional can be true or false in a way that goes beyond my conditional degrees of belief. Yet I also cannot shake the sense that my confidence in the conditional simply is my conditional probability.

I do not want a verdict so much as a negotiated diagnosis. Walk me through the strongest case for each possible move. What would it take for the contention that conditionals have truth conditions to be the thing that goes? What would it take for the equation to be the thing that goes? Is there a stable middle position, such as one that keeps the equation for a restricted class of contexts, or one that denies that conditionals are propositions in the specific sense the proof requires while still giving them assertability conditions? And is there a reading on which the intuition and the theory are compatible? Tell me not just which move you favor but what each move would cost.
