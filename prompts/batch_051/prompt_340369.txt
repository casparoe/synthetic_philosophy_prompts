In my Philosophy of Public Policy class we've been doing a unit on paternalism and nudging, and my professor keeps using the 2012 Bloomberg soda rule as her go-to example of a nudge. I've been arguing with my roommate about wether that's right. The way I see it, capping sugary drinks at 16 ounces in restaurants, movie theaters, stadiums, and food carts isn't a nudge, it's a ban on a product size — even if you could still buy two 16-ounce cups, and even though the 7-Eleven down the street could still sell you a 64-ounce fountain soda because of how the jurisdiction worked.

I know the rough story: the Board of Health passed it, the courts eventually knocked it down on the grounds that the Board overstepped, and the exemptions made it look pretty arbitrary. I don't need a recap of any of that. What I want is a careful analysis of the episode through the lens of nudge theory / libertarian paternalism. By the standard criteria, is the portion cap a nudge, a mandate, or something that shows the taxonomy is less clean than the textbook suggests? What would the theory have told the city to do instead if the goal was to cut sugary drink consumption while preserving freedom of choice — and is the freedom the cap actually left (buy two cups, get a refill) the kind the theory cares about, or is that just a formal way to keep calling it liberty-preserving? Which features of the episode does the framework explain well, and which does it misdescribe or go silent on? And then the reverse: what does the episode do to the theory? Does it shore up the bright line between nudges and bans, or does it show that the line is doing less normative work than its defenders claim once the choice being preserved is that thin?
