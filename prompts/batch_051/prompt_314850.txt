I'm a high school student writing a term paper on what emotions are, and I've read enough to know that the question is usually mapped onto three families of answers: emotions as judgments, emotions as perceptions of bodily changes, and emotions as felt qualities. You are a professional philosopher of mind. Please work through several candidate principles before committing, because I want an account that fits my actual reactions to cases, not just the standard label.

Here are my gut verdicts on cases, reported as honestly as I can:

1. A car backfires right behind me: I jump, heart pounding, before I even know what happened. I don't call that an emotion; it's a reflex.
2. Going over the crest of a roller coaster, my stomach drops. I don't call that an emotion either; it's just a physical sensation.
3. Alone at night in my house, I get the creeping sense that someone is watching me. I don't believe anyone is there, and I can't point to any danger, but I'd still call the creeping feeling an emotion — a kind of fear.
4. Standing at the edge of the ocean at night, I'm filled with awe. That's clearly an emotion to me, but I can't say what judgment it makes; it seems to bypass my thoughts entirely.
5. Watching a cringe video, I feel secondhand embarrassment for a stranger who doesn't know I exist. That's an emotion, even though it's about someone else's situation and not mine.
6. A beloved character dies in a novel, and I cry even though I know, at that moment, that this person never existed. Again, an emotion.
7. On Sunday evening, a heavy dread about the school week settles in without any specific trigger or belief that anything bad will happen. I'd call it an emotion, though a fuzzy one.
8. Skipping breakfast, I get "hangry" and snap at my sister; twenty minutes after I eat, it's gone. I don't think "hangry" names its own emotion — that's just hunger altering my mood.
9. A flat, "meh" feeling lasts for a week after a mild cold. I'd call that a mood, not an emotion.

The one I keep going back and forth on: a jump scare in a horror movie, when I know it's coming and I know it's a movie. My body reacts as if to a threat, and for half a second I'd say I felt scared — but is that an emotion, given that I never for a moment believed I was in danger?

What I want from you is the best account — one of the three families or a precise hybrid — that captures the pattern in these verdicts, stated precisely enough to make a clear prediction about the jump-scare case. Also tell me which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, or, if saving them all would require a gerrymandered principle, tell me which verdict I should revise and why that one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
