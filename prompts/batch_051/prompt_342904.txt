I'm a TA for an intro pragmatism class and I could use a second opinion on this short-answer question before I grade a stack of them. The question on the assignment was:

"Peirce, in 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear' (1878), writes:

'A clear idea is defined as one which is so apprehended that it will be recognized wherever it is met with, and so that no other will be mistaken for it. If it fails of this clearness, it is said to be obscure. This is rather a neat bit of philosophical terminology; yet, since it is clearness that they were defining, I wish the logicians had made their definition a little more plain. Never to fail to recognize an idea, and under no circumstances to mistake another for it, let it come in how recondite a form it may, would indeed imply such prodigious force and clearness of intellect as is seldom met with in this world. On the other hand, merely to have such an acquaintance with the idea as to have become familiar with it, and to have lost all hesitancy in recognizing it in ordinary cases, hardly seems to deserve the name of clearness of apprehension, since after all it only amounts to a subjective feeling of mastery which may be entirely mistaken.'

In your own words: (a) what is Peirce objecting to in the traditional 'clear and distinct' standard? (b) what rule does he propose as a third grade of clearness, and how is it supposed to solve the problem he identifies in the passage? (c) give one objection to that proposed rule."

Here is the student's answer:

"Peirce's complaint is that both Cartesian clearness and Leibnizian distinctness are diagnosed by introspection. Clearness is just a feeling of easy recognition, and distinctness is just being able to produce an abstract definition; both give a person a subjective feeling of mastery, which may be entirely mistaken. So he proposes a third grade of clearness, which says that the meaning of a concept is the conceivable practical effects of its object—the habits of action it would produce. But 'practical effects' can only mean observable effects, so the rule really says that the content of a concept is the set of observations that would verify or falsify statements involving it. If two claims have all the same observational consequences, they have the same meaning; if a claim has no possible observational consequences, it's empty. This is why Peirce is usually read as a forerunner of the verifiability criterion of the logical positivists. The main problem with the rule, I think, is that it turns truth into what works for us: once you make practical success the test of a belief, any useful or comforting fiction counts as true. Peirce's appeal to the long run doesn't fix this, since 'the long run' is itself one of those vague notions the maxim was supposed to clarify."
