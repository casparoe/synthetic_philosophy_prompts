I am drafting a position paper on whether national character is a coherent notion and whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about nations, and I want a consistency audit of the view I currently hold before I show it to anyone. Here is where I have landed, numbered for reference.

1. There is no such thing as a national character, if that means a set of durable psychological traits — punctuality, risk tolerance, deference, honesty — shared by all or most members of a nation and largely absent elsewhere.

2. But there are real and fairly robust cross-national differences in average behavior and social norms. It is not a fiction that some societies are, on average, more rule-observant, more tolerant of silence in conversation, or more direct in workplace criticism than others. These differences are statistical rather than essentialist: within-country variation is large, often larger than between-country variation, so knowing someone's nationality supports at most a weak prior about that individual.

3. For this reason, I think it is wrong in ordinary personal dealings to form expectations about a particular stranger's character — to assume, before meeting them, that they will be punctual, deferential, or honest — just on the basis of where they come from.

4. Yet when I travel or work abroad, I deliberately adjust my own conduct to the prevailing local norms: I queue as others queue, I soften or sharpen criticism as the setting seems to expect, I observe local conventions about appointments, greetings, and what may be said openly. I think failing to do this is a failure of respect, not a sign of neutrality.

5. I also think institutions act wrongly when they use a person's nationality as a factor in how they treat that individual — for example, which applicants to interview, whom to stop or search, how suspicious to be, or how long to detain someone at a border.

6. But I accept a principle that seems to me correct in a wide range of cases: when you lack information about an individual, the rational way to proceed — and usually the permissible way — is to reason from the best available group-level base rates. I would not condemn a physician who, knowing that a condition is markedly more prevalent among immigrants from a particular country, tests patients from that group more readily; nor would I condemn an insurer who prices risk by measured group regularities rather than pretending those regularities do not exist.

7. The case that most bothers me is this. Suppose an immigration officer has accurate aggregate information that travelers from certain countries overstay visas at several times the average rate, and, with no other signal, sends a greater share of such travelers to secondary screening. I want to say the officer is reasoning well and treating those travelers wrongly, but I cannot state the principle that makes the physician's use of prevalence rates permissible and the officer's use of overstay rates not.

I do not know whether this set of commitments is consistent; if it is not, I cannot tell which pairs are doing the damage, and I suspect at least one apparent conflict will dissolve under the right distinction while another will not.

Please structure your audit as follows, in this order and with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. **The position restated** (about 150 words): State in your own words the overall view these seven claims appear to add up to, so I can check it against what I actually think.
2. **Apparent tensions that dissolve** (about 250 words): Identify at least one pair of commitments that look contradictory but are not on closer inspection, and state the distinction that removes the appearance.
3. **Genuine tensions, ranked by severity** (about 500 words): Identify the real conflicts in decreasing order of how damaging they are to the package as a whole. For each, say which claims conflict, how severe the conflict is, and what each side would cost to revise.
4. **The least costly repair** (about 200 words): Propose the single revision that best restores consistency at the lowest philosophical cost, and say what it buys and what it leaves unresolved.
5. **Residual exposure** (about 150 words): Identify the surviving commitment that leaves the view most open to attack, and sketch the strongest criticism a skeptic could press against it.

Where I have left a distinction underspecified, say so rather than silently choosing one.
