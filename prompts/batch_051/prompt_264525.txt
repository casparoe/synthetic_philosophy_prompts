I write a fortnightly science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently preparing a profile of a philosopher who runs a much-read column in which she rules on whether a given body of observations licenses a particular generalization. She never states the rule she uses, but her record across years is remarkably stable. Here is the pattern. When the evidence was that every examined emerald had been green, she endorsed the generalization that all emeralds are green, but refused to endorse the generalization that all emeralds are grue — where something is grue if it has been examined before 2050 and is green, or has not been examined before 2050 and is blue. When the evidence was that every examined raven had been black, she endorsed the generalization that all ravens are black, but refused the generalization that all ravens are blaven — where something is blaven if it has been examined before 2050 and is black, or has not been examined before 2050 and is white. When every tested sample of copper had conducted electricity, she endorsed the generalization that all copper conducts electricity, but refused the generalization that all copper is condupril — where something is condupril if it has been tested before 2050 and conducts electricity, or has not been tested before 2050 and insulates. Across these and similar rulings, she consistently declines to project predicates whose application conditions turn on a particular future date or on whether the object has been examined.

The new case I want to predict involves a materials group that has synthesized a compound, X-7, which had never been produced before this year. Every specimen examined so far has a distinctive unit-cell symmetry; the group calls this symmetry rhombotactic and defines it purely structurally, with no reference to times, places, observers, or experimental history. They propose to project that all X-7 specimens are rhombotactic. I keep returning to three rules that all fit her earlier verdicts: Goodman's entrenchment rule — project only predicates with a history of successful use in past inductions; a ban on positional predicates — project anything not defined by reference to time, place, or observation; and a natural-kind requirement — project predicates that answer to a genuine structural or causal property. What does each rule say about X-7, which rule best fits the full record, and how do you predict she will rule? Finally, specify the further ruling — a future case with its predicate defined — that would most decisively separate the candidates that remain live.
