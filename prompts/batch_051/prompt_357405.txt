I've ended up convinced that the is/ought gap is real and not just a terminological scuffle: no string of purely descriptive premises validly yields a moral conclusion, and whenever someone seems to do it there's either an evaluative premise hiding in the background or an equivocation on a word like good, healthy, rational, or natural. I want this view stress-tested. Tell me what would have to be true, or what kind of case or argument should make me abandon or seriously qualify it. I'm less interested in the textbook objections—e.g., that Hume only meant it as a point about argumentative burden, not logic, or Moore's open-question argument, or Foot-style functionalist replies—and more interested in a map of where the position is genuinely exposed: thick concepts like cruel and courageous that seem to be both descriptive and evaluative at once, oughts that seem to follow from institutional facts and conventions, promising as a social practice, the claim that every description already carries evaluative valence, evolutionary or game-theoretic just-so stories, attempts to derive ought from facts about what a properly functioning human does. For each, tell me whether a committed defender should concede it counts against them or can honestly dismiss it, and whether it's fatal or just awkward. Also tell me which version of the gap I'm probably running if I'm honest—logical, semantic, epistemological, or just a norm about how to argue—and how much of my confidence depends on that choice. And be real about whether the view has been cushioned into unfalsifiability, and whether that would even be a problem for a view of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and lead with the bottom line. Keep it under 400 words.
