I'm drafting a paper on the internal structure of suffering-focused ethics, and I've become convinced that the label negative utilitarianism covers a family of positions that come apart once you press them on trade-offs, aggregation, and population. I want a ranking of the main variants by how well they withstand the canonical objections, rather than by their historical influence. The objections I have in mind are: the trade-off objection, according to which a pure negative view seems unable to say that any amount of happiness could justify even a single pinprick of suffering; the replacement objection, according to which such views seem to imply that a world with no sentient life is better than a world containing some suffering; and the aggregation objection, according to which they may license concentrating severe suffering on one individual in order to prevent a great many minor pains.

Please sort the following six variants into tiers, something like most defensible, defensible only with revisions, and seriously flawed, and give a sentence or two for each placement: (a) strict negative utilitarianism, on which only the reduction of suffering has moral value, and happiness counts for nothing except perhaps as an instrument for reducing suffering; (b) severity-threshold negative utilitarianism, on which suffering below a certain severity level trades off against happiness in an ordinary way, but above that level the avoidance of suffering has lexical priority; (c) capped negative-leaning utilitarianism, on which suffering and happiness both count, but the exchange rate is deliberately lopsided and worsens with severity, so that offsetting intense suffering requires enormous quantities of happiness even though no absolute lexical priority applies; (d) suffering-weighted prioritarianism, on which all welfare counts, but a given loss matters more the worse off the individual is, so that severe suffering receives disproportionately heavy but not absolute weight; (e) existence-constrained harm minimization, on which we should minimize suffering among beings who will exist anyway, but non-existence is not a harm to a merely possible being, so the view blocks any inference that extinction or non-procreation would be best; and (f) aversion-sensitive anti-suffering ethics, on which the badness of a painful state depends partly on the subject's relation to it, so that pain the subject fully accepts and does not disvalue is discounted relative to pain the subject rejects or dreads.

After the tiering, tell me where your ranking is least stable: which tier boundary a well-informed critic of suffering-focused ethics would most plausibly redraw, and what argument or empirical consideration would most likely change your mind about the top tier. Use American English throughout.
