My district has gone all-in on "resilience." Every kid takes a required advisory class where they watch videos about famous failures, fill out worksheets on mindset, and set (and get graded on) quarterly "grit goals." Meanwhile the same district has a grading policy where no assignment can score below a 50, test retakes are unlimited, and a kid can fail a semester and recover the credit with a packet over spring break that's basically impossible to fail. I honestly can't tell what theory of grit these policies add up to. Is resilience being treated as a trainable skill (worksheet in, grit out), a moral virtue that needs real adversity and real stakes to develop, an individual-level fix for what is at least partly an environment problem, or just a way to make failure feel optional while the district signals it's doing character education? What does the combination of "teach grit" and "never let failure actually stick" presuppose about what resilience is and where it comes from? If you had to write down the implicit theory, say where the two halves contradict each other, and say what a policy built on the opposite theory would change, what would that look like? And please seperate what's actually settled among people who study this from what's genuinely contested—don't hand me a contested view dressed up as consensus.
