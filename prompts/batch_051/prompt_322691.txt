I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on normativity, and in our reading group we keep circling around the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem. I tried to reconstruct an argument a friend made (I think he was playing devil's advocate), and it came out stronger than I expected, so now I can't tell weather the argument is airtight or quietly confused. It goes like this:

(1) A consideration is a reason for an attitude just in case it counts in favor of having that attitude.
(2) A consideration counts in favor of having an attitude just in case, in light of that consideration, having the attitude would be good or valuable in some respect for the person.
(3) So any consideration in light of which having the attitude would be valuable is a reason for that attitude.
(4) If a demon credibly threatens to torture you unless you admire him, then admiring him would be valuable for you (it keeps you off the rack).
(5) So the demon's threat is a reason for admiring the demon.
(6) But the demon's threat is the paradigm wrong kind of reason: it has nothing to do with whether the demon is admirable, and it looks like a reason to bring it about that you admire him, not a reason that bears on the admiration itself.
(7) So the only general analysis of reasons for attitudes I had on the table ends up counting wrong-kind reasons as reasons of the same sort as right-kind ones.
(8) Therefore the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction is either empty or has to be bolted on ad hoc, and we should probably give up on it.

That conclusion feels absurd to me: evidence that p and a bribe to believe p are not reasons in the same way. But I can't find a false step. If I have to choose, I'd most like to keep the distinction itself, even if that means giving up the value-of-having analysis in (2), since that analysis was the only general story I had about reasons for attitudes. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And if I reject (2), what am I actually paying?
