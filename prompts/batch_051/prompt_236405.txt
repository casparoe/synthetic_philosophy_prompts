I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of language, and the midterm included a question on vagueness. One student submitted the answer reproduced below. Please assign a score out of 20 and provide point-by-point comments that justify the score: identify what is accurate, what is inaccurate, and why, with reference to the philosophical positions themselves rather than a general impression.

Exam question: Compare supervaluationism and epistemicism as responses to the sorites paradox for the predicate "heap." Address (i) where each view locates vagueness — in the world, in our knowledge, or in meaning; (ii) whether each view accepts bivalence, and whether each view accepts excluded middle; and (iii) what each view says about the major premise of the sorites, namely, that for every n, if n grains make a heap then n−1 grains make a heap.

Student's answer: Supervaluationism (Fine) locates vagueness in meaning: "heap" is semantically incomplete, and the settled meaning determines its extension only up to a range of admissible precisifications. These precisifications agree on all clear cases but draw the borderline differently. A sentence is supertrue if it is true on every admissible precisification, superfalse if it is false on every admissible precisification, and neither otherwise. Since a borderline attribution such as "n grains make a heap" is true on some precisifications and false on others, the supervaluationist rejects bivalence. Supervaluationism nonetheless accepts excluded middle, because "n grains make a heap or n grains do not make a heap" is true on every precisification even when neither disjunct is true outright. For the sorites, the supervaluationist rejects the major premise. For any borderline n, the conditional "if n grains make a heap, then n−1 grains make a heap" is false on every admissible precisification, because each precisification must draw a sharp boundary somewhere. The universal major premise is therefore false and the argument is unsound. Epistemicism (Williamson) agrees with supervaluationism that "heap" has a single sharp boundary, and the two views differ only over whether we can know where it falls. The epistemicist accepts both bivalence and excluded middle: every borderline sentence is simply true or false. Consequently there really is a particular n such that n grains make a heap and n−1 grains do not. The major premise has exactly one false instance, but we are not in a position to identify which instance it is; our ignorance is just ordinary ignorance rather than a semantic gap. Epistemicism thus resolves the sorites by showing the major premise is false, while preserving all of classical logic and the T-schema.
