I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how people think about punishment. If a human judge imposed an unending sentence for a one-off, finite offense—say a teenager steals a car, gets caught, and is locked up forever with no possibility of release—we would not just call it harsh; we'd say the judge has stopped doing punishment and started doing something else, something monstrous. Yet a lot of the same people will happily accept that a finite human life of wrongdoing earns unending conscious torment in hell. The size difference isn't the interesting part to me; what's interesting is that the usual justifications people give for punishing at all—deterrence, reform, incapacitation, denunciation, proportional retribution—all seem to break at the infinite boundary, or get backdated after the fact to protect the conclusion.

I know there are stock moves available: sin against an infinite God has infinite gravity; the damned keep re-choosing their state forever; hell is self-imposed separation, so God isn't really a punishing agent in the ordinary sense; God sustains the damned in being but doesn't add extra pain; maybe the alternatives are worse. What I want you to do is take the strongest one of those—or a better one you can build—and see whether it actually justifies treating divine punishment as an exception to our ordinary proportionality judgments. Test it on nearby cases: would the same logic let a human state run an endless prison? Does it push you to one of the other main positions, annihilation or universal salvation, or would it make ordinary finite punishment look under- or over-justified? In the end, should a consistent person keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give up the ordinary judgment? I'm fine with the answer being that people are just inconsistent here, but only if you run the strongest attempt to dissolve the inconsistency and say clearly where it fails.
