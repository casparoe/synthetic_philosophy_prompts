I am a PhD student in psychology studying judgment under uncertainty, and I have been asked to run a one-off 90-minute session for an undergraduate philosophy of science seminar of about 18 students. They have little formal statistics, and I will not have a projector or any special equipment beyond paper and a whiteboard. I want the session to make them feel, rather than hear, why a vivid coincidence offered as an anecdote is usually weak evidence for a hidden cause, even when the person reporting it insists on how improbable it was. The conceptual target is the conjunction of three ideas: the birthday collision result, namely that a shared birthday becomes more likely than not at 23 people; the distinction between the probability of this particular coincidence and the probability of some coincidence or other given a large enough opportunity space; and the way anecdote selects on the hit while hiding the number of misses and the size of the search. I do not want a lecture up front; the force of the idea should come from what participants commit to, bet on, and discover, before anything is named.

Please design a runnable activity with rules, materials, a points-based incentive scheme that does not use real money, and a timeline for the full 90 minutes. I would like it to have two movements. In the first, participants should commit, publicly or with point stakes, to predictions about a birthday-style collision problem, see the outcome, and feel their own miscalibration. In the second, groups should be put in a position where they are rewarded for finding something that looks like a striking coincidence inside a large space of possible patterns or events, so that the striking discovery becomes predictable and the incentives do the teaching. If you think a different structure would serve the goal better, say so and justify it, but keep the no-lecture constraint.

Please include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three conceptual pieces above, and that leads participants to articulate for themselves what further information they would need before treating a real-world coincidence anecdote as evidence of a hidden cause. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief.

Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the claims you make, especially any quantitative claims about probabilities, timing, or how students will behave, and single out the point you are least sure of.
