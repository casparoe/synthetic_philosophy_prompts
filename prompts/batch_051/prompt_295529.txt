I help run an amateur philosophy forum for people who never studied the subject formally but enjoy reading and arguing carefully. For months, two of our regulars have been going back and forth about what thought experiments — one of them calls them intuition pumps, the other calls them staged scenes — actually accomplish. One of them holds what I will call the instrument view: a well-built thought experiment works like a controlled miniature, isolating a principle by stripping away real-world clutter so that the reaction it provokes carries genuine evidential weight. The other holds the persuasion view: the same devices are mostly high-powered rhetoric, and the vivid scene, forced choice, and emotional stakes produce a verdict that the "argument" is then arranged to match.

The two have agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the statement they would produce after working through it in good faith. Please write the full document, not an essay about writing one.

Act as an expert in philosophical methodology and the psychology of reasoning. Before writing, brainstorm several candidate formulations of each position and keep only the ones a careful defender of that position would sign. Pay special attention to separating disagreements about words from disagreements about substance.

The document must include:

(1) A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Make these non-vacuous. They should cover at least: how thought experiments are built and read; the fact that surface features (who is named, what is at stake, how the choices are framed) can shift intuitive verdicts; that some scenario families have survived decades of re-description while others have come to look like artifacts of their time; and that any usable scenario must compress away real detail, so something is always being left out. Then state what this shared ground does and does not settle.

(2) A table of exact disagreements. Each row should contain: the subject of disagreement; a statement of the instrument-view claim in wording its advocates would accept; a statement of the persuasion-view claim in wording its advocates would accept; and a ruling on whether that particular disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, with a one-line reason.

(3) A list of cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one party to revise their view. For each crux, give at least one argument, case, or empirical test that both sides accept bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show and what each side concedes the outcome would imply.

I suspect the cruxes will include (a) whether the persuasive force of a thought experiment can be separated from whatever probative force it is supposed to carry, and (b) whether re-describing a scenario in flat, affect-neutral language preserves the original question or quietly changes it. Develop these if they fit, and add more if the two sides would need them.

Two tests both regulars have already agreed are relevant, and the document should build them in. First, a matched-pair comparison: dress one principle as a vivid scenario for half the readers and as a dry structural description for the other half, then compare verdicts; each side must commit ahead of time to what a large drift means and what no drift means. Second, an adversarial rewrite: each side takes the other’s favorite scenario and rewrites it to trigger the opposite gut reaction while keeping the official argumentative structure intact; both must predict in advance where the rewrite will succeed and where it will fail.

End with a brief section stating which residual disagreements the two parties now regard as substantive rather than verbal, and which portions of their original argument they concede were arguments about words.

Write the document with clear headings and tight prose. Avoid rhetorical gestures and keep every sentence load-bearing.
