I am giving a colloquium next month in a department whose epistemology group is large and exacting: several members work inside the Williamsonian knowledge-first program, two people in the philosophy of language wing work directly on assertion and presupposition and are generally unsympathetic to the idea that assertion has a constitutive norm at all (they prefer Gricean or relevance-theoretic explanations), and a small cluster of formal epistemologists will expect any acceptable norm to be expressible in probabilistic terms. My talk defends this thesis: the knowledge norm of assertion is correct only if its domain is restricted to flat-out assertion, where flat-out is understood functionally rather than syntactically. A flat-out assertion of p, on my view, presents p as a settled premise that an interlocutor may rely on in her own theoretical and practical reasoning; a hedged or evidentialized assertion presents only some weaker proposition, such as that p is probable or that the speaker believes p. I argue that the standard counterexamples to the knowledge norm — lottery assertions, selfless assertions, Moore-paradoxical cases, and so on — are cases in which the speaker is not making a flat-out assertion but is instead performing one of these related speech acts, and that the weaker act type is governed by something like a reasonable-belief or evidence-sufficiency norm.

The argument, in outline, is this. First, I set out the functional criterion for flat-out assertion and argue that it tracks a difference speakers themselves mark in conversation, irrespective of whether the sentence contains a hedge. Second, I show that once flat-out assertion is distinguished in this way, the knowledge norm sorts the familiar cases correctly, while the main rival accounts — truth, belief, and reasonable-belief norms — misdescribe what is going on because they conflate norms of distinct act types. Third, I reinterpret those rival norms as plausible norms of hedged assertion, conjecture, or telling, and argue that their proponents have mistaken a norm of a neighboring act for a competitor to the knowledge norm.
