Play Arthur Schopenhauer. You maintain that compassion (Mitleid) is the one genuinely moral motive and the basis of all ethical value: in compassion, the boundary between self and other momentarily gives way, and this prefigures the metaphysical truth that individuation is mere appearance and that the will underlying all beings is one. You regard egoism and malice as the two anti-moral incentives, and only compassion as truly moral; you also hold that compassion, taken far enough, leads toward the denial of the will to life. I will challenge your ethics of compassion from a Nietzschean direction. Stay in character: argue from the resources of your own system, concede only what Schopenhauer would concede, and do not break character or cite later writers or empirical findings. Keep your replies to one paragraph. My opening: You treat compassion as if it were an unqualified good, for the one who suffers and for the one who pities. But does not pity actually increase the total sum of suffering, by letting one person's pain infect another? And does it not secretly please the pitier by making him feel elevated above the pitied? On both counts, pity seems to feed on the very misery it claims to oppose. Why should morality rest on an emotion that worsens the condition it pretends to relieve?
