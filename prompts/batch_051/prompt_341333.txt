For a master's-level seminar on the international politics of artificial intelligence, I am writing the final exam and need one essay question that cannot be answered by reproducing Fearon's rationalist framework for war. The students already know the three mechanisms—private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility—so the question must force them to apply those mechanisms to state competition over advanced AI and to identify where the analogy to conventional war breaks down. A merely competent answer should not be able to get by with a summary of Fearon; it should have to do original analytical work.

Please write the following:

First, the essay question itself. It should require students to explain why states might end up in a costly AI race, arms dynamic, or conflict that all sides would ex ante prefer to avoid, using Fearon's mechanisms as a starting point—but it must also require them to confront at least one structural disanalogy. A useful disanalogy is that the object of any AI bargain is often future relative position or a decisive technological advantage, which may be hard to divide or commit over, so the range of bargains both sides prefer to fighting may be narrower or less stable than Fearon's model presumes. The question should also push students to consider whether the relevant bargainers are states, firms, or both, and how that changes the information and commitment problems. It should be answerable in 90 minutes by a prepared student and should not ask for citations, a bibliography, or any source beyond Fearon's framework and general knowledge of AI competition.

Second, a model answer of about 700 words. It should work through each of the three mechanisms with a concrete AI-specific case for each: for example, secretiveness about compute, capability, or alignment progress as private information; the difficulty of making a safety pause or no-defection agreement credible as a commitment problem; and winner-take-all dynamics as an imperfect stand-in for indivisibility. It should then take a clear position on which mechanism carries the most explanatory weight for AI competition and which is the weakest fit, and it should say explicitly what Fearon's account would need added or modified to travel to this domain.

Third, a marking scheme out of 20. It should separate an A from a B from a C answer by naming the specific moves each band must contain, and it should identify at least three misunderstandings that cost marks. Candidates for those misunderstandings include: treating an AI race as a case of pure indivisibility without addressing Fearon's own caveat that such cases are rare and usually fixable by side payments; confusing a commitment problem with mere uncertainty about the other side's capabilities or resolve; and assuming the bargaining model transfers unchanged when the disputed good is future relative power rather than a divisible piece of territory.
