I direct the disease-control division of a county public-health department. Every few weeks my team proposes an intervention—water fluoridation, a contact-tracing phone app, aerial spraying of insecticide, vaccination outreach with incentives, a mask requirement—that reduces disease risk but also imposes some risk or burden on people who never individually consented. I need a reusable decision procedure we can run on any such proposal before it goes to the county health officer for sign-off. The reviewers have no formal ethics training and will have roughly an hour to apply the procedure, so it must be usable without the theory behind it. Take as its working basis the following position from the ethics of risk without consent: imposing a risk on someone without their consent is presumptively wrong, and the presumption is defeated only by particular features of the risk—for example, that it is part of a reciprocal scheme whose protections and burdens fall on the same people over time, that it is negligibly small and of a kind people already routinely accept, or that those exposed have a real post-hoc remedy—while aggregate expected benefit, by itself, is never enough. Convert that position into an operational procedure: list the questions in the order they should be asked, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a no, and mark the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests, including where a question must go to the health officer because it turns on values rather than facts. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) fluoridating the county water supply, (b) launching a contact-tracing app that records location without explicit opt-in during an outbreak, and (c) drone-spraying an insecticide over residential neighborhoods to suppress a mosquito-borne disease. Include at least one application in which the procedure gives a result we will not like—blocking something the team regards as obviously beneficial or demanding a safeguard we regard as excessive—and explain where that pressure comes from. End with the exact line \answer{...} containing a one-sentence summary of the procedure.
