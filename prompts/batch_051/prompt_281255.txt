I'm teaching Sophocles' Oedipus the King in a course on responsibility, and I want to use the play to test our intuitions about strict liability and mens rea—the mental element in a wrongful act—and the problem of punishing without fault. Oedipus killed his father and married his mother without knowing who they were; the acts themselves are the source of the pollution and plague on Thebes, and he ends by condemning himself, blinding himself, and asking to be exiled, even though the relevant intentions were missing or pointed at different objects. Read the play through this lens. Does it endorse something like a strict-liability conception of guilt, where the deed defiles regardless of what the agent knew or intended, or does it also give materials for the opposite view, that knowledge and intent are what make an act culpable? Where does the idea illuminate a particular choice or scene—his curse on the killer, his refusal to stop the inquiry, the self-blinding—and where does the play resist a clean philosophical reading, for instance in the killing at the crossroads, where there is culpable anger without any intent to kill his father? I want an honest reading that does not reduce the play to an illustration of a modern legal doctrine it predates, and that says what the play adds to the debate over fault that argument alone does not.
