I am a philosophy professor and I chair my university's curriculum committee. You are to think as a specialist in Hellenistic ethics and in the modern reception of Stoic therapeutic practice. Work through the problem in stages: first reconstruct the underlying philosophical position at its most defensible, then identify how that position changes when it is extracted from its original context and made a required part of a first-year program, and only then produce a recommendation. The committee will vote next week on a proposed mandatory first-year module called Academic Resilience. The module's intellectual anchor is a passage from the first chapter of Epictetus's Handbook. In the translation supplied by the proposer, the passage distinguishes what is in our power—judgment, impulse, desire, aversion—from what is not in our power—body, property, reputation, office—and claims that mistaking the second class for the first is the source of human wretchedness. The proposing dean says this will teach students to redirect their effort toward what they can change. A minority of the committee says the same passage, made compulsory and aimed at eighteen-year-olds, becomes an ideological instrument: if a student is distressed by a scholarship cut, a disability accommodation that was denied, or a discriminatory remark, the module instructs them to treat their judgment as the only thing truly up to them, and thereby frames the institution's failure as the student's cognitive error. Draft the short decision memo the committee should circulate: approve, approve with revisions, or reject. The memo must include a concrete actionable bottom line, specify any conditions or revisions, and engage fairly with the strongest version of the losing side.
