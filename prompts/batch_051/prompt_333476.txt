I am a high school student preparing an essay for a regional philosophy competition. My thesis is a version of evaluativism about pain: the view that a pain is not a neutral sensory experience to which an unpleasant reaction is later added, but that part of what it is to be in pain is to experience a bodily condition as bad, and that if this evaluative dimension were removed one would no longer be in pain at all rather than merely ceasing to mind it. The obstacle I keep facing is pain asymbolia, a clinical condition in which patients can still identify and accurately describe noxious stimulation as sharp, burning, and located in a body part, yet they deny that it bothers them and show none of the usual avoidance, grimacing, or distress. An opponent who accepts a representationalist view of pain — on which pain is the perception of bodily damage and its unpleasantness is a separate affective response — can say that such patients are still in pain, just pain that has stopped mattering to them. I want my essay to confront this hardest version of the objection rather than a weakened one.

Please construct a thought experiment to these specifications.

First, build a matched pair of two ordinary, clinically plausible episodes involving the same tissue damage in the same body part. In both episodes the person can localize the sensation and describe its quality — burning, stabbing, throbbing, and so on — with equal precision, and in both the person gives a complete verbal report. The episodes differ only in the evaluative dimension: the first person finds the sensation aversive and bad and urgently wants it gone; the second describes the very same quality and intensity but finds it entirely non-aversive, does not mind it in the least, and shows no motivational pull to remove it. The second person’s report should be credible and articulate, not the product of confusion or denial.

Second, arrange the pair so that a representationalist and an evaluativist give opposite verdicts on whether the second person is in pain of the same intensity as the first. Do not stipulate the verdicts; they should fall out of each theory’s own criterion for what it is to be in pain. The only empirical assumption the case may lean on is the basic coherence of the dissociation between accurate qualitative report and absence of aversion, which is exactly the possibility the two theories divide over. No exotic technology, no science-fictional intervention, and no dependence on a particular brain pathway should be required.

Then do the check. State each theory’s criterion of pain clearly, and show step by step why the representationalist must say the second person remains in pain of the same intensity while the evaluativist must say that what the second person has is not pain of the same intensity — and, in the strongest version of the view, not pain at all. Identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work, presumably the separation of preserved sensory-discriminative description from absent aversion, and explain what both theories would say if that feature were removed. Finish by giving the strongest reply a defender of evaluativism could make to stop the case from counting as a refutation, and say honestly whether the reply succeeds or merely relocates the burden.

This case is for the section of my essay in which I articulate the strongest objection to my own view, so a case that simply asserts that the second person is in pain, or that simply asserts the representationalist’s conclusion inside the scenario, would count as cheating; the verdict has to be generated from the theories themselves.
