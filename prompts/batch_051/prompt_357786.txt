I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm trying to get my own views on decision theory straight before a grad seminar on risk and rational choice this fall. I put together a one-page "where I stand" handout, and after rereading it, I honestly don't know weather the commitments hang together. I'd like you to audit the set rather than give me a lecture on the landscape.

Here is the set, in roughly the terms I actually think in.

(a) I think Allais-type preferences can be fully rational. For example, choosing a sure $3,000 over an 80% chance of $4,000 is not automatically a mistake, and a theory of rational choice should not rule it out as such.

(b) I think risk attitudes are a legitimate part of practical rationality. Two people can face the same bets, with the same utility function over money and the same well-calibrated probabilities, and it can be fully rational for one to be more risk-averse than the other.

(c) My preferred way to accommodate (a) and (b) is risk-weighted expected utility. On this view you evaluate a gamble by adding up utility increments from the worst possible outcome upward, where each increment is weighted by r(the probability that you'll do at least that well). Here r is an increasing function with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, and a risk-averse agent has r(p) ≤ p, so chances of doing better are underweighted even though the agent isn't wrong about the probabilities.

(d) I think stochastic dominance is non-negotiable: if act A gives an outcome at least as good as act B in every possible state, and strictly better in at least one state, then A must be strictly preferred. I know some rank-dependent theories violate dominance if the probability weighting is badly behaved, and I want my view to be roped off from that.

(e) I still can't shake Savage's sure-thing principle. If two acts have the exact same outcome on some event, then your preference between them should not depend on what that shared outcome is. This feels to me like a basic coherence requirement on preferences over acts, not the sort of thing you get to hand back when the Allais cases get uncomfortable.

(f) I also hold that in contexts where the same positive-expected-value gamble is offered many times independently, the rational stance converges on taking the bets: risk washes out across independent repetitions. But I don't think this shows that the one-shot risk-averse agent in (b) is irrational.

What I want is a consistency audit. Which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which are jointly fine? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, tell me exactly what the conflict is, then say what the cheapest revision would be, which commitment has to give and how much, and what that revision costs. If something in here looks like a problem but dissolves on inspection, say so explicitly and explain why. I'd rather have the hard news than a version of my view that quietly redefines one of the commitments to make it fit.
