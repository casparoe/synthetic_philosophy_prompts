I am reading a general-audience book about decision-making under uncertainty, and its treatment of Keynes has made me wary of trusting the rest of the book. The author presents Keynes as the thinker who first drew the standard distinction between risk and uncertainty, and then uses that interpretation to attack modern economic forecasting as a kind of pseudoscience. The passage I have in mind runs roughly as follows.

According to the author, Keynes's central contribution was to separate risk, where possible outcomes are known and can be given objective numerical probabilities, from uncertainty, where they cannot be enumerated or measured. The author claims that Keynes held that under uncertainty, probability judgments are merely subjective degrees of confidence with no objective standing, so equally well-informed people may disagree indefinitely without either being at fault. For this reason, the author continues, Keynes took the probability calculus to be essentially irrelevant to economic affairs: it applies to games of chance, not to judgments about an open future. The author then links this to Keynes's later claim that investment is driven less by rational calculation than by animal spirits, convention, and spontaneous optimism, since under uncertainty there is no defensible way to calculate. The book concludes that modern models built on rational expectations are therefore resting on a notion of probability that Keynes had already shown to be subjective and groundless.

Two parts of this struck me as possibly off: I had vaguely associated the risk/uncertainty vocabulary with Frank Knight rather than Keynes, and I had thought Keynes defended something like an objective logical relation between evidence and conclusion rather than subjective confidence. But I have no training in philosophy, and I can imagine the book is merely simplifying. Could you go through this summary and tell me what a careful reader of Keynes would accept, what is a common conflation or overstatement, and where the author quietly changes Keynes's position? I would also like to know whether the book's final charge against rational-expectations models follows from the parts of the summary that are actually accurate, or whether it depends on the parts that are not.
