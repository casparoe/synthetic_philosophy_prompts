I edit a journal of experimental psychology and cognitive science that receives a steady stream of papers on perception, introspection, and self-report. Too many submissions treat some class of item—a sensory state, a neural activation, a first-person account—as epistemically bedrock: something had rather than inferred, known simply by being had, and able on its own to stop a request for justification. My reviewers are not philosophers, and they have about forty-five minutes per manuscript. I want a reusable screening procedure: a numbered checklist they can run in order, which flags the specific places a manuscript invokes a given, distinguishes legitimate appeals to observation and first-person data from Sellars's myth of the given, and tells the action editor what to do next for each flagged item. The procedure should preserve the central dilemma the theory identifies—an item that merely causes a report does not thereby justify the claim the report supports, and once the item is described as evidence it has already been brought into a conceptual and inferential framework—and it should not reduce to generic advice about measurement validity or confirmation bias. Run the checklist on three submissions: a psychophysics paper treating a subject's looks-red report as a stopping point for disputes about color perception; a neuroimaging paper treating activation in early visual cortex as raw observation of a perceptual process; a qualitative study treating participants' first-person accounts as authoritative about the causes of their own behavior. For each, show what the checklist flags, what it deliberately does not flag, and what the editor should do. Mark explicitly which steps require editorial judgment the procedure cannot eliminate. Finally, pick one kind of otherwise strong paper the procedure will flag that an editor will be tempted to let through, and justify why the flag should stand.
