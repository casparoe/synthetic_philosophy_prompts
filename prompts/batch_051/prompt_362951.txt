I've been going back and forth on the epistemology of testimony, and I keep hitting a wall in the standard reading where Hume and Reid are treated as precise opposites. I was rereading Hume's *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding*, and in Section X, Part I ("Of Miracles") there is a passage that sounds like a flat statement of reductionism about testimonial trust:

"To apply these principles to a particular instance; we may observe, that there is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses."

Reid, in his *An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense*, appears to say nearly the opposite. As I understand him, he argues that there is an original principle of credulity: human beings are naturally constituted to accept what they are told and to speak truthfully, and this disposition is not derived from induction over past experience. He makes the developmental point that children rely on testimony long before they could have gathered the sort of empirical track record Hume says our trust is based on. So Hume seems to say trust in testimony is ultimately reducible to observed correlations between reports and facts, while Reid seems to say any such inductive assessment of testimony already presupposes a more basic, unreduced trust.

I want to know whether these two views can be reconciled without making one a strawman or a mere change of subject. I can imagine at least three moves: Hume is doing normative epistemology while Reid is doing descriptive psychology; Hume's general principle of custom or habit is itself a kind of innate or original principle, so Reid's credulity is just a special case of it; or Reid's principle is only a defeasible default, while Hume is explaining when testimony yields *probable proof* rather than mere initial acceptance. I don't trust my own verdict on which of these is honest and which is special pleading.
