I'm chairing a small philosophy department, and I keep going back and forth on a decision that feels like it has more philosophical structure than the rest of my committee seems to think. We have two graduate programs. One — call it Program A — is old and well-funded, with an endowed fellowship and travel support. The other, Program B, is newer and runs mostly on TAs and goodwill. A donor recently offered us a modest one-time gift, but with a frustratingly specific restriction: the money can only be used to top up the existing fellowship in Program A. Legally, Program B can't receive any of it.

At our executive committee meeting, one colleague argued we should refuse the gift. Her view is that accepting it would deepen the resource gap between the two programs and thereby make the department more unjust, even though Program B would be exactly where it is now. Another colleague pushed back, saying that refusing the gift wouldn't put a single dollar into Program B; it would just mean Program A misses out on something good. He called it a perverse kind of fairness that leaves nobody better of. She replied that this misses the point, because an outcome can be worse in one way (the department has less money overall) but better in another (it is less unequal), and the second way matters morally. I lean toward accepting the gift, but I'm worried I'm just rationalizing because it's easier to say yes to money. Is there a good philosophical case that the inequality concern should not, by itself, decide this? Or should I take her view more seriously than I do?
