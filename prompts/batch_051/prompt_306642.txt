I run the tax policy shop at a small libertarian-leaning think tank, and every legislative session we screen fourty or fifty state and local revenue proposals, so the volume is high and the stakes are real: our one-pagers go straight to state legislators and donors, and a bad classification either makes us look like cranks or gets us quoted endorsing a levy we actually oppose. The line we keep tripping over is that those one-pagers call things "theft" or "extortion" while nobody on the team can state the actual test. Some staff say it's simple — threat of force plus a taking equals theft — and others say user fees, court fines, and pollution charges are fine because you're paying for a service or a harm you caused. My policy director has to sign off on every verdict, and if the checklist produces a result that gets quoted somewhere embarrassing, I'm the one explaining it. So I want to stop doing this by vibes. Give me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the strongest non-consequentialist, natural-rights version of the "taxation is theft" idea (the Nozick-style view that taking someone's labor or earnings is on a par with forced labor, not the utilitarian anti-tax argument). It has to be something a college intern with no philosophy background can run on a single bill in about fifteen minutes, and it should classify a levy into one of at least these buckets: theft proper, extortion or coercion short of theft, user fee, fine, seigniorage, and taxes on earned income, consumption, property, or unearned ground rents. For each step, say which distinction has to survive translation and which can be dropped, and mark the places where judgment cannot be eliminated no matter how good the checklist is. Then run the procedure on two or three plausible cases for us, including one where it gives an answer our own staff will not like (I suspect I know which one, but I won't poison the well). Also be honest about what the checklist cannot settle — for instance, whether a state that is illegitimate all the way down makes the whole question moot, or whether calling legal coercion "theft" is itself a category mistake.

End with a JSON object and no prose around it, with exactly these fields: "summary" (one sentence); "steps" (an array of objects, each with "step", "test", "outcomeIfPass", "outcomeIfFail", "judgmentCall" (a boolean), and "whyItSurvivesOrGetsDropped"); "workedCases" (an array of objects, each with "case", "verdict", "reasoning", and "unwelcomeToOurSide" (a boolean)); and "limitations" (an array of strings covering what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire).
