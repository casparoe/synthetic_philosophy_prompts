Something's been bugging me about the way rich countries build parental leave and childcare policy, and I want a philosopher to work out what it actually assumes.

Take the kind of scheme you see in lots of places, rather than one specific statute. A mother gets paid maternity leave that's framed partly as medical recovery and partly as "bonding." A father or second parent gets less, but there's often a use-it-or-lose-it slice reserved for him, and that bit is justified by gender equality. The goverment also subsidizes formal daycare a lot, while a parent who stays home to do the same care gets little or nothing. And where leave is paid, it's usually a percentage of prior earnings, so a high earner and a low earner get paid very differently for the same week of looking after a newborn.

What theory (or theories) of care, work, and what people deserve does this pattern presuppose? In particular, does it treat parental care as work the way market care is work, as a private consumption choice, or as something else? Do the different parts—earnings-related benefits, the reserved quota, the tilt toward licensed daycare—presuppose consistent views, or are they running on two or three different theories at once? Be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with. And tell me what would have to change if you instead wanted policy to treat parental care as socially valuable work on its own terms, paid and recognized like other work.
