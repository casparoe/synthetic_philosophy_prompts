I retired a few years ago from a career in the geosciences and, rather late in life, have developed an interest in moral and political philosophy; one way this expresses itself is that I sit on the board of a mid-sized American family foundation and often end up as the board member who asks whether we are confusing measurement with judgment. We now face exactly that question. For the past decade the foundation has run its grantmaking through a cost-effectiveness framework: every grant must carry a theory of change with quantitative indicators, renewal decisions are driven chiefly by the numbers, and grantees may not reallocate funds between activities without prior written approval. We are reviewing a three-year grant to a small organization that works to strengthen the regulatory capacity of public-health and consumer-safety agencies in several low-income countries. The grantee met fewer than half of its agreed indicators, largely because midway through it suspended its planned public-awareness campaign and moved those funds into embedding technical staff inside the regulatory agencies themselves, a reallocation it never submitted for approval. The grantee's final report is candid that the original indicators were poor proxies for what it was actually learning on the ground, and an external evaluation we commissioned reaches the same conclusion: it identifies substantial but hard-to-quantify effects, including draft regulations that trace to the grantee's staff, new inter-agency coordination, and changed inspection practices that no single headline number captures. The program officer nonetheless recommends non-renewal, arguing that a disciplined funder cannot reward missed targets and unauthorized pivots. A minority of the board counters that this very reaction displays a measurability bias, and that our machinery has been quietly selecting against systems-level work, treating what is easiest to count as what matters, and pushing grantees toward cosmetic indicators.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's board meeting, in American English. It should recommend a concrete decision — renew, renew with conditions or a revised monitoring framework, or decline — together with any conditions or time frame the board should attach, and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than dismissing it. I am particularly interested in whether the unauthorized reallocation should count as a separate ground for non-renewal even if the pivot improved the work, and in whether our cost-effectiveness discipline is correcting for our own biases or institutionalizing them.
