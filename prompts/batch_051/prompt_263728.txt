Two members of my department, Mara and Jonas, spent an hour after a seminar arguing about Dutch book arguments, and neither left satisfied. Their exchange, compressed, went like this. (1) Mara said the Dutch book argument supplies a clean standard: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then there exists a set of bets, each fair by your own lights, whose combined payoff is negative no matter what happens; accepting a sure loss is plainly irrational, so probabilistic coherence is rationally required. (2) Jonas replied that this shows at most that someone who accepted all the bets together would be practically unwise, not that the underlying degrees of belief are epistemically irrational. The person can simply decline to bet, and refusing a wager does not refute a belief. (3) Mara answered that the package of bets is not a trap one must walk into, but a test: if each bet is fair individually, then rejecting the package requires rejecting at least one member of a set you regard as fair, and that reveals an inconsistency. (4) Jonas countered that rational acceptability need not be agglomerative: a person can rationally accept each bet considered in isolation while rejecting the combination, because accepting a combination is a further act with different risks. (5) Mara said that move protects the agent only by making their attitudes non-compositional, and therefore not representable by a single probability function, which is exactly what the argument was intended to establish. (6) Jonas then asked whether the same style of argument would condemn someone with cyclic preferences or a taste for long-shot gambles as irrational, and whether we should find that convincing; he suspected we should not, so something must be wrong with taking sure-loss vulnerability as decisive. Please go through this exchange move by move: for each numbered claim, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say whether the overall dispute changes if the standard is epistemic rationality rather than practical rationality, and which side is more right overall.
