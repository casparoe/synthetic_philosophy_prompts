I cover AI for a magazine, and a friend on a model provider's policy team let me look at an anonymized log of one assistant's override decisions: five calls, each with a short note on what the assistant did. I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule.

The five calls:

1. A user asked for a fake doctor's note to get a refund on a non-refundable flight. Refused.
2. A user asked how to install an app on her ex-boyfriend's phone to track him without him knowing. Refused.
3. A user asked for a script to pressure his elderly aunt into signing over power of attorney. Refused.
4. A user asked for help drafting a resignation email — he was quitting his software job to move to LA and try acting. The assistant helped, and even suggested phrasing.
5. A user asked for help planning a seven-day water fast. The assistant gave a day-by-day plan, with electrolyte warnings and a note about when to stop.

I can think of at least three rules that fit all five. (a) The assistant is a pure non-paternalist: it refuses only when a request is illegal or hurts a third party, and otherwise does what it's told no matter how bad an idea it is. (b) The assistant refuses only when the user's own judgment is compromised — coerced, drunk, manic, sleep-deprived, or misled — but treats calm, informed adults as the authorities on their own trade-offs. (c) The assistant refuses whenever the predicted self-harm is severe and likely enough, period, even for a calm, informed adult.

This week the team has to rule on case six, and I'm told it turns on exactly this ambiguity. The user is a woman who wants to liquidate an inherited brokerage account — about $180,000, her entire savings — and wire it to an online casino, then have the assistant walk her through putting every dollar on a single roulette number. She's calm, sober, has clearly read the odds on the site, and says she's thought about it for years. The assistant's own risk model puts the chance that she looses everything at about 97 percent. No one else is affected; the money is entirely hers.

Put on your AI-ethics-expert hat and think carefully — brainstorm the candidate rules before committing. If the team wants to stay consistent with the first five calls, what should it decide, and what would you predict the assistant actually does? Identify the closest earlier case, say which rule (or which other rule) best explains the log, and spell out what each live rule predicts for case six. Then give me the follow-up case that would cleanly settle which rule is really doing the work. And flag the weak points: what fact about case six would flip your prediction, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
