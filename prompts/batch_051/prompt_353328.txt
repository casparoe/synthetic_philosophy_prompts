I am a retired natural scientist, recently moved from the bench into philosophy, and I am drafting a short proposal for a private foundation that funds six-month projects in the philosophy of mind and action. Write the proposal itself (about 1,200 words), with no preface, on the following question: can a deliberating agent rationally assign determinate probabilities to her own future choices, or does deliberation crowd out prediction?

The tension I keep returning to is this. On one side, standard Bayesian practice treats a person's future choices as events among others; there is nothing special about the proposition that I will accept the second offer, and if I have degrees of belief about arbitrary propositions, I should have them about my own choices too. On the other side, deliberation appears to require treating one's options as live in a way prediction does not; the moment I settle on a probability for my choosing A over B, I seem to have turned a decision into an observed fact. My unexplained hunch, for what it is worth, is that the problem lies less in finding the right constraints on self-directed credence than in noticing that assigning such credences alters the deliberator's stance from agent to observer. I would like the proposal to pressure-test that hunch rather than merely build around it.

The proposal should do the following. State the question precisely enough that competing answers are mutually exclusive and testable. Explain why the standard treatments leave it open, discussing positions in your own words, such as reflection-style principles, Dutch book reasoning, and the split between evidential and causal decision theory where they bear on self-prediction. Describe the approach that would make actual progress and why it is better than the obvious alternatives, such as cataloging intuitions about cases or enumerating interpretations of probability. Say what a useful result would look like and what you would honestly count as the project's failure after six months. Finally, keep the scope realistic for six months of part-time conceptual work: identify which parts of the question the project would have to set aside and which it must answer.

You need not supply a bibliography or literature review; I will add the citations later. Begin with the proposal itself; do not preface it or describe what you are about to write.
