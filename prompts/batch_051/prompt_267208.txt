For a seminar on Madhyamaka I have been asked to circulate a statement of my own philosophical profile on Nāgārjuna before I present, so the rest of the group can audit it. I keep endorsing the following five claims in my notes:

(a) Emptiness is empty: the claim that all things lack svabhāva does not itself possess svabhāva, and this is a stable assertable claim.
(b) The tetralemma is universal: for any thesis about ultimate reality, the four alternatives — true, false, both, neither — are all to be rejected, and no fifth alternative is thereby affirmed.
(c) Conventional truth is not mere error: there is a genuine difference between a warranted conventional assertion such as ‘this is water’ and a delusive one such as ‘this is water with an intrinsic nature,’ even though both lack ultimate grounding.
(d) Madhyamaka is anti-foundationalist: no assertion, including the assertion that everything is empty, may be treated as a final philosophical position; all positions are provisional and therapeutic.
(e) The point of the tetralemma is to exhaust the impulse toward theoretical settlement, not to supply a hidden inexpressible truth that is somehow beyond the four alternatives.

What worries me is a passage in the Tathāgata chapter of Nāgārjuna’s Mūlamadhyamakakārikā. As I understand it, Nāgārjuna says there that a tathāgata is not properly described as empty, non-empty, both, or neither, and then argues that someone who is attached to the view that the tathāgata is empty has still not let go of grasping. I have been reading this as a local application of (b) to a special case, but I keep sensing that it may interact badly with (a) and perhaps (c), without being able to pin down how.

Are (a) through (e) jointly tenable? Please rank any tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally problematic, identify any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me which single revision would do the least damage while leaving the largest part of the package standing.
