I don't have any training in economics, but I've been reading about why markets sometimes seem to know things that no individual trader knows, and I came across an article that summarized a result called the no-trade theorem. According to the article, the theorem shows that under conditions of rational behaviour, trade should never happen at all. The reasoning, as the article presented it, runs like this: if one person wants to buy and another wants to sell at the same price, then each is revealing something by their willingness to trade; the buyer must think the asset is worth more than the seller does, which means they have different information. But if both are rational, each should immediately update on the other's willingness to trade. The buyer should ask why the seller is eager to get rid of the asset, and the seller should ask why the buyer is eager to acquire it; once they do, their beliefs converge, and the reason for the trade disappears. Since everyone knows this, nobody would offer a trade in the first place, and a market in which all participants were rational would dry up completely. The article then drew a strong lesson: because real stock markets see enormous trading volume every day, the assumption that traders are rational must be false, and the idea that prices aggregate information is self-undermining, since a price that perfectly reflected all available information would leave no one any incentive to trade or gather information, at which point the market could no longer price anything.

I found this puzzling, because the result sounds both very strong and yet strangely implausible: it seems to prove too much, and the article's conclusion about real markets seems to jump from a precise-sounding theorem to a sweeping claim about all trading. Could you help me sort out what the theorem actually says and where the article is being unfair? In particular, I'd like to know which parts of the summary a careful economist would accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations (for example, is "rational" really enough by itself, or does the theorem need extra assumptions such as common beliefs about the world?), what a defender of the idea that markets aggregate information would say in response to the article's conclusion, and whether that conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts of the summary.
