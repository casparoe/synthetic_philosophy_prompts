Consider a position that has emerged in the wild animal welfare literature but is rarely faced squarely by mainstream environmental ethics: a sentience-focused consequentialist should not treat habitat preservation as a good, because most wild animals plausibly have lives that are net negative — they die young from predation, starvation, disease, or exposure, and even many survivors may suffer more than they flourish. If so, then clearing forest, draining wetlands, or otherwise preventing an area from supporting life could reduce the total amount of suffering in an ecosystem, and might be morally better than leaving it intact. I want a map of how exposed this position actually is: what would have to be true for it to be wrong? Separate the distinct assumptions it rests on — the empirical claim about the average welfare balance of wild lives, the demographic claim about how habitat loss changes total population and its age structure, the axiological claim about how these outcomes should be aggregated, and the normative assumption that preventing lives of net-negative expected welfare is required rather than merely permissible. For each, say what would count against it, distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would be merely awkward, and indicate which escape routes are live rather than only logically possible, and whether defenders of the position would themselves concede them as real threats.
