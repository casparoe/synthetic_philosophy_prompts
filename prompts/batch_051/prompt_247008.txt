I am a novelist, and I am writing a book whose central character commits a serious crime after a childhood of severe abuse and neglect. My editor and early readers keep asking whether I consider the man morally responsible for what he did, and I have realised that I cannot answer that question consistently. I need a working definition of what it is to be morally responsible for a wrongful act — what I would mean by calling a character blameworthy — that I can apply across the whole book.

Here are the cases the definition should classify.

1. A man plans and carries out a murder for financial gain, after an ordinary, stable upbringing.
2. A woman assaults a stranger while in the grip of a delusion caused by an undiagnosed brain tumour.
3. A man who was beaten and neglected throughout childhood attacks a neighbour during a minor dispute.
4. A woman commits theft while severely addicted, having tried and failed many times to stop.
5. A soldier commits an atrocity after being ordered to do so and conditioned by months of training designed to override his inhibitions.
6. A ten-year-old child sets fire to a shop.
7. A man commits a spontaneous act of violence entirely out of character, which he cannot afterwards explain.
8. A woman, under no pressure and with full knowledge of the facts, breaks a promise for her own convenience.

I am confident that the tumour case is not one of responsibility and that the planned murder is; I also think the child is at most partially responsible. The abused man, the addict, the soldier, and the out-of-character act trouble me most, and I do not trust my intuitions about them.

Please propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, and say where it gives a verdict that surprises you or that you would want to revise.

Then I want you to test the definition against a deeper worry. It seems to me that a person can be responsible for an act only if he is responsible for the way he is — his character, motives, and habits of thought — because the act flows from the way he is. But he can be responsible for the way he is only if he is responsible for what made him that way: his temperament, his upbringing, his luck. And that in turn seems to require responsibility for still earlier causes, and so on backwards without any point at which he first becomes responsible for himself. If that regress cannot be stopped, does your definition imply that no one is ever morally responsible for anything? If it does, tell me whether I should revise the definition or accept that conclusion, and what either answer would mean for how I should judge my central character.

Please write your answer in British English.
