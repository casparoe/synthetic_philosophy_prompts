A buddy of mine has been going on about this bidding-war thought experiment he calls the dollar auction, and the argument he built out of it is bugging me. Setup: an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill. Highest bid wins the dollar and pays their bid. The runner-up also pays their bid but gets nothing. Bids go up in cents, there's no ceiling on the bidding, and pretend neither bidder has a hard limit on what they can afford, so "I'm broke" isnt what stops things.

Here's his argument:

(1) Say the bidding has already started and you're the one in second place.
(2) If you quit now, you pay your current bid and get nothing. If you raise by a cent and the other guy then quits, you pay your old bid plus a cent and get a dollar.
(3) Paying your old bid plus a cent for a dollar is a way beter deal than paying your old bid for nothing. So in that spot, raising is the smarter move, and quitting is just torching money.
(4) The other guy is in the exact same spot whenever he's the one in second place, so the same logic says he raises too.
(5) Therefore, once the bidding starts, neither of you can rationally be the one who quits. You keep outbidding each other forever—past the dollar, past ten dollars, past anything.

Now, that can't be the whole story, because real people do get out of bidding wars before they ruin themselves. But I honestly can't find the bad step. Step (3) feels airtight in the moment, and step (4) is just the same reasoning from the other chair. Is the argument valid, and if not, which step actually fails? If you reject (3), say what the person in second place should do instead, and why the one-more-cent reasoning is wrong even though quitting looks like throwing money away.
