In the cultural-evolution reading group I attend, there is a recurrent argumentative move I can't yet answer. Someone raises a hard case — a dance step that changes every time it is copied, a cooking technique transmitted by apprenticeship in which no two hands produce the same dish, an internet format whose content drifts while its framing stays rigid — and one of the senior students closes the discussion by saying that all of these are memes, and memes are replicators, so cultural evolution is just Darwinism on a different substrate.

I want to know what that one-liner could actually be claiming, because the word replicator seems to be doing several jobs at once. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims it might be making: for example, that something is copied from person to person; that cultural variants form lineages faithful enough for selection to produce cumulative adaptation without anyone intending it; that culture comes in discrete, particulate units with gene-like fidelity; or that Darwinian dynamics are substrate-neutral, so the analogy is more than a metaphor. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or probably false, and what kind of evidence would decide it. Then say which of these readings is the one actually doing the work when the line is used to settle whether some cultural phenomenon genuinely evolves, and whether the slogan survives that disambiguation as an explanation rather than a relabeling. Flag where your verdict is weakest, and tell me which argument or empirical finding would most plausibly change it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
