Below is a draft of a short essay I am developing, followed by a specific request. Please give me the substantive criticism a good journal referee would give, not line edits and not scholarly background; I am interested in whether the argument works, not in citations.

The revealed-preference tradition was built partly to rid economics of the old psychological conception of utility as an inner magnitude that agents consciously or unconsciously maximize. In its place, the theory starts from observable choices over budget sets and imposes consistency conditions on choices across overlapping menus; the weak axiom of revealed preference is the canonical starting point, with stronger generalizations for finite data. When the relevant conditions hold, standard results guarantee that the choices can be represented as maximizing a real-valued utility function. Utility, on this reading, is not a mental substance; it is a numerical index constructed from a pattern of choices.

But the as-if reading rests on a fault line. The consistency conditions are not properties of any single choice; they constrain a whole family of choices across different menus. When the data are finite, as they always are in empirical work, the usual rationalizability results are existence results only: many distinct utility functions fit the same observations equally well, and the conditions guarantee that at least one such function exists, not that a unique one does. Every substantive use of estimated utility — predicting how the agent will choose on a menu she has not yet faced, computing a welfare change, or recovering a true preference from inconsistent choices — therefore requires selecting among functions that are indistinguishable on the observed data. That selection is an act of projection: we must stipulate which regularities in past choice will survive into new contexts.

Here is the difficulty. If the claim that the agent acts as if she maximizes u is read strictly as a redescription of the observed choice data, then it licenses no projection; it is a compact summary, not an explanation or a prediction. If it is read as licensing projection and welfare analysis, then it amounts to a substantive assertion that the agent has a stable ranking of options across menus — just the sort of psychological or dispositional commitment that the as-if language was adopted to avoid. Revealed preference, on this view, has not replaced interpretation with observation; it has relocated the interpretation. The live question is not whether the data disclose an inner utility magnitude, but which coherence norm we are entitled to impose when we decide that an agent has one preference ordering rather than many menu-specific orderings.

This matters for behavioral welfare economics. When choice data violate the standard consistency axioms, the project of recovering true preferences is usually framed as separating an underlying utility function from the noise of context effects. But if the redescription reading of utility is correct, there is no hidden function to recover — only a modeling decision about how coherent an agent we are willing to posit. Disagreements about which choices to discard as mistakes and which to treat as informative are, on this picture, not empirical disagreements about a discoverable magnitude. They are normative and methodological disagreements about how to individuate the agent.
