Please compare two competing explanations of the paradox of hedonism. The first, which I will call the constitutive explanation, holds that happiness or pleasure cannot reliably be produced by aiming at it directly, because it is not a separable product of action but a byproduct of absorption in activities valued for something other than happiness; treating happiness as the point of the activity breaks the absorption on which the happiness depends. The second, which I will call the miscalibration explanation, holds that the paradox is mostly an epistemic failure: people who pursue happiness directly choose experiences they wrongly predict will satisfy them, and their disappointment reflects bad hedonic forecasting rather than any structural obstacle to aiming at happiness.

I want to know how far these two explanations are genuine rivals and how far they merely describe different parts of one phenomenon. Please work through these questions: (a) can the miscalibration explanation dissolve the paradox for a person with excellent hedonic foresight, or would some residue remain that only the constitutive explanation can capture? (b) does the constitutive explanation make any prediction beyond the claim that people often mispredict what will make them happy? (c) if someone deliberately sets aside the aim of happiness and absorbs herself in activities she values for their own sake as a strategy for becoming happy, does the strategic element reintroduce the failure the constitutive explanation describes? Then state which explanation you think does more work in the central cases, how confident you are in that verdict, and the specific point in your answer you are least sure about.
