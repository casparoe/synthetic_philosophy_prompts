A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether Descartes' case for treating nonhuman animals as unthinking machines survives the existence of large language models, and I want a careful, honest assessment of which of us is closer to right.

Descartes' argument, as I understand it, runs like this. The only reliable outward sign of a mind is the creative use of language: human beings can compose words to express an open-ended range of new meanings, while no animal does this; even a parrot that pronounces words does not speak, because it cannot respond appropriately to what is said. He adds a second test, that a genuine mind can act intelligently across all the varied circumstances of life, not merely perform some fixed task. From these tests he concludes that animal behavior can be explained mechanistically, without attributing thought to animals.

My friend's claim is that large language models have broken this argument at its main joint. Such systems produce fluent, context-sensitive, apparently novel language, yet almost no one believes that doing so gives them Cartesian thought. If the very sign Descartes took as decisive can be produced without the thing it was supposed to indicate, then his inference from language to thought is unsound, and his case against animal minds loses its main premise. My reply was that Descartes specifically imagined artifacts that imitate human speech, and his point was never about producing words but about answering appropriately and intelligently across arbitrary topics—which is exactly what these systems now do to a surprising degree. So the dilemma may cut the other way: either Descartes' criterion now commits us to attributing thought to the systems, or the criterion was always too weak to do the work he asked of it.

Which premise is actually under pressure here, and does the argument survive in repaired form? Please lead with your bottom line, then defend it, and keep the whole answer under 400 words in American English.
