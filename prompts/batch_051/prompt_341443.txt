Over the years my partner and I have been circling the same fight about voluntary simplicity as an ideal. I'm on the side that thinks wanting less and deliberately limiting your options is itself good—something close to a virtue, about not letting every passing preference harden into a need and not treating your desires as fixed. She's on the side that says frugality only matters instrumentally: money is fungible, the goal is to allocate it (and your attention and time) where it does the most good or buys the most real satisfaction, and if a $7 latte makes you substantially happier while you're already giving a lot, moralizing about the latte is mostly status signaling plus a failure to think at the margin. After a year we admitted we've probably been arguing past each other, but neither of us could write the document we'd both actually sign.

I want the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between us would produce. What claims can both of us accept about consumption, autonomy, signaling, preference formation, and what actually changes behavior? Where exactly do we part ways, in wording each of us would accept for our own side? What are the cruxes—I suspect one is whether there is any value to uneccesary restraint beyond its downstream consequences, and another is whether preferences for expensive or convenient things should be treated as fixed or as plastic and shapeable. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both of us would agree bears on it, and write down what each side predicts. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. If you conclude part of our dispute is verbal, say which part and why.
