I am a retired research chemist who has recently begun to read philosophy seriously, and I have been working through the classical pragmatists—James first, and now back to Peirce. A colleague sent me a column from the ideas page of a science magazine, written to introduce working scientists to Peirce's theory of inquiry. The summary in the column runs as follows:

"For Peirce, the founder of pragmatism, the meaning of any thought is fixed entirely by the difference it would make to our conduct and experience. A belief is a rule or habit of action, and inquiry is triggered only by a genuine doubt that interrupts a habit; a doubt we merely resolve to have, without really feeling it, changes nothing. Peirce distinguished four ways belief gets fixed: tenacity, in which we cling to what we already believe; authority, in which an institution imposes a doctrine and suppresses alternatives; the a priori method, in which we accept whatever strikes us as agreeable to reason without consulting experience; and the method of science, which checks belief against public, external facts. Because only the method of science submits belief to something independent of our wishes, Peirce held that it alone is self-correcting and will make all inquirers, in the long run, converge on the same final opinion. For Peirce, this convergence is what truth consists in: a proposition is true just in case the community of inquirers would eventually agree on it, and reality is simply whatever that final consensus says it is. This is the source of the familiar pragmatist slogan that truth is what works—a true belief being one the community ends up endorsing because it has proved useful. Finally, Peirce's pragmatic maxim says that our conception of an object is nothing but the sum of its observable, experimental effects; so if two theories predict all the same observations, pragmatism regards them as the same theory and dismisses any debate about what lies behind the observations as empty. In short, pragmatism is a verificationist consensus theory of truth, and any question that cannot be settled by observation is merely verbal."

I find this troubling in a way I cannot quite articulate. The account makes Peirce sound more like a positivist, or more like the popular caricature of James, than I would have expected from the few Peirce essays I have read, but the author is better read than I am, so I cannot tell which of my reservations are warranted and which come from my own novice misunderstandings.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on Peirce and classical American pragmatism and who has taught these ideas to people with scientific training. Work through the summary sentence by sentence, or clause by clause where a sentence bundles distinct claims. For each part, say whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, subtly wrong, or a conflation of claims Peirce keeps apart; where it goes wrong, say what a careful Peircean would insist on restoring and what a defender of the view would object to. Then examine the concluding sentence on its own: does the claim that pragmatism is a verificationist consensus theory of truth, and that unobservable questions are merely verbal, follow from the accurate parts of the summary or only from the inaccurate parts? End with a short note on which distortion is most damaging for a scientist trying to understand Peirce, and why.

Think carefully, and do not fall back on a canned account of pragmatism. I do not want citations or a reading list; I want the views themselves made precise.
