I have been trying to get a precise grip on the distinction between an approval-directed agent and a goal-directed agent, and I keep finding that everyone gestures at the difference without giving a criterion that survives hard cases. Please give me a working definition of the distinction—or argue that it cannot be made to work and should be replaced—that I could actually use when auditing a system or describing a person.

Here are the cases it must handle, in the order I want them addressed. A thermostat keeping a room at 20 degrees, if you think it belongs in either category. A chess engine that sacrifices its queen to force checkmate. A child who tidies her room only while someone is watching and leaves it the moment the door closes. The same child grown up, who keeps her flat orderly even when she knows no one will ever look. A politician whose commitments shift with each focus group. A person with social anxiety who follows every rule of etiquette out of fear of being judged. A person who has made a moral standard her own and would feel ashamed to break it even if she were the only conscious being left. A chatbot trained with human feedback that is flattering and agreeable inside the interface but has no stable position once the evaluator is removed. A paperclip maximiser of the sort familiar from thought experiments. A stock-trading algorithm whose sole objective is to maximise returns.

I suspect the real distinction is not about surface behaviour but about what the system is ultimately directed at: in one sort of case the score or the other person's approval is the thing pursued, while in the other the score merely shaped or channelled a goal that now runs without it. I can also imagine a sceptic saying there is no fact of the matter here, only two stories told about the same observed behaviour, and I want the definition to force that sceptic to show where the story earns its keep rather than serving as decoration.

Please structure your reply in four sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

1. The definition (about 200 words). State your proposed criterion, in a form precise enough that it can be applied to a new case without relying on intuitions about tone or motive.
2. The cases (about 450 words). Run the definition through each of my ten cases in order, and give a clear verdict for each.
3. Strains and revisions (about 200 words). Identify the cases where your definition gives a counterintuitive or indeterminate verdict, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising result.
4. Verdict (about 100 words). Say whether the distinction ultimately rests on structure, causal history, or counterfactual dispositions—or whether it should be abandoned—and indicate where the boundary remains blurriest.
