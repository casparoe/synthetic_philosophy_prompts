I am a PhD student in modern European history, and my working position is that the structuralist side of the Great Man debate is basically right for the Nazi case. I think the Versailles settlement, the Depression, the weakness of Weimar's party system, and long-running revanchist and antisemitic currents made some form of radical right-wing authoritarian takeover highly likely, so that even without Hitler Germany would probably have ended up with a regime broadly similar in kind, even if different in style and detail. On my view Hitler is best treated as a vector or accelerant, not an indispensable cause. I want a serious map of what would count against this position. What evidence, counterfactual arguments, comparative cases, or closer readings of the 1932–33 crisis should genuinely move me away from it, and which objections should I regard as merely awkward? What would the structuralist claim have to predict or rule out for it to be genuinely at risk? Does the view start to look unfalsifiable once it is hedged with "broadly similar in kind," and if so, is that looseness a defect or the right level of abstraction for a claim about large-scale causes? Finally, say which single consideration you think is most likely to change my mind and where my reasoning is most likely to fail.
