In a familiar passage from Lewis Carroll’s “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles” (1895), the Tortoise has accepted A, B, and C, but still refuses to accept Z:

“And why must I?”
“Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don’t dispute that, I imagine?”
“If A and B and C are true, Z must be true,” the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. “That’s another Hypothetical, isn’t it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn’t I?”
“You might,” the candid hero admitted.

Here are the standard responses to this puzzle that I already know and don’t need repeated:

1. Rule-as-premise response: the regress arises because the Tortoise treats modus ponens as an extra premise (C, D, E, …), but rules of inference are not premises; they are rules that license the move, so the demand for an added premise is misplaced.
2. Normative response: logic is normative—accepting A and B and the conditional commits one to Z—but a commitment is not itself a belief, so no further belief can supply the missing commitment.
3. Act/capacity response: inferring is something one does, a skill or capacity, not another proposition one believes; the Tortoise’s request is like asking for a further belief to make him exercise a skill.
4. Proof-versus-persuasion response: the dialogue conflates the objective relation of proof with the psychological process of being persuaded; a conclusion can be proved without the Tortoise being forced to believe it.

Is there another response to the Tortoise’s challenge that is genuinely different from these four—not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them? If there is, state it precisely and give an honest assessment of whether it succeeds. If you think the list is already exhaustive, say so and explain briefly why.
