I want a crisp operational account of "conceptual slippage" in the letter-string microdomain. Here is the setup as I understand it. In the classic task, a system sees that abc changes to abd—the rightmost letter is replaced by its alphabetic successor—and is then asked what xyz changes to. A rigid replace-the-last-letter-with-the-next-letter rule that treats the alphabet as circular would answer xya, because z wraps around to a. A rigid rule that treats z as having no successor would simply get stuck. But many human solvers, and the Copycat program on many runs, answer wyz. The standard explanation is that the relevant concepts slip under pressure: because z is the end of the alphabet, "rightmost letter" slips to the leftmost letter and "successor" slips to predecessor, preserving the abstract move of changing the exposed end of the string by one step in the direction the alphabet actually allows.

I want to distinguish genuine conceptual slippage from mere post-hoc storytelling or pattern matching. I propose the following four working criteria. A system exhibits genuine conceptual slippage on a letter-string analogy probe only when:

1. Pressure. The literal mapping is locally blocked—for example, a concept such as successor has no value at the relevant position in the alphabet.

2. Role preservation. The alternative answer preserves the relational roles of the blocked concepts, such as edge, direction, and distance, rather than being an arbitrary nearby-string or frequency-driven completion.

3. Systematicity. The same slippage appears on parallel probes that share the relevant structure but use different letters, without the system having been specifically trained or instructed on those probes.

4. Self-generated resolution. The system settles on the slipped interpretation in order to resolve the pressure, rather than executing an explicitly hard-coded branch for the failure case. I suspect this criterion is causal or internal, and therefore may not be applicable from the outside.
