I'm preparing to teach a fifty-minute section on metaphor in a second-year undergraduate philosophy of language course, and I'd like you to plan the lesson rather than explain the material to me. The students have already worked through Frege on sense and reference and Grice on conversational implicature, so they have some grip on the semantics/pragmatics boundary, but they have not done speech act theory or any psycholinguistics. The two positions on the table are Davidson's claim that a metaphor has no meaning beyond the literal meaning of the words — that metaphor is a use of language whose effects are causal and open-ended, not a species of meaning — and conceptual metaphor theory, on which metaphors are surface expressions of stable, systematic mappings between conceptual domains and thereby structure thought, not just talk. The students read the primary texts and came away thinking the two sides are merely talking past one another; my goal is to get them to see the sharper disagreement about where a metaphor's work is located.

I want the lesson plan to include, in this order: the sequence of ideas and what depends on what; a single carrying example that makes the contrast vivid and fair to both positions, with a justification of why it is better than the stock 'argument is war' or 'Juliet is the sun' examples (avoid those unless you can defend using one) — ideally an example where a literal paraphrase feels obviously inadequate, so students first feel the pull of saying there must be something more in the words before Davidson can try to dissolve that; the misconceptions students will predictably form — I suspect at least that Davidson thinks metaphors are meaningless and that conceptual metaphor theory predicts people consciously compute mappings every time they hear a metaphor — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected after the fact; one closing question I can put to the class whose answers would tell me whether they have actually distinguished metaphorical meaning from metaphorical use; and a short list of things I should deliberately decline to cover in this session and say so to the students.
