Three ordinary reactions keep pulling me in different directions. When I imagine a couple who would love, and could care for, a third child, I do not feel that a third child is, by itself, an improvement over two. When I imagine a city that grows denser and more crowded, I feel the average life getting worse. When I imagine the last small population of a species, I want to say the remaining members carry a weight that no average captures. Rather than resolve these reactions by picking a favorite case, I want the evaluative standard set down first.

Please write the desiderata for any adequate theory of population size and value: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse, the structural constraints it must satisfy, and the consideration behind each. Draw on ordinary cases (the extra child, the growing city, the remaining members of a species) as well as the standard problem cases, but describe the standard cases fully enough that each requirement stands on its own without a citation. Make sure the list commits, one way or another, on at least these points:

1. The theory must say explicitly how the value of a group relates to the well-being of its members: whether value sums, averages, or does something else, and whether that changes when we move from existing members' well-being to the well-being of additional members who would not otherwise exist.

2. It must not simply collapse average and total value. It must explain why average-like thinking seems right for some ordinary cases (the city that works worse as it grows) and why total-like or existence-like thinking seems right for others (the species reduced to its last members).

3. It must handle the case where adding people with lives worth living leaves no existing person worse off but lowers the average: is the larger population better, worse, or neither? The verdict should not be assumed; it should follow from the theory's stated commitments.

4. It must respect, or deliberately and explicitly reject, the person-affecting constraint. Many find it strange to say an outcome is better solely because of additional people who never exist in the alternative, since there is no one for whom it is better. But rejecting that constraint seems to require treating the creation of an extra life worth living as improving the world in the same sense as improving an existing life, which many find strange in the other direction. The desiderata should say which of these pressures is a datum and which is a negotiable preference.

5. It must take a stand on the Repugnant Conclusion in its standard form: whether, for any population whose members all have lives of high quality, there is some much larger population whose members all have lives barely worth living that would be better simply because it is much larger. The list should say whether avoiding this conclusion is a non-negotiable datum, a desideratum that may be traded against others, or a bullet a consistent theory can accept, and what the theory must then be able to say.

6. It must not assume the question is the same at every scale. Ordinary thinking distinguishes the extra child, the growing city, and the preservation of a species. The list should say whether these differ in kind or only in degree, and what follows from that for the theory's structure.

7. It must remain consistent with what is known about well-being: that adding people changes the shared environment, that individual well-being is partly comparative and context-sensitive, and that future people's identities depend on what is done now, so one cannot simply ask whom a policy harms or benefits.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps (totalists, averagists, person-affecting views, prior-existence views, and the like). After that, identify the subsets or pairs of requirements you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by quietly abandoning something that initially looked non-negotiable. That internal tension is the part I most need. Where several trade-offs are possible, say which single requirement is doing the most work to constrain the theory, and which one eliminates the largest share of familiar positions. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the existing theories; a short closing paragraph on how the main camps fare against the list is enough.
