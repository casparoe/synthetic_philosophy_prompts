Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in normative ethics, consent, and the ethics of risk. I need you to turn a half-formed idea into a short research proposal.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether it is wrong to impose a risk of serious harm on someone without their consent. She began with the strong claim that unconsented risk is itself a wrong, even when no harm materializes. I pressed the obvious objection: if that were right, ordinary life would be morally paralyzing, since driving, cooking, cycling near strangers, and hosting an event at which a guest might have an anaphylactic reaction all impose small risks of serious harm on people who never consented. She revised her view: those cases are different because the risk arises inside a mutually beneficial background practice, whereas what is wrongful is the act of extracting a targeted risk from a determinate other outside any such practice. I replied that "background practice" threatens to become whatever the agent already happens to do; the pedestrian struck by a delivery van has not, in any meaningful sense, joined the delivery system as a practice.

We ended by agreeing that we did not know how to draw the line. The half-formed idea I want to build the proposal around is that the familiar split between ex ante and ex post accounts of wrongful risk may be missing a more basic difference in how a risk attaches to a person. I suspect there is a principled distinction between risk that is embedded in a roughly reciprocal scheme of risk-taking (practice-embedded risk) and risk that is deliberately externalized onto a determinate other who is not a participant in that scheme (extracted risk). The difference is not simply magnitude, consent, or probability; it may be structural—a matter of whether the act treats the person as a participant in a shared arrangement or as a resource for the agent's activity.

Please turn this into a research proposal for a three-month summer fellowship in practical philosophy, about two pages in length. I know the broad conceptual terrain but have not read the specialized risk literature in depth, so refer to positions and arguments rather than named sources; I will add citations myself.

In the proposal, do the following:
1. State the research question precisely, in a form that has a determinate answer.
2. Explain why existing ex ante and ex post positions, and simple consent-based accounts, leave this question open.
3. Set out the method: what I would actually do, month by month; why this approach rather than obvious alternatives such as compiling intuitive judgments about vignettes or formalizing expected harm; and what kinds of concrete cases would have to be constructed.
4. Specify what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing.
5. End with a realistic assessment of scope, including what would be cut if the work runs long.

Please think through the structure before you write. Start by brainstorming at least two other research questions this material could support, and briefly say why you set them aside in favor of the one you propose. The deliverable is a plan for work not yet done, so the aim is to show both that the project is worth doing and that it can be done, not to defend the distinction as though it were already established.
