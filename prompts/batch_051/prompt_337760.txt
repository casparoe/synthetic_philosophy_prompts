Me and a friend have been arguing about why some games feel more "artistic" than others, and I've ended up with a homemade concept I want pressure-tested. I'm calling it shadow agency (working name, its probably bad). The idea is that a big chunk of what a game expresses lives in the actions that are salient to the player but not actually available: the button you reach for and isn't there, the dialogue option you'd pick but the game never offers, the walk-away move the game quietly doesn't implement. The claim is that these missing actions aren't a flaw or an oversight, they're a design material, like negative space. Shadow of the Colossus is my go-to example: the game never gives you a way to stop, or ask what you're doing, or refuse the next colossus, and I think the whole tragic pressure of it is carried by exactly that absence. Papers, Please is another: you're constantly shown ways you might help someone that the rules deliberately don't permit. And some game I won't name that makes you press a button to do the one thing you've been dreading — the dread seems to me to live in the missing alternative button, not the one you actually press.

I don't think this is just "affordances" — an affordance is what the environment offers as doable, and I'm pointing at the shape of possibilities that are refused but still somehow present to the player. And i know it smells like "negative space" in visual art, and maybe that's all it is, but in games the negative space is made of actions rather than marks, which feels importantly different. Is this actually a distinct thing, or have I just re-branded a known idea with more syllables? What does it get right, and where does it break? Most worrying to me: when does a missing action count as expressive rather than just a budget cut, a bug, or a player projecting something the game never signaled? And is shadow agency even a property of the game, or is it all happening in the player's head?
