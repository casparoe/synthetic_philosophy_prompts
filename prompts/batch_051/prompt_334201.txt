Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the norm of assertion: whether the fundamental rule governing flat-out assertion is that one must assert only what one knows, only what is true, or only what one believes. We left the argument roughly where we began, but something kept nagging at me, because I found myself reaching for an example neither of us could quite account for.

Consider sworn testimony in a common-law trial — or a deliberately simplified version of it. A witness must swear or affirm to tell the truth and not to hold back any part of it, and a witness who fails to do so can be punished; yet the offense punished is not simply false assertion, but, as I understand it, deliberately testifying to what the witness does not actually believe while under oath. A witness who guesses and happens to be right has not committed perjury, and a witness who testifies to a falsehood they sincerely believed is not a straightforward case of perjury either. At the same time, the law imposes a separate requirement that points in a different direction: witnesses must testify from their own first-hand knowledge, not from hearsay or speculation. So the practice appears to bind the witness only to truth in the oath, to punish the witness only for a certain kind of insincerity in the perjury rule, and to screen out some perfectly sincere testimony on the grounds that it is not known in the right way.

What norm of assertion does this practice presuppose? I want the implicit theory — or theories — reconstructed. Specifically: which parts of this practice strictly presuppose a particular norm, whether a knowledge norm, a truth norm, or a belief norm, and which parts merely fit one better than another? Do the three parts presuppose consistent things, or do they quietly conflict? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a defender of the knowledge norm, a defender of the truth norm, and a defender of the belief norm each claim the practice as their own, and where exactly would each have to strain to do so? Finally, suppose we took the knowledge norm of assertion as the only correct account and reformed the practice to match it throughout. What would the oath have to say, how would the crime of perjury have to be defined, and which familiar features of trial testimony would become indefensible?
