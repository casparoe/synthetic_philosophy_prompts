I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or linguistics, but I've been turning over something about how natural languages tolerate ambiguity, specifically homonymy, and I want an honest assessment of an idea I've been developing. In the systems I build, two bindings cannot share one name in the same scope without a collision; you can permit overloading only where the compiler has enough context—argument types, arity, namespace—to select a single candidate deterministically. Natural languages look as though they violate this constantly: "pitch" names a property of a sound, a throw in baseball, a sales presentation, and a resin; "tone" names a quality of sound, a muscle's resting firmness, a register of speech, and a conveyed attitude. Yet speakers rarely fail to parse one another in practice.

I've been calling my idea the "ambiguation budget." The central claim is that languages don't merely put up with homonymy; they allocate it. Each sense of a shared form carries a rough context signature—topic or domain, register, syntactic frame, collocations—and the senses of a single form persist only while those signatures remain disjoint enough that a plausible utterance rarely activates more than one. When two signatures begin to overlap productively, as in a music-tech meeting where both the acoustical and the commercial senses of "pitch" are live, the language responds over time: a compound emerges ("sales pitch"), one sense drifts toward another form, or the usage stays locked inside a niche register. The total unresolved ambiguity in a language at any moment is therefore something like a budget that usage keeps below the threshold where ordinary context stops disambiguating for free.
