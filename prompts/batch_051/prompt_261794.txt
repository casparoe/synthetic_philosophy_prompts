I'm a PhD student in decision theory and a buddy in my deparment gave me this version of Pascal's mugging, and now I can't stop turning it over. The conclusion looks absurd, but each step feels fine when I read it, and I can't tell whether the thing actually refutes expected utility theory or whether I'm just failing to see a quantifier being moved.

(1) Expected utility theory is the right account of instrumental rationality: pick the option with the highest probability-weighted sum of utilities.

(2) Utility is unbounded: for any finite value you can name, some larger finite value is better.

(3) Rationality never requires assigning credence zero to a proposition you can't rule out with certainty.

(4) You can't rule out with certainty that a mugger who promises to hand you U utils is telling the truth.

(5) So, for any promised utility U, you should assign the promise some positive credence p.

(6) Since p is positive, choosing U large enough makes pU bigger than the cost of handing over your wallet.

(7) So for any mugger there's a promise big enough that expected utility theory says: pay up.

(8) And once that's on the table, a second mugger can promise to undo the first mugger's payoff unless you pay him too, and a third likewise; the theory walks you into a predictable money pump.

(9) A theory of rational choice that walks you into a money pump on the strength of unsupported promises is no theory at all.

(10) Therefore expected utility theory, at least combined with an unbounded utility function, is false.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd like to keep (1) and (3), and I'm half-convinced the problem has to be (2). But I want to know whether giving up unbounded utility is enough, and what that costs, or whether the mistake is somewhere in the passage from (5) through (7). Please don't just gesture at the mugging being silly — I want the exact step and what rejecting or repairing it commits you to.
