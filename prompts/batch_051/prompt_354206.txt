Ok I'm a PhD student in risk regulation and this started as an argument with my officemate about whether everyday driving is worth it in micromort terms, you know, a micromort being a one-in-a-million chance of dying, driving coming out to something like one per 250 miles, a motorcycle maybe one per six, a skydive eight or ten. And I got stuck on the fact that we regulate speed limits and skydiving and workplace exposure one at a time, but nobody ever has to face a risk budget. So here is the mechanism I keep toying with instead of writing my prospectus.

Every adult gets a yearly allowance of, say, 1,000 micromorts, minted by the state with the total supply capped. Any discretionary activity with an actuarially stable micromort price, driving a mile, a skydive, an elective surgery under general anesthesia, a season of motorcycle commuting, requires spending credits from your ledger. A risk board posts the prices and you dint get to negotiate; run out of credits and the activity is illegal for you. Unused credits can be sold on an exchange, so prices float. You drive 15,000 miles a year, that's 60 credits. You also want to jump out of planes every weekend, that's another few hundred. Fine, if you've got them or can buy them.

Walk me through how this actually plays out, not whether it's moral. Who sells and who buys, what gets gamed first, which industries show up at the risk board on day one wanting their activity repriced, and does the cap actually reduce total voluntary risk or just sort it by income? I keep flip-flopping on weather it's a clever way to make people internalize the risk they put on others or just a market where poor people fund rich people's base jumps. Tell me where it fails first and whether that failure is fixable without turning the thing back into the command-and-control system it was supposed to replace.
