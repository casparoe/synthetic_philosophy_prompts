Working title: "The expensive-is-honest motte-and-bailey."

Notes:

The handicap principle as it shows up in blog comments is usually glossed as something like: costly signals are credible because only a high-quality individual can afford the waste. That's fine as a slogan, but it slides between two claims. Claim 1: honesty requires the signal to be differentially cheaper for the high-quality type. Claim 2: honesty requires the signal to be costly in absolute terms. A lot of people move from "this behavior is expensive" to "therefore it's an honest signal of some underlying quality," and I want to argue those are very different claims.

Absolute cost isn't necessary. You can get honest signaling at basically zero marginal cost if the signal is an index that physical or developmental constraints tie to the quality being advertised, so it can't be faked. And absolute cost isn't sufficient either: if a signal is expensive for everyone equally, and the benfit of faking it is bigger still, low types might happily pay the cost.

So the folk version looks like a motte-and-bailey. Motte: honest signals need the right cost asymmetry between types. Bailey: expensive or painful behavior is probably a credible signal, so I should update on it. People retreat to the motte when someone points out the problem, but keep applying the bailey in practice.

This matters to me because rationalist-adjacent spaces apply the bailey to stuff like: spending years on obscure blog arguments, keeping an intentionally broken sleep schedule while claiming it filters for productivity, getting expensive-but-practically-useless credentials, or performing costly unpopular opinions. I want to say the right question for each is "costly for whom, relative to what alternative, and does the cost difference track the quality that's actually being advertised?" Often the cost is just a one-time barrier, or a status norm, so it filters for free time, wealth, or willingness to be punished while it does little to signal the trait people claim to be reading.

One further move I'm considering: split "capacity-displaying costs" (only someone with lots of X could afford this) from "cost-as-commitment" signals (paying the cost changes your future incentives, the way burning a bridge does). The second kind can be honest even when everyone could afford the cost, so it also isn't a handicap in the absolute-waste sense. Blog discussions of the handicap principle rarely make this distinction, although i'd guess the academic literature implies it somewhere.

So: does this essay have a novel enough spine, or am I just relabeling a standard point? Are there clear cases where absolute cost really is doing the honesty work and my distinction would mishandle them? And is the motte-and-bailey framing fair to how the principle is actually used, or am i shadowboxing?
