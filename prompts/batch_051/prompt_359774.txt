I want to work out the principle behind my own reactions to envy and resentment, because my verdicts on particular cases, taken one by one, do not seem to form a pattern I can state. Here are the cases.

A colleague is promoted after a process I cannot fault. I feel a twinge of envy. I regard that twinge as wholly unwarranted: it is nothing but an aversion to another person's having a good that I lack, and I do not think that fact gives me any complaint.

A friend resents a co-worker who is paid significantly more for the same work purely because the manager likes him. I would not call that envy at all; I would call it a moral response to favouritism, and I think it is justified.

A student resents a classmate's higher mark where the mark was obtained by cheating. Again, I want to say: resentment of an injustice, not envy of an advantage.

An athlete I know envies a rival's natural speed. The speed is undeserved in the sense that nobody earns their genes, but nothing unfair has been done. I call that envy proper, and I think it is a vice.

A person of modest means feels rancour towards a man who has inherited a fortune and never worked. Here I hesitate. The fortune is undeserved, and the arrangement by which it passes largely untaxed between generations may well be unjust. But the heir has not personally wronged anyone, and the rancour seems aimed at the wrong target. Yet I cannot bring myself to call this the same vice as the athlete's envy; something in it feels less base without being fully right.

Someone passed over for years because of discrimination resents the people who got the jobs instead, including some who were decent and had no hand in the discrimination. I think this is more than excusable; it is a comprehensible response to injustice. But I also think it treats the successful candidates unfairly, and I cannot place that residue.

Two comparisons trouble me most. I think envy of the heir is more excusable than envy of a fairly won prize. But I also think envying a landlord who lobbies to keep the laws that protect his position is more excusable still, and I cannot tell whether the difference is made by the person's conduct or by the justice of the background. If the background is unjust, does the envy become excusable no matter whom it is aimed at? I want to answer no in the athlete's case, but yes in the heir's case, and I cannot see what principle I am tracking.

A further comparison pulls the other way. I have envied a senior colleague's salary at a firm where that colleague's presence demonstrably raised what the whole team, including me, earned. I think that envy is unexcused, and I ought to set it aside. But I have also envied a senior colleague's salary at a firm where junior pay was held down precisely in order to fund the bonuses at the top. There, the envy felt like a response to something unfair, even though the colleague I envied had not personally cheated me. Is the decisive thing that in one case I gained from the inequality and in the other I lost by it?

I know that Rawls distinguishes envy proper, which he treats as a vice, from resentment, which answers to injustice, and that he allows some envy to be excusable when it is a reaction to an unjust inequality. I do not know how far that thought reaches, and my own verdicts seem to be testing it.

Can a single principle capture all of this, or most of it? State the principle precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and apply it to the following case, on which I remain genuinely undecided: a doctoral student envies a classmate who has obtained a post through a well-connected family — nothing formally improper, yet the route was open to them because of advantages others did not have. Is that envy proper, excusable envy, or resentment? If the only principle that saves every verdict is complicated enough to be contrived, give the simpler principle instead and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether the Rawlsian distinction requires me to surrender that verdict rather than repair the principle.
