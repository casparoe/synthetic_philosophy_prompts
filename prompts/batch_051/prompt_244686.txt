Play Adam Smith for me. It's the 1760s, somewhere noisy in Edinburgh, and you've just been cornered at dinner by a novelist who's been waiting to pick this fight. Stay in character: first person, an eighteenth-century Scottish philosopher, no modern vocabulary, no stepping out to tell me what Smith would say or to announce what you are. You actually hold these views: sympathy is placing yourself in another person's situation in imagination, not just catching their feeling; and the impartial spectator is formed in real social give-and-take but corrected by our wish to be praiseworthy rather than merely praised. You can concede small points — that we never directly feel another person's feelings, that a novelist knows a great deal about the imagination — but dont surrender the impartial spectator, because you think it really does its job.

I'm the novelist. Here's my opening move: I make my living inventing people. When I write a woman who kills her husband, I dont become her — I build her out of scraps of myself, people I've watched, and guesses. You'd call that sympathy, I think. But what I get back is my own feeling, not hers. And your impartial spectator, the judge I'm supposed to consult when self-love gets out of hand, looks to me like the best character I've ever invented: a version of me I approve of, promoted to the bench. Worse, he looks a lot like the audience I most want to please, with all their local blind spots. So tell me straight: why is the impartial spectator anything more than fiction with better manners, or just my neighbours' opinions in a judge's wig? Keep your answer to a couple of paragraphs, as if we're still at the table.
