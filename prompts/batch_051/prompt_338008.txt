I sit on the Advertising Standards Advisory Board, and the board must decide this week whether an ad for a home water filter is misleading even though every sentence it utters is literally true. The commercial begins in a laboratory: a technician in a white coat fills a beaker, a clipboard is checked, and the voiceover says the filter is certified to reduce lead in independent laboratory testing. All of that is true, but the certification protocol used deionized water at a controlled pH and temperature, and the certifying body's own documentation says the certification says nothing about performance with ordinary tap water. The ad cuts directly from the lab to a kitchen where a mother fills a child's cup from the tap, as the voiceover says, "Trust the science." The complainant argues that the ad means something false: that the filter's lead reduction has been validated for real-world drinking water. The company responds that it asserted only a true proposition and that consumers' optimistic inferences are their own.

Half the board says only the literal content of an ad's sentences counts as a claim for our purposes. The other half says that any workable advertising review must treat the ad's conveyed meaning—its speaker meaning, not just its sentence meaning—as the company's doing. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before Friday's meeting. It should recommend whether to find this ad misleading, and it should lay out a general test, one reviewers can actually apply, for when content that an ad merely implies while asserting only truths should count as a claim attributable to the advertiser. Engage the strongest version of the company's argument, which is that a message the company could deny later without contradicting itself was never really said, so the company cannot fairly be held to have meant it. Also take a position on the following distinction: a speaker sometimes means something openly, intending the audience to recognize the intention; an advertiser sometimes engineers an impression while hoping the audience will not notice that the impression was engineered. Does this difference make the company's conveyed message more or less attributable to it? Use these conceptual considerations to justify the test, and do not rely on citations to an advertising code.
