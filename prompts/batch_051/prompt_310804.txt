Me and my friend got into an argument about The Hurt Locker that turned into an argument about courage, and now I have a half-baked distinction I want stress-tested while also getting an actual reading of the film, not the usual war-is-a-drug gloss every review gives.

The distinction I'm toying with: "martial courage" vs "peacetime courage." Martial courage is organized around being the kind of person who can face death and, when needed, deal out violence on behalf of a group; it's formed in lethal-conflict conditions and has it's own internal standards — steadiness under fire, not abandoning your people, not flinching from the thing in front of you. Peacetime courage is the kind that fits normal institutions: whistleblowing, public speaking, sitting with a dying parent, standing up to a bully. It also involves facing fear, but the fear is usually social or emotional rather than death or organized violence, and the excellence is about preserving life and order, not being good at risking them. The worry I have is that these aren't two species of one genus; they may have almost nothing in common except the word "courage."

So: read The Hurt Locker through that distinction. Pay attention to the ending, especially the grocery-store scene and his return to Iraq. Does the film show that a peaceful society needs martial courage, keeps it around as a vestige, is threatened by it, or has no place for it? And does the distinction survive contact with the film, or does the film mess it up — for instance by showing peacetime courage inside the war zone or martial courage inside the grocery store?
