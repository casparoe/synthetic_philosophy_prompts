I've been turning over an idea for a short essay on the paradox of tragedy, and I'd love a philosopher's read on the notes before I write the real thing. I'm a novelist, so my angle is less 'why do we enjoy King Lear' and more 'why do readers reread the death scene in a novel they know will wreck them, and still treat it as one of the best parts of the book?'

My working hunch is that most accounts either make the sadness unreal (we only pretend to feel it, or we're protected from it) or convert it (the sadness is secretly a subtle pleasure). I want to suggest instead that the sadness is genuine, and still unpleasant at the level of feeling, but that fiction changes the structure of the emotion rather than its intensity. In ordinary life sadness usually comes wrapped up with action demands: it pushes you to mourn, console someone, fix the situation, look away, do something. In fiction those demands are disabled, because the story won't answer anything you do, so what you're left with is something like unencumbered sadness: the full feel of the emotion without its claim on your agency. The payoff isn't in the sadness itself but in getting to feel a strong emotion with no pressure to manage it. I think that also explains rereading: on a first pass the plot keeps you hoping or dreading, but by the second pass those practical pressures are used up, so you can just sit inside the feeling.

Worries I already have: this might just be the old safety/distance view with the word 'agency' stapled on; and 'action demands' may be doing too much work, since plenty of ordinary sadness doesn't demand much action either. Specifically, I'd like comments on whether the split between an emotion's feel and its action-tendencies can do the work I need, and whether this is actually different from the standard views in a way that matters. Also, which part of the argument is most likely to collapse first?
