I've been trying to get clear on what makes something count as computing, becuase I don't want 'the brain is a computer' to turn out to mean only 'the brain is a physical system with causes.' My working criteria are: (1) finitely many distinguishable internal states, (2) a definite transition rule that maps current state plus input to next state, (3) the states are representations — they're about something the system is tracking, not just causes — and (4) the thing can in principle be reprogrammed to do a different task. Now take the lac operon in E. coli as the borderline case: lactose and glucose get sensed, a repressor and an activator integrate those two signals, and the lactose-metabolism genes come on only when lactose is present and glucose is absent — in effect a molecular AND gate with a NOT gate on glucose. Go criterion by criterion and say whether the lac operon computes. Then use the verdict to say what's left of computationalism: if it does compute, does that make the claim about brains trivial; if it doesn't, does the brain fail the same test? It's fine if the answer is that one of my criteria is doing all the work or should be dropped.
