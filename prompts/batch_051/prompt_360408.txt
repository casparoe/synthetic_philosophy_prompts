I sit on the medical policy board of a regional organ-procurement organization, and we must decide whether to authorize a controlled donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) protocol across our transplant centers. Under the proposal, when a ventilator-dependent patient has consented to withdrawal of life support and to donation, extubation occurs in the operating suite; death is declared after five minutes of continuous circulatory arrest with no attempt at resuscitation; organs are then recovered immediately, since longer intervals render most of them unusable. The board's governing rules allow organ recovery only after the donor is dead, and recognize death by either neurological or circulatory criteria. The board is split. Opponents argue that at five minutes the loss of circulation is not irreversible—the heart could in principle be restarted in many of these patients—so the donor is not yet dead, and proceeding would mean killing a dying patient by removing her organs. Proponents reply that the operative standard is permanence, not irreversibility: circulation has permanently ceased because no one will attempt resuscitation and spontaneous autoresuscitation after five minutes of asystole is rare in this setting. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before next week's vote, recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; it should give board members concrete criteria they can apply and engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument.
