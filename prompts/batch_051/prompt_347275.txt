A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about the zombie argument against physicalism, and we ended in worse agreement than we started: both of us could rehearse the other's moves, yet each of us kept producing a different position depending on how a case was phrased. We also noticed that we had been using the word conceivable to do several different jobs at once. My friend proposed that, before either of us defends a view again, we should build a sorting instrument that classifies people by the underlying commitments they actually reveal in their answers to concrete cases, not by the label they recognize or prefer. I want you to write that instrument.

It should be a questionnaire of eight to ten items for a philosophy discussion group whose members have heard of the zombie argument but know no technical apparatus. Each item should pose a concrete scenario and offer a small number of answer options; together the items should act as a classification tree with a scoring key. No item may use the words conceivable, possible, physicalism, physicalist, dualism, dualist, phenomenal, or qualia, and no item may name a philosopher or a named position.

The items must separate at least the following commitments: whether a respondent takes a behaviorally identical duplicate who entirely lacks experience to be a genuine alternative way a world could be, or merely a picture they cannot be made to cash out; whether they treat the absence of an explicit contradiction as a license to infer a real alternative; whether they think first-person acquaintance with an experience delivers a truth that no third-person description could entail; whether they think a scenario could seem coherent to them while an ideal reasoner would see it as incoherent; and whether they take modal intuitions of this kind to be evidence about reality at all or merely evidence about their own habits of imagination.

Build the scoring key as a decision space rather than as points attached to preferred positions: first identify the master questions whose answers define the cells, then locate the familiar positions inside the resulting space. The leaves should include, at minimum, a modal-rationalist dualist; an a posteriori physicalist who grants that the zombie is conceivable but denies the inference to possibility; an analytic or structuralist physicalist who denies that the zombie is genuinely conceived in the first place; an illusionist or eliminativist about experience; and a modal skeptic who regards the whole genre of modal intuition as idle for settling what there is. Where two of these cannot be separated by any case you can construct, say so explicitly and do not add a question that does no diagnostic work. Mark any answer combination that appears logically permitted but unoccupied, and diagnose each such empty cell briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

One position must be handled with particular care. My friend kept refusing to say whether the zombie scenario describes a real alternative, because he held that the question itself already imports a picture of experience as a separate, reified fact, yet he would not accept the illusionist's flat denial that there is something it is like. If the answer combinations allow it, include that position as a leaf; if they do not, explain which question it would fall through and why the instrument cannot currently capture it without adding an extra item.
