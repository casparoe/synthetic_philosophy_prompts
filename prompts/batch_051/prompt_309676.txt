I'm an economist by training — comfortable with dynamic programming, habit-formation models, reference-dependent preferences, and the distinction between decision utility and experienced utility — but I have not read the philosophical literature on well-being, and I keep running into a claim there that I don't know how to parse. The claim, as I understand it, is that novelty, variety, or adventure might be a component of well-being in its own right, rather than merely a tool for managing hedonic adaptation. The opposing view says that once you've correctly specified the utility function — habit stocks, diminishing marginal utility, reference points that drift upward after each new experience — the apparent intrinsic value of novelty disappears into the mechanics of adaptation. Yet the first view strikes me as capturing something real: many people report a fear of a boring life that is not just a fear of low average happiness, and I suspect a great many people would decline an offer to live the same genuinely pleasant week on loop for the rest of their lives, even with their memory reset each cycle. Would they be making a mistake on the economist's picture, or is there something about the structure of a life — not just its per-period experienced utility — that the apparatus is missing?

Please restate this debate as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: utility over streams of experiences, habit formation, reference points, love-of-variety, discounting, time consistency, and the decision/experienced utility distinction. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which can be represented only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the economist's toolbox at all? And for each piece of residue, tell me whether the failure is a limitation of the toolbox or evidence that the philosophical claim was less precise than it looked.
