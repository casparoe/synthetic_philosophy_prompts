In a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, we spent a session on the White Horse Dialogue, a text from the ancient School of Names whose central move is the slogan "a white horse is not a horse." As I understand it, the dialectician backs the slogan with two considerations. First, "horse" is used to name the shape while "white" names the color, so the two terms are not doing the same work. Second, a request for a horse can be met by offering a yellow or black horse, whereas a request for a white horse cannot, which is meant to show that the two expressions mark out different things. The session leader kept pressing the slogan as if it refuted the commonsense view that a white horse is obviously a horse. I suspect several distinct claims have been tangled together into one. Lay out the precise theses that the sentence "a white horse is not a horse" could be used to assert, arranged from the trivial to the radical, indicate which of them are actually supported by the two considerations I paraphrased, and say which precise theses the slogan would need in order to refute the commonsense view. Present it as a menu of readings I could bring to the next session.
