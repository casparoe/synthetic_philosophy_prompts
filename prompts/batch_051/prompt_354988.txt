I work as a software engineer and have never studied philosophy formally; the vocabulary I think in is data modeling and object-oriented design: entities and fields, class versus instance, rows, columns, primary and foreign keys, join tables for many-to-many relationships, object identity, references and pointers, schema constraints, and the difference between a relation as a schema element and a relationship as stored data. I recently encountered a philosophical argument called Bradley's regress concerning the unity of the proposition, and I want you to translate it into that vocabulary and tell me what survives.

As I understand the philosophical setup: take a simple fact or proposition such as "the apple is red" or "aRb." It contains at least two ingredients, the particular (the apple, a, b) and the universal or relation (redness, R). The problem is that a bare collection of ingredients — [apple, redness] or [a, R, b] — is not yet a proposition or fact; it is just a list. Something extra must bind the ingredients together into a unified thing that can be true or false. Call that something "instantiation" or "the tie." Bradley's regress is the argument that positing such a tie as a further relation only pushes the problem back: if instantiation is a relation standing between an object and a universal, then [a, instantiation, R] needs yet another relation to bind instantiation to a and to R, and so on without end. Worse, treating the tie as one more object or relation makes the proposition look like just another complex object related to its parts, rather than something asserted or judged.

Now do the translation. Restate the problem, the structure of the regress, and the main responses to it (for example: instantiation as a special non-relational tie, taking facts or propositions as primitive, treating a relation as unsaturated or as something function-like, or denying that the regress is vicious) using the constructs of my trade — rows, foreign keys, join tables, class and instance, fields, references, serialization, functions or methods, schema constraints. Be honest about the translation rather than force-fitting: for each philosophical move, say whether it maps cleanly onto some construct, maps only with distortion, or has no counterpart at all. Then give me the residue report. In it, identify which parts of the philosophical problem evaporate once restated in a data model, which parts survive unchanged, and which parts my vocabulary cannot even express. End with your verdict: is Bradley's regress a genuine problem about how constituents are unified, or is it largely an artifact of a notation for representing facts — and if your answer is "mostly artifact," what residual difficulty, if any, does my framework still leave unaccounted for?
