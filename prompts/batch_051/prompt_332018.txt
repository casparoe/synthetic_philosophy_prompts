In a metaethics seminar I attended, a presenter was defending the thesis that ordinary moral thought and talk commit us to there being stance-independent moral facts. Her central example was that a sentence such as 'torturing a child for amusement is wrong' is treated, in ordinary practice, as an assertion that can be true or false, and that its truth does not depend on the attitudes of any individual or group. During the discussion, a participant objected that this runs straight into the familiar point that there are no moral facts. The presenter paused, said she thought the objection conflated several things, but did not spell it out, and the conversation moved on.

I suspect the sentence 'there are no moral facts' can be used to express several quite different precise claims, and that only some of them would actually threaten the presenter's argument. I would like a careful disambiguation. For each distinct thesis the slogan could be stating — for example, a thesis about whether moral judgments are truth-apt, about whether moral truths would need to be objective or stance-dependent, about what kinds of things exist, or about how moral claims could be known — please do the following: state the thesis precisely; say whether it is close to trivial, a substantive and defensible position in metaethics, or too strong to be plausible; and explain whether that reading would undercut the presenter's argument or leave it standing. I would find a menu of readings with verdicts much more useful than a single interpretation.
