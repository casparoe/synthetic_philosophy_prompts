I'm not asking you to take a side on torture. I'm asking you to write the contract any answer has to sign before I bother reading it. The job for the theory: deciding when, if ever, agents of a state may deliberately inflict severe pain or terror on a person to get something out of them, where the decision has to be usable by someone drafting actual rules for fallible people under uncertainty, not just by a philosopher grading the exact case where the bomb is definitely wired, the suspect definitely knows the combination, and the timer is already running.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that forces it. I want the list to cover at least these, which the usual essays skip: the realistic case where there's a suspect and elevated risk but everything is noise; the coercions that stop short of rack-level violence (sleep deprivation, stress positions, mock execution, threats against family) and whether the line the theory draws there tracks a real moral difference or just a legal fiction; the uses that aren't interrogational at all, like making an example of someone or breaking a person for its own sake; and whether the theory has to respect the empirical finding that information given under severe pain is often worthless because the person will say whatever ends it. Mark which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the absolutists or the consequentialists smuggling in their conclusion. Then tell me which requirements you think can't be jointly met — in particular whether the single officer's desperate-moment permission and a stable refusal to license the institution can both survive, or whether one of them has to give. Say which criterion does the most to separate the serious theories from the rest. Dont give me a tour of named positions; the criteria are what I keep finding myself without.
