For a seminar on the philosophy of death, we have been reconstructing Bernard Williams's Makropulos argument against the desirability of endless life, and one participant offered the following sharpened version, which she says would carry an unacceptable consequence if it worked: it would license a hidden expiry date for ordinary finite lives. I want the immortality objection to survive, but I cannot accept that consequence, and I cannot find the step at which the reconstruction overreaches. Here is the argument, with her numbering.

(1) A stretch of life is desirable to continue only if the person has at least one categorical desire—a desire that gives a reason to go on living which is not conditional on already being alive (for example, finishing a book, seeing a child settled, or finding out how a project turns out). Merely wanting not to be bored, or filling time with agreeable diversions, is not enough.

(2) For any actual person, the categorical desires they can form are bounded by their character: the relatively stable configuration of cares, aptitudes, and sensibilities that makes their later stages recognizably continuous with their earlier ones. If a later stage's desires share nothing with that configuration, the later life is no longer the survival of the original person in the sense relevant to wanting it.

(3) A character so understood is a finite repertoire: it delimits finitely many kinds of engagement that could genuinely matter to the person, however long they lived, rather than merely occupy them.

(4) Categorical desires are exhaustible. A desire of that sort, pursued to completion or abandoned, is not an indefinitely repeatable source of reasons; replaying it, or cycling among the elements of the same repertoire forever, just is the condition of boredom rather than a continuing reason to live.

(5) From (3) and (4): for any actual person, there is a finite upper bound on how many stretches of genuinely categorical engagement their character could support before nothing in the repertoire would present itself as worth doing for its own sake.

(6) If a person lives past that bound, nothing in their future engages them non-instrumentally; their relation to their own future is boredom, a state in which further time is something to be gotten through rather than used.

(7) Therefore every actual person has a determinate length of life, fixed by their character, past which continued existence is a burden rather than a good, whatever pleasant diversions fill the days—in other words, a hidden expiry date.

(8) That conclusion is unacceptable. People in their eighties and nineties can discover, with surprise, whole new vocations, friendships, studies, or forms of care that were never latent in any earlier repertoire, and we neither treat them as having been replaced nor dismiss their testimony that they have found a new reason to live.

So at least one of (1)–(4) is false, or one of the transitions to (5)–(7) is invalid, or some term—'character,' 'repertoire,' 'replay,' 'boredom'—shifts its sense between premises. I would most like to keep (1). Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost the Williams-style tedium objection to immortality? In particular, identify whether the premise that must go is one the objection needs only in the infinite case, and whether the objection can be restated so that it no longer entails an expiry date for finite lives. Assess the argument on its own terms rather than dismissing it as un-Williamsian, but say explicitly which claims Williams's actual argument does and does not need.
