I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about generics — bare-plural statements such as 'ducks lay eggs,' 'mosquitoes carry malaria,' and 'sharks attack bathers' — and about what it takes for one of them to be true. By the end we had worked out a four-part test, and I want you to evaluate it. Please approach this as an expert in the philosophy of language, and think carefully before settling on your answer.

The test we arrived at is this.

(1) Prevalence. For a generic of the form 'Ks F' to be true, F must hold of most Ks; a property held by only a minority of the Ks cannot ground a true generic.

(2) Kind-based distribution. The Ks that are F must be F in virtue of being Ks, not in virtue of some shared accident or external circumstance that happens to correlate with kind-membership.

(3) Principled exceptions. Exceptions to (1) may be tolerated only when the non-Fs form a principled subclass of the kind — for instance, a subclass defined by sex, age, season, or developmental stage — so that the failures are due to that partition rather than to the kind's simply not being connected to F.

(4) Projectibility. On learning only that some individual is a K, you should be able to treat F as your default hypothesis about it, defeasible by evidence that the individual falls into a principled exception class of the sort allowed by (3).

The case we got stuck on is 'mosquitoes carry malaria.' My friend thinks our test pronounces it false, and that we should therefore reject the sentence; I think the sentence is clearly true, so the test must be what gives way.

Work through the case criterion by criterion. It plainly fails (1): at any given time only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes carry malaria. Criterion (3) does not obviously save it, because the non-carriers are not a principled exception class — they are the overwhelming majority — and if anything it is the carriers who are exceptional. Criterion (4) is also unfriendly: if you have to pick an arbitrary mosquito, you should not expect it to carry malaria. The only real support comes from (2), and even that is delicate: when a mosquito does carry malaria, it does not do so by accident; it does so because it is a member of the relevant vector species, with the physiology, blood-feeding behavior, and place in the transmission cycle that make infection possible. That looks like a connection grounded in kind-membership, not in coincidence.

Does 'mosquitoes carry malaria' qualify as true under the four-part test? If it does not, should the sentence go, or should the test be amended? If you amend the test, say exactly which criterion gives way and state the replacement clause. Then stress-test the amended test against the controls we both accepted or rejected: 'ducks lay eggs' (accepted, though it fails (1) and arguably (4) when male and juvenile ducks are counted), 'sharks attack bathers' (accepted, though rare), 'dogs are dachshunds' (rejected), and 'ducks are female' (rejected). Before committing yourself, brainstorm a few candidate amendments and try to construct counterexamples to each; then give your final verdict and say whether a single set of criteria can handle all of these cases, or whether we were trying to make one test do two different jobs.
