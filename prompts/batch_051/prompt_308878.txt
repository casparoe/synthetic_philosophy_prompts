I am a novelist finishing a multigenerational saga in which characters are addressed almost exclusively by given name in everyday life, while surnames are reserved for legal, archival, and liturgical contexts; over the course of the book, the narration advances the idea that a surname does not refer to an individual person at all but to a lineage—a scattered, temporally extended object—whereas the given name is the true individual designator. Next month I am to present this as a serious thesis in the philosophy of language at an interdisciplinary symposium, and I need to be ready for the question period, which I expect to be bruising.

The audience will include: one philosopher of language committed to a causal-historical theory of reference who treats proper names as rigid designators; a second philosopher of language committed to a descriptivist account, on which names encode identifying descriptions; a linguistic anthropologist who has done fieldwork on matrilineal clan names, patronymics, and teknonymy; and a genealogist from a national archive whose career has been spent tracing the instability of surnames through adoption, anglicization, marital change, and illegitimacy. The thesis I will defend is that surnames are not simply inherited proper names that happen to be shared, as the standard account would have it, but a distinct category of term—call them lineage designators—whose reference is fixed to a family line rather than to any one bearer; and that this distinction explains otherwise puzzling features of surname practice, including marital name changes, patronymics and matronymics, the strained feel of surname-only address, and the way a surname persists across the births and deaths of individual bearers while a given name does not.

Please act as an expert in the philosophy of language with experience preparing speakers for interdisciplinary question periods. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the objections each of those four audience members would most naturally raise from their own commitments. Then produce a Q&A prep sheet: the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular audience member would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply, an honest note on where the reply is weakest, and an indication of what I should concede and what I should hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why I should fear it. Throughout, flag which objection should most plausibly lead me to revise the thesis rather than merely answer it, and identify where the core reasoning—that given names and surnames are different kinds of referring device—is most likely to fail. Finally, tell me what counterexample, empirical finding, or argument would change your own assessment of how defensible the thesis is.
