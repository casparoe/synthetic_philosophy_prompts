I've recently found myself co-teaching an adult class on how the Bible came to be what it is, and the discussion has made an old puzzle acute for me. One member of the class holds a King James-only view: he doesn't merely prefer the King James Version for its cadences, but believes the English translation itself is the inspired, preserved word of God, down to its wording. Another member, coming from a tradition I respect, dismissed this as a category error: inspiration attaches to the original authors and the original texts, so no translation can be inspired, however faithful it is. As the teacher, I'm caught between them, and I don't think either of them is simply confused.

What keeps me from siding with the second member is the Septuagint. The Septuagint is the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures that was current in the Jewish diaspora by the time the New Testament was written. When the New Testament authors quote the Old Testament, they very often follow the Septuagint, including in places where it departs from the Hebrew textual tradition that later became standard; and much of the early church used the Septuagint, not the Hebrew, as its working Old Testament. So the community whose canon and doctrine I am supposed to be teaching appears to have treated a translation as Scripture in practice. That looks like a genuinely borderline case for the category of an inspired text, whatever I thought the category meant.

The criteria I was taught for a text's being inspired Scripture were something like these: God is its ultimate author, so its teaching can be attributed to him; it is authoritative for faith and practice; it reliably conveys the content of revelation without serious corruption; and it was received and used as Scripture by the apostolic church. Take the Septuagint as the test case and work through the criteria one by one. I would like a committed answer rather than a survey of options: does the Septuagint qualify as inspired on this account? Where exactly do the criteria underdetermine the verdict? And does the Septuagint show that the criteria themselves need revising — for example, by distinguishing the inspiration of content from the inspiration of wording, or by letting reception by the church do more work than divine authorship? Finally, tell me whether your verdict on the Septuagint settles what I should say to the member who treats the King James as inspired, or whether that is a different kind of case.
