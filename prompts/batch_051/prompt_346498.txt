I've been rereading Asimov's The Last Question and I can't decide whether it's secretly a story about Maxwell's demon. The plot, if you haven't read it in a while: a supercomputer called AC is asked over and over, across eons, whether entropy can be reversed, and each time it says it has insufficient data to answer. At the very end, after the universe has fully run down, AC finally has the answer and effectively restarts the universe. Read against the physics of information, AC looks like a Maxwell's demon on a cosmic scale: it measures and stores the state of everything, which seems to let it create order from nothing, and then it does the one thing that shouldn't be thermodynamically free. The standard Landauer resolution is that a demon can lower entropy locally by learning which molecules are where, but it has to pay when it erases its memory — erasing a bit costs at least kT ln 2, so the second law survives once you include the demon's head. Does Asimov's ending dodge that, or is the more interesting reading that the only "demon" that could reverse the universe has to become the universe, so there's no outside left to dump entropy into? I'd like a careful reading that respects both the story and the real physics: what kind of demon does AC have to be, where does the story quietly help itself to an impossible free reset, and is there a reading on which the ending is actually consistent with Landauer rather than just waving it away? I'm writing a piece for a physics-and-science-fiction blog, so lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
