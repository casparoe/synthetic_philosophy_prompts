I'm a philosophy professor, and here's an asymmetry I keep running into at faculty meetings, of all places. When somebody wants to create a new requirement—a new assessment, a new committee, a new form—we make them do all the work: they have to show it will work, estimate what it will cost in time, anticipate how it will get gamed, on and on. The new thing is guilty until proven innocent. But when somebody wants to remove an old requirement or dissolve an old committee, nobody asks them to show the removal will work; the old thing is innocent until proven guilty, apparently just by having survived. And this isn't only universities. I see the same asymmetry in arguments about tax law, traffic rules, social customs, whatever. Everyone demands a justification for adding a rule, but almost no one demands a justification for deleting one. I'm trying to figure out whether this is defensible or just status quo bias with a thesaurus. The natural defense is Chesterton's fence, with the older Burkean idea behind it: an inherited arrangement encodes the accumulated practical knowledge of the people who built and maintained it, much of it tacit and never written down anywhere, so you should not tear the fence down before you have found out waht it was put there for. But I'm not sure that argument carries all these cases, or only the ones where we already suspect on independent grounds that the thing is doing real work. Give me the strongest version of the Burke/Chesterton defense of this burden-of-proof asymmetry you can, and then an honest verdict on whether it actually justifies the asymmetry across cases like an obscure procedural rule nobody understands, an old piece of tax law with its own small constituency, and a social custom whose origin everyone has forgotten. Which of those should survive, and at what point does the asymmetry stop being prudence and start being inertia? And no formatting please—no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
