A few weeks ago I got cornered at a dinner party into an argument about a mutual friend who's bitter over a promotion. Laura, who's usually the one defending him, kept saying the real problem was his attitude. She closed with that line: "comparison is the thief of joy." In her mouth it did a lot of work: it made the bitterness itself the diagnosis, so the salary gap or the suspect process never had to be examined at all. But I've been trying to work through Rawls on envy, and he seems to pull apart two things that phrase runs together. Resentment, as I understand him, is a moral feeling: you think some treatment of you was unjust, and that's what you're reacting to. Envy is something else, pain that someone else has a good you want, quite apart from whether the good was fairly distributed. Then Rawls has this idea of excusable envy, where envy isn't exactly justified but it's understandable when inequalities run deep enough to injure people's self-respect. That makes the slogan seem too quick, because sometimes the comparison isn't stealing joy; it's more like an alarm that the distribution itself might be wrong. So I'm trying to pin down what "comparison is the thief of joy" could even mean as a claim. I can hear at least three or four reading of it, some of them obviously true in a boring way, maybe one just false, and one doing exactly what Laura wanted. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence might be making, say for each whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim, or false, and then say which reading would actually support Laura's use of it — and whether that reading survives the Rawls-ish worry that envy can be a signal of injustice rather than just a mistake in the mind of the envier?
