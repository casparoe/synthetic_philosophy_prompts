In my reading group on introspective methods in psychology, the same deadlock keeps reappearing. One side says introspection is fundamentally unreliable because the attempt to observe your own mental process alters or interrupts it—something in the spirit of Comte’s objection, though he put the point in terms of an organ dividing itself between observing and being observed. The other side replies that trained introspectors in the Würzburg and Titchenerian traditions produced stable, replicable reports, and that the real problem is that we lack an independent check on those reports. After a year of exchanging examples, neither side has moved. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this disagreement best understood as a verbal dispute about what should count as introspection, a hidden methodological disagreement about what would count as evidence for a report’s reliability, or a substantive disagreement about the nature of mental processes? And given the diagnosis, what kind of evidence or distinction could actually produce movement—or is the persistence of the debate a sign that the question was ill-posed from the start?
