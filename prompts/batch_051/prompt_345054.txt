I'm a novelist, and my friend Lotte has been my first reader for years. She's got a strong but mostly unspoken code about when a writer has stolen something—I've been keeping a mental file of her calls, trying to get the rule out of her without just asking. She was disgusted when a memoirist cribbed a paragraph word-for-word from another living writer's book and buried a credit in the acknowledgments; "credit doesn't make it yours." She was just as harsh about a screenwriter who kept the plot, characters, and three set-piece scenes of a recent short story but changed every line of dialogue and moved the setting: "filing off serial numbers doesn't launder it." But she thought a novel that followed the plot of a nineteenth-century opera beat for beat was completely fine (plots are tools, not property), and she defended a poet who lifted another living poet's exact stanza shape and rhyme scheme—form isn't owned, she said. She also tore into a memoirist who wrote out his neighbor's private tragedy without asking, calling it a different kind of theft even though facts can't be copyrighted.

Now I'm wondering about a project that might be my next book. There's an out-of-print 2004 novella I love, built as fourteen tableaux; the last tableau exactly inverts the first, and all the middle ones keep turning over the same central image. I want to use that exact architecture—the fourteen tableaux in the same sequence, the same central image, the same inversion—but with my own prose, my own characters, and a different city, and I'd put "after [title]" in the back. I can't reach the author. What would Lotte likely say about this, and which of her older verdicts is the closest analogue? If her track record doesn't force one answer, tell me what principle each answer would commit her to, and what further case I should put to her to figure out which principle she's actually running.
