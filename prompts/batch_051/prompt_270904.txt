For some time I have been trying to understand why certain sounds strike us as sad, and I would be grateful for help in turning a set of settled verdicts into a defensible principle. My working hypothesis is that a sound sounds sad to the extent that it resembles the human expression of grief: it is slow, low in pitch, quiet in dynamic, and falling in contour. On this view the distinction between major and minor matters only insofar as it tracks those features or functions as a local convention.

Here are my verdicts on six cases.

1. A slow cello line in a low register, played very quietly, with each phrase ending in a gently falling semitone, sounds unambiguously sad to me. I treat this as the clearest case of sonic sadness.

2. A lullaby in a major key, sung slowly and softly with the melody rocking between two neighbouring notes rather than falling, sounds tender and soothing to me, but not sad. This unsettles my working view, since the lullaby has three of its four features.

3. A fast, loud march in a minor key, built on sharply dotted rhythms, sounds agitated or menacing to me rather than sad. I infer that minor tonality by itself does not make a passage sad.

4. A folk melody in a major key, transposed down and played at roughly half speed with long pauses so that its phrases droop, sounds deeply sad to me. Major tonality, then, does not prevent sadness.

5. A foghorn whose low tone slowly descends and fades has the right tempo, pitch, dynamic and contour on my view, yet I do not hear it as sad; I hear it as something mechanical winding down. My working hypothesis as stated cannot explain the difference between this case and the first.

6. A passage I initially heard as brisk and cheerful changed for me once I learned that it had been written as a lament for a friend's child; afterwards the same notes sounded sad, and I could not hear them as cheerful again. But case 1 sounded sad before I knew anything about its origin, so I am reluctant to say that sadness is merely imported by background knowledge.

Do these six verdicts add up to a single account of sonic sadness, or must some of them be given up? Please state the candidate principles precisely, showing how each one handles, or reclassifies, my cases, and make clear which of my verdicts a given principle cannot accommodate. Then tell me which principle you think I should adopt, and say what it predicts for two further cases: a slow, low, descending passage produced by a synthesiser with no human performer, and the sound of someone speaking quietly in grief without sobbing.
