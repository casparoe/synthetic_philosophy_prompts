I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and a project has been forcing me into territory I would normally avoid: our company makes a mood-logging app, and the product team wants to market a feature that promises to help users eliminate sadness. I find myself uneasy about this, but I cannot say why without first getting clear on when a negative feeling is something to preserve rather than remove. So I wrote down my own verdicts on a set of concrete cases and would like you to help me extract the principle behind them, if one exists. (1) A woman loses her husband of thirty years and grieves for many months, sometimes weeping at unpredictable moments. I think this grief is fitting rather than an error, and that if a safe pill could dissolve it immediately, taking the pill would mean giving up something important even if she functioned fine afterward. (2) I watch a film about a doomed love affair and leave carrying a heavy, sad feeling for the rest of the evening. There was no real loss, yet I think this sadness is part of what made the film worth watching, not a glitch. (3) A colleague receives a code review that exposes rushed, sloppy work; he feels a sharp sting of shame for a day and then reworks the module carefully. I think the sting was good — if the review had left him feeling fine, he might not have fixed anything. (4) A family member has a depressive episode after a minor setback, wildly out of proportion to it: weeks of leaden sadness, self-reproach, and inability to take interest in anything. I think this is an illness and nothing about it is valuable; it should be treated and removed. (5) A man still feels acute guilt, decades later, about a small betrayal that his friend forgave long ago; the guilt is as strong as it was in the first year and serves no purpose now. I think this is excessive and not valuable, though I cannot say exactly why it differs from the widow's grief in case 1. (6) A friend tells me about a professional failure, and I feel a real pang of sadness on his behalf for the length of the conversation. I think this sympathetic sadness is part of caring about him, not a defect I would want removed.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts, stated precisely; what it predicts about a seventh case of your choosing that is not a trivial variant of these; and which of my verdicts, if any, it would force me to give up. I would rather have the conflicts stated honestly than receive a strained principle that pretends to save all six.
