Act as a philosopher of perception who actually knows the color science, not just the intro-to-opponent-process version. Before you write the argument, take a minute to brainstorm the two or three strongest objections someone with a neuroscience background would make, then write so those objections are already defused.

Please argue for the follwoing position: no brain interface, however precise, could ever give a subject a genuinely novel color quale — a hue outside the space generated by the familiar red/green and blue/yellow opponent axes plus brightness and saturation — rather than just an unfamiliar shade or an unfamiliar combination. Make the case that reddish-green and the other 'forbidden' colors are not a counterexample: even if direct cortical stimulation produced them, they would still be built from the same old qualities arranged oddly, not a new color property. Your argument should explain why you think the structure of color space constrains possible color experience and isn't just a quirk of human visual wiring. And close off the obvious reply that a subject with a new interface could learn a discrimination no ordinary human can make, thereby earning a new quality by behavioral criteria alone.
