ok so I'm finally writing up my view on whether conservation and wild animal welfare can actually be squared, and I've realized I might just have a pile of opinions instead of a position. Consistency check before I embarass myself:

(1) I'm an antispeciesist: the suffering of a wild animal is intrinsically bad and matters no less than equivalent suffering in a human or a companion animal.

(2) The right currency for evaluating changes to an ecosystem is aggregate welfare summed across the individual animals whose lives are affected — not species richness, not ecosystem services, not aesthetic value.

(3) Preventing habitat destruction is one of the clearest moral priorities there is. Draining a wetland, clearing a forest, or bottom-trawling a reef is normally a serious wrong, and I don't just mean because of carbon or the humans who live there.

(4) I genuinely don't know whether the average wild animal life is net positive or net negative overall — short painful lives for r-strategists versus whatever the flourishing amounts to — and I think the answer should change how I feel about conservation that increases populations.

(5) If most wild animal lives are net negative, then humans bringing about fewer such lives is a real moral gain and not just a sad side effect of economic development.

(6) All the same, I'd oppose any large-scale program to manage wild animal populations downward — mass culling, contraception, or suppressing predators — as arrogant, unworkable, or both.

My question is whether this hangs together. I can't tell which of these are really pulling against each other, which only look like they conflict from one angle, and which load-bearing claim I'd have to revise. Rank the substantive tensions by severity, say which pairs only apear to conflict and why, and for each real tension tell me what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs me.
