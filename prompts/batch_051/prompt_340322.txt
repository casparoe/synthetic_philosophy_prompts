I've been reading GEB on the side, not for a class or anything, and the part about where meaning is located has been stuck in my head. Hofstadter distinguishes the inner message (the actual payload, like the notes on a record or the words on a page), the outer message (the implicit signal that says this is a message at all, that someone meant it), and the frame message (the background conventions and knowledge needed to decode the outer message). His point, if I'm getting it right, is that meaning isn't stored in any single one of those layers; it only shows up when all three lock together, and asking "where is the meaning?" in the inner message alone is a category mistake.

This reminded me a lot of debugging distributed systems at work. Say a service receives a POST request. The body is just bytes until the Content-Type header tells you to parse it as JSON rather than protobuf or form-encoded data. But the header itself is only useful if both sides share the API contract, which is a whole other layer of conventions. When something breaks, I've seen engineers argue about whether the bug is "in the payload" or "in the contract," like the meaning of the request has a single address. But in practice the same JSON body can mean completely different things depending on the headers, and those can mean nothing if the contract hasn't been synchronized. So the GEB way of seeing it—meaning as a relation across levels rather than a property of one level—feels like it describes real systems better than my usual mental model.

Is that a deep structural parallel, or am I just restating the generic point that interpretation needs context? What would have to be true on the software side for GEB's stronger claim to transfer—that the location of meaning is the interaction itself, not any one layer—and where does the analogy break down because protocol layers are explicit, agreed-upon conventions, whereas GEB's frame messages usually depend on intelligence and sometimes self-reference?
