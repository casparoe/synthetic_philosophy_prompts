"In chapter 25 of The Prince, Machiavelli writes: 'So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her.' (Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 25, trans. W. K. Marriott). Explain how this passage fits into Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù, and evaluate whether the passage commits him to the view that fortune can be mastered by prudent preparation."

The student's answer:

"Machiavelli is pushing back on the view that everything is governed by fortune or God and that human planning is pointless. Fortune for him means the stuff outside a prince's control — political luck, timing, events. Virtù is not the same as Christian moral virtue; it's more like energy, boldness, and political ability. The river image is about how fortune feels unstoppable when it's actually happening, but the real damage comes from not having prepared earlier. The line about valour not having prepared to resist her is the key: fortune only shows its power where virtù didn't do its job first. So in this passage virtù basically comes down to prudence, the foresight to build barriers and defences before the flood arrives. That commits him to thinking fortune can be mastered by prudent preparation, although he admits at the start of the chapter that fortune rules about half of our actions, so maybe 'mastered' is too strong — you can only contain it within the half that's yours to direct."
