My friend and I have been going back and forth about the argument from the poverty of the stimulus, and I've realized we're never going to get anywhere until we settle a prior question: what actually makes a stimulus impoverished? Not the full argument for innate knowledge — just a working definition of the term, the kind of thing that tells us when an input underdetermines what the learner ends up with in the interesting way. I want a definition that gets these cases right: (1) a child picking up a first language from fluent, messy, mostly-positive everyday speech; (2) a kid who hears plenty of past tense forms and still says "goed" for a while; (3) an adult learning French in a classroom with explict corrections and grammar rules; (4) a zebra-finch chick that has to hear an adult's song during an early sensitive period to sing it properly later; (5) a large language model pretrained on an enormous corpus of text that nonetheless fails consistently on certain very rare syntactic patterns; and (6) a human infant learning to walk.

I'm pretty sure (1) is the paradigm case and (3) is not, but the songbird and the LLM cases make me care whether the concept is about the amount of data, the absence of negative evidence, or underdetermination of the output by the input in a more abstract sense. Give me a definition, run it through all six, and be honest about where it gives the wrong answer, no answer, or forces you to defend a verdict you didn't expect.
