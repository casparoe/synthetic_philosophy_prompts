I keep noticing a position — in comment sections, in a couple of friends, and honestly in myself on alternate Tuesdays — that I don't know how to classify. The people I mean are fine saying there are infinitely many natural numbers, so long as that just means "no last one": you can always add one more, and the sequence never gives out. They're fine with 0.333..., with limits, and with "the series 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ... converges to 2." They're even fine with the diagonal argument, but only read as a game: whatever list of reals you bring, here's one you missed — so no list can ever be finished. What they balk at is the next step: "therefore there is one completed set containing every real number, and it's bigger than the completed set of all natural numbers." They say that step quietly adds an object the earlier sentences never licensed.

It isn't finitism, at least not in the way I've seen the word used: they don't think the numbers stop at some huge n, and they'd happily say there are infinitely many primes. So it's not that they want everything finite. The thing they resist is specifically the move from "the process can always be continued" to "the whole thing is already there, all at once, and we can compare sizes."

Does this exact package of commitments have a name? I half-remember a history-of-math kind of distinction between the infinite as a process and the infinite as a finished thing, but I don't know if that view can actually afford to accept the diagonal argument, or wether accepting the diagonal argument forces the completed set on you after all. And where would such a person land on Hilbert's hotel — do they call it a trick question about a hotel that can't exist, or can they keep its conclusions some other way? I'd rather you carve the boundary than hand me a label: what separates this from finitism, constructivism, and whatever else sits nearby, and would one of those views collapse into this if you pushed on it? And say what would change your mind about your classification — what I'd have to catch one of these people saying, or what mathematical fact, before you'd reclassify them, and where the classification is most likely to be wrong.
