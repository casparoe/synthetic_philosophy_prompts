I've been puzzled by a pattern in how different activities do or don't accumulate improvement. Some fields — debugging a program, testing a prediction experimentally, building something that works or collapses — give fast, unambiguous feedback, and they seem to progress: later practitioners can do things earlier ones couldn't. But other activities give feedback that is just as fast and unambiguous, and yet what accumulates is mainly individual skill rather than a body of knowledge that gets handed on. Think of a demanding game with immediate, precise feedback on every move and an extensive shared record of winning strategies; even then, players don't advance the way an engineering discipline advances, and each new player starts near the bottom, rebuilding private judgment almost from scratch. So clear feedback cannot be the whole story. What separates feedback that produces cumulative, transmissible progress from feedback that only produces competent practitioners? Does this distinction have an established name, and where does it leave the thought that verifiability is what makes a field advance? I'd like a committed answer — take a definite position on where the line is, name the closest existing concept only if it genuinely fits, and mark the boundary cases rather than listing options.
