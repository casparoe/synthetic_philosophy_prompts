Since I joined the grants committee of a small family foundation last year, I have been watching effective altruist reasoning shape real allocation decisions in ways my applied ethics seminar never quite captured. Time and again, colleagues argue that a proposal with clean, quantifiable metrics is better by default, that pursuing a high-earning career is an obvious way to do more good, and that institutional and political work is too diffuse to take seriously. I now want my students to confront these three lines of critique, the institutional critique, the critique of earning to give, and the critique of measurability bias, not as a list of familiar objections but as arguments that interact and jointly challenge an entire way of comparing options.

Please write for me one final-exam essay question for an upper-level applied ethics course, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. Students will have three hours for the exam and should spend about 75 minutes on this question, aiming for 900 to 1,100 words. The question should be built around an invented but realistic allocation decision, for example a small donor choosing between a well-evaluated direct intervention and a policy or institution-building intervention whose effects are deferred and diffuse. Students must be required to do three things: reconstruct each of the three critiques as an explicit argument with identifiable premises and a conclusion; explain how the critiques bear on each other in the decision rather than merely concatenating them; and defend a verdict that could not be reached by reciting the slogan that systemic change is important. Please make the crux a distinction students will not have been taught under a settled label, such as the difference between an intervention being assessable and its being choiceworthy, or measurability bias as a feature of a funding portfolio rather than of individual psychology, and ensure the question cannot be answered by listing textbook objections.

The marking scheme should name five or six must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, and should explicitly distinguish an A from a B answer. An A answer should, for instance, treat the three critiques as mutually reinforcing rather than additive, identify the silent premise in the inference from a proposal being measurable to its being best, and notice how earning-to-give reasoning presupposes a story about replaceability and institutional background that the other two critiques call into question. A B answer will reconstruct each critique accurately but keep them separate. Please also note one typical error per must-hit point. The question and model answer should not depend on named authors, specific texts, citations, or outside reading; anything a candidate needs should be supplied inside the question.
