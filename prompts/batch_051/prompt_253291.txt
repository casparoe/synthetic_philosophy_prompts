I've been trying to sort out my views on envy and resentment for a seminar paper, and I'd rather get an audit of my commitments than a lecture, because I genuinely can't tell wether the problem is one claim or the whole package. Here's where I've landed:

(a) Envy is a vice. It's not merely unpleasant; it aims at the wrong thing — at the other person having less, not at me having enough — and a just society shouldn't flatter it by treating it as evidence of anything except a character flaw.
(b) Resentment is different. It's a fitting response to being wronged, and it carries information: if people resent something, that's usually a reason to look for an injustice rather than to lecture them about their attitudes.
(c) I buy the Rawlsian thought that envy becomes excusable when inequalities are so large that they undermine the self-respect of those at the bottom. At that point, calling someone's reaction 'envy' instead of 'resentment' starts to feel like a vocabulary trick.
(d) But I also think principles of justice shouldn't be built on the emotional reactions of the people involved. Envy and resentment are data to be explained, not premises to argue from; a theory has to stand without leaning on how the people at the bottom feel.
(e) So my practical view is: a society can legitimately limit inequalities partly because of what big inequalities do to self-respect, but it should justify those limits in terms of what people are entitled to, not in terms of envy.

Does this set hang together? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most consistency overall.
