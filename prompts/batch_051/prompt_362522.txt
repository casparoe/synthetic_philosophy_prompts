I spent my working life in organic chemistry and only got into philosophy of language and religion after I retired, so this may be a beginners confusion, but its been nagging me. The other week my nephew, who is studying comparative literature, told me I had not really read The Brothers Karamazov because I read it in English. That started me thinking about a wider asymmetry. If a line in an English Karamazov puzzles me, the natural move is to ask what the Russian says, and the Russian is treated as settling the matter; the translation is understood to be a lossy approximation. The same goes for a contract drafted in German but signed in English: if the two diverge, the source language controls. But in religious settings people often seem to invert this. Greek-speaking Jews and early Christians treated the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, as authoritative and even inspired, not as a second-hand substitute for the Hebrew. And KJV-onlyists go much further still: they treat the King James Version as supernaturally preserved, so that when the English conflicts with the Greek or Hebrew manuscripts, some will say the English is the correct reading.

So ordinary practice treats a translation as derivative and the source as normative, while a lot of religious practice treats a particular translation as fully the word of God. Is there any principle that justifies that difference, or is it just an inconsistency people live with for communal reasons? I would like the strongest principle you can find, tested against a nearby case: a community that knowingly keeps using a mistranslation because it is theirs. Then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped.
