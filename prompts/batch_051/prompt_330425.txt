I have come to accept a position about the structure of inquiry, and I want to know what it would take to give it up. The position is this. Certain commitments — that there is an external world, that the past was not created a moment ago, that the hands in front of me are my own — are not best understood as extremely well-supported empirical beliefs, and they are not known in the way ordinary facts are known. They are hinges: they function as rules that make evidence-gathering and doubt possible, while wearing the surface appearance of ordinary descriptive claims. On this view, asking what evidence supports them, or how probable they are, is not just difficult but a category mistake, because they are the fixed points relative to which evidence and probability get their sense.

I can see the danger. The claim that these commitments are not ordinary empirical beliefs is only a short step from the claim that no experience could count against them, and only another short step from a position that has quietly stopped being about the world at all. I want to know how real that danger is, and more generally how exposed the view is. What would count against it? I am not after the standard first-year objections, and I am not after what an unsympathetic critic would say. I want a map of the view's vulnerabilities. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, marking clearly (a) which considerations would show the view is false or collapses into a rival account, (b) which show only that it has been drawn too coarsely or with too sharp a boundary, and (c) which a committed hinge theorist would rightly set aside as misreading the position. Then give an honest verdict on whether the position, as usually defended, has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether being insulated in that way is a defect for a view of this kind or a feature of its subject matter.
