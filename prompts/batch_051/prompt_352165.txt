After a career in the natural sciences, I have lately been reading the philosophy of mathematics, and I keep coming back to Eugene Wigner's 1960 essay "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences." The passage I cannot get past is this one, where Wigner sums up the situation of the working scientist:

"We are in a position similar to that of a man who was provided with a bunch of keys and who, having to open several doors in succession, always hit on the right key on the first or second trial. He became skeptical concerning the uniqueness of the coordination between keys and doors."

I want to use this image more rigorously than Wigner does. The analogy raises at least two burdens for any serious account of the role of mathematics in science: it must explain, or explain away, the repeated fit between mathematical structures and physical phenomena, and it must say whether that fit is unique or whether other formulations would have worked about as well. Before I read further, I would like the full specification of what a theory of mathematical explanation in the natural sciences must do.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher of science and mathematics. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming candidate requirements in the open before you commit to a final list. Then write the desiderata: the phenomena such a theory must explain or explain away; the cases it must get right, saying explicitly whether you class the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas and the hexagonal cell structure of honeycombs as genuine cases of mathematical explanation or as cases to be defused; the distinctions it must not collapse, including mathematical representation versus mathematical explanation, prediction versus explanation, and indispensable mathematics versus convenient notation; and any results from mathematics or physics it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp.

Then turn the list back on Wigner: are the two burdens I drew from the key-and-door analogy both genuine requirements, or does one of them ask for too much? Flag any subset of your requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Finally, briefly score the main kinds of position you know against the list — platonist indispensability views, mapping or structuralist accounts, fictionalism — but keep the weight on the criteria, since the criteria are what I actually want.

Wherever you make a substantive claim, say how confident you are in it, and single out the one point in the list or in your reading of Wigner that you are least sure of and why. Write in American English.
