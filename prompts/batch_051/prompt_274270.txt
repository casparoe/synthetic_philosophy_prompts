I keep going back and forth on whether this is a real structural analogy or just my brain pattern-matching, and I'd like someone to check it.

In moral psychology, the situationist critique says the cross-situational consistency of things like honesty or compassion is surprsingly low: behaviour tracks local features of the situation (being in a hurry, a phone-booth dime, an authority figure in a lab coat, a nice smell) more than it tracks a stable underlying trait. Virtue ethics, by contrast, seems to posit exactly the kind of disposition that should produce the right action across relevantly different situations.

This looks to me structurally identical to the distinction in machine learning between a model that has learned invariant features that generalise out-of-distribution, and a model that has fit spurious correlations in the training set and falls apart under distribution shift. The situationist subject would be like a model whose "honesty" behaviour is keyed to nuisance variables, so the moment you change the background it does something different; the virtuous person would be like a model that tracks whatever is genuinely invariant across situations. Even the situationist idea that we have only "local" virtues with narrow eliciting conditions sounds like a classifier that only works on one distribution.

Am I onto something, or is this just an analogy that flatters both sides? If it's real, what exactly would a situationist need to show to demonstrate that moral behaviour is fitting spurious correlations rather than just being a noisier version of the right thing? And where does the analogy break—maybe because ethics isn't trying to predict behaviour, or because the "target function" a virtue is supposed to track isn't fixed the way a benchmark label is? If the parallel is partly real, what should transfer from ML to the debate, and what shouldn't?
