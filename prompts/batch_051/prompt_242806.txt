Work has got me thinking about this. Our team is being forced to make the model's outputs "legible" before it ships, and after two weeks of meetings I'm convinced nobody actually knows what legible means. I want a working definition of what makes an AI output understandable — which is what I think everyone's reaching for when they say "legible," though they also seem to mean readable, verifiable, and trustworthy all at once. Not the internals of the model, just the thing a user actually sees. I've got a list of cases I need the definition to sort:

- A correct answer with no explanation. (Not understandable, clearly — or at least it better not count.)
- A plain-English explanation that says "the model flagged this transaction because the account is two days old and the amount is forty times its median, and here's the most similar past transaction" — and you can check both claims. That one seems clearly understandable.
- A fluent step-by-step explanation that a seperate model wrote after the fact, that doesn't track what the original model actually did, but is coherent and plausible. I keep calling this legibility theater but I can't say exactly what's missing.
- A printout of a linear regression with 200 engineered features and coefficients. You can read every number, but I couldn't tell you what the model "thinks" about anything a person cares about. Is this understandable in a thin sense only?
- A 40,000-node decision tree where each branch is a rule a person can state, but the whole thing is a book. Every part is understandable; is the whole?
- A model that says "73% chance this is a fracture" and nothing else. It's calibrated, so it tells you exactly what it believes and how strongly. Is that an understandable output, or just a readable one?
- A chess engine that gives a move plus the ten forced lines that follow. You can verify each line by hand, but nobody can hold the whole tree in their head. Does being checkable make it understandable?
- A doctor's diagnosis with the only reason being "I've seen a lot of these." I'd call that understandable to a patient, but it's barely an explanation.

I suspect "understandable" is doing at least two jobs — one about whether you can verify or reconstruct the reasoning, and one about whether the output gives you something you can trust and act on. Propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, say which of my verdicts your least sure about, and tell me where the definition has to be revised (or where my verdict should move instead).
