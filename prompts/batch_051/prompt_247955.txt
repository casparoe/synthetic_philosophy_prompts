I have been teaching a unit on divine command theory, and I keep coming back to a passage in Plato’s *Euthyphro* in which Socrates asks, in effect, whether the holy is loved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is loved. With a single God, the dilemma sharpens: is something morally right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is morally right? On one side is the theory: moral facts are grounded in God’s commands, or more broadly in God’s will. That theory has real virtues—it explains moral obligations as commands backed by an authority, it unifies the moral law under a single source, and it makes sense of the bindingness of morality in a way that naturalistic accounts often struggle to do. On the other side is a firm intuition I cannot shake: if God commanded something I regard as an atrocity—say, the torture of a child for amusement—it would not become good just because He commanded it. But when I try to accept the alternative, that God commands it because it is antecedently good, I seem to give up the explanatory priority that made the theory attractive; God becomes, at best, a spokesman for a standard independent of Him.

I am not asking for a history of the debate. I want a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—for me to accept that God’s command could make anything good, even the seemingly unthinkable? What would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned—for divine command theory to avoid the second horn without becoming vacuous? And is there a reading of “grounded” on which both survive: for example, by saying that God’s nature, rather than God’s will, is the standard, so that the commands are not arbitrary and yet morality still depends on God in some substantive way? In answering, present both sides at their strongest before trying to dissolve them.
