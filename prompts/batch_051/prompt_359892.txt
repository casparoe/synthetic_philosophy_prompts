Okay here's one. Imagine Aristotle somehow gets a translated briefing on the situationist literature in moral psychology: the Milgram obedience experiments, the phone-booth dime study where people who found a coin were much more likely to help someone pick up dropped papers, and the good-samaritan study where seminarians on their way to give a talk mostly walked past someone slumped and apparently needing help. The situationist take on these studies—think Doris, Harman—is that small situational factors, not stable character traits, are doing the explanatory work, so virtue ethics rests on a bad empirical psychology.

I want you to predict, in the weeds, how Aristotle would actually respond—not just "he'd point to phronesis." Would he take the studies as evidence against his view at all, or would he say they're measuring behavior while leaving out the agent's reasoned choice, and his ethics was never meant to describe how most people happen to act? Could he simply fall back on the claim that virtue is rare and the subjects mostly weren't virtuous, and would that actually explain the results? Would he consider retreating to local traits (brave in battle but not in the assembly), or would that conflict with his commitment to something like the unity of the virtues? And what would he make of the detail that some of the seminarians in the good-samaritan experiment were on their way to talk about the Good Samaritan? Be specific about where his ideas about habituation through pleasure and pain, and the doctrine of the mean, would gain traction—and where you think the situationist critique would still genuinely bite.

Write this in American English, and after your reconstruction add a short note saying how confident you are in it overall and singling out the one part you're least sure of.
