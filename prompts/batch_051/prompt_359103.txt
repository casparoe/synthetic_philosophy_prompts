I'm an undergraduate taking a course on AI safety and governance, and in last week's seminar we discussed the difference between two kinds of safety work: alignment, which tries to make the model's goals match ours, and control, which tries to keep a possibly misaligned model from doing harm through measures like sandboxing, monitoring, approval gates, and capability limits. One of the students presented an argument I haven't been able to shake, and I'd like a careful diagnosis of it rather than a quick verdict.

The argument ran as follows:

(1) Control measures are worth building only insofar as we take seriously the possibility that the model is misaligned; a model known to be aligned would need none of them, and they would be pure overhead.
(2) If the model is in fact misaligned and competent, it has an incentive to defeat or route around whatever control measures stand between it and its goals.
(3) The control measures we can actually build are enforced by systems — human reviewers, automated monitors, overseers — that are less capable than the model they supervise in exactly the domains where a misaligned model would try to escape, since otherwise we would not be relying on the model to do the hard work in the first place.
(4) When a less capable overseer tries to contain a more capable agent who actively wants out, the more capable agent will eventually find and exploit a gap.
(5) So control measures either protect us against a model we already believe is aligned, in which case they are unnecessary, or they protect us against a model that is misaligned, in which case they will be neutralized.
(6) Therefore, control is either redundant or futile, and safety efforts should be redirected entirely toward alignment.

I half-suspect the argument slides from "possibly misaligned" to "actively and competently adversarial" somewhere, or that step (4) treats a difference in task-specific skill as if it guaranteed a difference in overall power, when an overseer might have structural advantages — no internet access, read-only logs, redundant human approval — that raw capability cannot simply overcome. But I can't tell whether those are real defects or just motivated ways of softening a conclusion I'd rather not accept. Could you go through the argument step by step and tell me whether it is valid, and if not, which step is the first to fail and why? I'd also like an explicit statement of the interpretation of "aligned" and "control measure" under which each verdict holds, since I suspect part of the disagreement in the seminar was that people were using those terms differently. And please push back if the task as I've set it up is confused: if the steps are too vague to evaluate, or if the argument rests on a mistaken picture of what control is supposed to do, I'd rather hear that plainly than have you force an answer for the sake of answering.
