After a long argument with a friend about what heritability estimates do and do not entitle us to conclude, I came away holding a deliberately deflationary position. I want you to map what would count against the position as I state it, not against a looser version of it.

Here is the position. A heritability coefficient — whether from a classical twin design, a SNP-based estimate, or another variance-partitioning method — is a local, population-level statistic. It records, for a particular population in a particular range of environments at a particular time, how much phenotypic variance is statistically associated with genetic variation as measured in that design. From this, almost none of the inferences people habitually draw from such numbers follows. It does not say what fraction of a given individual's trait is "genetic." It does not by itself tell us how much an environmental change, intervention, or policy could move the trait. It does not license an inference from within-group heritability to between-group causation. And it does not pick out a stable property of the trait, since the coefficient can change when environments, measurement, assortative mating, or the genetic composition of the population changes. I further hold that gene–environment correlation and gene–environment interaction make the very idea of a single correct additive partition of variance more fragile than textbook treatments suggest, and that within-family designs matter more than headline between-family estimates if one wants evidence about what a particular environment or intervention would actually do.

My friend resisted this. He was inclined to treat high heritability for a trait such as height or general cognitive ability as a reason to expect little from environmental manipulation, and to treat within-population heritability as pressure toward partly genetic explanations of differences between groups. I left the argument persuaded that those moves are the very errors the deflationary view is meant to block.

Please tell me what would genuinely count against this deflationary position, and sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be. Which empirical results, formal considerations, or arguments would be fatal to it, which would force only a qualification, and which would be no more than awkward? Be careful to distinguish evidence that would count against my position from evidence that would merely embarrass a sloppier version of it. Say what a committed deflationist would themselves concede counts against them, not only what critics would say. Assess honestly whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind; if some component risks nothing at all, identify it and say whether that matters. Finally, tell me which argument or empirical result would most plausibly change your assessment — or ought to change mine — and where your reasoning about this is most likely to fail.
