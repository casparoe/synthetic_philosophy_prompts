I've been kicking around a principle I call the Bonsai Principle and I want an honest take on whether it's a real idea or just a way to flatter my own bad decisions. The claim: a sunk cost is only truly 'sunk' if it left your preferences untouched. If the money already spent changed what you actually care about—say, six years of violin lessons that turned you into someone who now genuinely wants to practice—then the cost wasn't a pure loss, because the thing it bought, a changed self, is still around. Treating it as a loss would be like calling a bonsai tree you've been pruning a waste of effort every time you water it. So the principle says: ignore a past expense iff it didn't move your preferences; but if it did, you have to evaluate continuing against the preferences you have now, not the ones you had before you paid.

Concrete example: I have an expensive road bike I barely use. I've talked myself into thinking the purchase made me 'a cyclist,' and that's why I keep riding it. But I honestly can't tell if the preference is real or whether I'm just rationalizing a two-thousand-dollar ouch. And that's the circularity I keep hitting: the principle tells me to check whether the cost changed me, but the only person available to answer is the me who already paid. Thats the part that feels suspicious.

So: is the principle actually coherent? Has someone already worked out basically this idea under a better name (I'm not after a reading list, just whether it's a known move)? And where does it break—what case would make it give clearly dumb advice?
