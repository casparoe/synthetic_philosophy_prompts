My friend and I were up way too late last night arguing about Dennett's three stances—the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance—and whether what they pick out are real patterns in the world or just prediction tricks. He kept insisting the design stance is physics with the details skipped and the intentional stance is where the actual explanatory action is, while I said he was being way to quick. The argument died in a pile of thermostats and chess computers, and I woke up wanting a harder case.

So take the decision to launch the Space Shuttle Challenger on January 28, 1986, and analyze it through the three stances. I already know the story—the unusually cold night, the O-ring concerns, the engineers recommending against launch, the managers deciding to launch anyway—so dont waste space retelling it. What I want is a careful mapping. What would an ideally informed physical stance have predicted about those O-rings, and where does it stop being tractable? What does the design stance genuinely add here—knowing what the shuttle and the O-rings were for, what their normal operating assumptions were—and how much of that is just skipped physics? And what does the intentional stance explain about why the launch went ahead anyway: whose beliefs, goals, and risk judgments did the causal work, and is attributing them just a placeholder for neural detail we happen not to have?

Then turn it back on the theory. Where does each stance strain or go silent in this case? Does Challenger show that the design stance collapses into the physical stance, that the intentional stance is unavoidable, that the three are layers rather than rivals—or something less tidy? Most of all, I want to know what this episode does to the claim that the stances track real patterns and not just useful fictions. Be honest about the parts the framework can't get a grip on. And please write everything in American English.
