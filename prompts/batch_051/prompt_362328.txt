As someone who came to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way people assess self-binding, depending on whether the self doing the binding is singular or collective. An individual who knows he may act against his own considered judgement can arrange to have his hands tied in advance: the problem gambler who signs a self-exclusion order, the smoker who gives his cigarettes to a friend with instructions not to return them however much he pleases, the patient who writes an advance directive refusing certain treatments should he later lose competence. Most people do not regard these arrangements as surrenders of autonomy. They regard them as ways an agent exercises autonomy over a later, more wayward version of himself; he rules himself by borrowing force from outside.

Yet when a political community does something structurally similar — when a majority or a constitutional assembly entrenches a bill of rights, a supermajority rule, an independent central bank, or a fiscal rule precisely so that future majorities cannot easily reverse the arrangement when provoked, frightened, or tempted — many of the same people describe the arrangement as an offence against democracy, a dead hand governing citizens who never consented to it. The admiration flips to suspicion, even though in both cases an earlier, more considered set of intentions is deliberately made harder for a later set of intentions to dislodge.

I want to know whether this asymmetry can be justified, or whether it is a confusion about individuals and collectives that we should give up. Give me the strongest principle you can find that permits the individual case while condemning the collective one — or that convicts both, or vindicates both. Test the principle against nearby cases: a person who takes irreversible vows, a legislature that adopts a procedural rule it cannot suspend by simple majority, a treaty that binds successor parliaments. Then say which of the ordinary judgements ought to be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and why.
