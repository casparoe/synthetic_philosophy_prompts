I'm trying to systematize my views about whether every statement must be true or false, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern in my gut verdicts on the following cases.

For "The number of people in this building right now is even," I have no doubt it is either true or false, even though I don't know which; my ignorance doesn't create any gap. For "The current Emperor of Brazil is left-handed," I don't think it is simply false — it seems neither true nor false, and so does "The current Emperor of Brazil is not left-handed." Yet I would unhesitatingly call "It is not the case that the current Emperor of Brazil is left-handed" true. For "A woman who is exactly 1.65 metres tall is tall," in a context where 1.60 metres is clearly short and 1.70 metres clearly tall, I also want to say neither true nor false; but I still think "Either she is tall or she isn't" is true. And for "The coin I will flip tomorrow will land heads," I want to say it is neither true nor false today, even though by tomorrow night it will obviously be one or the other; again I accept "Either it will land heads or it won't" as true today.

Is there one account of truth-value gaps that delivers all of these, or should I distinguish several different ways a statement can fail to be true or false? If something has to give, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? Please also say what the resulting view predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: "The largest prime number is odd."
