I'm trying to work out what I actually think about summing harms across people, by checking my gut on a bunch of cases. In every case I can help exactly one of two groups of strangers, no one is more at fault, and I have no special obligations to any of them. My verdicts, roughly in order of confidence:

1. One stranger drowning vs five strangers drowning: I save the five. Easy.
2. One stranger dying vs five strangers each losing an arm: I save the one from dying, even though I know that leaves five people worse off.
3. One stranger losing both arms vs twenty strangers each losing a finger: I save the twenty, but it makes me uneasy.
4. One stranger losing an arm vs any number of strangers each getting a three-day migraine: I save the arm, no matter how many migraines there are.
5. One stranger permanently paralyzed from the waist down vs a million strangers each with a stubbed toe: I save the paralyzed one. Also easy.
6. The one I keep flipping on: one stranger going fully blind vs a thousand strangers each losing sight in one eye.

I've been trying to run a rough principle: harms below some severity threshold can be added up across people, harms above it can't be outweighed by any number of harms below it, and harms at the same level can be counted. But verdicts 3 and 4 look like a problem for it, since a finger and an arm are different yet I aggregated fingers, while migraines and an arm are different and I didn't. I also keep half-remembering something like a contractualist test: the principle has to be justifiable to the person who would bear the biggest complaint, and maybe numbers count only after you've addressed the worst-off. But I can't make that fit verdict 3 either.

Is there a better principle that captures my verdicts, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and what does it say about the blindness case?
