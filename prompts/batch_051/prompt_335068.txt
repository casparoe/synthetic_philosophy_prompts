I'm from a small town that's been losing people since before I was born, and theres this thing about leaving that I cant get to line up. Every spring our high school makes a huge deal out of the seniors going off to college, especially the smart ones: "getting out," "making something of yourself," "dont let this town hold you back." Most of them never move back, and nobody treats that as a loss — if anything, not moving back is the whole point. But a few years ago the only big factory in the county packed up and moved to another state where wages were lower, and people still talk about it like the owners personally robbed everyone. The company basically said the same thing the graduates say: we have to go where the opportunity is, we cant stay just out of loyalty. Even my economics teacher says workers should be mobile and go where their skills are worth the most, and then in the next breath says the factory had no right to leave. So we're cheering individual people for leaving town for a better deal, and damning a company for leaving town for a better deal.

Is there actually a principle that makes those two exits different, or is it just that one of them is our neighbor and the other is a corporation? I want the strongest version of the distinction you can build, and I want you to test it against the nearby cases yourself instead of inventing a rule that only fits exactly these two. Then commit: either the asymmetry is justified and you can tell me the actual rule we're applying, or it isnt, and you should say whether that means we should stop treating the graduates as heroes or stop treating the company as villains. Dont give me a list of positions and call it a day. Take one.
