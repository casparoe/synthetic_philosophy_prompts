I'm a PhD student in philosophy of perception, and I'm torn about cognitive penetrability. I don't want to be told which side wins; I want to know which should yield — the theory, the intuition, or neither — and what would justify that.

On one side, the case for cognitive impenetrability impresses me: perception is fast, modular, and informationally encapsulated, and how things look or sound is not directly altered by what I believe, want, or expect. Illusions persist under full knowledge — the lines in the Müller-Lyer illusion look unequal though I know they are equal — and wishful seeing, if it worked, would make vision less stable and less useful than it is. The picture has real systematic virtues: a clean division of labor between encapsulated perception and revisable cognition, with top-down states affecting perception only indirectly, through attention, stimulus selection, or downstream judgment, memory, and report.

On the other side, I have a firm intuition that some conceptual learning changes the experience itself. After a year of learning bird calls, the morning soundscape is no longer an undifferentiated chorus; distinct calls stand out in a way that seems like an altered auditory experience, not a shifted label after the fact. A radiologist looking at a chest film seems to see the nodule pop; that difference seems visual, not merely a difference in what the two of us would say. The standard defensive moves — that training only redirects attention to features already in the signal, or that it changes recognition, criterion, memory, or post-perceptual judgment while leaving "perception proper" untouched — feel suspiciously convenient, yet I can't tell whether that reaction is warranted.
