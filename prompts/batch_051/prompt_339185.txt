Ever since my AP European History teacher brought up Whig history — the tendency to narrate the past as if it was inevitably sliding toward the present and to grade every old idea by how close it got to what we now believe — I've caught myself doing it all the time, and honestly I've made it into a policy. When I hit an old theory or institution that got replaced (phlogiston, the miasma theory of disease, the four humors, mercantilism, even some early socialist plans), I treat the fact that it lost as pretty good evidence that it was worse, and I don't make myself reconstruct why it felt reasonable to smart people at the time. My friend argues its exactly the Whig fallacy: a view can win or lose for reasons that have nothing to do with being right — power, fashion, economics, accident, or the wrong questions getting asked. She says I should judge each view on its own terms instead of using "what survived" as a filter. But I'm a student; I can't reargue every dead theory from scratch, and the survived-to-the-present heuristic usually does point me toward the ideas worth knowing. Is my policy defensible, or is it just bias with a time-management excuse? More specifically: is there a fixed-up version of the rule that keeps the efficiency without the Whiggishness, and how would I tell from the inside when I'm applying it badly?
