I am putting together a reading group on imprecise probabilities — the view that an ideally rational agent's doxastic state can be represented not by a single subjective probability function but by a set of them, with the set itself doing normative work — and I want to fix the target before we argue over particular systems. The group is mixed: the economists keep pressing the line that any set of probabilities can be redescribed as a precise distribution over possible first-order distributions plus higher-order uncertainty, while the philosophers reply that the redescription changes the normative commitments. I am not asking you to adjudicate that dispute directly. I am asking you to write the desiderata that a theory of rational imprecise credence must satisfy in order to count as a genuine alternative to precise Bayesianism rather than a notational variant of it, a merely coarse-grained precisification, or a license for arbitrary indecision. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say explicitly which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the proprietary commitments of one camp.

At minimum, sort out the following candidate demands. The theory should recover ordinary Bayesian conditioning as the special case in which the agent's evidence is probabilistically unambiguous. It should not expose the agent to a sure loss, but it also should not be reconstructable, without loss, as a precise Bayes model over a richer state space that includes uncertainty about one's own probabilities. It should make sense of ambiguity-sensitive choice in the Ellsberg style — roughly, the pattern of preferring known-proportion gambles to unknown-proportion gambles for reasons that are not ordinary risk aversion — either by vindicating the pattern or by explaining why it is irrational without making the whole framework pointless. It should state how the set of probabilities updates on new evidence, and it should face the phenomenon usually called dilation, in which conditioning on an event appears to expand the range of probabilities assigned to a payoff-relevant event; it may embrace dilation or avoid it, but not ignore it. It should be paired with a workable rule for choosing among acts, and should say whether ambiguity aversion is rationally required, permitted, or a mistake. It should also take a stand on something like the reflection principle rather than silently violating it, and it should be axiomatizable enough that accepting it is not a bare stipulation. I am especially interested in whether some subset of these is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect there is trouble wherever the updating rule and reflection meet, and also wherever dilation collides with the intuition that receiving costless information cannot rationally make an agent less confident in a decision-relevant way, but I want a considered view. Flag every pair you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single requirement would eliminate the largest share of the positions currently on the table. A brief scorecard of the main families of views against the list is welcome, but the criteria and the cases behind them are what I most need before the group meets.
