I work on an LLM product team, and there is a pattern in my industry that I keep getting stuck on. We call confidently false model outputs "hallucinations" rather than "lies" or "errors." We ship chat interfaces that produce a single fluent, authoritative-sounding answer with no confidence display, while our model cards and API docs warn that outputs may be inaccurate and should be independently checked. The training objective — next-token prediction plus tuning on human preferences — tracks truth only indirectly, if at all. These practices seem to quietly commit us to a particular view of what a language model is and whether it can assert, believe, lie, or bullshit, and I don't have the vocabulary to make that commitment explicit.

Write your response in the following titled sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. The practice and the puzzle (about 150 words). Restate the specific practices — the naming convention, the product-surface choices, the documentation caveats, and the training objective — and say exactly where they appear to conflict.

2. The presupposed view of assertion and agency (about 400 words). Reconstruct the picture of mind, assertion, and truth that these practices presuppose. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say which rival views it quietly rules out. Consider, for example, whether "hallucination" presupposes a perception-like glitch inside a machine that is otherwise trying to report what it knows, and whether treating outputs as things that can be disclaimed rather than asserted presupposes that the model is not a speaker with intentions.

3. Lying, bullshit, or error (about 400 words). Work through the three-way distinction often associated with Harry Frankfurt, adapted as follows: lying is deliberately saying false things to deceive; bullshit is speaking with indifference to the truth of what one says; error is aiming at truth and missing. Where do LLM outputs best fall given the training objective and the interface design? Defend a specific classification, or explain precisely why the system, the output, and the deploying company must be classified differently.

4. Engineering consequences of replacing the picture (about 300 words). Suppose the field gave up the view reconstructed in section 2 and instead treated these systems as bullshitters or as error-prone truth-tellers. What would have to change concretely in product design, documentation, training objectives, and evaluation metrics? Be specific enough that I could take the list to a product meeting.

5. Verdict (about 200 words). Is the industry's current combination of naming, interface, and disclaimers coherent? Which picture should practitioners actually commit to, and why?
