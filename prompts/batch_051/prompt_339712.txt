I've been running a semester-long reading group on accuracy-first epistemology, and we've been stuck for three weeks on the same question: which scoring rule we're supposed to use. The whole program, as we've been reading it, says you justify epistemic norms by treating accuracy, understood as closeness to the truth, as the thing that rational credences should promote, and you make "closeness to the truth" precise with a scoring rule over credences. That's all fine until you notice the choice of rule is doing real work. The Brier score and the log score are both strictly proper, so neither gives you an incentive to report anything other than your actual credence, but they're not interchangeable. With three propositions you can get outright reversals: when the first of three propositions is true and the other two are false, the credence function (0.9, 0.1, 0.1) beats (0.95, 0.12, 0.12) on Brier loss (0.030 vs 0.0313), but the second beats the first on log score (-0.307 vs -0.316). And once the rules disagree about which credence function is more accurate, they seem to disagree about which further epistemic moves are licensed in some of the arguments we've been reading.

Here's the impasse. A third of the room thinks there's a fact of the matter about which scoring rule is the real measure of accuracy, and that we just haven't found the decisive axiom yet; they keep proposing extra constraints as if the right rule is out there waiting to be characterized. Another camp thinks that's backwards: 'accuracy' doesn't have enough content to pin down a single rule, so picking Brier over log is partly a choice about how to trade one big error against many small ones, an epistemic risk attitude rather than a discovery about accuracy itself. And a couple of people think the whole detour shows scoring-rule arguments are a bad idealization, and that epistemic norms should be justified by coherence or consistency instead. My own hypothesis is that this is a genuine underdetermination problem, not a verbal dispute, and that each side is smuggling in an extra axiom it finds obvious but can't argue for. But I can also see the case for calling it a disagreement about what 'accuracy' means, or about whether epistemic rationality is consequentialist at all. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the debate, not a survey of the literature, and a concrete idea of what would count as progress: a uniqueness theorem for some plausible axioms, a no-go theorem, a sharper distinction that dissolves the fight, or an argument that we should stop expecting a single rule to do the job.
