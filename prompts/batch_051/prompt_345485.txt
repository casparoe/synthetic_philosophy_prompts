I am an undergraduate currently taking a seminar in the philosophy of social science, and next week I am presenting a paper defending the following thesis: Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis should be reconstructed not as a causal explanation of how ascetic Protestantism produced modern capitalism, but as an account of what is often described as elective affinity — a relation of meaningful fit between certain Reformation moral-psychological dispositions and a new way of conducting economic life. In the paper I argue that the familiar debate about whether culture can be a cause is badly posed as long as it treats values as causal variables before asking under what descriptions values enter agents' deliberation and become practical reasons. I claim that Weber's discussion of the idea of a calling and of worldly asceticism is doing rationalizing work: it shows what economic conduct meant to the actors and why it presented itself to them as urgent and obligatory, rather than specifying a mechanism that moved them.

The seminar includes four likely questioners, and I want to be ready for each. One is a sociologist committed to analytical sociology, who thinks any serious social explanation must identify a mechanism and is suspicious of appeals to meaning. One is a historian of early modern Europe, who believes culture-based explanations overstate doctrinal coherence and understate the explanatory weight of prices, demography, and state formation. One is a philosopher of social science who accepts that reasons can be causes but insists that a rationalizing explanation must show how the action follows from the agent's beliefs, desires, and values. The instructor is a Weber specialist who, I suspect, has little patience for readings that take liberties with the text.

Draft my Q&A preparation sheet for the session: the six or seven objections I am actually most likely to get from this audience, each phrased the way the relevant audience member would put it, ordered by how damaging the objection would be to my thesis, with a two-minute reply to each and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it would be the hardest one to answer.
