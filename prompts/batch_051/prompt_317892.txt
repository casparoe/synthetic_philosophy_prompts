For a feature I'm writing about how online platforms manufacture trust between strangers, I've been comparing the feedback systems on a few of the big ones, and I keep noticing that the designs seem to pull in different directions. On Uber, after nearly every trip I rate the driver and the driver rates me, and the whole setup is roughly symmetrical. On Airbnb, host and guest also review each other, but the reviews are double-blind: the site won't show me the host's review until I've written mine (or the window lapses), supposedly so neither side can retaliate. On eBay it's lopsided the other way: buyers can leave sellers negative, neutral or positive feedback, but sellers can no longer leave buyers negative or neutral feedback at all — only positive, which ends up reading less like an evaluation and more like a reciept. All three are described as "reputation systems," yet they operationalise reputation in ways that don't obviously fit together.

I'd like to understand what each design quietly presupposes about what a reputation is. Does it presuppose that reputation is a predictive record of future behaviour, a score held as private property, a reciprocal social gesture, a deterrent that works by fear of being marked down, or something else? Is there a single coherent idea underneath all three, or do they embody genuinely different philosophical commitments? And if a platform really did treat reputation as, say, a purely predictive record rather than a reciprocal social ritual, what would have to change about its rating mechanics? I'm less interested in which system works better in practice than in what each one commits its designers to, and whether those commitments are consistent.
