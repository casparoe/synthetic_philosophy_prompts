In what follows I give six candidate planks in the case for a moratorium on training runs above a high compute threshold. Rank them from the one that, if its factual claims held, would carry the most justificatory weight for the moratorium on its own, to the one that carries the least—or that cuts against the moratorium understood as a compute-based measure. For each item, give one or two sentences explaining its placement. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: give the full ranking first, and only then the reasoning. Do not restate the question or add an introductory sentence before the ranking.

(a) Race dynamics and coordination. Each major lab can assure itself that its own contribution to risk is small, while all know that the sum of contributions may be catastrophic, and competitive pressure pushes against unilateral caution. A pause is best defended not as a permanent halt but as a coordination device for breaking a race to the bottom.

(b) Differential progress and option value. Safety techniques, rigorous evaluation, and interpretability are improving more slowly than capabilities are advancing. A temporary pause preserves the option of crossing the next capability threshold only after defensive knowledge has caught up.

(c) Unrecoverable warning shots. The first decisive evidence that a model class is dangerous could be a catastrophe, or a near miss of a kind that gives no guarantee of a second chance. If warning shots are not reliably survivable, the evidential burden should lie with those who propose to continue scaling.

(d) Offense–defense asymmetry. The marginal frontier training run plausibly increases the ability of bad actors to cause harm more than it increases the ability of defenders to avert harm, especially through dual-use capabilities in biology, cyber, or weapons design. If that asymmetry holds, the expected marginal run is negative until defense catches up.

(e) Compute overhang. Algorithmic efficiency keeps improving, so capabilities that once required a very large training run can later be obtained with a much smaller one. A fixed compute threshold therefore controls the wrong variable and risks creating false assurance.

(f) Enforcement externalities. A moratorium that lacks credible verification and broad participation would shift dangerous work to actors with the weakest safety practices. Its force depends on enforceability, not only on whether a pause would be good under full compliance.
