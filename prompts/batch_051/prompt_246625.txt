For my early modern course we've been going over the two main readings of Hume on causation: the old regularity reading (causation is just constant conjunction) and the New Hume skeptical realist reading (there are real necessary connections or secret powers in objects, but we can't know anything about them). A friend gave me this argument, and it seems to show the New Hume collapses under the copy principle. (1) Every simple idea is a copy of a prior impression. (2) We have no impression of necessary connection in objects; the only impression of necessity we have is the felt determination of the mind when it is carried from cause to effect by custom. (3) So we have no idea of a real necessary connection in objects. (4) If we have no such idea, then saying there are secret powers in objects anyway is empty talk — the words have no content. (5) So the New Hume isn't a real middle position: it either turns into regularity theory, or it's leaning on a notion that, by the copy principal, means nothing. (6) And if that's right, Hume can't have been a New Hume, since he held the copy principle. I find the argument weirdly convincing step by step, but the conclusion seems too strong — it would rule out not just the New Hume, but any claim that there might be things we can't conceive of. I'd most like to keep (2) and I'm not eager to ditch the copy principle. Where does this go wrong, if it does, and what do I pay for rejecting that step?
