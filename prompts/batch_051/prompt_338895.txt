I serve on the standing ethics board of a war college, and we have, over the years, issued decisions on whether a given fighter is morally permitted to use lethal force against opposing combatants — whether, on our view, that fighter retains what is sometimes called the combatant’s privilege. We have treated the moral equality of combatants as our starting point, but we have carved out exceptions, and we are no longer sure which principle our precedents actually embody. Our earlier decisions, stripped of identifying details, are these:

1. A conscript in the army of a state fighting a war that is, on our shared judgment about the justice of the war as a whole, unjust. He knows the war is unjust, but he is not a volunteer; he kills only enemy soldiers and otherwise complies with the laws of armed conflict. We ruled: permitted.

2. A volunteer in the same army and the same war, fully informed that the war is unjust, who also kills only enemy soldiers and complies with the laws of armed conflict. We ruled: not permitted.

3. A volunteer soldier in a just defensive war, who kills only enemy soldiers. We ruled: permitted.

4. A civilian who takes up arms against an invading force in a cause we judge just, without uniform, insignia, or state authorization, and directs force only at invader combatants. We ruled: permitted.

5. A foreign mercenary hired by the unjust side, fully informed of the war’s injustice, who kills only enemy soldiers and follows the laws of armed conflict. We ruled: not permitted.

6. A conscript in the army of the just side, who kills only enemy soldiers. We ruled: permitted.

Now a new case comes to us. A volunteer enlists in what is in fact an unjust war, but he has been systematically and plausibly misled by his government’s propaganda and sincerely, reasonably believes the war to be just. He kills only enemy soldiers and otherwise follows the laws of armed conflict. We must decide whether he is permitted.

We are less interested in the bare outcome than in the reasoning. If we want to be consistent with our own precedents: which candidate rule or rules fit all six earlier decisions, what other competing rules fit them just as well, what does each rule predict about the new case, what principle would we be adopting with each choice, and if our precedents leave us underdetermined, what further case would best settle which rule we are really committed to? Also, please distinguish what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the ethics of war, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
