I teach early modern philosophy and I’m putting together a worksheet on Locke’s Some Thoughts Concerning Education. I want to turn the book’s scattered advice into a kind of precedent-exercise, so I’m having students read the passage where Locke says (I’m paraphrasing for class) that esteem and disgrace are the most powerful incentives to the mind once a child has learned to relish them, and that if you can make children love credit and dread shame you’ve put the true principle into them. Then I give them four of his calls, as I reconstruct them:

1. A child is beaten for forgetting his Latin vocabulary. Locke says no; forgetting is a defect of memory, not a fault of the will, and should be corrected gently.

2. A child is given a sweetmeat as a reward for doing a lesson. Locke says no; material rewards make learning a mercenary transaction, and the reward should come from esteem and reputation.

3. A child refuses to stop throwing stones, and after repeated commands says "I won't" in a spirit of defiance. Locke says beating is now permissable, after gentler means have failed, because the issue is obstinacy and contempt of authority, not the original fault.

4. A child cries and sulks after losing a game. Locke says no beating; this is childish peevishness, not obstinacy, and should be managed by shame or neglect rather than blows.

New case: an eight-year-old, for the first time, tells a falsehood to his tutor: he says he has read the assigned chapter when he hasn't. The tutor has followed Locke's advice about esteem and disgrace, and the child lies because he cannot bear to lose face. How should Locke rule on this act of lying, if he wants to be consistent with himself? Which of the earlier rulings is the closest analog? If his precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would he be adopting with each of the two most plausible resolutions, and which further case would settle it?
