I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on collective decision-making and group agency — eighteen students, one eighty-minute session. Design a classroom game that makes participants feel the force of the discursive dilemma: individually consistent judges, voting issue by issue, can produce a collective set of commitments that no single judge holds, and the two natural repairs give opposite verdicts. The game should divide the class into six three-person panels and give each panel a concrete three-proposition case (for instance, a disciplinary decision in which two factual findings are supposed to determine a penalty) with complete, internally coherent case files for each role, each including a private rationale that makes that role's votes intelligible. Structure the exercise so participants first vote item by item on all three propositions and do their own tallying, with no hint from the facilitator about what to expect, so that each panel discovers for itself that its aggregated answers are jointly unsatisfiable. Then run a second round — with the underlying individual votes held fixed — in which the same panels must resolve the difficulty using either premise-based or conclusion-based aggregation, three panels assigned one rule and three the other, after which the two camps compare the verdicts they reached from the same underlying votes. Include rules, printable materials, a points-based scoring scheme that uses tokens rather than money, and a debrief that maps what happened to the doctrinal paradox, to the difference between aggregating judgments and aggregating preferences, and to why the premise-versus-conclusion choice is substantive rather than merely procedural. The debrief should introduce these labels only afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, participants quietly revising their votes to restore consistency, or one panel spoiling the reveal for the others — and how to handle each failure. Finally, say explicitly which feature of the paradox the game cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply.
