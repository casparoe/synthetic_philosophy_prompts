In my intro philosophy class we've been doing origin essentialism, the idea that someone couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg because then that person would be a different person, not you — like if your parents had conceived a month earlier, that kid couldn't of been you even if they had your name and grew up in your room. I get the intuition, but I'm an identical twin and it kind of breaks the example for me, because me and my brother came from the exact same sperm and the exact same egg and we're obviously two different people. So sharing a sperm and egg isn't enough to be you, which is weird if the principle is supposed to be about "the" origin.

The variant I keep thinking about is this. Take the usual case but don't change the sperm or the egg. Instead change only what happens five days after fertilization: in one world the zygote stays whole and a single baby is born, and in another world that same zygote splits (the standard identical twin scenario) and two babies are born. If I'm the singleton in the first world, which of the twins in the second world, if either, is me? Or did the question stop making sense once the split happened, even though the split-world twins each trace back to the same zygote I traced back to? I want someone to actually work through where "the origin" is supposed to be in the original argument — the sperm/egg pair, the zygote at conception, something later — and then say whether the twinning case just adds an ugly patch (like "the origin is the zygote, unless it divides") or whether it shows the standard sperm-and-egg way of stating origin essentialism was too coarse and needs to be revised. Please don't just say twins are a special exception; the whole point is that the special exception might reveal that the rule was sloppy.
