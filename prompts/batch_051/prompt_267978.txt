People reach for "what if everybody did that?" all the time, but only against some things. Cheat on your taxes, cut in line, water your lawn during a drought, take more than your share from a shared well because you're just one person — the question comes out immediately. But nobody says it to the person who decides not to have children, even though everyone deciding that would mean no more people, or to the smart, healthy thirty-year-old who becomes a glassblower (or a TikTok dance historian, whatever) instead of a nurse or an electrician, even though everyone choosing that way would make the world fall apart. That looks like an inconsistency in how people wield a principle they treat as decisive. So: what is the best principled distinction here, the one that catches the tax cheat and the line cutter but lets the childless person and the glassblower off the hook? Or does the principle actually catch all four and the ordinary judgments are just inconsistent? Build the strongest version you can, stress-test it with at least one extra case you invent, and say whether we should keep the asymmetry as ordinary practice has it, drop it, or reverse it. Then tell me how confident you are in that verdict and which single step or example in your argument you're least sure about.
