I'm writing a paper on why we shouldn't be too relaxed about the idea that priors wash out in Bayesian inference. Here are the limitations of the wash-out story I've already got on my list: (1) the standard convergence theorem that says the posterior becomes approximately normal and the prior washes out only applies under regularity conditions, and breaks down when the parameter is on the boundary or the likelihood isn't smooth enough; (2) if a direction in the parameter space is unidentifiable from the data, the posterior in that direction stays exactly where the prior put it; (3) the wash-out story is usually told within a single model, but uncertainty across models, or across non-nested hypotheses, is much more prior-sensitive and doesn't wash out the same way; (4) in high-dimensional or nonparametric settings the choice of prior can determine whether you get decent convergence at all, so the prior is doing real work rather than disappearing; (5) wash-out is an asymptotic claim, and in realistic finite samples the prior can still dominate, especially when the likelihood is weak.

I'm looking for another limitation of the wash-out story that is genuinely different from these five — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of them. It doesn't need to have a named theorem attached; a clear conceptual point is fine. I half-suspect thats all there is, but I'd like someone to try anyway. Then tell me honestly whether the limitation you came up with is a serious problem for the wash-out view or a bit contrived, and how it relates to the items on my list.
