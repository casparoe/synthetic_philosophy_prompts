I'm a PhD student in internet governance, and I spend one day a week on the trust and safety team of a mid-sized forum-hosting site. We get removal demands from maybe forty countries, but there are only three of us triaging them and none of us are lawyers. Right now we're basically making it up request by request, and when there's no lawyer in the room we default to whichever country's law is loudest. I want something better.

Could you write me a decision procedure we could actually run in under half an hour per request, for deciding whose law should govern a piece of user speech? The tension I keep circling is between a country-of-origin approach (roughly: the law of where the content is uploaded, or where the platform is based) and a country-of-destination or effects-based approach (the law of every place where the content can be read, or where the harm lands). I don't want the procedure to just default to the most restrictive law, since that's a race to the bottom, but I also can't pretend foreign laws never matter, given users can be arrested at home for what they post on our site.

Please set out: a default rule, the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than law. The founders have to sign off before we remove anything that isn't clearly required by law. Be honest about which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as tests. And please keep the answer in British English.

Then run the procedure on these three cases, including at least one where it gives an answer the team will not like:

1. A user in Germany posts a satirical mockery of a foreign head of state; that country sends a removal demand under its criminal-insult laws. We have no office, staff, or assets there, but the user sometimes travels there to visit relatives. The post is legal in Germany and under our own terms.

2. A user in the UK alleges, with documents, that a mining company in Country X is falsifying safety reports about a tailings dam. Country X has a vaguely worded law against false information deemed harmful to the economy, and has sent a formal takedown demand. Our legal advice is that we could be sued there if we don't remove it, but only if we ever want to operate or raise capital there, which the founders haven't ruled out.

3. A user in a country with strict blasphemy laws posts a respectful but critical theological essay that is fully legal where we are based. The user hasn't asked us to take it down, but their country's prosecutor has brought charges for insulting religion in similar cases. A human rights NGO has asked us to keep the essay up and, seperately, to consider geo-blocking that country so that readers there can access it less conspicuously. We genuinely don't know whether geo-blocking would protect readers or just signal that we're helping them evade the law.

Also say what the procedure cannot do, and where we should spend money on a real lawyer instead of relying on it.
