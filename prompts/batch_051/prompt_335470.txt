I sit on my jurisdiction's limitation-period commission, which sets the periods after which a prosecution may no longer be brought. Our settled precedents are: no limitation period for murder; 25 years for attempted murder; 15 years for armed robbery; 10 years for residential burglary; 2 years for simple assault; and 1 year for shoplifting. We must now set a period for a newly classified offense with this typical profile: the offense is a serious assault committed by a stranger, leaving the victim with a broken jaw, and on our internal scale of gravity it ranks above residential burglary and below armed robbery; such cases typically leave no physical or documentary evidence, and the only witnesses are passers-by whose identities are often hard to establish and whose memories, by common agreement, become substantially unreliable within a few years. If our goal is to decide consistently with our precedents, what period should we recommend? Which of our precedents is the closest analog, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine this, what principle would we be adopting under each plausible recommendation? Finally, what further case would most cleanly distinguish the competing principles? I want the period to express which feature of a wrong really explains why the passage of time matters, rather than merely splitting the difference between adjacent numbers.
