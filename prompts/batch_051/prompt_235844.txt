A friend and I got into it over dinner about why some fields seem to make steady, cumulative progress while other fields full of smart, well-funded people just cycle through fads or stay stuck. Her line was that the difference is mostly about feedback: fields like aviation or software advance because you ship a thing and find out quickly and clearly whether it works, whereas fields like macroeconomics or education research get slow, noisy, confounded feedback and so they can't accumulate much. I found myself pushing back, but not from a consistent position—I kept thinking this might be close to tautological, or that it gets cause and effect backwards, since a field may only get tight feedback once it has already isolated the right variables and built tools (randomized trials, wind tunnels, test suites) that manufacture verifiability. And I also worry that fast feedback can reward short-term, non-generalizing tweaks, so raw feedback speed can't be the whole story.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who disagree about this? One should defend something like the feedback-first view: clear, rapid error-correction is the main driver of progress, and the main difference between fields is how much of it they can get. The other should argue that verifiability is mostly downstream of how decomposable or homogeneous the subject matter is, and that a field's feedback loops are themselves an intellectual artifact that has to be built rather than a natural resource. I want genuine back-and-forth: they should respond to each other's examples, grant some points, and push hard on the weak spots, so neither side is a strawman. It'd be nice if they tussle over a specific case, like A/B testing in tech or randomized controlled trials in development economics, as long as the disagreement stays substantive.

One more thing: I'd like the dialogue to seperate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and not present a contested view as the consensus. If reasonable specialists would part ways on some point, the characters should mark that rather than one of them winning on the basis of a disputed premise. Something like 1200–1800 words.
