I'm taking a philosophy of mathematics course and we've been on structuralism and the identity problem — the "is 2 an element of 4?" business. I tried to turn the Benacerraf-style point into a step-by-step argument, but I can't tell whether it goes through or whether I've smuggled something in. Here's what I have:

(1) There are at least two set-theoretic reductions of the natural numbers that both get all the arithmetic right. On the von Neumann reduction, 4 is the set {0,1,2,3}, so 2 is an element of 4. On the Zermelo reduction, 4 is the set {3}, so 2 is not an element of 4.

(2) Whatever numbers really are, the arithmetic facts are non-negotiable: any adequate reconstruction has to make 2+2=4 and all the rest come out correct.

(3) The two reductions agree on every arithmetic fact, but they disagree on a purely set-theoretic fact, namely whether 2 is an element of 4.

(4) If two reconstructions agree on all the facts that constrain what numbers are, and yet disagree about some further statement, then that further statement has no determinate truth about the numbers themselves.

(5) Therefore "2 is an element of 4" is not a real fact about the natural numbers; it's only relative to a coding convention. And if elementhood facts are just relativized away, the natural numbers are not literally sets.

I keep suspecting step (4) is doing to much work, but I can't make the objection precise. Couldn't someone who believes numbers really are sets just dig in and say that exactly one of the two reductions is the correct one, even if arithmetic can't tell us which? Or does that turn the true reduction into something unknowable in a way that's suspicious? Also, even if step (4) is right, does (5) actually follow, or is that a leap from "no determinate answer to the membership question" to "numbers are not sets"? Tell me which step fails, if any, and what the strongest version of the argument would look like.
