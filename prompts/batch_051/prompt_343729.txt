I run a small mediation practice in a city where coalitions keep trying to hammer out agreements between groups that hold genuinely incompatible values—recently a mosque and a gay-straight alliance sharing a community center, and a hunting-rights group and a rewilding nonprofit on a land-use board. I had to mediate a fight between two housing cooperatives over a proposed meat ban in shared kitchens, and it got messy because both sides kept calling the same arrangement a "compromise" but meant very different things by it. Its only recently that I realized most people use "compromise" as a warm word for whatever they can get away with.

Around the same time I was reading John Morley's *On Compromise*, and in the first or second chapter there's a passage where he argues, roughly, that practical life consists in the constant adjustment of claims that will never be reconciled, and that the person who treats every such adjustment as a betrayal mistakes the conditions under which ideals become effective. He says something like: the real choice is not between pure principle and pure corruption, but between different ways of adjusting and dividing. That reframed the whole thing for me. The interesting question isn't whether to compromise, but what makes one compromise different from another.

What I want is a procedure, not a theory. Take the underlying principle from that passage—that compromises between value systems can be genuinely better for both sides, not just a failure of nerve—and turn it into a reusable decision procedure for a mediator. It should help me distinguish a moral trade (each side gives up something they value less to get something they value more, so by their own lights the agreement beats no deal) from a corrupt compromise (one side is caving to power, or both are splitting differences in a way that purchases peace at the price of clarity about what they stand for, without any real gain). Write the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to the parties because the question is values rather than technique. It has to be usable in a two-hour facilitated session by someone who isn't trained in philosophy or economics. Then run it on two worked examples: one where the procedure approves the deal, and one where it tells me to refuse to facilitate because the proposed compromise isn't a trade at all. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
