I'm a novelist doing research for a book set among abolitionists in the late 1850s, and I keep tripping over a line in Frederick Douglass's Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?" (March 26, 1860). He's trying to clear away what he calls the "dust-throwing" of people who treat the government and the Constitution as the same thing, and then he writes:

Thus, for instance, the American Government and the American Constitution are spoken of in a manner which would naturally lead the hearer to believe that one is identical with the other; when the truth is, they are distinct in character as is a ship and a compass. The one may point right and the other steer wrong. A chart is one thing, the course of the vessel is another. The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong. If the Government has been governed by mean, sordid, and wicked passions, it does not follow that the Constitution is mean, sordid, and wicked.

In my novel a character makes a structurally similar move about a church's creed versus its clergy, insisting the creed can be sound even when every officeholder corrupts it, and I keep realizeing I don't actually know how much weight that move can bear. So I'd like someone to lay out the different precise claims "The Constitution may be right, the Government is wrong" could be expressing. What kind of rightness is at stake — textual, moral, functional? Does "the Government" mean the current administration, the elected branches, the officials, or the whole apparatus including courts and police? Is the claim about what the words require, about what officials have historically done, about what the framers intended, or about some deeper character of the thing? For each reading, I'd like a verdict on whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and which readings would actually do the work Douglass needed against the Garrisonian position that voting and holding office were tainted by complicity with slavery. I'm not after the single "correct" interpretation — more a menu with some honest judgments about which versions can carry a real argument and which just sound good.
