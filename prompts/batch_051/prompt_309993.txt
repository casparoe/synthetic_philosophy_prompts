A well-known line in global historiography says you can't write history from everywhere at once: any narrative has to be told from somewhere, so a supposedly universal history ends up silently centered on one place—in practice, usually Europe. Lately I've been wondering whether that argument is still alive now that you can more or less ask a large language model trained on a huge multilingual corpus to produce an account that no single located historian authored, and the model can aggregate sources from a lot of traditions rather than just recycling a Western synthesis. Suppose that part works as advertised. Does the old argument survive this development—does it still show that an everywhere-at-once history is impossible? Or does the standpoint simply move into the training data and the prompt, or does the argument need to be restated? I care about what happens to the argument, not wether LLMs are good historians overall.
