Exam question. Explain Quine's argument in Two Dogmas of Empiricism against the analytic–synthetic distinction. Reconstruct his diagnosis of why the distinction resists precise definition, including the role of synonymy, and his appeal to holism. State exactly what his negative conclusion does and does not establish.

Student answer. Quine targets two dogmas of modern empiricism. The first is the analytic–synthetic distinction: analytic sentences are true solely in virtue of meaning, while synthetic sentences depend on how the world is. The second is reductionism: every meaningful sentence is translatable into a sentence about immediate sense experience.

Quine argues that the first dogma cannot be formulated precisely. Logical truths such as No unmarried man is married are analytic in the narrow sense, but a sentence such as All bachelors are unmarried counts as analytic only in the broader sense, because replacing bachelor with unmarried man turns it into a logical truth. The distinction therefore depends on a working notion of synonymy. Quine examines three ways of explaining synonymy. Definitions cannot ground it, since dictionary definitions merely report prior usage and stipulative definitions merely create a local synonymy. Interchangeability salva veritate also fails: in a purely extensional language, two coextensive predicates such as being a creature with a heart and being a creature with a kidney are interchangeable, so they come out synonymous even though they plainly are not, which shows the criterion is too strong. If we instead allow the adverb necessarily, we can restrict interchangeability to genuinely synonymous predicates, but this is circular because modal notions already presuppose analyticity. Semantic rules can declare sentences analytic in an artificial language, but they leave unexplained what analyticity amounts to in a natural language such as English.

Quine then turns to the second dogma. He rejects reductionism by arguing that experience never confronts a single sentence in isolation; sentences face the test of experience only jointly, as parts of whole theories. From this holism he concludes that a putatively analytic sentence such as All bachelors are unmarried could be refuted directly by experience, and therefore that there are no analytic truths. The analytic–synthetic distinction is thus a distinction without a real difference, and any further use of it in philosophy is illegitimate.
