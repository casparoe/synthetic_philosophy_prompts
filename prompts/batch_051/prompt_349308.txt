My philosophy-of-mind professor has spent the term ruling on a series of cases in a unit on functionalism and multiple realizability. Her verdicts so far: a human with tissue damage, firing C-fibers, and the full pain profile (withdrawal, distress, avoidance learning, and report) is in pain; a human whose C-fibers fire but have been surgically disconnected from the rest of the system, with none of the pain profile and no report, is not in pain; an octopus with an anatomically very different nervous system but the full pain profile is in pain; a silicon robot with no biological tissue whose circuits implement the full pain profile is in pain; and a puppet that screams and recoils from a fixed tape, with no internal state registering damage or adjusting its behavior, is not in pain.

Next week's case is this: a billion volunteers with radio handsets arrange themselves so that their aggregate activity exactly implements the functional organization of a human in pain, with signals playing the roles of tissue damage, aversive state, and memory, and with outputs including withdrawal and a pain report; the aggregate exhibits the full pain profile, but no volunteer feels anything and the realizer is a distributed collection of persons and radios rather than one unified system.

Predict her verdict. Give exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your predicted verdict and the main reason; (2) the principle that best explains her entire record of verdicts; (3) the strongest competing principle and what it would predict about the new case; (4) the closest earlier case and why it does not settle the matter; (5) a further case that would settle the competition between the two principles.
