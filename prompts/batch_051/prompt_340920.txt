I serve on the board of a regional conservation authority that approves or denies permits to alter wetlands, and I need to circulate a decision memo before next week's vote. The case is a landowner's request to drain and grade a 40-acre seasonal wetland — a complex of vernal pools that fill in winter and dry out by late summer — and convert it to row crops. The staff report recommends denial on the usual grounds: the pools provide flood storage, filter agricultural runoff, and support dense populations of native amphibians and invertebrates. Last night a friend and I argued about the case at length, and he believes the staff report has the ethics backwards. The species the pools support are r-selected: they produce enormous numbers of offspring each spring, and almost all die before reaching maturity, from desiccation, predation, disease, or starvation. If those short lives are on balance more suffering than pleasure, he argued, then the habitat value the staff report celebrates is itself the moral problem. Draining the site would cause a one-time wave of deaths among the animals now present, but it would prevent that same mass of suffering from recurring year after year; on a net-welfare accounting, approval would be the more humane outcome. He thinks our enabling statute's directive to serve the public interest is broad enough to include animal welfare, even though this board has always construed it as limited to human interests such as flood protection and water quality. He also stressed that in a functioning wetland the misery is not incidental but structural: the herons, snakes, and invertebrate parasites the site supports exist only because something else is being eaten or consumed.

I am not convinced, and I want the memo to test where his argument fails — or to expose where my resistance is merely habit. But I do not think a routine denial that ignores the welfare argument would be responsible, and I do not want the board to vote on a simple approve-or-deny motion that leaves the question unaddressed.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of three courses — deny the permit, approve it, or approve with conditions — and close with a concrete, actionable bottom line. The reasoning should engage the strongest version of the welfare case, including the distinction between the one-time suffering caused by the conversion and the recurring suffering of future generations that would be prevented; the question whether preventing lives that would otherwise never exist counts as a benefit at all; and the difficulty of letting an unmeasured claim about aggregate animal welfare override the measurable losses in flood control, water quality, and biodiversity. Whichever side loses, the memo should say plainly where its strongest argument goes wrong or why it is outweighed. Please write the memo in American English.
