I keep feeling like the old future-contingents / sea-battle problem and the linear-time vs. branching-time distinction in model checking are somehow the same problem in different clothes, and I can't decide whether the resemblance is deep or just me pattern-matching. In linear temporal logic, a formula like eventually p is evaluated on a single history, so at any state it's either true or false; in branching-time logic you can't say eventually p bare, you have to say whether it holds on all possible future paths or on at least one. That maps, to my eye, onto whether a claim like there will be a sea battle tomorrow already has a truth value, or whether its only true-or-false once a branch gets actualized. If the mapping is real, do any results transfer? LTL and CTL are expressively incomparable — some things can only be stated in one of them — and I wonder whether that tells us anything about what natural-language will-talk is doing, or whether ordinary will is just ambiguous between a universal reading and an actual-history reading. Also, the philosophical side has this thin-red-line problem: evaluating plain future-tensed claims in a branching model seems to require quietly designating one branch as the actual one, and that designation is hard to state in the semantics without baking in a privileged path. Is that the same difficulty as trying to evaluate an LTL formula on a distinguished run of a branching system, or am I importing a problem by assuming that a plain will has to be evaluated at all? I want a verdict on whether this is a genuine identity of structure or a loose analogy — and if it's loose, exactly where the mapping breaks and what the parallel mistakenly predicts.
