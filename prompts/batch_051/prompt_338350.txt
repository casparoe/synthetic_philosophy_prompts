I keep running into a claim about the force of 'what if everyone did that?' in discussions that span Kant's universal-law formula, Hare's universal prescriptivism, and Singer's generalization argument. The claim is that the move is not a disguised estimate of the consequences of widespread compliance. It allegedly does a different job: it restates an agent's private plan as a public rule and lets the agent detect a partiality or special-pleading mismatch between the treatment they are claiming for themselves and the treatment they would prescribe for everyone else. On this view, a person can agree that everyone's doing the act would be disastrous while their own act would be harmless, and still be rationally moved, because the wrongness lies in the exception they are making for themselves rather than in the forecast of aggregate harm.

I want to know whether this claim is true of ordinary moral judgment, and whether it can be true in a way that matters for normative ethics rather than only for moral psychology. Design the best feasible test you can to separate the hypothesis that the universalization step tracks an impartiality/special-pleading judgment from the hypothesis that it is only a noisy proxy for the expected value of the individual act or of universal compliance. Make the design concrete: what vignettes or stimuli you would use, what you would measure, and what pattern of results would count for each reading. Then say exactly what a positive result would show and what it would leave open — in particular, whether it could ever show that universalizability is a source of moral reasons rather than a psychological byproduct. Finally, tell me which result or consideration would most change your assessment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
