A colleague keeps pressing the following argument about the paradox of tragedy on me, and I can see that it must go wrong somewhere, but I can't locate the step. It seems to prove too much: because its engine is a general principle about negative valence and rational preference, it would force us to say of any enjoyed activity with a painful or unpleasant component — the burn of a spicy dish, fatigue in a long run, fear on a roller coaster — that either the component doesn't actually feel bad, or we value it only as a separable cost we must pay. I want a precise diagnosis, not a survey of familiar positions.

(1) Sadness is partly individuated by its felt valence: a token state counts as sadness only if its phenomenology includes negative valence — to be sad is, among other things, to feel bad.

(2) When a tragedy works on us, we are in a token state of sadness; we are genuinely sad about the characters' plights.

(3) We enjoy the sadness itself, not merely some incidental pleasure that happens to accompany it: we seek tragedies out, we describe the sadness as part of what we want from them, and we would not prefer a version of the same production identical except that it left us unmoved.

(4) A rational person can prefer a token state that has negative valence over its absence only if that negative valence is an outweighed cost — a badness accepted for the sake of some further good that is, in principle, separable from the bad state itself.

(5) Therefore, if tragic sadness had negative valence, our enjoyment of it would be enjoyment of an outweighed cost; we would be tolerating the sadness as the price of some further, separable good.

(6) But that is not how tragic enjoyment works. We do not treat the sadness as a price to be paid. We treat it as part of what we value in the experience, and there is no further good, separate from the sadness, that we can identify as what we are ultimately after.

(7) So tragic sadness does not have negative valence.

(8) By (1), then, tragic sadness is not sadness.

(9) But that contradicts (2). So one of the premises, or one of the moves between them, must be rejected.

I want to keep (2), and I want to keep the thought that our preference for tragedy over something merely pleasant is not irrational or confused. So where is the pressure? Walk through the candidate steps, say which one I should reject — or, if the argument is sound, say exactly what bullet I have to bite — explain why the tempting step looked right, and make explicit what rejecting it costs: what a theory of emotion, valence, or rational enjoyment would have to say for the rejection to be defensible. Then tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and in the specific claims within it, and single out the point you are least sure of.
