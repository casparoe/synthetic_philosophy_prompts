Okay, I’m writing up my Hume profile for a workshop and would rather find the cracks before my colleagues do. I’ve defended each of these separately this term, but I havent ever seen them all in one place, so I want a cold consistency audit. I’ll number them so you can point at pairs.

1. I take the New Hume line: Hume did believe in real necessary connections, causal powers, in the objects themselves. His repeated remarks that the powers and forces of nature are hidden from us should be read literally as a claim about what is there, not as metaphor or as mere agnosticism.

2. I also accept the Copy Principle in its usual form: every simple idea is derived from a preceding impression. Since there is no impression of an object-side necessary connection, there is no idea with that content; the phrase would be empty.

3. The two definitions of cause he gives, in the Treatise and the first Enquiry, define the only idea of cause we possess and use in inference, not causation as it is in the objects. Roughly one definition runs in terms of constant conjunction and the other in terms of the mind’s transition. I think it is a mistake to treat either as an analysis of what causation is in the world.

4. When we actually use the expression necessary connection, its literal content is exhausted by the felt determination of the mind to pass from the idea or impression of a cause to that of its effect. Everything else the expression suggests about the world is projection.

5. Accordingly, a causal claim about the world, as something we can intelligibly assert, never has more content about the objects than a suitably strong constant conjunction or counterfactual dependence claim. The necessity ingredient is a fact about us, not an extra feature the claim predicates of the objects.

6. As a matter of interpretive method, I read the Treatise and the first Enquiry as giving one continuous position on causation. I would need a lot of textual pressure to attribute a change of mind between them, and I dont think that pressure is there.

Now, before I put this into a handout or teach it as a coherent package, I want the audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be? Rank the real tensions by severity, tell me the cheapest fix at each point, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If one of the six has to go, I can live with it, but I’d rather know now.
