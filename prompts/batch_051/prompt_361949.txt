I teach philosophy of history, and I keep going back and forth on the standard presentism argument—the historiographical one, not the metaphysics of time. The usual version of it says historians can't escape the standards of their own time: the questions they ask, the facts they single out as worth explaining, and the concepts they use to describe them are all supplied from the present, so even the historian who vows to be neutral is still doing the present's bidding. Now suppose someone tries to get around this by building what I'll call a past-internal history machine. They train a language model only on the surviving text from one earlier period—say, seventeenth-century England—and have it summarize that period's own disputes using only conceptual distinctions that are actually marked in the corpus. No importing later words like 'class,' 'secularization,' or 'race' unless the corpus itself uses them. A present-day historian then checks the output only for whether it hangs together, not for whether it matches current historiography. Suppose the result is a plausible, internally consistent, corpus-grounded account that no present historian would have written, its an account built without any of our later categories. Does the standard presentism argument survive this? I can see three answers: the argument is dead, because the vocabulary and the selection of what matters now come from the past itself; it's just been moved up a level, because we still chose the period, the corpus, and what counts as coherence; or it needs repair, with the inescapable presentism located in the act of asking questions rather than in the descriptive vocabulary. I'm interested in which premise of the usual argument is actually under pressure here, and whether the argument can be restated so that it still says something true.
