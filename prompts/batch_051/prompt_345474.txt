I'm taking a graduate course on optimization and agency, and we spent a week comparing two stories about how capabilities get built: gradient descent on a designed, differentiable objective versus natural selection groping through a space of genomes. I left the seminar inclined toward the view that natural selection is, as an optimizer, strikingly stupid when set next to gradient descent. Its updates carry no gradient information and essentially no credit assignment through the phenotype; variation is sampled almost blindly, the only signal is a noisy scalar fitness that evaluates whole organisms after the fact, and the whole process is myopic and staggeringly wasteful. On this view, the fact that it produced intelligence at all is a low-probability draw from an astronomically large sample, not evidence that blind, gradient-free search is any good.

I want you to tell me, played straight, what would count against that view. What observations, formal results, or developments in biology or evolutionary computation should force me to abandon it or weaken it substantially? Sort the defeaters by decisiveness, from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether it is a live possibility or merely a logical one. I am especially unsure whether the comparison is rigged: gradient descent gets human-designed architectures, curated data, and hand-picked loss functions, while natural selection is judged naked, and I want to know whether the fair comparison cuts the other way. I would also like an honest verdict on whether the view, as I have stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. One of the five should be the overall assessment of whether the view has been insulated from evidence.
