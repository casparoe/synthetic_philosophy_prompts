I've been reading a popular neuroscience book about predictive processing after a friend recommended it as the best account of the brain as a prediction machine, and I came away pretty convinced. But when I tried to summarize it for my roommate he pounced on the part that sounded least plausible to him, and now I can't tell weather the problem is the book or the way I told it.

The book's summary, as best I can reconstruct it, goes like this. The free energy principle says the brain has one and only one goal: minimize surprise. It does this by keeping an internal model of the world, issuing top-down predictions about the sensory input it expects next, and treating any mismatch between prediction and input as prediction error, which it uses to update the model. On this view, perception is a controlled hallucination — mostly the model's best guess, with actual sensory input just reining the guess in so it doesn't drift too far from reality. The same logic then extends to action: because surprise is bad, we move so as to bring the world into line with our predictions and to keep ourselves in familiar, predictable, low-surprise settings. The article also says the free energy in the name is literally the physicist's free energy, the energy available to do work, and that organisms stay alive by spending that energy to keep uncertainty down. From this it draws what it calls the logical conclusion: the principle predicts we should all prefer to lie in a dark, silent room forever, since that is the least surprising environment possible, and our failure to do so is a famous objection the theory has never really answered.

I'm hoping someone can audit this the way I'd need to if I had to teach it next week. Which parts are accurate, which are standard simplifications, and which would a careful defender of the free energy principle flatly reject? Does the move from perception to action in the summary misstate the view, and does the dark-room conclusion follow from anything true in the summary? I'm also foggy on what exactly is supposed to be minimized — surprise, prediction error, uncertainty, free energy — are those just synonyms, and does the theory's answer to the dark-room problem turn on which one we pick? And is the book allowed to slide between predictive processing and the free energy principle as if they're the same thing, or is that itself one of the errors?
