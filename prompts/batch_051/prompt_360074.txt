I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a reported piece about Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles — roughly, the idea that if two things shared every property, they wouldn't be two at all. I've been interviewing philosophers and writing down my own views as I go, and I want a consistency check before I build the piece around them. I'd rather not find out in my editors comments that I've been smuggling in a contradiction.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) The unrestricted version of the principle is trivially true and not interesting: a sphere will differ from b sphere in having the property "being identical to sphere a," but that just names one of the things we were trying to tell apart. So the only version I care about is restricted to pure, qualitative properties — mass, shape, composition, distance relations, the stuff that doesn't name the object.

(2) Black's two-sphere universe seems genuinely possible to me: a world with nothing in it but two exact-duplicate iron spheres, same size, same composition, sitting two miles apart in otherwise empty space. I can't find a contradiction in it.

(3) But the standard "they occupy different regions" reply doesn't do it for me. In a perfectly symmetric void, the regions are only distinguishable by pointing at the spheres themselves; using location to tell the spheres apart feels circular unless I'm a substantivalist about space, and I lean relationist — space is just the web of distance relations among material things, not a container that exists independently.

(4) I'm also drawn to bundle theory: a material object just is its properties, with no substratum, bare particular, or hidden thisness underneath holding them together.

(5) I reject haecceitism. I don't think there's a possible world that differs from the Black-universe only by the two spheres swapping places, and I don't think "being that very sphere" is a real property.

(6) I treat conceivability as a fallible but real guide to possibility. I can hold the two-sphere world in my head in vivid, coherant detail and can't make it stop looking possible, so I take (2) as well-supported, not a mere hunch.

(7) I also think a three-sphere version arranged in an equilateral triangle would be just as possible as the two-sphere version, but a version where one sphere is iron and the other gold is in a different category entirely — that one seems unproblematic precisely because they differ intrinsically. I want my finished view to explain that contrast.

Could you go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which are just compatible? For the real tensions, rank them from most to least severe and say what has to give in each case and what the cheapest fix would be. Then tell me whether there's a single revision that would clean up the most at the least cost. If a pair only looked like a conflict, tell me why it dissolves — I don't want to overcorrect and throw out something I don't need to.
