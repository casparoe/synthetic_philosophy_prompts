I am a novelist, and I am preparing a craft lecture on the intuition pump as miniature fiction. I want to argue that in many famous thought experiments the persuasive work is done not by the argument but by narrative devices, which fix the reader's answer before reasoning has had a chance to begin. To make that claim stick, I have been assembling a taxonomy of those devices, and I would like someone to try to break the taxonomy open.

Here is the list as it stands.

1. Selective concretization. One branch of the choice is written with grain-level specificity — the warm water, the child's face, the weight of the lever — while the other is left abstract, as in an appeal to the greatest number. The vivid branch starts with a larger share of experienced reality and therefore of intuitive authority.

2. Verdict-carrying vocabulary. The act is described in words that already contain the evaluation — sacrifice, murder, defenseless — so the reader is invited to endorse a loaded description rather than to assess a bare event.

3. Causal ellipsis. A long, uncertain, socially embedded chain of consequences is collapsed into a single silent step — press the button, one dies, five survive — while probabilities, side effects, and second-order consequences are edited out of the picture.

4. Fixed agent-matrix. The reader is assigned a role — bystander at the switch, transplant surgeon, pilot — and given a short menu of motions, so that options not on the menu never become available to the imagination and a forced choice comes to feel like the whole of choice.

5. Anchor-then-erode sequencing. An opening case is offered whose answer is so obvious it will not be contested; the case is then varied in small steps, and the original verdict is carried along after the feature that justified it has been quietly removed.

6. Availability engineering. The scenario is built around an image selected for ease of recall or recent priming, and the conclusion inherits its felt force from the image rather than from the reasoning.

7. Single-axis arithmetic. The decision is framed as balancing a single quantity — lives, years of suffering, utility — while every other dimension is silently ignored, so the arithmetic settles the matter before the reader notices what was not counted.

8. Narrative pre-judgment by sequence. Before the moment of choice, the vignette briefly characterizes: someone is made sympathetic or callous in the opening lines, and the later act is already judged by the order in which the story was told.

I suspect this list covers nearly everything; that is exactly why I want to be shown that it does not. Give me one further device of the same kind — a way an intuition pump persuades by its telling rather than by its reasoning — that is not on this list and is not a relabeling, a special case, or a combination of items already here. It should be something a working novelist would recognize as a distinct lever in a narrative. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good: whether it names a genuine mechanism or only puts a new phrase on something I have already collected. Say how it differs from the closest item on the list, and what sort of thought experiment it would tend to distort most.
