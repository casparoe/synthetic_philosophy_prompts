Prepping for a philosophy of language seminar and want a consistency check before I commit to anything. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(i) Words have meanings: 'book' contributes something stable everywhere it shows up, which is why novel uses are intelligible immediately.
(ii) A lot of words are deeply polysemous. The object, the text, the reservation, the accounting record — these aren't one meaning that context narrows, they're different senses of 'book,' and the difference belongs to the language, not just to the occasion of use.
(iii) There's no fact of the matter about how many senses a word has or where one sense ends and the next starts. Dictionaries carve the same word up differently, and a new use shades into a new sense so gradually you can't catch the moment it appears.
(iv) Meaning is nothing over and above use — regularities in how speakers deploy a word are what make it mean what it does.
(v) A one-off novel use doesn't mean the word already had that sense; it gets a new sense only once it catches on.

I can't tell whether this is a coherent package or whether I'm quietly holding both ends of an argument — distinct senses, no principled way to count them, and a use-first account of meaning — and just hoping nobody pulls on the loose end. Which commitments actually conflict, which only seem to, and what's the least costly fix? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than saying they're all equally bad.
