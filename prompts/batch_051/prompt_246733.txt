I have long defended and taught the at-at view of Zeno's paradoxes of motion: a body's moving at an instant is nothing over and above its occupying different places at different times, so the dichotomy and the Achilles are dissolved by the convergence of the relevant infinite series, and the arrow never requires an intrinsic state of motion at an instant. I would like a careful map of what would count against this position. Which physical facts, mathematical results, or metaphysical commitments about instants, the continuum, or the possibility of completing an infinite sequence of acts in a finite time would, if true, force a defender of this view to revise or abandon it? Distinguish the defeaters a committed at-at theorist would concede as genuine from those they would dismiss as importing the very notion the view is meant to explicate. Say, too, whether the position still risks anything once its standard qualifications are added, or whether it has been insulated in a way that is itself a weakness. Please rank the defeaters from seriously damaging to merely awkward.
