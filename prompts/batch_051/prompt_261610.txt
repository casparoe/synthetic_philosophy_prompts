I keep coming back to a passage in Descartes' Discourse on Method, part five, where he's explaining why animals can't think. He says "stones and other inanimate bodies" can't combine the way we do in speech, and that even a "magpie or parrot" can say words just like us, but "cannot speak as we do." I don't mean the famous "I think therefore I am" bit—this is the part where he says if there were machines that looked like animals, we'd have no way to tell them from real animals, but if there were machines that looked like people, we could always tell, because they couldn't arrange words to make their thoughts understood. What I'm wondering is: this test seems to rely on something deeper than just "can they talk." If some animal species actually did pass his test, would that show he was wrong about animal minds, or would it just show he was using speech as a proxy for something more general—like being able to put thoughts together in a flexible way? And does his machine idea backfire on him? If a machine could be built that passed his test, he'd have to say the machine is either thinking or somehow faking it, but if thinking is a real thing that a machine can do, then the line between human and machine gets messy in a way his "animals are just bodies" conclusion doesn't seem to allow. So who's actually doing the argumentative work here: the test, or the assumption that animals fail it? I want to know if his position has a gap in it, not just a factually wrong conclusion.
