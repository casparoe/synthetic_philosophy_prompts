I'm taking a seminar on causation in the philosophy of science, and we have been working through Woodward's interventionist account: the idea that causal claims are claims about what would happen under manipulations of the putative cause, where a proper intervention is supposed to be surgical — it changes the cause while cutting it off from its usual causes, and it affects the outcome only through the cause. We have now reached the circularity worry: the notion of an intervention is itself specified in causal terms, so some philosophers charge that the account explains causation only by presupposing it.

I want to test the account against a concrete episode rather than the usual barometer-and-storm or flagpole-and-shadow examples. The case I have in mind is John Snow's work during the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these. Snow mapped the deaths, argued that they clustered among people who drew water from the Broad Street pump, and persuaded the local authorities to remove the pump handle. Mortality in the area then fell, though Snow himself allowed that the epidemic may already have been declining, so the fall is not by itself decisive. Around the same period he also compared households served by two South London water companies, one drawing from a sewage-polluted part of the Thames and one from a cleaner source, and found a much higher cholera death rate among customers of the polluted supply; this comparison is sometimes called the Grand Experiment.

Analyze this episode through Woodward's interventionism. I want a careful mapping rather than a broad analogy. In particular: (1) Does removing the Broad Street pump handle count as an intervention on exposure to contaminated water with respect to cholera, and if it falls short of a textbook intervention, exactly where and how much does that matter? (2) What was Snow, by Woodward's lights, entitled to conclude from the handle removal alone, and what does the Grand Experiment add or fail to add as a quasi-intervention? (3) How should the invariance condition figure here: what generalization was being supported, and in what sense do the two studies bear differently on its invariance? (4) Does the episode sharpen or blunt the circularity worry, given that Snow's manipulation was itself justified partly through causal assumptions about water, sewage, and cholera?

In the Verdict, say whether the episode is on balance a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case Woodward's account only partly anticipates. Throughout, separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people working on this material, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I am not looking for a bibliography or further reading; I want the analysis argued out from the theory.
