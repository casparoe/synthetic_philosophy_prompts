I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size fintech and I don't have any philosophy background, so feel free to correct sloppy thinking. After a nasty outage last quarter our postmortems keep coming out weirdly predictable: once we know the root cause, the timeline reads like the whole thing was inevitable, and somebody always says "anyone looking at that dashboard would of seen it coming." A friend who studied history told me this is the exact problem historians worry about — the narrative fallacy, where after the fact you turn a messy, contingent sequence into a tidy story with one inevitable ending. After a war or a revolution, he says, every side's account does this retroactively. That framing clicked for me.

So our CTO floated a mechanism and I want a sharp expert to pressure-test it, not just to say whether it's good. For every production incident, the review gets split in two. Before the investigator is shown any logs or the timeline, they get only what on-call had at the moment of the page: the alert text, dashboard state up to detection, and the Slack chatter from then. They have 30 minutes to write down their top three hypothesized causes with probabilities, plus what they'd do to test each one. That prediction sheet gets frozen and timestamped. Only then are they given the full timeline and they write the postmortem in the normal way. At the end, every claim of the form "the cause was X and signal Y pointed to it" gets compared against the frozen sheet; if X wasn't in the top three or got less than 20% probability, the claim gets stamped "hindsight" and cannot be used to assign blame, to say someone should have caught it, or to justify new alerts.

Play the role of an expert on hindsight bias, the narrative fallacy, and how organizations construct stories about failure. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming all the ways the different players — the investigator, the on-call engineer, the manager doing performance reviews, the team whose service broke — would game or be squeezed by this. Then walk through what it actually incentivizes with respect to how reports get written, who looks competent, and what alerts get added. Include the second-order effects on how early people escalate sketchy signals. Where does it fail first, and can the failures be fixed without the frozen predictions becoming so mushy they're worthless?
