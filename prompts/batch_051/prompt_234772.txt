You are a philosopher of cognitive science with an experimentalist's sense of what a measurement can and cannot show. I want a careful design argument, not a literature survey. A friend in the effective-altruist community defends the scale argument: insects number on the order of 10^18, so even a modest credence that they are sentient makes expected insect suffering dominate many familiar cause areas. My standing objection is that the modest credence is doing no empirical work: it floats free of any observation that could move it. That should not be true of a claim about brains and behaviour. Design the single strongest test or observational comparison that would raise or lower our credence that an insect (choose the taxon and justify the choice) is sentient — that there is something it is like to be it, with positively or negatively valenced states — rather than merely nociceptive. Specify the manipulation or contrast, the readouts, the result pattern that favours each interpretation, and exactly which part of the scale argument the test would still leave undetermined: valence, moral weight, the number of individuals, or the bridge from individual sentience to aggregate expected suffering. Begin by distinguishing the subclaims so we know what the test is a test of.
