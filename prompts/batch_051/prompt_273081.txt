I've been turning over a conflict between a view I find theoretically compelling and an intuition I can't get rid of, and I want something more than a verdict: I want the terms of the negotiation.

On the theory side, I'm drawn to what I've seen called the friendship model of filial duty, associated with Jane English. The claim, roughly, is that the language of debt is simply the wrong category for what grown children owe their parents. No one can incur a debt for benefits they never solicited and could not refuse; being raised is not an unsecured loan, and parental sacrifices, however large, are gifts rather than credit extended. When the relationship is good, what remains is not repayment but mutual care of the sort friends give one another, grounded in the ongoing relationship rather than in an outstanding balance. The view appeals to me for its systematic virtues: it dissolves in one stroke a great deal of false guilt, it explains why an estranged child of cruel or neglectful parents owes nothing, it captures why healthy families don't keep accounts, and it assimilates a mysterious duty to a well-understood one.

On the intuition side, consider a parent who made real sacrifices — decades of labor, forgone career, savings spent — and an adult child who bears no ill will but has let the relationship go cold. It seems false to me, maybe even indecent, to say that this child owes the parent nothing until and unless a friendship happens to regrow. Filial duties don't feel optional the way friendship duties are: they seem to survive the death of affection, and they seem grounded in those past sacrifices in a way the friendship model treats as almost unintelligible. The theory keeps telling me the intuition is really about love choosing to express itself; the intuition keeps insisting that there is also a claim, not chosen, that outlasts the love.

I don't want to know which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that's dropped — for me to accept that an emotionally distant child of non-abusive, self-sacrificing parents owes them nothing beyond what a current friendship would require. And what would have to be true for the theory to be amended or abandoned — what a residual, non-debt, non-friendship duty to one's parents could consist in, and whether the friendship model could absorb it. Is there a reading of 'owe' on which both survive, or is one of the poles built on a confusion? If the whole contrast between debt and friendship is misframed, say so plainly; I'd rather have the framing dismantled than get a reconciliation that papers over it.
