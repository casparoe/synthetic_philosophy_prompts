Was arguing with a friend last night about the foundation she works at — they've got a new "measurable outcomes" push — and I kept trying to name a thing I have now seen in maybe four different organizations, but I don't trust my vocabularly to pin it down. It is not the usual cheating thing; everyone knows about fudged numbers, gaming the metric, teaching to the test. What I'm after comes later, once the metric is "clean." At the nonprofit it was clients served per quarter; at my old teaching job, graduation rates; at a hospital committee I watched, door-to-balloon time. In each case the number started as one piece of evidence about whether the real goal was being met, and no one ever gamed it. But over a few years it became the language the whole place thinks in. Meetings get organized around it, budgets are justified in terms of it, people stop saying "this project is good because…" and just say "this moves the number."

Here's the weird part: ask anyone privately and they will tell you the number is not the mission, and they mean it. Get them in a planning meeting, though, and the mission is decoration. The metric has not stopped measuring the underlying thing; it has swallowed it. My friend says this is just Goodhart's law in slow motion. I don't think that's right — Goodhart is about the measure stopping being a good measure once it becomes a target, but here the measure keeps working fine; the targetness itself is the problem, almost a kind of organizational aphasia. Is there an established term for this later, quiet stage, as distinct from gaming or Goodhart proper? And if it does not have one, what would you call it, how would you define it, and where are its edges — is it a cognitive slip inside individuals, a dynamic of organizations, or a property of the metric itself?
