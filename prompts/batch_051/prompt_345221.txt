I'm teaching a short module on AI safety for a mixed group of philosophy and computer science undergrads, about 24 students, one 90-minute session. One idea I keep circling back to is scalable oversight via debate: the thought that you might have two AI systems argue in front of a human judge who can't check the underlying facts, and the human is supposed to get at the truth just by seeing who argues better. I find students nod along, but they don't really internalise how weird it is to rely on "sounded more convincing" when the systems might be better rhetoricians than truth-tellers. So I want to run an in-class activity where they actually experience the gap between winning a debate and being right, from the judge's side.

Design me a runnable classroom game for that 24-person, 90-minute setting. The participants should be split into roles and make real choices, and the lesson should hit them before anyone names it. The core takeaway I want them to leave with is that, under human judgement of arguments, a debater can be persuasive without being accurate, and that the judge's inability to check the facts is not a minor inconvenience but the central problem. I'd like the activity to need no real money, use only things I can print or project, and fit in about 50 minutes with time for debrief. Tell me the rules, roles, materials, a payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the underlying idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what part of the debate-as-oversight idea the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
