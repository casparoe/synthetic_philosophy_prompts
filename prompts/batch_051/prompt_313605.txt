An acquaintance who works on the ethics of persuasion keeps a list of settled judgments about influence cases. She says that a salesperson who falsely reports that two other buyers are about to bid is manipulating; that a company that tucks a mandatory arbitration clause into a dense welcome packet after the sales pitch led with a generous warranty is manipulating; that a negotiator who truthfully explains why a deadline really does force an immediate choice and lays out the available tradeoffs is only persuading; that a parent who gives a teenager an honest, detailed argument for why she would regret skipping a family obligation is only persuading; and that an influencer who posts an enthusiastic product review without disclosing that it is sponsored is manipulating. I want to know what she would say about this new case: a grief counselor, near the end of a session with a recently widowed client, tells a true and affecting story about another widow who found comfort in making a large donation to a housing charity, and then, through tears, suggests the client might do the same; the charity is genuine and the counselor has no tie to it and takes no commission. Would she call this manipulation or legitimate persuasion? I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule she seems to be applying, the other rules that would fit her earlier verdicts just as well, which of those rules diverge on this new case, what you think she would actually say and why, and what further case would best tell the competing rules apart.
