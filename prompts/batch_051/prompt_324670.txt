I'm a software engineer, and a running argument on my team has started to feel like philosophy. We build systems that select actions under constraints, and one engineer says it's only legitimate to talk about the reasons our system has for a choice when the reason hooks into the system's own goals; another says there are reasons that simply apply to a situation whether or not the system happens to be built to care about them. I've since learned that philosophers distinguish something like internal reasons (tied to an agent's existing motivations) and external reasons (applying independently of them), but I don't yet have a working definition of what it means to "have a reason" that I can actually apply. I'd rather not have this turn into a debate about AI vocabulary; I want a definition of the ordinary notion, if there is one, that holds up when I have to explain our system's behavior to non-engineers without saying anything false.

Here are the cases I need the definition to handle. (1) A lifelong smoker who genuinely wants to quit and whose doctor has told him smoking is killing him. (2) A chess engine that finds a brilliant but risky sacrifice — does it have a reason to make the move, even though it has no desires at all? (3) A person raised in an insular community who has never been taught to value free inquiry and feels no pull toward leaving — is there a reason for them to leave or to want freedom? (4) A parent who wants a martini this evening but has promised to drive a child to a school event in an hour. (5) A deeply depressed person who cannot bring themselves to want anything — do they have reasons to eat, bathe, and return calls? (6) A nineteenth-century surgeon who knows nothing of germ theory and has no concept of handwashing before a delivery — is there a reason for him to wash his hands? (7) A rock on a hillside that will roll downhill if dislodged. Cases 1, 4, and 7 feel clear to me (yes, yes, no), the chess engine feels like a different, thinner sense of the word, and I keep going back and forth on 3, 5, and 6. If you think the word does two jobs — one internal and one external — give me both definitions and say which cases each one covers, and flag any case where ordinary usage simply refuses to settle. Run your definition through all seven cases, tell me where it gives an answer that surprises you, and then revise it or defend the surprise.

Please answer in American English.
