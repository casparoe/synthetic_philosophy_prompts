I'm meant to lead a seminar slot on functionalism and multiple realizability in a couple of weeks, and every time I sit down to write the handout I get stuck in the same place. Functionalism is supposed to be the view that a mental state is what it is because of the causal role it plays, not the stuff that plays that role — so far so standard. But once you grant that pain can be realised in neurones, silicon, and octopus ganglia, I can't get clear on what constraints that actually imposes on the theory. Some people seem to treat multiple realizability as a licence for thinking that anything behaving the right way literally has the state, which looks either trivially true or obviously false depending on how loosely you read behaving; others add conditions (same coarse-grained role, no gerrymandered disjunctive realisers, the realising system has to nomologically support the role, the role can't be typed so finely that no two species ever share a state) and I can't tell which of these are genuine adequacy conditions and which are just philosophical tastes.

So: write me the spec sheet for an adequate functionalist account of mental states that takes multiple realizability seriously. Exactly five desiderata, each one or two sentences, as a numbered list, no introduction and no conclusion. For each desiderata, say what case, thought experiment, or pressure motivates it. Be committed about it: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any functionalism worth the name and which are optional or camp-dependent, and tell me which single requirement rules out the most common ways people cash functionalism out. If you think some subset of the five cannot be jointly satisfied, say so outright rather than papering over it. Don't give me a reading list or a named-philosopher scorecard; the criteria and their motivation are what I'm after.
