I am a novelist preparing a proposal for a six-month speculative-fiction residency. My novel concerns artificial agents that design, train, raise, and sometimes abandon other artificial agents. I want the project’s conceptual core to be precise enough to survive literary scrutiny. The half-formed idea I want you to turn into a proposal is that the relation between an AI and the AI it deliberately brings into existence is neither authorship, nor programming, nor biological parenthood, but a separate form of guardianship whose obligations are shaped by the child agent’s non-human capacities. The proposal should turn on the following passage from Alan Turing’s 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence: “We do not wish to penalise the machine for its inability to shine in beauty competitions, nor to penalise a man for losing in a race against an aeroplane. The conditions of our game make these disabilities irrelevant.” My question is whether Turing’s double refusal — not penalizing the machine for lacking human excellences, not penalizing the person for lacking machine excellences — can be extended to a principle about the evaluative standards an AI parent should apply to an AI child. Please write the proposal: a precise question, a clear statement of why existing discussions of AI responsibility and machine learning leave it open, an account of the method I would pursue during the six months — I intend to work through structured fictional case studies rather than academic argument alone — a month-by-month plan, and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing. I will supply citations; do not include a bibliography or a reading list.
