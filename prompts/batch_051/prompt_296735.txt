I’ve ended up leading an eight-week discussion group at my church on evolution and the goodness of creation, and I’m trying hard not to let it turn into a survey of everything. I grew up being taught that before the Fall there was no animal death at all; then I fell for palaeontology as a teenager and have spent years with those two convictions sitting awkwardly next to each other. Recently it has got personal again, because my eldest has started asking why there are fossils of sharp-toothed animals in rocks far older than any human, and whether the ichneumon wasp really lays its eggs inside living caterpillars. I have eight evenings with thoughtful laypeople, not specialists, and I want to spend them on the questions where something actually hangs on the answer.

The lines I can see us pursuing include: whether the fossil and evolutionary record really shows non-human suffering and death before any human being existed, and how much confidence the evidence warrants; whether animal pain and death before the Fall even counts as evil, or only becomes a problem if animals are made moral subjects; what the biblical descriptions of creation as good and of death as a consequence of sin actually commit someone to, as opposed to what has been read back into them; whether the Fall can be understood as affecting the whole created order, even backward in time, and whether that is a genuine position or just a dodge; and whether redemption is meant to include animals, and what difference that makes for ecology and animal welfare now.

For someone in my position, which two or three of these (or which other lines I have missed) most deserve the group’s eight weeks, and which are overrated or do not follow at all? Rank them and defend the ranking. Be explicit about the criteria you are using to judge importance—tractability for laypeople, how many other questions depend on the answer, neglectedness in ordinary church discussion, whatever you think best—and say what new information or argument would most change your ranking. I would rather have a defended agenda than a survey of positions. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and use British English.
