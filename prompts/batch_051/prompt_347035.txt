I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop called "Texts, Intentions, and Objective Functions," run jointly by my research group and a law school's legislative studies program. The room will be split between appellate lawyers and judges on one side, and machine learning researchers who work on reward models and AI alignment on the other. The organizers' premise is that statutory interpretation and AI objective specification are one problem at two cadences: an author with purposes fixes a text or an objective, and a less-informed agent later has to act under it in cases neither party fully anticipated.

Please write the remarks, about 1,100–1,300 words. They should give both halves of the room a shared frame: textualism and purposivism as two strategies for acting under an underspecified objective. Treat textualism as optimizing the enacted public meaning and accepting that you will sometimes follow the text past the point where the inferred purpose would point elsewhere; treat purposivism as optimizing an inferred latent purpose and accepting that you will sometimes read in goals that were never enacted. I want the bridge between the two fields to be a matter of mechanism, not mood. The spine of the speech should be one slightly contrarian claim: the debate between textualism and purposivism is less about what a statute means than about which failure mode is easier to audit, litigate, and correct—and the reward-specification literature, from Goodhart's law to specification gaming, should make us suspicious of both pure positions, because an objective's spirit is often not merely unenacted but genuinely underspecified even from its author's point of view. Close by posing the single question the two fields should spend the two days on, stated sharply enough that a judge and a reinforcement learning engineer would each know how to start on it before the first coffee break.

Do not cite cases or papers, and do not settle the debate. Keep the register formal but precise. Avoid flattering either audience: the speech should challenge at least one comfortable assumption held by a confident textualist and at least one held by a confident purposivist. Technical terms are fine only if each one is defined in the space of a sentence.
