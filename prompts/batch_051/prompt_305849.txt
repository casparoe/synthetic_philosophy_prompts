I'm a novelist, and I write about argument and persuasion, which is why I sit as the public trustee on the board of a small foundation that funds public reasoning. The proposal before us this week would pilot an "argumentation league" run as a spectator sport: teams, a regular season, televised finals, Elo-style rankings, and a shot clock. Its distinctive rule is that contestants are not assigned a side. Each team must state its actual view on the question, argue for it, and be scored on how much it improves its own position over three rounds, with points for entertaining the strongest objection, conceding a point that has been answered, and ending with the most defensible version of its view, whether or not it is the view the team began with. Half the board thinks this is a serious way to train honest reasoning under pressure; the other half thinks the scoring will be gamed from the first round, because teams will sandbag—open with weak versions of their views so that the "improvement" judges reward is large—and audiences will learn to watch strategy rather than think.
