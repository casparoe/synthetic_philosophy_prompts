Ok here's an argument I keep chewing on about Dennett and the self as a center of narrative gravity, and I can't tell if its actually valid or where exactly it goes wrong. It goes:

(1) On Dennett's view, the self is a center of narrative gravity: an abstraction we posit to make sense of the ongoing story of a person's life, in roughly the way a center of gravity is an abstraction we posit to make sense of how a body's mass is distributed.
(2) A center of gravity isn't a concrete part of the body; it's a mathematical point, not something you'd find by opening the object up.
(3) Similarly, the narrative self isn't a concrete part of the person; it's not a little homunculus in the brain.
(4) But something that isn't a concrete part of a system can't itself be the thinker, decider, or moral agent for that system; it's just an explanatory posit.
(5) So on Dennett's view, the self can't think, decide, or be morally responsible.
(6) But each of us is a self — that is, each of us just is one of these centers of narrative gravity.
(7) Therefore, none of us can think, decide, or be morally responsible.

The conclusion seems plainly false, so something has to give. Which step fails, and why? Or does the argument actually go through?
