Here is an asymmetry in ordinary moral thought about population size that I want to settle. When we judge how well a group is doing — a city, a family, a country — we tend to reason in terms of averages: a smaller city whose residents are flourishing is the better city, and adding more residents at the same average level of well-being is not usually taken to make the city better off. But when a single member of such a group dies, we do not shrink the loss by the size of the group: the death of one resident in a city of ten million is not treated as one ten-millionth of the loss it would be in a village of ten, and losing a child in a large family is not treated as a smaller loss than losing a child in a small one. In individual cases we seem to sum; in evaluating the group as a whole we seem to average. Is there a single principle that generates both halves of this asymmetry, or is it only a psychological habit that cannot be jointly defended? Give the strongest candidate justification you can find, test it on nearby cases (a group that gains a member rather than losing one; a species losing its last members versus losing one member among many; a community whose members are all made slightly worse off), and state whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
