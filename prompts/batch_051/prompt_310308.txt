In my philosophy seminar on social norms—this term we have been working through the difference between convention, etiquette, and moral obligation, and in particular the question of whether conventions can create duties for people who never agreed to them—two students in my section have been locked in an argument about a visiting student's conduct. They asked me to referee, and I honestly cannot. I would like a move-by-move adjudication.

The case is this. An exchange student spending the full academic year in a U.S. city comes from a country where pedestrians treat red lights as advisory when the street is empty and where tipping in restaurants is not customary. He queues when a line has already formed, saying any reasonable person would. But he regularly crosses against the light when no traffic is coming, and although he now knows that tips function as part of servers' income, he leaves about eight percent rather than the customary fifteen to twenty, saying he would gladly pay higher menu prices but will not adopt a custom he never joined.

(1) Daniel largely defends the visitor. Jaywalking and the size of a tip, he says, are local conventions, and conventions create obligations only for people who have joined them—through explicit agreement, tacit acceptance, or long membership in the community. A visitor is a beneficiary of hospitality, not a party to the local compact. The familiar advice to follow local customs when abroad is prudential guidance for avoiding resentment, not a moral requirement. We would not demand that a local tip twenty percent in a country where the service charge is included and tipping is a pure gift, so we cannot demand that the visitor tip here merely because the local percentage is different.

(2) Rosa answers that some conventions are not contracts but ongoing schemes that work only if enough people honor them. A queue exists because strangers are each willing to defer; if enough people jump it, the thing others were cooperating to produce begins to dissolve. Anyone who walks up to an existing queue and takes advantage of the order others are maintaining has a duty of fair play not to exploit their compliance. On her view, what matters is the situation the visitor actually enters, not the culture he was raised in.

(3) Daniel grants the queue case but says it proves his point. The duty not to cut derives from a general principle—do not exploit the cooperative efforts of the people around you—and it would bind a local in exactly the same way. What is binding is the interaction, not the local custom as such. Tipping is different. It is not a coordination device among equals; it is a wage subsidy by customers, made necessary because employers may legally pay servers a subminimum wage. Paying eight percent does not unravel a shared public good; it pays one worker less. If a wrong occurs there, it is committed by the employer and the legal wage structure, not by a visitor who declines to adopt a custom he finds arbitrary.

(4) Rosa replies that this is too clean. The server accepted the job in a labor market in which tips are an understood part of compensation, and menu prices are set on that premise. The visitor has known this for months and continues to order table service. A principled refusal is one thing; quietly paying below the customary rate while accepting service is closer to taking a discount at the server's expense. To say "I never joined the practice" while eating repeatedly in establishments where the practice is part of the economic background, she says, is to use convention as a shield rather than to take it seriously as a reason. As for crossing against the light, she argues that long-term visitors are guests in a meaningful sense and owe their host community default compliance with easy, low-cost norms, not only because visible disregard can erode a norm that others rely on, but because deciding to reside somewhere for a year presumes a weak form of acceptance of its shared practices.

They asked me which of them was right at each step. I could not settle it. Please adjudicate: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each; state what the visitor is actually morally required to do about the tipping and the jaywalking; and explain what general principle should determine when a local convention generates obligations for visitors, as well as what, if anything, both debaters are missing.
