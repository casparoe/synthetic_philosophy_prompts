Here's something I keep going around on. Since 2008 the big central banks have bought government bonds on a huge scale with newly created reserves, while insisting that this isn't 'printing money'—that it's 'providing liquidity' or easing financial conditions. The official picture seems to be that a bond and a reserve deposit are both just claims, so swapping one for the other doesn't create wealth; it only changes the form in which existing wealth is held. But suppose the same central bank instead credited the government's account with freshly created money so the government could send people checks. Everyone would call that a catagorically different thing: helicopter money, money-financed fiscal policy, actual new purchasing power rather than mere liquidity. What theory of wealth, money, and liquidity does that distinction presuppose? Does the first practice only make sense if you think wealth is ultimately real stuff and money is just a veil over it, or can the line between liquidity and wealth be drawn some other way that doesn't collapse in the second case? And is the distinction doing genuine conceptual work, or is it mostly a way of making one balance-sheet expansion sound less scary than the other?
