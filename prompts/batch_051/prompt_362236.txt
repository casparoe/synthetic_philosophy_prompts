I keep going in circles on this and could use a real negotiated verdict. On one side I have a view that feels basically right: suffering is bad no matter whose it is, and I can't find a principled reason that a baby sparrow starving in a nest matters less than the same amount of suffering in a mammal or a human. If that's true, then a thriving habitat is just a place where a certain ratio of welfare to suffering gets produced, and I keep reading that for most wild animals the ratio is awful — huge clutches, almost all offspring dying painfully young, predation, parasites. On that view, if clear-cutting a particular forest or draining a wetland made the total amount of suffering go down, it starts to look like a harm-reduction intervention rather than a crime. But then I imagine someone actually doing it — draining a marsh because the invertebrate larvae in it are mostly dying — and every part of me says no, absolutly not, and the refusal doesn't feel like a calculation. The ecosystem seems like the wrong kind of thing to flatten into a ledger of individual experiences; it's more like the harvesting-the-healthy-patient case, where the right response is a side constraint rather than a bad trade.
