Two of my colleagues, Lea and Dan, were arguing after a seminar on Otto Neurath’s picture language. For context, Neurath’s project was to build a standardized visual vocabulary of pictograms, with rules for combining them, so that the same presentation could be read across languages without translation. Lea had said that this works for naming concrete objects and showing quantities but breaks down as soon as it has to express logical structure. Dan said that was unfair, because Neurath never required every element to be a naturalistic picture; the point was that a conventionalized symbol system could function as an international auxiliary for teaching and statistics.

Lea replied that the real problem is not convention itself but the absence of a genuine grammar. You can superimpose a red slash on a pictogram to mean absent or not, but how does the system distinguish the proposition that not every worker is unemployed from the proposition that no worker is unemployed? Dan said you can arrange standardized pictograms in rows, with a worker sign, a crossed-out factory sign, and an additional marker for some versus all. Lea said the marker is doing all the logical work and is no more pictorial than a word. Dan asked whether the letters in the word not are themselves pictorial, and why a picture language should be forbidden from using non-pictorial logical elements if the whole system remains teachable without translation. At that point they noticed I was listening and asked me to referee, but I honestly could not.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step. Who is right about which specific points, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? In your answer, distinguish the question of whether pictures can carry truth-evaluable content from the question of whether they can carry it without relying on conventions that function like logical vocabulary. Also say whether Neurath’s own project is best understood as a claim about replacing language or as a claim about reducing translation barriers, and whether Lea’s demanded distinction between not every and no is a fair test, given that many natural languages handle the same distinction with particles rather than pictures.
