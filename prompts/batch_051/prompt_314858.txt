I've been asked to write up my philosophical profile on consequentialist cluelessness for a reading group and want an audit of whether it hangs together. Here it is. I'm an expected-value maximizing act-consequentialist. I think cluelessness is pervasive: for almost any ordinary action, the long-run consequences are largely unknowable, and those unknowable effects plausibly dominate the short-run effects I can see. I'm still confident about some specific comparisons — e.g., giving a couple grand to a well-run malaria charity is better than spending it on a new laptop for me. That confidence rests on the short-run effects being one-sidedly good while the long-run effects are roughly a wash between the two options. I lean on a distinction between simple cluelessness (I can't see even the first-order effects) and complex cluelessness (I can see the first-order effects but not the higher-order ones), and I think most of my decisions are complex rather than simple. I also think the sign of the long-run effects of almost any action could easily flip, so I can't strictly rule out that the laptop ends up better all-things-considered. I dont think any of this pushes me toward satisficing, deontology, or a precautionary principle. And I think the useful question in practice is rarely whether an action maximizes expected value but whether it is robustly best given what I can currently see. Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in tension and how badly. For each pair in tension, say how severe it is and what would have to give. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and flag any apparent tensions that actually dissolve on inspection. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating of the question.
