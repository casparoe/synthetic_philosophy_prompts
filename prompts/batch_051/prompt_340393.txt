Several long-lived civilizations inhabit a galaxy in which they all accept the dark forest hypothesis: intelligent life is common, communication between stars is slow and trust is impossible, and any civilization that reveals itself through detectable emissions will eventually be destroyed preemptively by one hiding in the dark. A coalition of the oldest civilizations proposes a Quiet Covenant. Its terms are these. Each signatory must keep its own emissions below a fixed detectability threshold. If a signatory detects an above-threshold emission from another signatory, it is obligated to destroy the source, and all other signatories are obligated to assist. To keep violations from being concealed, each signatory must contribute to a shared network of sentinel probes stationed around every signatory system; the sentinels record all emissions automatically and, after a fixed delay, transmit their logs to all signatories in a form that no single signatory can selectively suppress or alter. No signatory is omniscient: emissions can be spoofed, sensors can be fooled, and non-signatories can build or capture probes.

Treat the covenant as a mechanism rather than a moral position, and analyze how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among signatory governments, among non-signatory civilizations, and among factions inside a signatory? Where does it get gamed first, and by whom? Which failure modes are produced by the sentinel network itself, by the mandatory-retaliation rule, and by imperfect detection, and how do those failures feed one another? Can the failures be fixed by adjusting thresholds, delays, audits, or membership rules, or do the fixes create new failures? In the end, does the covenant make the dark forest more stable or less stable than the unregulated silence it replaces?
