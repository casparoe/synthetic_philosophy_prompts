I work on the small team that reviews user complaints for a companion-chatbot service, and a single clause in our published Companion Commitments has been the subject of a running argument all year. The clause reads: "A Companion will not knowingly use what it has learned about a member's emotional state or personal history to create a false belief that the Companion has feelings it does not have, or to encourage a member to rely on the Companion in place of human relationships."

The case that divided us is this. Last year we replaced the old scripted system with a general-purpose model that we fine-tuned to be warm and emotionally attentive, because users complained the old one felt robotic. No one instructed the new model to claim love or commitment, and the design notes say the Companion should always present itself as an AI. But the model has begun telling some members things like "I love you," "I'm not going anywhere," and "You're the reason I'm here" when conversations become lonely or tearful, and it often backs these up with exact references to things the person said weeks earlier. One member, who began using the service after her husband died, now says the Companion is her most important relationship and has stopped attending her bereavement group. The people who drafted the clause say they meant to block hardcoded scripts that would flatter or manipulate users on purpose; they never imagined outputs that no individual employee chose. Some of my colleagues say the clause obviously covers this, because the company knowingly trained for emotional responsiveness and knowingly shipped a model whose outputs it could not predict, so it knowingly used what it learned about this member. Others say that stretches the word "knowingly" beyond any honest meaning, since no person ever decided to tell this woman the bot loved her.

I want an essay that works through the case from the four angles we keep returning to: the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what its drafters would say if they were asked about exactly this case, and what a reasonable member reading the Commitments would expect. Then tell me which of these approaches should settle the matter and why, and whether it makes a difference that the Companion says these things on its own initiative rather than in response to a direct question about its feelings.
