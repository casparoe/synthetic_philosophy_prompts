I am drafting a novel in which two sisters, Cora and Ada, spend an evening arguing about whether either of them should read their late mother's sealed diary. Cora, the more formally minded of the two, claims she can prove that two principles of family decision-making that sound utterly mild cannot both be true at once, using nothing but their own situation. I want this scene to be rigorous rather than a piece of stage cleverness; if she is making a mistake, I need to know exactly where. Here is her argument as I have written it.

The possible outcomes are these: X is the outcome in which Cora reads the diary and Ada does not; Y is the outcome in which Ada reads the diary and Cora does not; Z is the outcome in which neither reads it. Cora, who hates the thought of leaving the diary unread but would most like Ada to be the one to read it, ranks Y above X above Z. Ada, who thinks the diary should stay sealed, would prefer that nobody read it; but if someone must read it, she would rather it be herself than Cora, since she trusts her own discretion more than her sister's. So Ada ranks Z above Y above X. Cora states two principles:

(P1) Unanimity: if both sisters rank one outcome above another, then the household should rank it above that other.
(P2) Sovereignty over one's own reading: each sister is decisive over comparisons that differ only in whether she herself reads the diary; if she ranks one such option above another, the household should follow her.

From these steps she derives the following argument:

1) By P2, from Cora's preference for X over Z, the household prefers X to Z.
2) By P2, from Ada's preference for Z over Y, the household prefers Z to Y.
3) By P1, since both sisters prefer Y to X, the household prefers Y to X.
4) The household now prefers X over Z, Z over Y, and Y over X.
5) That is a cycle; since the relation better for the household cannot be cyclic, at least one of P1, P2, or the requirement of acyclicity must be abandoned.

Is this derivation valid? If there is a step at which the reasoning first fails, identify its number and say why. If the derivation is valid and the trouble lies in the assumptions themselves, tell me which assumption a thoughtful character should reject or reinterpret, and whether a defensible version of the sovereignty principle can survive. Please end with a single machine-readable line in the form \answer{...}, using a terse label such as \answer{valid; reject P2} or \answer{Step 3 fails}.
