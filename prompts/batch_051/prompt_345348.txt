I co-run an evening discussion group that's mostly rationalist-adjacent folks, and we've managed to relitigate the 'man of his time' defence three meetings in a row: whether Jefferson's slaveholding, Churchill's racial views or his bombing of civilians, and Columbus's whole project get excused or at least mitigated by the fact that everyone around them mostly thought the same way. The problem is the conversation immediately collapses into people swapping cached verdicts, and the defence gets used as a kind of motte-and-bailey where the speaker retreats to a weaker claim when pressed. I want to run a 90-minute session for about 12 people (no reading beforehand, and I'd rather nobody have to know more than a generally informed person knows about the three figures) that makes participants feel both the genuine pull of that defence and the exact point where it runs out. The idea should come out of the structure of the activity, not a mini-lecture.

What I'd like is a structured deliberation, something like a tribunal or sentencing commission but not just a debate: there should be rounds, roles, and some small stakes (tokens or scored verdicts, no real money) so that people actually change their minds under pressure rather than performing. Design it so participants first judge the figures under modern standards and get comfortable with their own severity, then the rules change to make different versions of the defence available one at a time, so people catch themselves reaching for each version and can feel what it buys and what it costs. In particular I want the activity to force apart three things that usually get smeared together in that phrase, each pulled seperately so they can be told apart in the debrief: the epistemic excuse (they couldn't reasonably have known better), the companion-in-guilt point (everyone around them did it, so singling them out is unfair), and the stronger relativist move (later standards simply don't apply to them). The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto each of these, say what resisting any one of them commits you to, and note where participants themselves changed verdicts and which lever did the work. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be blunt about which feature of judging actual historical figures a roleplay can't reproduce and I'll need to supply in the wrap-up. No reading lists, no citations — the game itself should carry the content.
