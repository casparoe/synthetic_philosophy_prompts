I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about when a refusal to trade — or the terms someone offers — should be treated as information, and my gut verdicts on a set of cases are plainly not hanging together. I'd like a principle that makes sense of them, or at least tells me which verdicts to revise.

Here are the cases, judged the way I actually judge them.

1. At a coin fair, a dealer offers me a gold coin at well below its normal auction value. I don't buy, because the only plausible explanation for the discount is that the dealer knows something that makes the coin worth less. This seems obviously right to me.

2. The CFO of the company I work for quietly sells a large block of stock two weeks before earnings. I treat this as bad news and trim my own position. Fine.

3. The same CFO sells a block on the first trading day of every quarter because a pre-announced, pre-scheduled plan says so. I ignore it. Fine — and importantly different from case 2.

4. A local meteorologist with a strong forecasting record offers me even odds on rain tomorrow, though by my own estimate rain is 65 percent likely. I decline the bet. Not because I think the terms are favorable to him, but because his willingness to bet tells me his information beats mine. I'm comfortable declining, yet this case nags at me, because if his offer is a signal against the bet, why should he ever accept a bet from anyone?

5. An apparently anxious seller lists a four-year-old laptop at 70 percent of the going rate on an online listing site. I pass: the discount is dressed-up evidence of a hidden defect. Fine.

6. A friend refuses to sell me a painting even though I offer twice what an identical one recently sold for. I read the refusal as information about his attachment to the painting, not about the painting's market value. Fine — but notice that I'm not treating a refusal to trade as evidence against the deal in the financial sense.

7. A quant fund I respect runs a model that identifies mispriced stocks, but its traders deliberately avoid acting on large positions in exactly those names. One of them explained that, by the time a big order fills, the counterparty has already voted with their information, so the fill itself is evidence that the model is wrong. I find this disciplined, but it seems to imply that no one should ever trade purely on an informational edge — and yet markets are full of exactly such trading.

8. The same fund is perfectly willing to take the other side when the counterparty is an index fund mechanically rebalancing, because a rebalancing order is not an information event.

What I want is a principle that covers these eight cases: one that says when another person's willingness to trade, or the terms they offer, should count as evidence against the deal, and when it shouldn't. I'd like it stated precisely, with the clause that handles each case, and I'd like to know what it predicts about cases I haven't listed — for example, a retail investor who sells a stock simply because it has fallen and they can no longer stand the loss: are they giving the market information, or just supplying liquidity?

And I need you to be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. The general version of the reasoning in cases 1, 4, and 7 seems to lead toward a no-trade conclusion: if everyone treats the other side's willingness to trade as adverse information, discretionary trade on private information should unravel. Cases 3, 6, and 8 are the ones trying to keep trade possible. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close to all of them — and if not, which verdicts should I give up?
