Question: Maxwell's demon appears to be able to sort fast and slow molecules and thereby produce a usable temperature difference from a uniform gas without expending work, seemingly violating the second law. In the modern treatment due to Landauer and Bennett, does the demon actually violate the second law? Explain the roles of information acquisition, storage, and erasure in the resolution.

Student answer: Taken at face value, the demon violates the second law: it separates fast from slow molecules, producing a usable temperature difference from an initially uniform gas with no compensating entropy increase elsewhere. The modern conclusion, however, is that the second law survives once the demon's own information processing is included in the accounting. The demon must record the outcome of each measurement in a memory, and because the memory is finite, old records must eventually be erased to make room for new ones. Erasure is logically irreversible, and Landauer's principle sets the lower bound on its thermodynamic cost: erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 of energy as heat into the environment, increasing the environmental entropy by at least k ln 2 per bit. Over the complete cycle of measuring, sorting, and erasing, this entropy increase compensates for the entropy decrease in the gas, so total entropy does not fall. The crucial step, as Landauer showed, is measurement: in order to determine a molecule's velocity, the demon must physically disturb the molecule, and this acquisition of information is what actually costs at least kT ln 2 per bit. Sorting itself can then be carried out without further thermodynamic cost. Information is therefore physical, and the second law is safe because acquiring information inevitably dissipates heat.
