I have recently been seconded to the team re-curating our regional museum's human prehistory gallery, and at the same time my regular work has me reviewing proposals for an agricultural development fund; the question of how we should think about the origins of farming has therefore become unexpectedly personal.

The two institutions seem to me to share a tacit philosophy, and I want to know exactly what it commits them to. The museum's current gallery treats the shift from foraging to agriculture as an unambiguous ascent: the final Paleolithic room is small and dim, with captions about harsh and uncertain lives, and the visitor steps from it into a bright Neolithic room full of granaries, craft specialization, and proto-cities, captioned as the dawn of civilization. The fund I review for finances the sedentarization of the few remaining foraging and semi-nomadic groups in the region; proposals are scored by increases in cultivated area, yield, and market participation, and one that defended foraging as a coherent way of life with goods of its own would be dismissed as sentimental.

Neither institution acknowledges the darker reading of the same transition, in which the average person may have paid for the change with their body. Skeletal evidence suggests early farmers were shorter than the foragers who preceded them and show more signs of nutritional stress and infectious disease; the earliest farming settlements exhibit steeper inequalities in grave goods; and stored grain seems to have made famine and concentrated authority possible in new ways. One can accept all of this and still hold that the transition was a necessary precondition of everything that came later. But that is a claim about what the change made possible, not about whether it made individual lives go better.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of value, well-being, and history that these practices presuppose. What view of human flourishing is doing the work when both institutions present the move to farming as progress while treating worse average health, more disease, less leisure, and greater inequality as costs that need not be weighed? Which rival views of a good human life does this pattern quietly rule out — for instance, a view on which ample leisure, bodily health, and rough equality count more than cultural elaboration or sheer numbers? Does the practice strictly presuppose that early agricultural life was better, on balance, than the foraging life it replaced, or is it also compatible with the skeptical conclusion that the Neolithic made the average person worse off? Are the museum's and the fund's presuppositions consistent with each other, given that one looks back at a completed transition and the other intervenes among its last survivors? And if the underlying theory were replaced with one that judged the Neolithic by the quality of individual lives rather than by what those lives made possible, what would have to change in the gallery captions and in the fund's scoring criteria, and what would likely remain the same for reasons of irreversibility or feasibility rather than principle?
