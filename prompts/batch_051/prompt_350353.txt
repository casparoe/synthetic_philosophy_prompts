I've been trying to wrap my head around a design choice in how the big AI labs train models, and I want to know what it presupposes philosophically. The default setup is outcome-based supervision: the reward model that grades the model's attempts only ever sees the final output. It doesn't matter whether the chain of thought was elegant or full of confusions, as long as the final answer is right or preferred by humans. But for math and coding tasks, several groups now use process-based supervision instead: a reward model scores each intermediate step as correct or incorrect, so a model gets penalized for a wrong step even if it later stumbles into the right answer. The wierd thing is the same labs use outcome supervision for open-ended chat and process supervision for math, and by most reports process supervision produces better final answers and more interpretable reasoning anyway.

What I want to know is what this practice commits you to about what a solution, or a piece of reasoning, is. Does scoring the intermediate steps presuppose that those steps have value in themselves, not just as means to the final answer? Or is it just a credit-assignment trick that is philosophically inert and fully compatible with a purely outcome-based view of what makes an answer good? And what view of a task's normativity — what makes one response to a task better than another — would make the mixed pattern, outcome for chat and process for math, coherent rather than ad hoc? I'm also curious what would have to change in how we train and evaluate these systems if someone actually believed the process-based view and applied it across the board.

3. What it strictly presupposes vs. merely fits with (about 250 words): I want that difference drawn clearly.
4. Where the practice is underdetermined (about 150 words): say where the same engineering choices are compatible with more than one of those rival theories.
5. What changes on a fully process-based view (about 200 words): concretely, what would have to change in training, evaluation, and the tasks we'd trust these models with.
