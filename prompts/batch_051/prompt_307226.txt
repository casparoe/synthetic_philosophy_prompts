Suppose someone says of their spouse that they would love them just the same if they ceased to be kind, funny, or loyal, because their love does not depend on how the spouse scores on such measures. Many people find that not romantic but faintly pathological, while "I love you because you are kind" sounds like a perfectly good reason for love. Yet a parent who loves a child just the same no matter how the child turns out is doing exactly what we think a parent should do, and a parent who made love conditional on the child's continuing to deserve it would be considered monstrous. So we seem to demand both that love track the beloved's qualities and that it outrun them, and we apply those two standards unevenly as between romantic and parental love. Is there a principled account of love's reasons—of the sort that sees love either as appraising value already present in the beloved or as bestowing value through loving—that makes this pattern defensible? Work out the strongest such account and give an honest verdict on whether it succeeds or whether one of the ordinary judgments should be given up.
