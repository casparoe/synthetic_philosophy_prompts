I’m a PhD student working on social epistemology and the ethics of emerging media. My dissertation chapter concerns what some call the epistemic backstop: the idea that, at least in recent decades, the availability of photographic, audio, and video recordings provided a kind of default check on spoken testimony — if someone’s report was challenged, we could in principle appeal to a recording of the event. A familiar worry is that deepfakes break this backstop: once convincing synthetic recordings are cheap and widespread, the existence of a video can no longer play the same checking role.

I’ve been collecting the responses to this worry that are standard in the literature, and I have the following list:

1. Cryptographic authentication and provenance tracking, so that only signed recordings from trusted devices count as backstop-quality evidence.
2. Relying on live, in-person witnessing and locally co-present epistemic communities as the new backstop.
3. Institutional deflation: treat recorded evidence as just one more fallible source and rely instead on the general reputation of media organizations.
4. Improving public epistemic hygiene — detection skills, source-checking habits — so people become less vulnerable to deceptive recordings.
5. Legal deterrence and platform-level moderation to keep deepfakes out of circulation.

I have a feeling the literature contains at least one further response that is not a rewording, special case, or combination of these. Please think carefully and propose exactly five candidate responses of the same general kind — strategies for restoring or replacing the epistemic backstop after deepfakes — that are not on my list. For each candidate, state it in one or two sentences, and then in the same numbered point say briefly how it differs from the items above and whether you think it is any good. Act as an expert in social epistemology and philosophy of technology. Brainstorm before finalizing, but then output only the five-item numbered list, with no introduction or conclusion.
