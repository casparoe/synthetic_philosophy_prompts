I am a retired geochemist, now a volunteer on the review committee of a regional natural-history collection. After four decades of classifying minerals and microfossils, I have set myself to reading philosophy of biology, and I have become interested in assembly theory's attempt to replace loose talk of "complexity" with something operational. In that framework, an object is not "complex" because it looks intricate; it is complex in the relevant sense if its assembly index is high — roughly, the fewest recursive construction steps needed to build the object from a fixed alphabet of elementary building blocks, where any intermediate product may be re-used — and it is abundant, meaning many near-identical copies occur in the finite pool under examination. The central idea I want to turn into a reusable procedure is this: high assembly index plus high copy number is a signal that the object's abundance is not plausibly explained by unguided combinatorial assembly, and that a selective process, biological or technological, is the better explanation; a low assembly index, by contrast, carries no such signal even at high copy number, because configurations that are easy to assemble can be reached by many unguided routes.

My committee meets once a month. We must decide, usually within three or four hours and without computation, which accessions to flag for expensive further analysis as objects whose form requires a selection-based explanation, and which to leave in the ordinary mineralogical inventory. The objects before us are things like a branching mineral dendrite, a tray of several hundred near-identical perforated shell beads, a single visually elaborate lump that may be an ornament or a concretion, suites of flint flakes, and slabs covered with repeating microscopic spheres. We have hand lenses, a microscope, comparative trays, and no budget for sequencing or isotope work unless a flag is raised.

Write a decision procedure my committee could actually run on a candidate object: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with "do not flag"; where it stops with "flag for selection-based explanation"; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question has become one of judgment rather than calculation. Mark those judgment points as such, and be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

The procedure must confront at least the following difficulties, rather than gliding over them. The assembly index changes if we change the elementary building blocks or the level of granularity at which we describe the object. Copy number changes if we redraw the boundaries of the sample or loosen the tolerance for counting two objects as "the same." And some objects that genuinely owe their form to selection have a low assembly index, just as some high-index, high-copy patterns may turn out not to be selected.

Then run the procedure on three cases: the branching mineral dendrite; the tray of several hundred near-identical perforated shell beads; and the single visually elaborate putative earliest ornament. Include among the outcomes at least one answer my committee would rather not accept, and conclude by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.
