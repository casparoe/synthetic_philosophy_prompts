I am a novelist, and I am writing a novel about a wealthy democracy that, over roughly a decade and without a single clear rupture, loses most of what made it democratic. I have built a small theory to give the decline a shape before I write the scenes, and I would like it checked before I let it carry the architecture of the book.

I have been calling the theory the ladder of dirty moves. Its core is that democratic health is less a matter of what the written rules say than of a shared, usually tacit sense of which political moves are simply off the table: imprisoning a rival on a contrived pretext, altering electoral rules mid-cycle to retain power, turning the state broadcaster against the opposition, encouraging organised thugs to turn up at rallies. In a healthy democracy, I claim, even ruthless politicians treat some of these as unthinkable, and what keeps the democracy healthy is that shared sense of the unthinkable more than the formal rules.

Backsliding, on my account, is the process by which an unthinkable move is walked step by step into the normal: first it is floated, then attempted once behind a deniable pretext, then repeated, then defended as precedent, then taken for granted. Each rung requires willing politicians, compliant lawyers, and distracted or exhausted voters, but the real action is in the shared sense of what one can no longer be shamed for. The central, testable-sounding claim is that what backslides first is not the constitution or the party system but this shared grammar of illegitimate moves; the institutions follow, sometimes years later. The mechanism is deflationary: every tolerated breach becomes public evidence that one can do such a thing and survive, which lowers the expected cost of the next breach for every actor at once. I think this captures the pattern in Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, and increasingly in the United States, better than accounts built on formal rule-breaking, because the operators in those places have usually kept some legal or administrative pretext in place. In the novel, the first rung is an incumbent government floating the prosecution of a former finance minister for decisions taken by the whole cabinet; four years on, prosecuting rivals has become ordinary.

I know the phrase democratic backsliding is already current. What I want to know is whether my theory is more than a dramatised restatement of it. Is the theory coherent, or does the shared grammar collapse into the formal rules once one asks where grammar ends and rules begin? Has something like the underlying idea already been worked out, and is my version then only a literary metaphor for it? And where does it break? Most importantly, who decides which moves are illegitimate without importing a partisan standard, since one side's normal procedure is another side's dirty move; and can the ladder explain why some breaches normalise while others produce a backlash and are abandoned, or does it merely re-describe the successful cases after the fact? I would rather learn now whether the spine of the novel rests on a real distinction or on a well-articulated confusion.
