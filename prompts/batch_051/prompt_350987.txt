Since I retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading my way through Bayesian confirmation theory, and a comparison keeps presenting itself that I am not competent to settle on my own.

The first side is what the philosophical literature calls the problem of old evidence. Suppose a fact E was already firmly accepted by the scientific community before a hypothesis H was proposed, and H would explain or entail E. Under the usual idealization of acceptance, the probability of E is 1; if H entails E, the probability of E given H is 1 as well; so the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E) cannot exceed 1, and by the standard Bayesian criterion E cannot confirm H after the fact. Yet the history of science treats exactly such facts as strong support for newly proposed theories. The way Newtonian mechanics absorbed the planetary regularities codified by Kepler, and the way general relativity retrospectively explained the long-observed anomaly in Mercury's perihelion, are the stock illustrations. The most discussed remedies are counterfactual: subtract E from the background information and ask how much E would have raised the probability of H against that revised background.

The other side comes from my former professional life. In the statistical practice of the laboratory, one learns early that if the data are inspected first and the hypothesis or the analysis is then chosen because those same data suggest it, those data cannot also count as independent confirmation of the hypothesis. That is the rationale behind pre-registration, corrections for multiple comparisons, and holding out a validation sample. The diagnosis there is also chronological: observations gathered before the hypothesis was fixed stand differently from observations gathered after it.

I cannot decide whether this resemblance is deep or superficial. Both positions seem to say that a datum cannot be used twice, once to produce a hypothesis and again to support it. But the mechanisms look different. The old-evidence problem is generated by a degenerate probability assignment, P(E)=1, while the post hoc testing problem is generated by a sampling model of what else could have been observed or concluded; one is repaired by a counterfactual reconstruction of the past, the other by a prospective commitment about the future. Moreover, the Bayesian formalism is normally advertised as indifferent to history, since the posterior is supposed to depend only on the prior and the likelihood rather than on when a hypothesis happened to occur to anyone. If the analogy is sound, it seems to reintroduce exactly the order-dependence that the formalism had promised to do without.

So my question is this. Is the analogy picking out a genuine constraint on confirmation, perhaps that the hypothesis tested must be fixed independently of the evidence that tests it, in which case results ought to transfer between the two domains? Or are these only superficially similar problems, one epistemic and the other a methodological caution about error control? Please state the mapping explicitly, say what would have to be true of both sides for a result from one to transfer to the other, and identify at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that is in fact false.
