I'm trying to get a handle on what Adam Smith actually means by "colony" in the Wealth of Nations, because he uses the word for a lot of different arrangements — the Greek settlements, the Roman ones, and the modern European ones in America and the West Indies. It seems to me he thinks the ancient Greek ones are the clearest case: people leave because the home territory can't support them, they set up a new political community, and the mother city has no direct authority over them. The Roman ones he describes as more like garrisons or land grants inside conquered territory, still subject to the mother city. And the modern European ones are different again because they arise from no necessity (he says the utility has been great but the original interest wasn't so plain and distinct).

So here's my attempt at a loose Smithian criterion for counting something as a "colony": (1) there is some sort of emigration or settlement from a home country, (2) the settlers retain some tie of origin or imitation, but (3) the crucial thing is whether the settlement is treated as an emancipated child (Greek style) or as a dependent plantation (Roman style), and (4) the economic relationship is important — whether the mother country grants the settlement a monopoly or restricts its trade to benefit itself.

Here's the borderline case I keep going back and forth on: the East India Company's settlements and territories in Bengal in the mid-18th century — before the Crown takes over in 1858 but after the Company has acquired the diwani (revenue rights) and is effectively governing large territories. Is that a colony? Some of Smith's language about the monopoly of the colonial trade would seem to include it as part of the "East Indies" trade, and he talks about the Company's government of those territories. But the people "emigrating" there are largely company servants, soldiers, and merchants, not settlers in the Greek sense of people who need land and set up a new polis. And the relationship with the home country is partly commercial, partly fiscal, partly military — but the inhabitants are not really subjects in the same way as American colonists. So does it qualify under the criteria above? If not, which criterion is doing the most work, and does the case show the criteria themselves need revision? Also, where exactly would Smith draw the line between a "colony" and a "territory under a trading company's government," and does his own analysis give us reason to think that line matters for his critique of empire?

Oh, and don't just give a verdict — say what would change your mind. Like, which argument or piece of evidence about the East India Company's role would most force you to flip the answer, or where your reasoning is shakiest.
