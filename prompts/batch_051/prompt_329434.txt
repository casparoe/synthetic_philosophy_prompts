At a dinner party recently, a friend described her neighbor with genuine admiration: he repairs his own appliances, saves nearly half his income, buys almost everything secondhand, and lets nothing usable go to waste, and the whole table agreed that he was living a life to be admired. Half an hour later, the same people dismissed a proposal for public campaigns urging households to consume less, and for local policies nudging people toward lower-consumption lifestyles, as paternalistic, economically naive, and joyless. I have since noticed the same split in my own reactions: frugality shows up as a moral achievement when it appears in a person, but the systematic promotion of frugality — making a whole population more frugal — seems suspect, even faintly absurd. We praise being frugal and yet resist wanting everyone, as a matter of policy or culture, to be frugal.

I would like you to work out whether anything justifies this asymmetry between the individual-level judgment and the collective-level one, or whether it is simply an inconsistency we live with. Give me the strongest principle you can find that would generate both verdicts — perhaps something about autonomy and the wrong of paternalism, perhaps something about how individual thrift fails to aggregate into collective benefit (the paradox of thrift, the worry that if everyone saves more, demand falls and the shared effort backfires), perhaps something about frugality being an excellence of disposition rather than a quantity of consumption, or a stronger principle of your own. Then test that principle against adjacent cases: we publicly promote other virtues such as exercise, literacy, charitable giving, and the avoidance of smoking without much discomfort, and we have tolerated and even celebrated campaigns for thrift during wartime, while a person who merely hoards is not admired the way the neighbor who mends and reuses is. End with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dissolved into a single, more consistent position on frugality.
