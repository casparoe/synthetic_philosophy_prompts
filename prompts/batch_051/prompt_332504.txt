I am a PhD student in philosophy of science working on the foundations of probability, and my reading group has been stuck for several sessions on how modal and probabilistic notions relate to one another. The standard formal framework invites a certain asymmetry: a probability space is given by a set of outcomes plus a measure, and modal-sounding categories are then read off from the measure, so that an event of probability one is almost sure and an event of probability zero is almost impossible. Yet familiar examples seem to pry these apart. A fair spinner landing at exactly a specified point on its circumference has probability zero, while landing somewhere in an arc of positive length has positive probability; the specified point is not thereby an impossible outcome. Similarly, an event can have probability one without being necessary. Everyone in the group accepts the examples. What we cannot agree on is what they show.

My own view is that they are a symptom of a deeper conceptual priority rather than a harmless technicality. I would like you to write a committed argument for the following position: probability talk, whether epistemic or objective, presupposes a prior modal structure—a space of possible outcomes and a distinction between the possible and the impossible—while the modal structure itself can be characterized without appealing to probabilistic notions. In that sense, modality is conceptually more fundamental than probability. Please defend a definite version of this claim rather than surveying the alternatives. Give the strongest reasons you have, explain how they handle the probability-zero-but-possible cases, and state the most serious objection to your position and how you would answer it. You may frame the argument in your own terms, but do not leave the conclusion open for me to choose.
