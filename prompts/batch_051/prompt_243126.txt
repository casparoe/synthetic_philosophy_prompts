For a workshop on the ethics of social comparison, I need a document that two participants have agreed to co-author after a year of arguing past each other. One defends a celebratory success norm of the kind often associated with a certain reading of the American Dream: public admiration of exceptional achievement is broadly healthy, ambition and excellence should be honored rather than envied, and a culture that does this gets more achievement, more honest signaling of talent, and benefits that diffuse to everyone. The other defends a leveling norm of the kind associated with Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome: there is something valuable, socially and even epistemically, in the collective impulse to deflate or cut down those who rise conspicuously above others, because reflexive admiration is easily gamed by self-promoters, blinds people to luck and unearned advantage, and corrodes equality and solidarity. Neither is a caricature. Each grants that her own norm can misfire—admiration can decay into sycophancy toward the powerful, and leveling can decay into crab-bucket spite toward anyone who merely tries—and each is willing to revise specific commitments, though not yet her overall position.

Write the joint statement this collaboration should produce. Begin with a numbered list of claims both can sign: about how success is actually allocated between effort and luck, about what ordinary people tend to feel and do when someone rises above them, about the distinctive harms of reflexive admiration and reflexive cutting-down, and about what a healthy culture of achievement would have to accomplish for either side to be satisfied. Then give a disagreements table: for each remaining disagreement, state the claim in wording each side would accept as her own and in a neutral formulation both would accept as the shared statement of what is at issue. Then identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect two cruxes are whether the resentment behind leveling norms is sensitive to deservingness or is aimed at distinction as such, and whether admiration and envy are genuine opposites or can coexist in the same person toward the same person at the same time—but add or replace cruxes if the document's logic calls for it. For each crux, describe a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that case will show. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal, say which part and why. Keep the document one both could actually sign: no side should have to accept a formulation of her own view that she would disown, and no agreed claim should be so weak that it does no work.
