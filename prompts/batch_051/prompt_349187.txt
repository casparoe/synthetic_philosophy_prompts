I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of biology course, and a pattern keeps appearing in our readings that I have never seen given a precise name. In Darwin’s account, the appearance of design is produced by natural selection: variations arise without any foresight of what would be useful, and selection preserves whatever happens to work, so the resulting organism looks as if it had been engineered, though no part of the process understood what it was doing. In Turing’s account, a machine can perform tasks that would be called intelligent if a human did them—calculating, problem-solving, even conversing—while the underlying process is purely mechanical, with no inner grasp of the task or the meanings involved. In both cases there is a kind of inversion of our ordinary explanatory instinct: instead of explaining competence by appeal to a competent mind, the account explains competence by appeal to a process whose components are, taken individually and collectively, blind or uncomprehending.

I am not asking for a bibliography, and I do not especially care about the label. What I want to know is whether this exact configuration—competence at the level of the whole, absence of comprehension at the level of the underlying process—has a well-defined name and a settled conceptual role, or whether it is just a loose family resemblance among cases. If it does have a name, how does it differ from nearby ideas such as emergence, functionalism, or the idea of a blind process? If it does not have one, what would you call it, and what would be a boundary case that tests whether something is a genuine instance rather than a merely superficial analogy?
