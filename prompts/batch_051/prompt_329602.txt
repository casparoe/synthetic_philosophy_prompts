A popular science-policy essay I read defends public funding for cosmology by comparing it to buying lottery tickets. Each observational program or theory group is a ticket; the jackpot is a discovery that eventually pays off technologically, in the way that work on quantum mechanics or relativity later produced spin-offs. The essay concludes that because jackpots are unpredictable and some tickets do win, public funders should hold a large portfolio of tickets rather than trying to pick winners.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a general defense or attack on funding cosmology. First give the mapping explicitly, as a table: what corresponds to the ticket, the ticket price, the odds, the jackpot, the winner, the organizer, and the other players. Then say where the mapping breaks. In particular, identify which features of real lotteries the analogy imports silently that research lacks (for example, a known and fixed prize structure, known or estimated odds, zero-sum payout, negative expected monetary return, and no side benefits from the act of buying), and say which of those disanalogies matter for the essay’s conclusion. Also say whether the analogy can represent the value of cosmological knowledge when it never produces an application, or whether it only licenses funding by promising one. Finally, state whether the portfolio conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy if you think one is available. Keep your answer to no more than 750 words.
