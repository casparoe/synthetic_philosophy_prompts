I work in machine learning, so I am on very comfortable ground with the optimization side of things — loss landscapes, local minima and saddle points, learning rates, momentum, stochastic versus full-batch gradients, non-stationary objectives, distribution shift, population-based training — but my grasp of evolutionary biology is mostly at the level of general-audience accounts. Lately I have been trying to get clear on an argument in biology about whether natural selection is best understood as an optimizer. On one side is the picture of selection as a hill-climber on a fitness landscape: populations accumulate many small improvements, move up local slopes, and produce the appearance of design without foresight, though the process can get stuck on local peaks and cannot cross a valley to reach a higher one. On the other side are critics who say this picture is at best misleading: fitness is not a fixed scalar like a loss function, because it depends on the composition of the population itself and on a changing environment, including other evolving lineages; selection has no gradient to follow, since variation is undirected and the process is more like repeatedly filtering among random perturbations than descending along a gradient; and there are standard cases in which selection lowers mean fitness or drives traits to extremes that harm the population, such as meiotic drive, sexual selection runaways, and arms races with pathogens. There is also a formal result I have seen paraphrased — that the expected rate of increase in mean fitness due to selection equals the additive genetic variance in fitness — which sounds oddly close to saying that the learning rate of evolution is set by the variance of whatever happens to be available to select from.

I would like you to perform a careful translation for me. Restate the optimist's position and the critics' objections, one by one, in the vocabulary I actually use: objective functions, gradients and their absence, learning rates, stochasticity, landscape geometry, non-stationarity, distribution shift, population-based search. Be honest about where the mapping is merely analogical rather than exact. Then give me the residue report: which claims survive the translation cleanly, which had to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in how I think about optimization. The part I most want your judgment on is whether the residue is a limitation of my vocabulary — for instance, that it assumes a fixed objective and a clean separation between the optimizer and the thing being optimized, whereas in evolution the loss is itself generated by the very creatures whose parameters are being tuned — or whether it shows that the idea of natural selection as an optimizer was less precise than its partisans made it look. If possible, name one or two predictions the gradient-descent picture would make about evolution that are simply false, and one or two that it gets right.

As a secondary question, I would like your view on what my own field should take away from this. Does natural selection, as you have translated it, contain anything that modern optimizer design lacks and should envy — open-ended search without an explicit objective, indefinitely maintained exploration, robustness to radically non-stationary environments — or is it mainly a cautionary tale about a weak baseline method that happens to have run for a very long time?
