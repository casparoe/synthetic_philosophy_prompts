I write about economics and institutions for a general-interest magazine, and I want to think through a policy counterfactual in enough detail to explain the relevant mechanisms to readers who are not specialists. Suppose a large, wealthy democracy with a mature tax administration — call it Asteria — abolishes its existing estate and gift taxes in 2030 and replaces them with a single progressive tax on wealth received over a person's lifetime, rather than on wealth transferred by the donor. Every resident for tax purposes has one cumulative lifetime receipts account. All inheritances and lifetime gifts above a 15,000-unit annual exclusion per donor per recipient are credited to the recipient's account at fair market value on the day of transfer. The first 600,000 units of lifetime receipts are exempt; receipts between 600,000 and 5 million are taxed at 25 percent; receipts between 5 million and 20 million at 40 percent; and receipts above 20 million at 55 percent. The tax is owed by the recipient, not by the estate. Spousal transfers are deferred until the surviving spouse's death, at which point the couple's remaining wealth is treated as received by the next-generation beneficiaries. Transfers to the state and to bona fide operating charities are exempt and do not consume the lifetime allowance. Assume the compliance infrastructure is roughly as effective as it is for the ordinary income tax, so the interesting question is legal adaptation and incidence rather than collapse through simple concealment.

Predict, with your chain of reasoning visible, how this reform reshapes the transmission of dynastic wealth and the character of equal opportunity after roughly two generations. I want mechanisms more than a verdict. How do very wealthy families reorganize holdings, the timing of gifts, family businesses, trusts, charitable vehicles, and residential or citizenship choices? Which adaptations are genuine substitutes for direct inheritance, and which are mostly cosmetic? What happens to succession in owner-managed firms, to the financing of adult children's housing and education, and to the marriage market among the very wealthy? Do young adults in the second generation actually face materially more equal prospects, or does the reform mainly reroute advantage into expenditures that are hard to tax and hard to observe? And what political feedback determines whether the system survives forty years? For each major link in your argument, indicate whether it is robust, contingent on specific empirical parameters — and which ones — or a speculation, and state what would have to be true for your overall prediction to be wrong.
