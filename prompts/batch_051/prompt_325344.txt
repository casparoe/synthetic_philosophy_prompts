I have been teaching the standard unit on collective action for years—Hardin's tragedy of the commons, Olson's logic of collective action, and the familiar distinction between public goods and common-pool resources—and I have come to suspect that the tidy pessimism of those models does too much work. Students leave the unit confident that rational actors will underprovide public goods and overexploit common-pool resources unless coercion, privatization, or small-group norms intervene. I would like you to test that tidy story against a case that looks, at first glance, like a refutation of it: the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer, adopted in 1987 and eventually ratified by every United Nations member state, and the subsequent phase-out of chlorofluorocarbons. I am not after a history of the treaty. What I want is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions: theory illuminating the episode, and the episode testing the theory.

Begin by classifying the good at stake. Is stratospheric ozone best understood as a public good, a common-pool resource, or something else, and does that classification shift over the course of the episode? Then identify the relevant actors—states, the major CFC-producing firms, with DuPont as the central case, atmospheric scientists, publics, and environmental organizations—and reconstruct, at least schematically, their options and payoffs at two moments: before the discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole forced the issue, and after viable CFC substitutes and trade sanctions against non-parties had altered the payoff structure. Spell out what the standard theory would have predicted at each moment, and say where the episode conforms to that prediction and where it departs from it. In particular, address whether the success is best explained by resources already inside the theory—a small number of major producers, private gains available to early movers, and the excludable club of parties backed by trade measures—or by factors the theory is poorly equipped to handle, such as the political force of risk perception, moral concern for distant populations and future generations, deference to scientific consensus, or the design of the negotiation process itself.

Finally, give your verdict on what the case shows about the theory. Does Montreal refute the tragedy-of-the-commons model? Does it turn out that the ozone layer was never a commons or public good of the problematic sort, so the theory was never really at risk? Or does the episode instead expose the theory's silence on precisely the mechanisms that mattered? Please begin with a one-paragraph bottom line stating your overall verdict, then develop the analysis, and keep the entire response to no more than 900 words.
