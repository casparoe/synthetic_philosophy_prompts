I'm rereading The Poverty of Historicism for a reading group and I keep going back and forth on two bits of the book that seem to pull in opposite directions. Early on, Popper argues that we can't predict the future growth of knowledge: if we could know today what a future discovery will be, we'd already have made it, so the content of future knowledge is necessarily closed off to us now. That move is doing a lot of work in the book, because if knowledge growth is unpredictable then no science of history can forecast the social future. But later, when he discusses the Oedipus effect, he describes the influence of a prediction on the predicted event as something that can itself be the object of social study—sometimes you can even use the effect to foresee how a public forecast will change behavior. That sure sounds like he's claiming a regular causal pattern in social life, the sort of thing he says historicists wrongly posit.

Can these two be reconciled? Maybe the first is a logical point about knowledge while the second is an empirical tendency. But if social scientists can know enough about prediction effects to predict their consequences, haven't they got a piece of a law of history? Or is Popper only saying the Oedipus effect is possible and sometimes explainable after the fact, not predictable in advance? I want a reading that doesn't just save him by changing definitions—tell me whether the tension is real, and which reading of the Oedipus effect is the honest one given his overall argument.
