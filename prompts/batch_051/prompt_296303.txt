I retired from physical chemistry a few years back and have been reading my way through those short "big ideas in philosophy" pieces, which leaves me knowing just enough to get suspicious but not enough to check anything. The piece on Grice left me with that exact feeling, and I can't tell weather the problem is the article or my own reading. Its summary of him went roughly like this:

"Grice's big move was to separate what a sentence means from what a speaker does with it. Speaker meaning, on his account, is just the speaker's intention to produce a belief in the hearer. The gap between the two is an implicature: when I say 'It's cold in here' and mean 'please shut the window,' the request is implicated, not said. Implicatures are generated by the Cooperative Principle and its maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner, which speakers normally obey; when a speaker seems to flout one, the hearer reasons to the implied message. Grice also held that implicatures are non-detachable, meaning they don't disappear when you reword what you said, and that they are cancellable, which is the test that separates what is said from what is implicated. The article closed by saying Grice's real lesson is that all communication is implicature, since even the most literal remark depends on the hearer inferring the speaker's intention."

Something about the speaker-meaning definition and the non-detachability claim sets my teeth on edge, and that closing sentence feels like a leap, but I keep going back and forth about which misgivings are real. Go through it nearly clause by clause and tell me which parts a careful Gricean would accept, which they'd call fair simplifications for a general reader, which they'd flatly reject, and whether the closing lesson follows from the parts that are accurate. Assume I haven't read Grice directly and won't be looking anything up — this has to stand on its own.
