Last week I was at a small dinner party where two friends, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument about Bostrom's simulation argument and longtermism. I've been thinking about it ever since and honestly couldn't referee it, so I want a careful point-by-point adjudication.

At one point Dana pulled up Bostrom's paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" on her phone and read the bit where he says: "Therefore, if we don’t think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears." Her claim was that this cuts far deeper than people usually admit. She said that if you accept this move, then the kind of fanaticism longtermists have about the far future — where you sacrifice concrete present benefits for tiny chances of steering a galaxy-scale future — loses a lot of its force. Her reasoning: either we're in a simulation, in which case our actions matter mostly for the simulated world and probably not for the base-civilization far future we were trying to help, or we're in base reality, in which case Bostrom's point means we shouldn't expect that far future to include lots of simulated minds of our kind. So the astronomical expected-value calculations are doubly suspect.

Marcus pushed back. He said she was treating the disjunction as if it told you which horn you were on, but it doesn't. The argument says at least one of three things is true; if you condition on being in base reality, then it tells you to be more skeptical that posthumans will run ancestor-simulations, but it doesn't tell you the far future is small. A base-reality civilization can still have enormous longevity and enormous numbers of descendants without ever running simulations; the only thing it blocks is expecting that those descendants will run lots of such simulations. And even if we're in a simulation, the far future inside the simulation could still be huge, and the fact that it's called a simulation doesn't make it less real as a moral landscape. Dana replied that he was dodging the relevant probability: the longtermist case depends on our being in base reality, at an early stage, able to influence the expansion of life into the cosmos — and the simulation argument pushes against exactly that cluster of assumptions. Marcus then said she was smuggling in the idea that simulated lives count less, or don't count, and that's a huge moral premise she isn't defending. Dana said she wasn't claiming simulated lives count less; she was claiming we have no purchase on them from inside, and the expected-value argument needs purchase. They went around a bit more, and at that point someone changed the topic.

I was left with the sense that both were being selective. Can you go through this exchange and say who's right at each step, what both sides are missing, and who is more right overall about whether the simulation argument dampens far-future fanaticism? Please answer in American English.
