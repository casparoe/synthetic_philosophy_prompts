I am currently taking a course on the epistemology of artificial intelligence, and we have been working through the claim that cognitive offloading to AI systems can erode genuine understanding even when the offloaded answers are correct. I would like to think through how one could actually test this claim rather than merely gesture at it. Suppose two groups of students learn the same unfamiliar subject, but one group is allowed to use a capable language model whenever they wish while the other learns without it. Assume the model's answers and explanations are accurate, so the comparison is not about exposure to falsehoods. Later, both groups are tested with no AI available, and they perform equally well. One interpretation is that the AI group has merely redistributed cognitive labor across a human-tool system without losing anything of importance. A rival interpretation is that the equal performance is an artifact of the test: the test may measure only the ability to produce correct answers on familiar-format questions, and that ability can remain intact while the students' understanding—their grasp of the explanatory and inferential structure of the material—has been eroded or never formed. Design an experiment that would more cleanly separate these two hypotheses. Be specific about the manipulation, the outcome measures, and the pattern of results that would favor each reading. Then say what an apparently clean result would and would not settle: is the residual ambiguity a practical limitation of the experimental design, or does it point to an unclarity in the concept of understanding itself?
