Over the past few months I have been turning over a question that began as a practical worry and has become harder the longer I sit with it: how much risk may one person impose on another without asking first? I find myself drawn to a simple and demanding principle — that it is wrong to expose someone to a risk they never agreed to bear, because it treats their health and safety as a cost the risk-taker may spend for their own convenience. The principle earns its keep in many places at once. It makes sense of why secret medical experiments, dumping industrial waste upstream of a town, drunk driving, and quietly testing an unsafe product on unknowing customers all feel like instances of the same underlying wrong. It also offers a single test: was the person's agreement obtained, or not?

Yet I have an intuition I cannot dissolve. If the principle is read literally, an ordinary day becomes a long sequence of small moral violations. When I drive to the grocery store, I impose some risk of injury on every pedestrian and cyclist I pass, none of whom consented. When I cook a meal for a new guest, there is some probability they have an allergy or intolerance I never thought to ask about. If I attend a small gathering while coming down with something, I expose the room. Almost nobody seeks explicit consent for these things, and it seems not merely impractical but wrong-headed to say that ordinary social life is a standing moral failure. But the standard escape — calling this tacit consent — also strikes me as strained: a pedestrian using a crosswalk has not agreed to be hit; they have only gone about their business.

I am not looking for a winner. I want to see what each side would have to establish before it could change my mind. Make the strongest case for debunking the intuition: that we have simply normalized a vast structure of unconsented risk because it would be unbearable to notice it, and that everyday life is morally worse than we admit. Then make the strongest case for amending or abandoning the principle: perhaps consent is the wrong tool for small, reciprocal, or reasonable risks; perhaps risks below some threshold are not the kind of thing agreement governs; perhaps living among others comes with a standing authorization for the ordinary risks we all impose on one another. Then give your own negotiated verdict — could both the principle and the intuition survive in some form, and at what cost? Please also say how confident you are in that verdict and which point in your argument you are least sure of.
