In my AP Psychology class, my teacher uses one image to settle nearly every debate about why people think, feel, and behave the way they do. She says the human mind is a Swiss Army knife. Evolution, in her telling, assembled it by adding one specialized implement at a time, each shaped for a specific problem in our ancestors' environment: a language implement, a face-recognition implement, a mate-preference implement, a cheater-detection implement. Crucially, she insists that there is no general-purpose blade in the handle, and that this is why the mind should not be compared to a desktop computer running one flexible program. On the strength of this picture, she concludes that when a behavior or pattern of thinking appears in essentially every human culture, we should posit a dedicated, built-in mechanism behind it, and then ask what ancestral problem that mechanism evolved to solve. She used jealousy this way: it is cross-cultural and apparently automatic, so, she argued, it must be a dedicated evolved module rather than a social construction or a byproduct of something else.

I can tell this analogy is carrying a real argument, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. Please audit it. Give the correspondences first: what is supposed to map to the knife itself, the individual implements, the manufacturer, the process of adding a new implement, and the missing general-purpose blade. Then show where the mapping breaks or quietly imports features of a physical tool collection that a mind may not share — for example, the implements in a knife are physically separate, deliberately designed in advance for one foreseen job, and fixed once the knife leaves the factory, whereas evolved systems can be repurposed, can serve several functions at once, and develop through interaction with an environment. For each disanalogy, tell me whether it actually matters for her conclusion or is only a harmless difference. Finally, say whether her conclusion survives the audit or whether the Swiss Army knife image was doing the argumentative work; if the image fails in an important way, describe a better mental picture or a cleaner set of distinctions that could replace it.
