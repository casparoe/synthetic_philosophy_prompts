I spent my working life in earth science and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so bear with me if I'm mangling a standard move. My group has been working through gratitude—what we owe to people who have helped us—and I put together an argument for our next meeting that now bothers me because the conclusion is stronger than I can accept.

(1) If gratitude to a benefactor is a kind of debt owed to a particular person, then how much you owe them should track what that person actually deserves credit for.
(2) What a benefactor deserves credit for is the trying—the intention, the effort, the decision to put themselves at risk—not the outcome, since the outcome is always partly ruled by luck: whether the rescue works, whether the gift actually helps, whether the act misfires through no fault of theirs.
(3) So the person who tries to save you and succeeds, and the person who tries just as hard and fails, should be owed exactly the same gratitude.
(4) But that seems flatly wrong: I would owe far more to the person who actually pulled me out of the river than to someone who tried just as hard but lost me to the current.
(5) Therefore either gratitude tracks outcomes after all, in which case a benefactor's claim on me depends partly on luck, or gratitude isn't really about what the benefactor deserves.

I'd like to keep both (1) and (4), but I suspect I'm trying to have it both ways. Does the argument fail somewhere, and if not, which premise is least costly to give up and what do I lose by dropping it?
