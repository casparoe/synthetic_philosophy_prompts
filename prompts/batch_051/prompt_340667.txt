I'm a novelist, and I've had a rule since my first book: I never go back and re-judge a scene. The first time I read a chapter back and decide 'this is true' or 'this is false,' that verdict is final. My reasoning has always been that a second read is just the same brain certifying the same brain — a comitee of one pretending to be two — so it can't add anything and it mostly feeds my instinct to rewrite everything to death. I always figured this was harmless writer's superstition. But now I'm researching Löb's theorem for a novel about an AI trying to prove its own reasoning is sound, and the formal problem keeps rhyming with my rule: the AI can't establish its own reliability using machinery it has reason to distrust, and re-checking with that same machinery doesn't obviously buy it anything. So here's what I actually need to sort out, for me as much as for the book: is my no-second-read policy defensible, or am I just running the Löbian obstacle as a personal drafting policy? And if it isn't defensible, is there a version of it that keeps the benefit — not rewriting myself into endless second-guessing — without the circularity?
