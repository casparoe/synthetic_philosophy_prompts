I help run a small community discussion series on ideas, and our next session features a guest who will be defending the position most associated with Thomas Szasz: roughly, that what gets called mental illness is best understood as a metaphor or myth rather than as a literal disease, that only bodies can be diseased in the strict sense, that much of what psychiatry treats is conflict, socially troublesome behavior, or trouble in living rather than underlying pathology, and that psychiatric coercion is therefore much harder to justify than is usually assumed. I have read around the topic, but I am not an academic, and I keep noticing that arguments about Szasz turn on exactly what he is being taken to claim, with defenders and critics often talking past one another.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest that do more than ask her to restate the view? I want no generic biography questions. I want at least one question on each of the following, and for each a follow-up keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give, so that the conversation pushes past slogans: (a) what myth or metaphor amounts to here, and whether it denies the reality of the suffering, incapacity, or danger that gets categorized as mental disorder; (b) the objection that the line between a diseased body and a troubled life is too sharp, given that many accepted neurological conditions were long diagnosed by behavior before any lesion was found, and that many physical diagnoses too have blurry and socially shaped boundaries; (c) what the view implies about involuntary commitment and coercion, and whether a Szaszian is committed to non-intervention even when someone is dangerous as a result of delusion; and (d) what a psychiatrist on this view is actually doing when a distressed person asks for help — what, if not an illness, is being treated, and what would make that help legitimate.

Because so much depends on contested readings of Szasz, please have the questions separate what is reasonably settled in the scholarship from what is genuinely disputed — for instance, broad features of what he claimed versus the dispute over whether his view is fundamentally a conceptual claim about disease, a political claim about liberty, or both. Where a question leans on a disputed reading, add a short note under the question saying so rather than presenting that reading as the consensus.
