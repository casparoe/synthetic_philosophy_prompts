I am not a philosopher; I have spent a lot of time around industrial chemicals through my job and have read whatever safety documentation I could find on my own. Something about the way these substances are regulated puzzles me, and I would like someone to unpack what it actually assumes. When a company wants to introduce a new chemical, a new food additive, or a new drug, the default is suspicion: the manufacturer has to arrange the testing and produce the evidence, and until it can demonstrate safety, the substance does not reach the market. A credible-sounding mechanism of harm is usually enough to keep a novel product off the shelves until the company can rule it out. But when a substance has already been in wide use for decades—a solvent, a pesticide, or a building material introduced before modern testing rules existed—the default seems to flip. Such substances are grandfathered in, often remain on the market even though no one has shown them to be safe by today's standards, and the people who claim to be harmed by them are usually the ones who must gather evidence and prove the harm, as if the substance were innocent until shown guilty.

The result is a striking asymmetry: a molecule introduced next year and an otherwise similar molecule introduced in the 1960s can face opposite starting assumptions even when our actual knowledge of the two is comparably thin. I want to know what theory of risk and uncertainty this pattern presupposes. Does it presuppose that long-standing use is itself a form of evidence of safety? Does it presuppose that people should bear the burden when they seek to disturb an established arrangement, while newcomers bear it when they seek to create one? Does it depend on a distinction between known hazards and mere uncertainties, mapped somehow onto old and new substances? Or is the whole thing a political compromise with no consistent underlying view at all? And if a country decided to apply a symmetrical precautionary standard—treating an unknown hazard the same way whether the substance is new or old—what would actually have to change in how chemicals, drugs, and building materials are handled?
