Consider five candidate accounts of how slurs derogate their targets. (1) Truth-conditional: a slur's pejorative content is part of its literal truth conditions, so a sentence such as "Smith is a K" asserts both that Smith falls under the group term and that Smith is contemptible on that ground. (2) Presuppositional: the slur's pejorative content is a presupposition, which projects through negation and question formation. (3) Conventional implicature: the slur contributes non-at-issue pejorative content alongside a neutral at-issue classification, where the at-issue content is the sentence's main asserted point. (4) Expressivist: the slur semantically expresses a negative attitude rather than asserting any pejorative proposition. (5) Prohibitionist: the slur is offensive because using it violates a standing social prohibition; semantically it is just a synonym for its neutral counterpart.

Now require any adequate account to explain two data points together. (A) Reclaimed in-group use: a member of the targeted group can say "we are proud to be Ks" without derogating or demeaning anyone. (B) Stubborn out-group offense: an outsider who says, with apparent sincerity, "I do not mean anything bad by K, and I explicitly deny the contempt the word usually carries" still causes offense. Rate each account from 0 to 10 for how well it jointly explains (A) and (B) without ad hoc epicycles, rank the five accounts from best to worst, and give one sentence of justification per rating. Finish by stating which account, if any, genuinely earns its fit to these data rather than buying it with special assumptions.
