Me and my friend have been going back and forth on something from my econ class, where we read a bit of the preface to Bernard Mandeville's The Fable of the Bees. The part I can't get out of my head is where he says: "that what renders him a sociable animal, consists not in his desire of company, good nature, pity, affability, and other graces of a fair outside; but that his vilest and most hateful qualities are the most necessary accomplishments to fit him for the largest, and, according to the world, the happiest and most flourishing societies." My teacher connected it to the whole "greed is an engine of prosperity" thing and then moved on, like it was obviously settled one way or the other. But to me it sounds like Mandeville is mixing a conceptual move (he's basically redefining what should count as a vice versus plain self-interest) with an empirical claim (societies that allow or channel greed actually get richer and more powerful).

So here's what I want to know: if you wanted to actually test the empirical part of that claim about greed or acquisitiveness, how would you do it? Like, what would the closest thing to a real test or natural experiment look like, what data would you compare, what result would count as supporting Mandeville, and what result would count against him? And then, say the test came out in his favor — what would it still not settle? I'm thinking it definately wouldn't settle whether greed is still morally a vice, or whether the prosperity was worth it, or whether the result is really about "vice" at all rather than just ordinary self-interest under decent institutions.
