I have been studying how algorithmic risk scores are used in decisions such as pretrial release and loan approval, and I am caught between a compelling theory and a firm intuition. The theory is that fairness is a matter of error rates: a fair algorithm should have the same false positive rate and the same false negative rate for every group. That standard is clean, checkable, and tied directly to the harms a mistaken decision actually causes. My intuition is that fairness also requires predictive parity: when the algorithm labels two people high risk, that label should be equally trustworthy regardless of their group, because a label that is right more often for one group than for another seems to amount to different standards for different groups. The problem is that these two demands are, in the standard formalization, mathematically incompatible in ordinary cases: when groups have different base rates, a classifier cannot generally satisfy both. I find that result impossible to accept as a last word, because fairness should not become unreachable the moment a dataset is realistic; it seems more plausible that one of the formal requirements was misstated. I want a negotiated verdict rather than a side. Please make the strongest case for abandoning predictive parity, the strongest case for abandoning error-rate equality, and the strongest case for abandoning my assumption that a fair classifier must satisfy both at once, and say what each move would cost; if there is a reading on which they are compatible after all, I want that spelled out as well.
