I'm taking a course that touches on both moral luck and epistemic luck, and I keep noticing a structural parallel I can't decide about. In the resultant moral luck debate, the standard case is two drunk drivers with identical recklessness: one hits and kills someone, the other is pulled over before anything happens, and the first is blamed far more. In Gettier cases in epistemology, someone has a justified true belief, but the belief is true only because the world happened to cooperate in a way unconnected with their justification, so we deny that they know. Both seem to involve a verdict that swings on something outside the agent's control — the harmful outcome in the moral case, whether the belief ends up true in the epistemic case — rather than on the agent's internal merits. But there is also an obvious asymmetry: the Gettier verdict is uncontroversial, whereas it is hotly contested whether resultant-luck differences in blame are appropriate at all. Is this parallel deep or superficial? Specifically, is the anti-luck condition that epistemologists add to justified true belief the same kind of thing as the control condition that moral philosophers use to criticize resultant-luck verdicts, or does the analogy fall apart when we ask what each field is trying to capture? It would help me if you stated the mapping between the two cases, said what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one debate to the other, and identified a prediction the analogy makes that fails. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested in each of these debates, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
