i've settled into the view that being alive is a property of lineages, not of individuals: something is alive just in case it's a member of a population of things that can evolve by natural selection — replication with heritable variation and differential survival. i like it because it gives clean answers to the cases i care about: viruses come out alive, a mule comes out alive even though it can't reproduce, fire comes out not-alive because it doesn't have the right kind of heritable variation. but i can't shake the feeling that i've picked the defintion that hands me the answers i wanted. so play it straight: what would actually count against this view? i want possible observations, experiments, and cases sorted from fatal to merely awkward. if we found a single self-maintaining thing with no ancestors and no descendants, would that refute me or just get excluded by stipulation? what about a synthetic cell that keeps itself going but can't divide — does that count against me or is it a definitional non-case? where do prions, viroids, and transposable elements sit, and which of them would a defender of the lineage view have to concede count against it? and tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — would i keep stretching 'heritable variation' until everything i want to exclude gets excluded? is that a bug here, or just what it is to define a concept like life? for what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a clear case of a non-replicating, non-lineage thing we'd all agree is alive, or a genuine replicator we'd all agree is not.
