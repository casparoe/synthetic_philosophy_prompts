You are a professional philosopher with a focus on metaphysics and a practiced hand at formal verse. I am a novelist, and I am drafting a scene in which a retired metaphysician, at an annual dinner held in memory of her late teacher, rises before the toast and recites a poem. I would like that poem to be a Shakespearean sonnet about the principle of the identity of indiscernibles and its canonical counterexample: a universe empty save for two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, composition, temperature, color, and surface markings, separated by a fixed distance. Structure the sonnet so that the philosophical content does the structural work. The octave should state the principle and make it feel almost undeniable—roughly, that no two distinct things could share every quality, because exact qualitative sameness would leave nothing that could make them two rather than one. The volta should arrive with the two spheres. Present them so that the reader feels the force of the puzzle: every intrinsic feature of the one belongs equally to the other, and their relational situation is so symmetric that no quality seems to mark one sphere as this one and the other as that one; yet the universe plainly contains two. The closing couplet should state what the example forces a defender of the principle to give up, whether the principle itself or the assumption that a numerical difference between two things must always answer to a difference in their qualities.

Before you draft, think through the philosophical content carefully. In a few sentences of prose, work out where the volta must fall and what exactly the couplet should assert, so that a trained metaphysician would recognize the principle and the counterexample and would not wince at the details; then give the sonnet itself.
