In my advanced seminar on the concept of the sacred, I have repeatedly failed to give my students a definition of sacrilege that survives past the first hard case. I would like you to propose a working definition of the term—not a dictionary gloss but a criterion that tracks distinctions worth drawing.

Consider these cases:

(a) A tourist carves his initials into the stonework of a medieval cathedral.
(b) A protester burns a national flag during a demonstration.
(c) A research team legally edits the genome of a species that a minority religious community regards as a living manifestation of the divine, without consulting the community.
(d) A comedian performs a knowingly offensive monologue in which he calls God a fiction.
(e) A person tears pages out of a holy book and uses them as rolling papers while alone at home.
(f) A museum exhibits a photograph of a crucifix submerged in urine, with the artist insisting the work is a comment on the commercialization of religion.
(g) An engineering firm drains a lake that an indigenous community holds to be the dwelling place of an ancestor-spirit, in order to build a road.

My own views are firm only at the extremes: (a) and (e) are sacrilege; (b) and (d) are not, though they may be offense of an adjacent kind, and (d) is probably better called blasphemy. I have no settled view on (c), (f), and (g). A good definition should also explain why sacrilege is not the same as causing offense: an act can be sacrilegious with no believer present to be offended, and an act can deeply offend believers without being sacrilege. Please also say whether the concept can coherently extend beyond religion to national, natural, or cultural forms of sacredness, or whether doing so stretches it past its use.

Structure your answer as follows: (1) Proposed definition, one or two sentences, with the criterion made explicit (roughly 150 words). (2) Application: run the definition through all seven cases and report what it delivers in each (roughly 400 words). (3) Breaking points: identify where the definition gives the wrong answer, no answer, or a verdict you would not endorse before hearing the case (roughly 200 words). (4) Revised definition and defense: restate the definition in light of the breaking points and defend any verdicts that remain surprising (roughly 250 words).
