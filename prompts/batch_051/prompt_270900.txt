I run a quarterly seminar for machine-learning engineers and data scientists who build and maintain anomaly-detection systems in fraud, manufacturing, and IT operations. They are fluent in density estimation, autoencoders, one-class classification, and incident triage, and they have a working familiarity with directed acyclic graphs and the do-operator, but they have never been asked to say when two flagged anomalies should count as instances of the same underlying mechanism. Next month I have seventy-five minutes, and the goal is to move them from an operational criterion — an anomaly is whatever the model flags — to a defensible criterion for grouping anomalies by mechanism, one that could guide fixes and forecast recurrences. Plan how I should teach this; do not give me the explanation itself. In your plan, specify: (1) the order in which the component ideas must arrive and the dependency relation between them (for example, statistical deviation, mechanism as an invariant causal structure, intervention, the grain or level at which a mechanism is individuated, and sameness of mechanism); (2) the single concrete running case I should carry through the whole session, what it must make possible at each stage, and what it must not inadvertently suggest; (3) the misconceptions I should expect from this audience, and where in the sequence each should be headed off — I anticipate at least these: that similarity in feature space, a shared root-cause label in the incident tracker, or co-occurrence in time amounts to sameness of mechanism, and that a latent representation that separates anomalies is already carving nature at its mechanistic joints; (4) what I should explicitly bracket or refuse to settle so the session does not collapse into the advice 'just add a causal graph to the model'; and (5) a closing question or exercise whose answer would distinguish someone who has learned to say 'mechanism' from someone who has understood why the anomaly detector's outputs underdetermine which anomalies share a mechanism.
