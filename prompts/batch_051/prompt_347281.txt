I run a fortnightly reading group for bright non-specialists — no formal logic, but they read LessWrong and enjoy arguments where a harmless-looking premise detonates. Next session I have one hour and a whiteboard to teach Fitch's paradox of knowability: the derivation that the principle that every truth is knowable, where knowable means some possible agent could know it at some time, collapses into the principle that every truth is known. Do not give me the explanation itself; plan how I should deliver it. I want the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what: knowledge as factive, knowledge distributing over conjunction, the distinction between knowable and known, and the construction of the conjunction of an arbitrary proposition p with the claim that no one will ever know p. Give me the one concrete carrying example that does the real work, and say what it must have and lack; in particular, say why it should not lean on self-reference or be mistaken for a Moore-paradox sentence. Then map the misconceptions I should expect — I suspect at least the worry that this is the Liar in disguise and the thought that the argument must somewhere assume omniscience — and say exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. End with one question I can put to the group to check whether they have actually got it rather than merely nodded along. Finally, say what you would deliberately leave out at this level and why. Throughout, separate what is settled — for instance, the validity of the core derivation under the stated assumptions and classical propositional logic — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether the paradox refutes anti-realism or merely forces restriction of the knowability principle; do not present a contested reading as the consensus. British English, please.
