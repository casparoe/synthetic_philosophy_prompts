A working definition: a cultural item—a language, a tradition, a practice, an art form—has intrinsic value just in case its value does not reduce to the welfare or preference-satisfaction of the people currently associated with it. Suppose, for the purposes of this exercise, we accept four criteria for such value: (1) transmission, meaning the item has been handed down across generations rather than assembled recently; (2) distinctive content, meaning it encodes a way of organizing experience or a range of expressive possibilities not duplicated elsewhere; (3) irreplaceability, meaning its loss could not be made good later by reconstruction from documents; and (4) final valuing, meaning at least some current bearers treat it as an end and not only as a means to income, recognition, or prestige.

Now consider the following case. A language has exactly two remaining fluent speakers. They ordinarily converse in the regional majority language and turn to the ancestral language only when a linguist is present, or during an annual commemoration whose words they can render but no longer fully understand. The language is comprehensively documented: grammars, a dictionary, and several hundred hours of recorded speech are held in an archive. Local authorities propose a revival program, but the only young people who express interest say they want to learn it because official minority status would bring school and land benefits; the two elders likewise say they are keeping the language alive mainly to help their grandchildren secure those benefits.

Does this language still qualify as an intrinsic cultural good under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, flagging any criterion that underdetermines the verdict. Then say whether, in your view, the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision, and whether whatever value the language has is better captured by the idea that cultural goods can matter beyond the people who currently hold them.
