I've been chewing on this for months and want a professional philosopher to help me see if there's a real pattern. I don't know any of the technical words, so I'll just lay out my gut calls on a bunch of cases. Please think carefully and maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate principles before you commit; I'd rather you tell me which of my gut calls a clean theory has to drop than give me a loophole-y theory that saves every one of them.

Case 1. When I was little I thought the morning star and the evening star were two different stars. Turns out they're both Venus, so the "two" were never two. Once I knew that, I felt like it couldn't have been otherwise — but you couldn't know it just by sitting and thinking; somebody had to go look at the sky. So: something had to be true, but we needed eyes to find out. That feels right to me.

Case 2. I used to think water was just whatever falls from clouds and quenches thirst. Now I know it's H2O. If some alien liquid was clear and drinkable and fell from clouds but wasn't H2O, I'd say it's not water, just a very good water-imposter. So water being H2O also feels like it had to be true, again only knowable by doing chemistry.

Case 3. Suppose I discovered I'd mixed up most of the famous facts about Napoleon — the battles, the exile, all of it attached to the wrong person in my head. I wouldn't say Napoleon turned out to be someone else; I'd say I had wrong stories about Napoleon. The name stays stuck to the actual guy even when my descriptions fail. But if I use a phrase like "the general who lost at Waterloo," and it turned out some other general lost that battle, I'd say the phrase was about that other general all along. So names and descriptions don't work the same way. Right?

Case 4. I read once that the meter used to be defined by this exact metal bar in a vault in France. If the bar is the definition of a meter, then "that bar is one meter long" is true just by the rules of the game, no tape measure needed. But I can totally imagine the very same bar being a bit shorter. So here's one that's certain without checking, yet could have been otherwise. I cant fit that with the first two.

Case 5. Here's my wrench. Diamonds and graphite are both pure carbon — same element, just arranged differently. I'd never say a diamond is graphite. But then why do I insist that anything water-like has to be H2O? If the word "water" tracks hidden chemical structure, why don't diamonds and graphite collapse into one thing? Unless a kind word tracks something finer than just the formula, and then I'd like to know what.

My rough guess is this: names and substance words are more like tags that stay glued to one thing or one kind, even when the world could have been different. Descriptions are more like job listings that pick whoever fills them. And some tag-truths can't be false but still have to be discovered by looking. The meter bar seems like the exception, though.

So: is there one principle that handles all of these, or close? If my rough guess is in the neighborhood, fix it where it breaks. Tell me which of my gut calls the cleanest version has to abandon, and what the principle you settle on would say about a case I keep going back and forth on. Imagine another planet where the lakes and rain are made of a different molecule — not H2O — but the stuff looks and behaves exactly like our water. Do the people there have water or not? And if not, are they wrong when they say "the water is cold"? Dont tell me it's all just words; I want to know which of my instincts I should trust and which I should give up.
