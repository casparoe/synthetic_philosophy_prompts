You are a grader for an undergraduate political philosophy course. You will be given an exam question and one student's answer. Assign a grade on a 10-point scale (whole or half points). Along with the grade, provide the point-by-point written comments that justify it: work through the answer claim by claim, note what is correct and what is not, and explain how those findings determine the grade. The assessment should itemize rather than rest on a general impression. Do not ask the student for citations or suggest further readings. Write the assessment in American English.

Exam question: Explain the two principal restrictions Locke places on acquiring property by mixing one's labor with unowned things, and say how the two restrictions differ. Then apply the relevant restriction or restrictions to this case: in an arid region, a settler fences off the only spring, builds cisterns, and sells the water to other settlers who have no alternative supply. Does the settler's title to the spring satisfy Locke's restrictions on acquisition? Defend your answer.

Student's answer: Locke's labor theory begins from self-ownership: each person owns their own body and therefore owns the labor their body performs. When someone mixes their labor with an unowned thing, the thing becomes theirs, because the labor was already theirs and is joined to the object. Two restrictions limit this acquisition. First, the spoilage restriction: a person may take only what they can use before it perishes, since taking what will rot in one's possession needlessly withholds it from others. Second, the Lockean proviso: appropriation is legitimate only while enough and as good remains in common for others. The two differ in that the spoilage restriction concerns waste of what one has already acquired, whereas the proviso concerns the sufficiency of what remains for others after acquisition. With the invention of money, the spoilage restriction drops out in practice, because durable metal can be accumulated without perishing; this, for Locke, explains how legitimate inequalities of wealth arise.

In the spring case, the settler's title fails the proviso. The relevant test, on Locke's view, is whether enclosing the spring leaves the other settlers worse off than they would have been had the spring remained in common. Since they must now buy water from him and have no alternative source, they are worse off under that baseline, so the appropriation violates the proviso and the settler's title is unjust. Had there been other equally good springs left unenclosed, the appropriation would have left enough and as good for others, and the title would have been legitimate.

Grade this answer on a 10-point scale and write the point-by-point comments that justify the grade.
