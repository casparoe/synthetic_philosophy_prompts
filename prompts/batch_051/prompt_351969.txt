I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I keep returning to a confusion that I think is under-taught in those circles: what it actually costs to give up bivalence. The local orthodoxy is broadly Bayesian and deflationary about truth, and people will casually say that a statement is "neither true nor false" — a vague one, a self-referential one, one with a failed presupposition — without noticing that they have moved from a claim about degrees of belief to a claim about what there is for a belief to be about, and without noticing the bill that comes due.

I have to lead a 90-minute session for a rationalist-adjacent reading group: 10 to 15 people, mostly quantitative, some programming, no formal training in philosophy of language or non-classical logic. They know modus ponens and Boolean semantics, and "probabilities over propositions" is their default picture. I want to teach them the distinction between bivalence (every proposition is either true or false) and the law of excluded middle (P or not-P is a logical truth), and I want them to understand why a person might deny the first while keeping the second. The session should make them feel the pull of truth-value gaps, and then make them feel the price.

Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it. The deliverable is a teaching plan, not a lecture script.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths shown:

- "The arc" (~300 words): the dependency structure of the session — what has to be in place before what, in what order, and why that order is forced rather than optional. The session as a whole should build toward the revenge problem for gappy semantics — roughly, the problem that once you admit "neither true nor false" as a status, your own semantic claims (or an operator such as "it is not determinately true") seem to generate a new gap at the next level up. The audience should only meet that problem after they have already committed themselves to gaps.
- "The carrying example" (~250 words): the single running case that carries the whole session. I assume borderline cases of a vague predicate will do the work — a heap, a color chip, "tall" for a particular person — but tell me which one, and what features it must have and must lack. It should not let the audience write the case off as mere ignorance or measurement error. If there is a better non-vague entry point, argue for it.
- "Misconception map" (~300 words): the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, in the order they will raise them — I expect the first is "this is just subjective uncertainty, a probability strictly between 0 and 1" and the second is "a truth-value gap is just a third truth value" — and where in the sequence each should be headed off, and how, and which ones to leave partly unaddressed so the revenge problem can do its work.
- "Check for understanding" (~150 words): one question for the final ten minutes that would show whether they have understood the gap theorist's burden, as opposed to merely acquiring new vocabulary. It should not be a reading check; it should be a question someone could answer well only if they had actually tracked the distinction between syntax and semantics.
- "What to leave out" (~100 words): what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I have in mind dialetheism and gluts, intuitionistic semantics, and the full supervaluationist machinery — and a sentence I can say when someone pushes past the line.

One further constraint: this group has read a great deal about maps and territories and about dissolving questions as "fake frameworks." Use that idiom where it helps, but do not let the session end in "truth is just a map and everything is a matter of definition," which I expect this group will find tempting. The plan should anticipate that move and make it feel insufficient by the end.
