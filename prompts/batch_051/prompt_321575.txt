I find myself drawn to a view about the limits of experience, and it clashes with an intuition I cannot shake. The view is this: the space of possible colour experience is not an open palette but a structure imposed by the opponent-process architecture of the visual system. Colour channels are organized into mutually inhibitory pairs — red against green, blue against yellow, light against dark — and a colour experience is a state of this structure rather than a free combination of independent hue ingredients. On this picture the forbidden colours, chief among them a reddish green, are not merely hard to produce; they are excluded by the machinery that constitutes colour experience, much as a Euclidean triangle cannot have four sides. The view has systematic virtues: it explains complementary afterimages, why some hues such as red and green feel uncompounded while others such as orange feel like mixtures, and why nobody reports a reddish green under normal viewing. It also yields a tidy criterion: a colour experience is possible for a system with this architecture only if it can be realized as a state of the opponent channels, since those channels are not incidental correlates of colour but the structure in virtue of which colour experience has the character it does.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that a sufficiently precise brain interface could give me a colour I have never had. If a device could stimulate the red-green opponent pair in a pattern ordinary vision never produces — bypassing the mutual inhibition, driving the relevant populations with an unnatural timing, or exploiting plasticity until a new opponent axis forms — why should the space remain closed? The particular channels evolution gave me look like a contingent fact about my wiring, not a boundary on the possible, and it seems as though there is something the interface would be doing: producing an experience I could only describe as a new colour, perhaps a reddish green, perhaps something with no place in the familiar space of qualia at all.

I want to know which should yield. Give me the strongest version of each side and a negotiated verdict. Is the intuition merely an artefact of how imagination works — the way we can picture novel colours only by covertly recombining familiar ones, so that the act of imagining a reddish green secretly uses the two components whose combination the theory rules out? Or is it tracking a gap in the theory: that opponent-process constraints describe one biological implementation and remain silent about what a sufficiently modified, augmented, or differently organized system could undergo? Or is the dispute shallower than it looks once we draw distinctions — between a new hue and a new colour experience, between what is possible with current wiring and what is possible after intervention, and between either of these and what is metaphysically possible? Tell me what would justify each resolution — debunking the intuition, amending the theory, or showing the two are compatible — and which resolution you would defend, and why.
