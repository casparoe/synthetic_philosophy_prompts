I was rereading the part of Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding where he says that animals expect the usual thing after a familiar object just from habit, and that human causal reasoning is essentially a fancier version of that same habitual transfer. I want you to use that bit to make an argument about Goodman's grue problem, but I dont want the exegesis. Suppose someone trains two dogs. Dog A gets fed every time a screen turns blue. Dog B gets fed every time the screen turns blue before next Tuesday at noon and green after that; until next Tuesday the two dogs have had exactly the same experiences of the screen, the same timing, the same food. At the moment before Tuesday noon, both dogs see the screen turn blue. Hume's animal-custom story would say each dog now feels a similar expectation because each has been habituated in the same way. Make the strongest argument you can that this is false: that the two dogs do not, in fact, have the same custom, because the environment that shaped their habits was governed by different regularities, and that a purely dispositional reading of custom can distinguish them in a way a purely formal green-versus-grue induction cannot. Then tell me whether that defense of Hume actually solves Goodman's problem or just relocates it to a claim about environments.
