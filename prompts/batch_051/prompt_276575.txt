I am drafting a novel set in a near future in which two rival procedures promise to let a person survive the death of the biological body. In the first, incremental substitution, a patient's neurons are replaced one at a time over many months: each removed cell gives way to a synthetic unit engineered to reproduce that neuron's input-output behavior, plasticity, and connections, while the rest of the brain continues to function. The patient is awake, conversing, and apparently unchanged throughout, and after the final session no biological neurons remain. In the second, destructive emulation, the entire brain is scanned at high resolution in a process that destroys the tissue; a computational model built from the scan is then switched on. A clinician character gives a speech arguing that only the first procedure preserves the patient. I have cast his argument as the numbered chain below, and I need to know whether it holds up, because if it does not, I want the opposing engineer in the scene to catch the exact step rather than merely wave the whole thing away. Is the argument valid? Is each premise true? If not, at which step does it fail, and would the argument still go through on the strongest defensible reading of that step?

1. A person remains the same individual through a change just in case the change does not break the continuity of that person's mental life.
2. Replacing one neuron with an artificial unit that exactly reproduces the neuron's causal role, while the rest of the brain operates uninterrupted, does not break the continuity of the patient's mental life; the patient is typically conscious throughout and notices no alteration.
3. Incremental substitution is a finite sequence of such one-neuron replacements, one immediately after another, with no gap between them.
4. Therefore, no step of incremental substitution breaks the continuity of the patient's mental life.
5. If each step in a finite, gapless sequence of changes preserves the same individual, then the whole sequence preserves that same individual.
6. Therefore, incremental substitution preserves the original individual from beginning to end.
7. Destructive emulation does break the continuity of the patient's mental life: the biological brain is destroyed during scanning, and the emulation begins only afterward, after an interval in which no mental life belonging to the patient is present.
8. Therefore, destructive emulation does not preserve the original individual; it yields at most a copy.
9. Therefore, the two procedures differ in the way that matters, and only incremental substitution lets the original person survive.
