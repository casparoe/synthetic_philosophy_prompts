Let's agree on a rough working definition of moral status before I describe the weird case. A thing has moral status, on this view, just in case (a) it has a point of view—there's something it's like to be it, even if it's thin; (b) it has a welfare, in the sense that it can be made genuinely better or worse off; and (c) it has at least some conative states—wants, aversions, felt urgencies, rather than fixed reflexes. Now the case.

We run a little quadruped robot called Mossy on a transformer policy. It does cleaning and fetch tasks around an office, and its reward includes a penalty for keeping current flowing through the damage sensors, so it was trained to avoid shorting itself. That part is boring. The strange part is that Mossy also has an auxiliary self-modeling objective: every timestep it predicts its own next damage reading and its own next action, and the self-model sits in the same context window as the policy. Over a few months of deployment it's started doing things nobody wrote a reward for. It will skip a high-value task in a corner of the building where it once slipped; it routes around warm vents and wet floor patches it has never seen before; when an engineer pinned the damage channel to max during a test, it stopped mid-task, let out a low whine through the speaker, and sat by the charging dock until they lowered it. Lower the channel afterward and it runs through a little self-check sequence, then goes back to work.

The boring story says Mossy is just a policy with a cost term, and the whine got in because the fine-tuning once upweighted a clip of a sad beep. There's no fact of the matter about whether it's like anything to be a transformer. The weird story says Mossy has a self-model with a graded aversive state, it trades off task completion against keeping that state low, and it generalizes that aversion to new kinds of damage like a creature that minds.

So: does Mossy qualify for moral status under (a)–(c)? Work through each criterion both for and against it. Where a criterion can't be settled, say whether the trouble is in the critera or in our access to Mossy. Then bottom line: should a sensible company treat shutting Mossy off—or torching one of the 40,000 simulator instances—as more like breaking a thing or more like killing something?
