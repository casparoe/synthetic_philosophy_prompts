I work on a team that builds a detailed agent-based simulation of our company's distribution network. The agents follow a small set of rules we wrote ourselves, so the rules and the starting conditions are completely known to us. And yet, whenever management asks what will happen under a proposed change, the only reliable way we have found to answer is to run the whole simulation forward and watch it unfold. A new colleague has been pressing me on this. He argues that our inability to jump straight to the outcome is just a symptom of incomplete understanding: if we genuinely understood the rules and the initial state, we should be able to skip the step-by-step run and produce the result directly, or at least give a much shorter derivation of it. In his view, having to run the thing is an admission that we don't really know how the system works. My instinct is that he is wrong, but I can't articulate why. Is there a principled reason that a system whose rules we know completely could still resist any shortcut, so that running it is not a confession of ignorance but the only way to find out what it does? Or is my colleague right that this simply reveals a gap in our understanding? Please give me your considered verdict, and then tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most plausibly push you to the opposite conclusion, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
