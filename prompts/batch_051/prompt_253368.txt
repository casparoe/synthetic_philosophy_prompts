For the past several years I have been one of five trustees of a small charitable trust established by my great-aunt, who died before I joined the board. The founding deed states that the income is to be used to relieve poverty among elderly people in our county, and it can be amended only by a three-quarters vote of the trustees. We have lately been discussing whether to apply for perpetual status so that the trust could continue indefinitely, and the prospect has made me anxious in a way I find difficult to make precise. It is obvious to me that some changes of purpose are healthy: the elderly population of the county has changed, the state now does some things that charities used to do, and we have learned over the years what kind of help actually reaches people. But it is also obvious, or feels obvious, that a board could move the trust by small steps, each plausible in its own right, until it is funding something my great-aunt would have thought wrong, and the connection to her intentions is no longer recognisable. I am not trained in philosophy or law, and I keep going back and forth between two flat rules, one that would freeze the trust forever and one that would let any well-meaning board rewrite it. Neither can be right. I want something the board could genuinely use: a reusable decision procedure for proposed amendments to the trust's purposes, with the questions we should ask in order, what should count as a satisfactory answer to each, where the procedure should stop with a no, and where it should hand the decision back to the trustees because the matter is one of judgement rather than method. As far as possible, ground it in what is actually understood about how people's values change, why some commitments remain stable, and how to distinguish learning something new from replacing one value with another.

Then run the procedure on two amendments we have actually discussed. In the first, the trust would extend its purposes to relieve poverty among disabled adults of working age in the same county, on the grounds that my great-aunt's underlying concern was with people whom the welfare state leaves without adequate support, and that the elderly are just one such group. In the second, after a prolonged shortfall in income, we would merge with a much larger national charity, keep the trust's name attached to a smaller fund, and direct one third of the income to that charity's overseas relief work, on the grounds that the trust otherwise risks becoming too small to do anything worthwhile. I suspect several of us will support the second for financial reasons, so the procedure should be capable of telling us something we do not want to hear. Please be clear, as you go, about which parts of the procedure rest on settled findings and which rest on genuinely contested claims, and please do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write the whole response as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter to a fellow trustee: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
