I have been turning over an argument from a seminar on vagueness, and I cannot tell whether it is a proof of something deep or a reductio of its own premises. It runs as follows.

Fix a canonical setup: grains of sand poured into a cone on a table. The argument is meant to show that "heap" has a sharp cutoff, and then to generalize that result to every vague word that matters.

(1) Every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false.
(2) For every natural number n, the sentence "A conical pile of n grains makes a heap" is meaningful, and its truth value, if it has one, is settled by the pile and the meaning of the word.
(3) So for every n, the sentence is either true or false.
(4) A one-grain pile is not a heap, and a million-grain pile is a heap; moreover, adding one grain to a conical pile never turns a heap into a non-heap.
(5) From (3) and (4), there is a least number k such that a k-grain pile is a heap; hence k grains make a heap while k−1 grains do not.
(6) So "heap" has a sharp boundary: one grain separates the last non-heap from the first heap.
(7) But no competent speaker can identify k, and no possible investigation of usage, psychology, or the pile itself could discriminate k from k−1 or k+1; the evidence underdetermines every candidate boundary.
(8) Therefore there is a determinate boundary in the semantics of "heap" that no fact of use fixes and no evidence could reveal.
(9) The same pattern recurs for "bald," "child," "alive," "safe," "permissible," and "proof": a last hair, a last second, a last weakening, a last part per billion, a last dollar of cost, a last inferential step — each fixed but undiscoverable in principle.
(10) So wherever law, medicine, or morals deploys a vague term, some verdicts are made true by hidden thresholds that no one could ever know.

That conclusion is meant to recommend epistemicism about vagueness, the view that vague predicates have sharp, unknowable boundaries. But it also looks like a reductio of premise (1), or of the step from (6) to (8): it proves too much. I find (1) through (7) hard to deny and (10) hard to accept, and I suspect that is only a preference.

Diagnose the argument. If a specific step fails, name it and explain why it looked compulsory; if the reasoning is valid, say which premise is least costly to give up and what surrendering it costs. In doing so, set out the strongest version of each rival diagnosis: the epistemicist's, which accepts the hidden boundaries as an epistemic limit, and the supervaluationist's, which holds that a vague sentence is true only if it holds on every admissible sharpening of the predicate, false only if it fails on every admissible sharpening, and neither otherwise — rejecting bivalence for borderline cases while preserving classical logic's theorems. Which diagnosis pays the lower price, or is there a third that undercuts the choice?
