I'm a philosophy professor, and for a graduate seminar on divine command theory and its critics I've been reconstructing an argument I once heard about the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite dispute over the status of moral value. I find myself unable to decide whether it is a sound routing of one side of the debate or whether it proves too much. Here it is as I currently have it.

(1) The Ash'arite position, as I understand it, holds that an act's being just or obligatory is constituted by God's commanding it: the command is metaphysically prior, and there is no normatively relevant feature of the act that obtains before or independently of the command. (I set aside the weaker, merely epistemological reading that finite reason cannot discover good and evil without revelation; the premise here is the constitutive one.)

(2) If (1) is right, then for any act A, had God commanded A, A would have been just and obligatory; the act itself contributes no standard by which the command could be assessed as correct or incorrect.

(3) The Ash'arite wants to accept exactly this, since it preserves divine sovereignty: nothing outside God's will obliges or constrains Him.

(4) But then the sentence "God commands only what is just" reduces to the tautology "God commands only what God commands," and God's justice becomes an honorific with no descriptive content.

(5) A predicate that is merely honorific in this way cannot carry the weight theists put on it: it cannot explain why God merits obedience, as opposed to our having merely prudential reasons to comply with power.

(6) Yet a central conviction in the tradition — shared by the two schools in their different ways — is that God is just (ʿadl) in a substantive sense that licenses obedience and marks the difference between God and a tyrant.

(7) Therefore, Ash'arism cannot accommodate the conviction expressed in (6); the only way to keep a substantive divine justice is the Mu'tazilite alternative, on which acts have good- and bad-making features accessible to reason and God commands the good because it is good.

The conclusion seems to me too strong — it would make Ash'arism, a school that shaped so much of the subsequent tradition, the victim of a three-minute Euthyphro, and I doubt the dispute is that one-sided. But I cannot yet name the step I am most prepared to surrender. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and why is it less costly than giving up (6)?
