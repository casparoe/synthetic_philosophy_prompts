I want to press a conflict that has come to organize my thinking about mourning and simulation. I hold, and teach, a broadly continuity view of grief: mourning is not the work of detaching from the dead but of renegotiating a relation to them, and much of what the bereaved do with keepsakes, letters, and recordings is exactly that work. The view is theoretically attractive because it unifies a wide range of practices under one description, avoids positing any special wrong-making property peculiar to simulation, and predicts that a griefbot—a conversational model trained on a deceased person's texts and voice—is objectionable only insofar as it deceives, manipulates, or predictably harms.

Yet I cannot shake a firm intuition. When I imagine a child talking each evening with a simulation of a dead parent, or a widow asking a reconstruction of her husband whether she should remarry, the practice does not feel continuous with rereading letters. There seems to be something different about a responsive artifact: it invites the bereaved to treat absence as reversible, supplies an interlocutor who never challenges, withdraws, or imposes limits, and converts the dead person into a standing resource for the living. I find myself believing that part of mourning is taking on the absence of the dead, and that a griefbot is not a new way of doing that but a way of declining to do it.

Present both sides at their strongest and offer a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be debunked as novelty aversion or the work of the uncanny valley? Should the continuity view be amended—for example, by distinguishing memorial media from substitutive media, by locating the wrong in modeling the dead without their actual consent, or by denying that a simulation can bear the normative weight of a relationship? Or should one side simply be given up? Do not merely pick a side: say what would justify each move—debunking, amending, abandoning, reconciling. And state which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is least secure.
