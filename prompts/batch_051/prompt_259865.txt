Write a short story that treats the idea of a "free-floating rationale" as a concrete feature of its world: a reason, justification, or argument that continues to do normative work after the person or persons who originally held it are gone, and perhaps after no living mind any longer fully understands or endorses it. The story should not merely illustrate the idea but place it under pressure. Ask, through the fiction, whether such a reason remains a genuine reason once no one stands behind it, or whether it has become something else — a rule, a relic, a custom, a kind of ghost in the machinery of justification.

Set the story in an isolated city-state whose constitution and legal code were written more than a century ago by a legal philosopher and a small circle of clerks, all now long dead. Unusually, the texts do not just state rules; they preserve the argumentative reasoning behind each rule in considerable detail. The city still governs itself by these writings, though living judges increasingly regard many of the original rationales as mistaken, incomplete, or grounded in assumptions no one shares anymore. A dispute has arisen — you choose its subject — in which one party insists that a particular written rationale, though its authors are dead and its reasoning is now widely rejected, still settles the case by its own standing; the other party insists that a rationale no living person endorses has already lost its authority and should be read as a museum piece, not a reason.

Write the story as this dispute unfolds, focusing on the human choices and consequences rather than on abstract argument. Let the narrative dramatize what it means for a reason to survive its reasoners, and end on a note that leaves the question genuinely unsettled rather than comfortably resolved.
