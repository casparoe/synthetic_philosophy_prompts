Give me a working definition of envy that separates it from resentment, righteous indignation, admiration, and schadenfreude, and that gives a definite verdict for each of the following cases on two questions: whether the state in question is envy at all, and whether, if it is, it should be counted as a vice, a justified response to injustice, or a signal that something in the situation is unjust.

1. A colleague receives a promotion through a process you believe was fair, and the promotion costs you nothing; you feel a sour pain at their success and quietly hope it will unravel.
2. A boss gives a bonus to a crony who did none of the work, passing over a clearly more competent employee; the passed-over employee is angry at the unfairness, not distressed by the crony's good fortune as such.
3. A friend performs brilliantly and you feel lifted by it, eager to learn from them; the feeling has no pain in it.
4. A colleague is promoted over you through a process you suspect, but cannot prove, was rigged by the boss's favoritism; your unhappiness is partly at the unfairness and partly at their success.
5. A worker in severe poverty feels a persistent, bitter ache at the wealth of a billionaire, tied to a sense that the inequality itself humiliates them and injures their self-respect.
6. A friend achieves something you badly wanted; you feel pain at the contrast, but it sharpens your resolve, and you genuinely wish them well.
7. A rival who once humiliated you fails publicly, and you feel a flush of pleasure at their failure.
8. A child cries that's not fair! when a sibling gets more ice cream, although the parent treated both equally.

I take case 1 to be clear envy and a vice, and case 2 to be resentment rather than envy. Cases 4 through 8 are the ones I cannot sort confidently. I suspect the ordinary word is doing two jobs: it names a painful emotion, and it carries a built-in verdict that the pain is somehow inappropriate. Rawls complicates this by treating some envy as excusable when it responds to inequalities so severe that they undermine self-respect, which seems to keep the pain while withdrawing the usual verdict.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, mark any verdict that feels wrong or any case left undecided, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Say whether, on your definition, the poor worker's ache at the billionaire in case 5 is still envy, and whether we need two senses of the word rather than one. Finally, tell me which of your eight verdicts is least secure.
