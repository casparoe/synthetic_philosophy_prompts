I'm putting together an application for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in philosophy, and I need a two-page research proposal. My half-formed idea sits in the consequentialist cluelessness debate. The usual debate asks whether consequentialism can still guide action when we cannot predict the long-run consequences of ordinary choices. I want to argue that the most stubborn cases of cluelessness are not really failures of prediction but failures of act-individuation. When I consider whether to buy a new shirt, the question is incomplete until I know which act I'm evaluating: buying the shirt, buying the shirt rather than donating the money, or buying the shirt as one instance of a standing policy of moderately self-interested consumption. These descriptions carry different downstream consequences and apparently different expected values, and no amount of empirical information settles which description picks out the act to be assessed. If that's right, the standard expected-value reply to cluelessness may be answering the wrong question: it shows how to estimate consequences once an act is fixed, but not how to fix the act.

Turn this into a proposal that does all of the following: states the question precisely so that it has a determinate answer; explains why the existing positions leave it open — you can discuss the skeptical view that cluelessness undermines consequentialism's action-guidingness and the expected-value response without worrying about citations, since I'll add them; lays out an approach and argues for it over the obvious alternatives, namely improving the decision-theoretic machinery or abandoning consequentialism; says what a useful deliverable would look like for someone working in normative ethics; gives an honest account of what would count as the project's failing; and sets a realistic scope for twelve months.

End by flagging the commitment I'm asking of you: the proposal shouldn't oversell the idea. Include a short self-critical note saying which argument or consideration would most likely show that the act-individuation framing is wrong, and where the proposal's reasoning is most likely to fail.
