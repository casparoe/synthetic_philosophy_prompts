I am a novelist, and I have a standing rule for the worlds I build: I refuse to count two things as distinct unless I can say what makes them different — some intrinsic feature or some relation to other things in the world. If a reader asked why the story treats them as two, I want to be able to answer with the difference itself, not merely with the fact that I listed two names. When I cannot give that difference, I revise until the supposed two collapse into one or their distinction is written into the story. I have trusted this rule for years as a way of keeping invented worlds from filling up with distinctions that do no work.

Recently I started sketching a story set in a universe that contains nothing but two perfect spheres of identical size and composition, a fixed distance apart in otherwise featureless space. There is no third thing, no observer, no history, and nothing about either sphere by which it could be singled out from the other. My draft insists that there are two spheres, and the scene turns on their being two; but by my rule I should refuse to write them as distinct unless I can state the respect in which they differ. I could cheat — give one a tiny scratch, a different spin, or just stipulate the duality — but that seems to abandon the very thing the scene is trying to think about. I know the case is close to Max Black's two-sphere universe, but my question is about my rule as much as about the metaphysics.

Is my rule rationally defensible as a writer's policy, or is it the identity of indiscernibles dressed up as craft discipline? Is there a defensible version of the rule that preserves its use elsewhere while permitting the two perfect spheres? And if I do write the spheres as two, what in the story, if anything, makes them genuinely two rather than one thing counted twice?

Please give me a committed answer: take a definite position on how I should write this scene, on what formulation of the rule, if any, I should carry forward, and on where the difference between the spheres lies — with reasons I could use in my work.
