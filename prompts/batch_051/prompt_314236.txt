I am a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on intergenerational justice and the ethics of discounting, and over the term I have been trying to formulate a position I provisionally call identity-discounting. I would like an honest assessment of it.

The position starts from an observation and a claim. The observation is that people do not in practice treat all parts of their own lives as equally significant for practical reasoning. I am, for example, far more moved by the prospect of pain next week than by the prospect of pain thirty years from now, even after reflection. The claim is that this is not simply a rational failing. I suspect that what justifies caring more about some future experiences than others is not their date but their bearer in the only sense that matters: the degree to which the affected future self is continuous with the self now choosing. By continuity I mean the overlap in memories, values, projects, habits, and attachments between the person who decides and the person who will live with the decision. My proposal is that a future interest should receive weight in my present choices in proportion to that continuity. If the person who will exist in twenty-five years shares little with me now, I may be permitted to give their interests correspondingly little weight.

I intend this as a normative principle, not merely a psychological description: I am claiming not only that we happen to discount in this way, but that we may, or perhaps ought to. I also take it to be distinct from pure time preference, because temporal distance does no work by itself on my view. Two outcomes equally distant in time could receive different weights if the relevant continuity differs, and an outcome at any distance would receive full weight when the choosing and the experiencing selves are fully continuous. I concede that continuity and temporal distance normally travel together; the cases that matter are those in which they come apart.

A concrete case: choosing whether to forgo present consumption in order to save for a retirement that a psychologically distant version of me will enjoy looks, on this picture, less like a duty of prudence than like a gift I may or may not decide to make. Collectively, the same idea would seem to permit, or at least explain, our easy deferral of costly action on climate change and public debt, since the beneficiaries and the payers are different people with little continuity between them. I find that implication disturbing, and part of what I am asking is whether it genuinely follows.

I am aware that this stands near longstanding debates about personal identity and about whether a human life should be treated as a single unit of concern, and that there is a familiar philosophical discussion of whether diminished psychological continuity justifies diminished concern. What I want to know is whether my version is coherent and distinctive. Has it already been worked out, and if so under what name and in what strongest form? Give me what you take to be the three most serious objections, and tell me which, if any, are fatal rather than merely costly. In particular, I would like to know where the continuity criterion breaks down, or changes its character, when we move from my future self to another person alive today, and then to future generations.
