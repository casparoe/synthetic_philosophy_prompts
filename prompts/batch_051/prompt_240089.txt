I spent 30 years in seismology and only got into philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if this is half-baked. I keep coming back to Bertrand's paradox—the one where you ask for the probability that a chord of a circle chosen 'at random' is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on how you generate the chord. People usually say the problem is that 'random' is underspecified. I think that's right but it's the beginning of an answer, not the end.

I've been calling my idea 'generative indifference.' The claim is that the standard mistake is applying the principle of indifference to outcomes (chords) directly. Outcomes don't carry probabilities on their own; only generating mechanisms do. So when a problem says 'choose at random' without fixing a mechanism, indifference should be applied one level up: list the genuinely distinct ways a physically embodied agent could actually draw a chord—random endpoints, random radius plus random angle, random midpoint in the disk, maybe a few more—put a flat prior over those mechanisms, and average. For the three classic mechanisms you get (1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4)/3 = 13/36.

It feels satisfying because it doesn't privilege one parameterization, but I can already hear two objections. First, haven't I just moved the underdetermination up a level? Why those mechanisms and why equally weighted—isn't that the principle of indifference again, now over an infinite and no better behaved space of mechanisms? Second, what counts as 'genuinely distinct' as a mechanism, given that two procedures can be the same under one representation and different under another?

So: is generative indifference a real idea or just the underspecification point in a lab coat? I have a vague memory of a phsyicist who handled Bertrand's paradox with a symmetry argument and I know the maximum-entropy people have opinions. Am I just rediscovering that? Give me the three strongest objections, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just limiting the idea's scope.
