Courts in many legal systems do something that, described from the outside, looks paradoxical. They will say that a statute's words, read literally, require a particular result, but then refuse to apply the words to produce that result because the result is absurd; and they will justify the refusal by saying that no reasonable legislature could have intended such an outcome. This is not confined to typographical errors or miscopied cross-references. A court might hold that a fire-code rule requiring an escape ladder on every building of more than one storey — where building is defined as any structure having a roof and walls — does not, despite its words, apply to a child's two-storey dollhouse, because fire safety cannot have been meant to regulate toys. In the same opinion the court may also insist that when the words of a statute are plain the interpretive inquiry ends, for judges are not free to rewrite what the legislature enacted. The same system thus seems to hold both that enacted words bind and that enacted words give way when what they would produce is sufficiently foolish.

Reconstruct the philosophy of legal interpretation — and of the relation between an enacted text and the law it makes — that this practice presupposes. Does the practice commit the law to the view that what governs is the legislature's intended meaning rather than the public meaning of the enacted words? Does it presuppose that a rational legislator cannot be taken to will an absurd result, and if so, is that a psychological claim about actual legislators or a normative idealization of them? Does it instead presuppose a rule-of-law value, namely that the citizen is entitled to read her legislature as speaking minimally reasonably and not self-defeatingly? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely permits: an intentionalist, a textualist who treats absurdity as a kind of drafting mistake, and a purposivist who treats text as only evidence of purpose might all reach the same result in an easy case, so say how much about those theories the doctrine actually settles. Then say what would have to change elsewhere in the surrounding legal order if the presupposed picture were replaced, first, by a strict textualism under which the enacted words are the law even when the outcome is foolish, and second, by a thoroughgoing purposivism under which the text may be set aside whenever a judge discerns a better statutory purpose. End with your own judgment: is the absurdity doctrine a single coherent practice resting on one defensible view of statutory meaning, or is it a label for an unresolved standoff between rival commitments?
