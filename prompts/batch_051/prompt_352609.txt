I'm putting together a session for an intro epistemology class where I want to do norms of assertion through Othello. The debate is about what standard someone has to meet before they may properly flat-out assert something: knowledge (assert only what you know), truth (only what is true), or defensible belief (only what you're in a good enough epistemic position to believe). The usual test cases are things like whether you can properly tell someone their lottery ticket lost before the numbers are announced, even though it's overwhelmingly likely. I'd rather run the question through the Iago-Othello-Desdemona triangle. What strikes me is that Iago is extremely careful about what he flat-out says versus what he only insinuates, while Othello is the one who keeps demanding proof before he will judge, and then Othello ends up making the big false life-destroying assertions while Iago stays mostly below the threshold of assertion. Does the play take a side on which of these norms actually matters? Is the tragedy best understood as Othello violating a knowledge norm, or does it show the whole debate is aimed at the wrong thing because the real damage is done by insinuation and implicature, which arent assertion at all? I dont want the play reduced to a case study for a theory. Tell me where the theory illuminates specific scenes or choices, where the play pushes back, and what a student who has read it once should walk away thinking about assertion after the class.
