I'm a software engineer with no formal background in academic philosophy, and I want a rigorous comparison of two concepts that keep surfacing in my work on AI systems. When people claim a system is safe or aligned, they tend to rely on one of two very different kinds of evidence. The first is average-case: the model gets a low error rate on a held-out test set, achieves a high mean reward over training episodes, or posts strong average scores across benchmarks. The second is worst-case: a red team tried and failed to elicit catastrophic behavior, a formal method verified that some specified bad property never holds over a specified class of inputs, or an adversarial analysis bounds the loss for every input in some set.

Please compare these two kinds of guarantees as concepts, in more depth than the ordinary engineering discussion. In particular:

- State what each kind of guarantee actually establishes, including what its implicit adversary or assumed distribution is. Address whether average-case guarantee is even a coherent label, given that an empirical average over a test distribution is a measurement rather than a formal guarantee, and distinguish empirical estimates from mathematical claims where that distinction matters.

- Explain why an average-case result, however strong, can be misleading when the property at stake is catastrophic failure rather than ordinary task performance. Be specific about distribution shift, tail events absent from the evaluation distribution, and the difference between a system mostly doing what we want and a system robustly avoiding a narrow class of unacceptable behaviors.

- Explain the corresponding failure modes of the worst-case framing: an underspecified input space or threat model, an adversary much weaker or much stronger than anything realistic, and a formally verified property that fails to capture the intended safety property. Discuss what it takes for a worst-case claim to be meaningful rather than merely rigorous-sounding.

- Assess whether these are two points on a continuum of evidential strength or two different kinds of claim altogether, one roughly statistical and one roughly logical. Use that assessment to say how a small team that cannot afford to pursue both should choose a default, and what conditions should trigger a shift in emphasis toward the other.
