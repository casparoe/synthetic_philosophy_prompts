You are a moral philosopher working on the grounds of moral status. Think carefully before you answer, and begin by sketching several candidate principles before you settle on the best one.

Consider this pattern in ordinary judgment. If someone proposed that a human being with a smaller or less complex brain should count for less morally — that their pain mattered less, or that harming them was less serious — most people would treat the proposal as not merely mistaken but bigoted. Brain size, cortical folding, neuron counts, and similar neural measures are treated as morally irrelevant when applied among humans. Yet many of the same people are entirely comfortable saying that a mouse's pain matters less than a human's partly because the mouse has a much smaller and less complex brain, and that an animal's moral standing tracks, in part, the sophistication of its nervous system. So a brain-based gradient in moral status is treated as an enlightened basis for animal ethics and as a discredited relic when applied within our own species.

I want the strongest principle you can find that yields both verdicts, and an honest verdict on whether it works. Does the asymmetry rest on species-typical capacity rather than individual variation, on a threshold below which the question changes kind, on a difference between equal respect and graded care, or on the thought that the intra-human case is poisoned by its history in scientific racism? Or is this an inconsistency we should give up — and if so, in which direction: stop discounting the mouse, or accept that individual neural variation among humans could matter?

As you answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on moral status, do not present a contested view as the consensus, and be explicit about your own confidence.
