I work as a machine learning engineer with a sideline interest in AI alignment, and I keep noticing a move in the literature that I don't have a word for. The pattern involves two different kinds of safety or performance claims. One kind says that, averaged over the situations a system is actually expected to encounter, it fails with only some small probability. The other kind says that the system will behave acceptably no matter what situation someone deliberately constructs, even one designed specifically to break it. These are obviously not the same claim, and each is reasonable in its proper place. But I repeatedly see people pass from one to the other without marking the transition. For example, a paper will prove a small average failure rate under a particular training or evaluation distribution, and then the abstract or a later citation will word the result as if it established safety against an adversary actively searching for the worst possible input; I once followed a citation chain in which, by the third paper, the result had picked up exactly that stronger reading. In the other direction, I have seen entire proposals dismissed because a reviewer constructs a single pathological input on which the system fails, while the author objects that the input is astronomically unlikely in any plausible deployment and that the proposal was only ever intended as an average-case improvement.

What unsettles me is not that strong and weak notions of guarantee coexist. It is that people seem to reach for whichever notion wins the local argument, without acknowledging that the notion has changed, and the switch itself usually carries the argument's weight. Is there an established name for this specific pattern, the unmarked slide between guarantees that hold on typical inputs and guarantees that hold against deliberately adversarial inputs? I know the two underlying notions are standard; I am asking whether the slide itself has been singled out and analyzed. If the nearest concepts are something like motte-and-bailey or ordinary overclaiming, I would want to know exactly where the boundaries lie: when is such a slide merely harmless shorthand for a well-understood distinction, when is it honest uncertainty about the relevant distribution, and when does it become the failure mode I am describing? If no established term fits, I would welcome a proposed term with a definition precise enough to apply when reading or reviewing papers.
