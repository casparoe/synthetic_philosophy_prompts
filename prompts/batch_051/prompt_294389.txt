I run an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on rationality and risk—sixteen to twenty students, mostly economics, philosophy, and statistics majors—and I have an eighty-minute session coming up. I want the session to make students feel, before they are told, that a decision which looks good in expectation can ruin an individual who repeats it over time, and that this is not a failure to understand the math but a difference between two questions: what happens to many people at one time, and what happens to one person over many times. The students should not hear the concepts named—no "ensemble average," "time average," or "ergodicity"—until the debrief. I want a gambling game with real but small stakes (say, a modest prize for the top bankroll and a light penalty for the bottom, in points or snacks) in which each participant repeatedly wagers some fraction of their current wealth on a fair coin. Please choose concrete payoffs yourself and verify them: the expected wealth multiplier per round should exceed one, the time-average growth factor at full stakes should be less than one (that is, the long-run growth rate is negative), and there should be some interior stake whose long-run growth rate is positive. Use numbers simple enough that students can do the arithmetic on a phone or mentally. (If it helps: heads multiplies wealth by 1+r and tails by 1−s; I want (1+r)+(1−s) over 2 to be greater than 1, the product (1+r)(1−s) to be less than 1, and some fraction f strictly between 0 and 1 such that 0.5 ln(1+rf) + 0.5 ln(1−sf) is positive.)

The design should be arranged so that the tension emerges from what happens rather than from the facilitator explaining it. Ideally, the cross-sectional mean bankroll in the room rises while most individual trajectories fall, and the students who consistently bet their full wealth because "the expected value is positive" visibly crash. Include a structured phase in which the group compares the mean bankroll to each person's own path and tries to explain the discrepancy themselves before you supply the vocabulary. The debrief should then map the moments of the game onto the distinction—why maximizing expected value per round and maximizing growth over time come apart, why the cross-sectional mean gets pulled up by a few lucky players, and where the growth-optimal stake sits, without ever labeling it to the students as the Kelly criterion.

Work as an expert facilitator who knows how to design classroom games and can do the supporting arithmetic exactly, and think carefully before committing: brainstorm at least two candidate payoff structures, check them against the conditions above, and present only the stronger one in the final answer. Please give the design in exactly the following sections, in this order:

- "Aim and payoff check" (~150 words): restate the learning aim in your own words, state the chosen r and s, and show that the three conditions hold.
- "Setup, materials, and rules" (~200 words): the room setup, the materials, the record-keeping each student uses, the exact betting procedure, and the small stakes.
- "The game, minute by minute" (~450 words): the phases with a rough timeline, including exactly when and how the facilitation moves happen and what the facilitator deliberately refrains from saying.
- "Debrief script" (~350 words): the ordered questions that lead the group from their observed results to the distinction, followed by the conceptual mapping from specific moments in the game to the idea.
- "Predictable failures and fixes" (~200 words): what will likely go wrong when the activity is run and how the facilitator should handle each.
- "What the activity cannot reproduce" (~150 words): an honest note on which features of the underlying idea the exercise cannot generate on its own and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.
