You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral responsibility and the metaphysics of social groups. I am drafting a position paper on corporate moral agency and I want a consistency audit of a set of commitments before I present the paper. Please think through the view as a whole rather than checking each commitment separately: begin by working out how the commitments are meant to fit together, then probe for points where they pull against one another.

Here is the view I have settled on.

1. Certain long-lived and highly structured organizations — large corporations are the paradigm — are genuine moral agents. Their decision procedures, division of epistemic labor, and role structures enable them to form intentions and act for reasons. These corporate intentions are non-summative: they may not be identical with, or reducible to, the intentions of any individual member or to any aggregation of the members' intentions.

2. A corporation of this kind can be morally blameworthy as a corporation. I hold, in particular, that a company can be blameworthy for an outcome such as the systematic concealment of a lethal product defect through its testing and reporting procedures, even when no identifiable employee or executive was individually blameworthy, because the fault lay in the interaction among procedures and roles rather than in any one person's choices or mental states.

3. I accept a broadly Strawsonian account of moral blame: to blame an agent is to hold that agent to a moral demand through reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation, and those attitudes are fitting only toward agents that can themselves participate in the practice of holding others and being held responsible. Blame is for members of the moral community, not for mere causes or hazards.

4. I am equally confident that corporations have no phenomenal consciousness. There is nothing it is like to be a corporation, and a corporation cannot itself feel guilt, compunction, or remorse over and above what its members feel.

5. Nevertheless, I hold that resentment and indignation can be fittingly directed at the corporation itself. The thought that I resent a company for concealing a defect for two decades can be literally true and apt, and it is not merely shorthand for resenting its executives or for blaming some scattered collection of individuals.

6. I also insist that corporate intentionality is ontologically unremarkable: facts about a corporation's intentions supervene on facts about individual mental states together with the organizational structures, roles, and decision rules in which those individuals act. There is no group mind floating over and above its members.

7. Finally, I regard corporate and individual blameworthiness as non-rival. Holding the corporation blameworthy neither adds to nor subtracts from the blame that falls on individual employees, and I reject any view that treats corporate blame as nothing but an indirect scheme for distributing blame across individuals.

I am not sure that this set hangs together. Please take your time and reason carefully. First, distinguish the commitments that are genuinely in tension from those that are only apparently in tension, and for each apparent tension explain why it dissolves. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally problematic. Third, for each genuine tension, say what would need to be given up or qualified, identify the least costly revision, and explain what that revision would cost elsewhere in the view. Do not flag a conflict unless you can state precisely which commitments conflict and why they cannot jointly be held as written.
