You are a game theorist and philosophy instructor who designs classroom experiments, and you have particular experience making subtle formal concepts emerge from live games rather than from lectures. Take the task seriously, think carefully about the design before committing to it, and then produce a single polished, step-by-step plan.

You are designing one 75-minute session for 18 advanced undergraduates in a philosophy of economics course. They already read normal-form payoff matrices and understand strict dominance by pure strategies, but they have not yet been taught that a strategy can be strictly dominated by a random mixture of other strategies, or that such dominated strategies can be removed and the test applied again to what remains.

Your assignment is to design a runnable classroom activity around the following two-player game. Players are Row and Column. Payoffs are listed as Row, Column.

| Row \ Column | Left | Centre | Right |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | (10, 8) | (6, 0) | (0, 2) |
| Mid | (2, 0) | (2, 8) | (4, 2) |
| Down | (4, 2) | (2, 2) | (1, 2) |

The activity should get participants to feel the force of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies when domination by mixed strategies is allowed. By the end, they should see that a strategy can be strictly dominated by a coin flip between two other strategies even though no pure strategy dominates anything, that each deletion can unlock further deletions, and that the procedure can yield a sharp prediction even where pure-strategy elimination stalls immediately.

For your own planning, here are the facts the debrief must eventually make explicit, but which the participants should discover rather than be told up front. In this game, no strategy is strictly dominated by any pure strategy. However, Down is strictly dominated by a 50:50 mixture of Up and Mid: that mixture gives Row expected payoffs 6 against Left, 4 against Centre, and 2 against Right, while Down gives 4, 2, and 1. With Down deleted, Right is strictly dominated by a 50:50 mixture of Left and Centre for Column, since the mixture gives expected payoffs 4 against Up and 4 against Mid, while Right gives 2 and 2. This was not true before Down was deleted, because against Down all three of Column's strategies yield 2, so a mixture of Left and Centre could not strictly beat Right. With Down and Right deleted, Mid is strictly dominated by Up. With Mid deleted, Centre is dominated by Left. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, with mixed strategies allowed, therefore selects the profile (Up, Left).

Notice also that Down and Right are the strategies that look safe: Down never gives Row less than 1, and Right always gives Column 2. Part of the point is that these cautious strategies are strictly beaten by randomization between riskier alternatives, and that this fact only becomes visible once mixed strategies are admitted into the dominance test.

Design the activity in phases, with roles or group work, a realistic timeline, and a payoff system that needs no real money. It should begin with a short round of actual play of the full game to generate data, then move to a structured protocol in which small groups try to delete strategies they would never choose. Arrange the phases so that participants first stall on the absence of any pure-strategy dominance, then are forced to consider whether a coin flip between two strategies could give a reason to discard a third, then realize that deleting a strategy changes which further strategies can be discarded. Do not put the derivation above into the participants' materials; structure the activity so that they generate it themselves. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto the formal idea, explains the order dependence of the eliminations, and is honest about which feature of the idea the live activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.

Before giving the final plan, brainstorm in a scratchpad about two or three candidate activity structures and how each would handle the crucial transition from pure to mixed dominance. Then present one polished plan, including rules, materials, timeline, debrief, and an explicit note on the most likely thing to go wrong the first time it is run and how a facilitator should respond.
