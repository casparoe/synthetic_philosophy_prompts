I am a PhD student in philosophy of language, and in three weeks I will present a paper at our departmental colloquium defending the following thesis about the meaning of literary texts. I distinguish three things. First, the actual authorial intention behind a passage. Second, the ahistorical semantic content of the text considered as a public artifact. Third, what I call reader-warranted authorial content: the meaning that a member of the author's primary intended readership, using only the linguistic and genre conventions current for that readership, would be most justified in attributing to the author on the basis of the text. My thesis is that literary interpretation tracks this third thing, not the first or the second. Authorial testimony outside the text—letters, diaries, later interviews—is relevant only insofar as it makes visible something a member of that primary readership could already have been warranted in attributing; it cannot introduce a meaning that was not textually available to that readership. When two or more incompatible readings are each maximally warranted given those conventions, I hold that the work's meaning is indeterminate between them rather than ambiguous.

The audience will be our department's philosophy of language and mind colloquium. The faculty includes two Gricean philosophers of language who think literary interpretation is a more disciplined extension of ordinary utterance interpretation; a senior philosopher of art whose position is close to actual intentionalism and who suspects that any warranted-intention view makes what counts as interpretation objectionably relative to a constructed audience; a philosopher of literature sympathetic to the New Critical tradition, who is an anti-intentionalist about textual meaning and cares about protecting readings that no historical audience could have recovered; and a group of graduate students working in experimental philosophy of interpretation, who are suspicious of any view that builds in a privileged notion of a competent reader.

My argument, briefly, is this. If actual intention fixed content, then a text whose author's intentions are irrecoverably lost could never be called meaningful or correctly interpreted, yet we routinely interpret recovered and anonymous texts. If ahistorical artifact semantics fixed content, then the text's meaning would not shift with evidence about its period or genre, yet such evidence plainly changes what passages say. Reader-warranted authorial content explains both: it makes meaning independent of the author's private states while still tying it to historically situated conventions, it explains why anachronistic readings are errors, and it explains why recovered texts remain interpretable. Extratextual testimony, on my view, is a method for recovering what a historical reader could have seen, not an independent meaning-conferring act.

Phrase each objection in the terms and with the emphasis the relevant person would use, and indicate whether it comes from the Griceans, the actual intentionalist, the anti-intentionalist, or the graduate students. For each objection, tell me the two-minute reply I should give, what I should concede immediately, the point I should hold, and where the reply is soft or what follow-up I should expect. End with the question I should be most hoping nobody asks, and say briefly why it would be dangerous.
