A friend who has been reading epistemology gave the following summary of the debate over higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia:

"Higher-order evidence is evidence about your evidence, rather than evidence bearing directly on the question you are trying to answer. If you are trying to determine whether someone is guilty, the eyewitness's testimony is first-order evidence; evidence that you are sleep-deprived, that you are susceptible to a statistical bias, or that an equally intelligent and equally informed person has reached the opposite conclusion is higher-order evidence, because it bears on the reliability of your take on the first-order evidence. The central lesson of this literature is that a rational person must take such evidence into account by becoming less confident in whatever conclusion they reached on the first-order evidence: to ignore it would be to treat your own reasoning as mysteriously exempt from the fallibility you recognize in everyone else. On the standard view, when you discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with you, the rational response is to split the difference between your two credences — if you are at 0.8 and she is at 0.2, you should each move to 0.5. It follows that someone who learns of a peer's disagreement and simply keeps his original credence is being epistemically akratic: he believes p while holding that his total evidence no longer supports p. This is the epistemic analogue of judging that one should not have another drink and then having one anyway, and it is just as irrational. The main worry one hears about this whole picture is that it leads to a regress or to skepticism, since if you are required to distrust your reasoning whenever you acquire higher-order evidence against it, you will eventually have to distrust the very reasoning by which you assess the higher-order evidence."

I suspect this summary is mostly accurate but that it runs together several positions that people working in the field insist on keeping apart, and I do not trust my own ear well enough to say exactly where the joints are. My sense is that the opening characterization is roughly right, that the move from "higher-order evidence should lower your confidence" to "you should split the difference" is too quick, and that the charge of epistemic akrasia is doing more work than the accurate parts of the summary license — but I cannot make this precise.
