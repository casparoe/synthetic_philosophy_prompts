For an independent study in the philosophy of religion at my high school, I have been working through a standard version of the problem of petitionary prayer. The problem, as I am setting it up: if God is omniscient, perfectly good, and immutable, a petition can seem unable to do any work—it cannot inform God, cannot improve God's plan, and cannot move God to do what God was not already going to do. So a defense of petitionary prayer has to explain either how a petition can still make a difference, or why its value does not come from making that kind of difference.

I have collected what I believe are the standing replies, written out in my own terms:

1. The transformative reply. Petitionary prayer changes the one who prays rather than God; it reorders desires, cultivates humility, gratitude, and dependence, and any effect on the world comes through the changed petitioner and their subsequent action.
2. The obedience reply. Petitionary prayer is commanded by God, and its value lies in obedience, worship, and dependent address itself, independent of the outcome.
3. The relationship reply. The asking is itself the point; petition constitutes or deepens a personal relationship with God, and its value is in that communion rather than in obtaining the thing requested.
4. The divinely-ordained-means reply. God immutably and from eternity willed to grant certain goods through the petition as a means or condition, so prayer can be a genuine secondary cause of the outcome without any change in God; this includes views on which God makes the requested good conditional on creaturely request, participation, or consent.
5. The revisionist dissolution. The problem is an artifact of immutability or exhaustive foreknowledge, so the practice is defended by revising theism—for example, by holding that God can genuinely respond to requests in time.
6. The skeptical-theist reply. Since we cannot know God's reasons for granting or not granting a particular request, we are not in a position to conclude that a given prayer made no difference; combined with the command to pray, this blocks the move from apparent futility to real futility.

I am now fairly sure this list covers the standard options, and I want to move past it. Please give me a reply to the problem that is not on this list—one that defends the intelligibility or value of petitionary prayer without merely restating, specializing, or combining the options above. Then explain what the new reply adds, how it relates to the closest item or items on my list, and whether you think it succeeds on its own terms. A reply that collapses into one of the listed positions will not count.
