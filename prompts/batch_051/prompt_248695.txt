In a seminar on epistemic logic we have been working through Fitch's paradox of knowability. The anti-realist begins from the modest-looking principle that every truth is knowable: for any proposition φ, if φ is true, then it is possible that φ is known, in symbols φ → ◇Kφ. Fitch's derivation purports to show that this principle, together with the facts that knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, collapses the principle into the extravagant thesis that every truth is actually known. Suppose there is a true proposition p that nobody knows. Then the conjunction of p with the claim that nobody knows p is also true, so by the knowability principle it must be possible to know that conjunction. But knowledge of the conjunction would require knowledge of each conjunct: knowledge of the first conjunct gives that somebody knows p, while knowledge of the second conjunct gives, by factivity, that nobody knows p. Contradiction. Hence, under classical logic, there can be no unknown truth.

Please make a sustained argument for the following position. The anti-realist should restrict the knowability principle to what we may call Cartesian truths: propositions whose being known is not logically self-undermining. The Fitch conjunction is not Cartesian, since the supposition that it is known leads immediately to contradiction; the restricted principle therefore never demands that it be knowable, and the paradox is blocked. Your task is not merely to state this restriction but to defend it. Show that the restriction flows from the anti-realist's own understanding of truth as answerable to our cognitive capacities rather than from a merely defensive maneuver, and show that the restricted principle still commits the anti-realist to something substantive—that a rich class of non-trivial truths is knowable—rather than collapsing into a triviality. Then answer the strongest objection, pressed hard rather than strawmanned: that the restriction is ad hoc, carving out exactly the diagonal conjunctions that cause trouble, and that it quietly surrenders the universality that made the original principle philosophically interesting. Assume classical logic throughout; do not evade the paradox by retreating to intuitionistic logic or to a non-factive reading of knowledge.
