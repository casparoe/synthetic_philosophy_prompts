Consider the utility monster objection to utilitarianism. In its standard form, the objection says that if one being got vastly more well-being than everyone else from each additional unit of a scarce resource, utilitarianism would require transferring resources to that being until the point where its gains were outweighed by others' losses—potentially leaving everyone else near destitution. The objection appears to depend on the premise that extreme differences in how efficiently different beings convert resources into well-being are possible. Now suppose there were strong empirical grounds for thinking that resource-to-well-being conversion is strictly bounded: beyond a modest resource level, no sentient system gains further well-being from further resources, and the ceiling is roughly the same across all sentient systems, so no system can be orders of magnitude more efficient than another. Does the utility monster objection survive this change? Say which premise the finding bears on, and either give a repaired version of the objection that does not depend on extreme differences in conversion efficiency, or explain why it cannot be repaired and what the residual worry about utilitarian distribution should be called.
