I am trying to determine whether there is a single defensible principle behind my judgments about elegance as evidence in mathematics and science, or whether some of those judgments are only aesthetic habits I ought to abandon. The underlying question is whether elegance is ever evidence of a theory's truth, and if it is, what exactly it is evidence for. I have set out my verdicts on several cases below; some of them pull against others, and I am unsure how many of them I can hold at once.

1. A formal constraint of remarkable simplicity and symmetry leads to equations that refuse to work unless an unobserved entity exists, and that entity is later discovered. The example I have in mind is the way the relativistic wave equation for the electron seemed to force a positively charged counterpart of the electron, which was then found. I take this to be a case in which elegance was genuinely truth-conducive. The clean formalism could not be made consistent without the new particle, and I regard that forced prediction as evidence that the formalism was tracking nature rather than merely pleasing us.

2. A broad theoretical framework of great internal beauty has for a long time produced no distinctive confirmed prediction that its rivals would not also make. I find the framework intellectually magnificent and worth developing, but I do not find its beauty to be strong positive evidence that it is true. Beauty moves me; it does not much move my degree of belief.

3. An explanatory scheme fits every known phenomenon by adding one more mechanism of the same type whenever an anomaly appears, with no principled limit and no independent test for each added mechanism. I distrust such a scheme almost completely. Its unlimited flexibility seems to me a rational defect rather than an aesthetic one, and I would bet against it even if no better rival were available.

4. Two models of the same data fit equally well by the usual statistical standards. One has three adjustable parameters; the other has forty. I prefer the simpler model, but I am unsure whether my preference is epistemic or merely practical. I may favor it only because it is easier to use, less likely to overfit the next dataset, and easier to falsify, without thereby taking it to be more probably true.

5. Ten data points lie close to a straight line. One model is the straight line; another is a degree-nine polynomial that passes through all ten points exactly and oscillates wildly between them. Here I go further than in case 4: I think the straight line is more probably true, not merely more serviceable. This case pushes me to say that simplicity sometimes does real evidential work in favor of truth.

6. In fundamental physics I am open to the idea that the world is elegant at its deepest level, so that a beautiful theory is to that extent more likely true. In biology or macroeconomics I expect baroque, path-dependent, historically contingent mechanisms, and an elegant model of such a mess lowers my confidence slightly until it has proven itself through prediction. But I cannot state which difference in the subject matter explains why the same theoretical virtue should be evidence in one domain and a warning sign in another.

7. Conversely, when a proposed fundamental theory is too tidy — when it seems shaped precisely to produce the few quantities we already know and shows no strain anywhere — I become suspicious, much as I do in case 3. I begin to think the elegance is in the construction of the theory rather than in the world. This pulls against case 1, where I credited a strained elegance that forced an unwelcome prediction. I do not know how to say when elegance indicates that a theory is constrained by nature and when it indicates that one has selected the theory for its elegance.

8. In pure mathematics I do not treat elegance as evidence for the truth of a conjecture at all. A beautiful but non-rigorous proof sketch leaves my belief in the conjecture almost unchanged. Proof or counterexample settles the matter; beauty is a guide to which conjectures deserve effort, not a reason to think them true.

I would like you to do three things. First, state the best principle, or small set of principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, and for each clause indicate which of my cases motivates it. Second, say what the principle implies for at least one further case I have not listed — for example, a computationally expensive model whose architecture is praised for its elegance, or a heavily parameterized model whose forecasts nevertheless outperform simpler rivals. Third, be candid about which of my verdicts a defensible principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would revise that verdict or the principle.
