I am an undergraduate taking a philosophy of biology course, and we have spent several weeks on mechanistic explanation. The position we keep returning to is that a biological explanation earns its keep by identifying the component parts and activities whose organization produces the phenomenon, and that its explanatory force comes from that productive organization rather than from subsuming the phenomenon under a regularity or predicting it statistically.

The difficulty I keep hitting is that I cannot tell, in any particular case, whether a proposed mechanism actually possesses that explanatory force or whether it is merely a causal-sounding redescription: the investigator already knows the phenomenon, fills in a plausible boxes-and-arrows account guided by the phenomenon, and changes vocabulary without adding content. The two can look indistinguishable, and the claim that a genuine mechanism identifies the real parts just pushes the problem back, since identifying the parts is part of what is in dispute.

Please take one ordinary case, such as a signal-transduction pathway or the establishment of a concentration gradient, and design the strongest test you can of the claim that a proposed mechanism explanation is explanatory because its identified parts and activities are organized to produce the phenomenon, rather than being a post hoc redescription. Be concrete about what observation or intervention would count for the claim and what would count against it. Then tell me what a positive result would establish and what it would leave untouched—specifically, how much of the distinction between a genuine mechanism and a redescription can be settled empirically, and how much ultimately rests on a philosophical view about what it is to explain.
