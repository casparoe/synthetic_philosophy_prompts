Okay so I'm a philosophy PhD student and I've been teaching a little undergrad seminar on self-knowledge and speech acts, and we spent a chunk of it on Moore's paradox. The thing I keep failing to get across is that the weirdness isn't that the sentence is a formal contradiction — it isn't — but that there's a live sense in which you can't get outside your own assertion of p to simultaneously report that you don't believe p, at least not while meaning both. So here's what I'd like: a villanelle (yes I know, but hear me out) where the two refrains are a pair of rhyming lines, one of them a first-person present-tense assertion of something ordinary — rain, say, or that the bus is late — and the other a first-person present-tense denial that the speaker believes the thing just asserted. The refrains should also be able to stand as the two halves of a single Moore-paradoxical sentence if joined by 'and' or a semicolon, so grammar does the job at the end. Keep both refrains stubbornly first-personal and present-tense, since the puzzle collapses the moment you shift to past tense or third person, and part of what the poem can show is how much the whole thing depends on that. The villanelle's form should do the philosophical work: each refrain is perfectly fine seperately in its own stanza, and the poem keeps alternating them, but when the form forces them together in the final couplet the speaker ends up committed to the Moore-paradoxical pair. Make it good enough that an undergrad with no background would still feel the poem flinch at the end, but also someone who knows the literature (omissive vs commissive, the debate about whether it's about assertion or belief) would see you know what the puzzle actually is. I don't need it to mention Moore or the technical terms.
