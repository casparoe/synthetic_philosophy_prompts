I'm taking a course on Buddhist ethics, and I keep flip-flopping on whether it's secretly consequentialist. On one hand, when I try to explain why the precepts make sense, the deepest story I can tell seems welfarist: we abstain from killing, lying, stealing and such because those actions tend to cause suffering to sentient beings, and a mind trained not to do them gets less clouded and causes less harm downstream. Even the bodhisattva ideal starts looking like a long-run commitment to reducing suffering on the largest scale, across rebirths. That's a tidy theory with real explanatory power; it gives the precepts a point and a decision procedure in one move.

But I also have an intuition I can't shake, which is that the quality of the act and the direction of the will matter in a way the ledger doesn't capture. Suppose I can tell a small lie that will clearly prevent more distress than it causes; even granted that the math comes out in favor of lying, it still feels like there's a stain in the telling, and that someone who refuses to lie isn't just miscalculating expected value. It also feels like the point of the path is to become a certain kind of person — truthful, restrained, honest with yourself about your intentions — not to run a cost-benefit analysis over the world. I don't want the standard reply that consequentialism can value that kind of character instrumentally; that still lets the ledger call the shots in the end.

Give me the strongest version of both sides, and then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the consequentialist reading to be amended or dropped, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — maybe by separating the criterion of rightness from the point of the practice, or something else. Please answer in American English.
