I'm currently taking a course on causal inference and its philosophical foundations, and we have been reading about directed acyclic graphs, d-separation, the Markov condition, and the assumptions that causal discovery algorithms rely on. Our teaching assistant distributed a short handout summarizing why minimal Bayesian networks are supposed to track causal structure, and one paragraph in it has been bothering me ever since I read it. It says:

"Faithfulness is the assumption that makes causal discovery possible. It says that the probability distribution over a set of variables matches the true causal graph: every independence in the data corresponds to the absence of a causal connection, and every dependence corresponds to the presence of one. Minimality is just a weaker version of the same idea — the true graph is the one with the fewest arrows among all graphs that capture the observed associations. That is why the standard algorithms work the way they do: you search for the sparsest Bayesian network consistent with the data, read the arrows straight off as the causal structure, and you are done. The method has one known weakness. Faithfulness can fail when two causal pathways with opposite signs cancel out, and such cancellations are common in messy systems like biology and economics; outside clean textbook cases the whole procedure is therefore on shaky ground."

I cannot shake the sense that this paragraph is collapsing several distinct claims into one — Markov, faithfulness, and minimality are doing different jobs in the actual framework — and that the cancellation objection in the final two sentences is being allowed to do more work than it should be. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate from what is a common simplification or an overstatement, indicate which parts a defender of faithfulness-based causal discovery would accept or reject, and tell me whether the conclusion drawn in the last sentence actually survives the corrections.
