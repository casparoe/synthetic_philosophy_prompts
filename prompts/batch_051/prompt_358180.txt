In my philosophy of mind course we have been working through Dennett on the intentional stance, and I have built an argument that seems to leave the question I wanted to answer — whether language models believe what they output — without a stable sense. Each step looks right to me, but the conclusion would generalize to human believers too, which makes me suspect something has gone wrong.

(1) On the intentional stance, attributing to a system the belief that P is warranted when treating the system as a rational agent who believes P, with roughly coherent goals, yields the best available predictions of its behavior.

(2) Which stance yields the best available predictions depends on the predictor's purposes and computational resources. For a transformer-based language model, the physical or design stance could in principle predict outputs from weights and input, and with enough compute we would not need belief ascriptions at all; yet for the same model, the intentional stance is often the only practical way to anticipate what it will say.

(3) So whether the model believes that Paris is the capital of France is not fixed by facts about the model alone; it shifts with the behavior being predicted, the grain of description, and the predictor's limitations.

(4) But whether a system believes P is a fact about that system, not about who is trying to predict it or how much time they have.

(5) Therefore the intentional stance either gives no determinate answer to whether language models believe what they output, or it makes belief-attribution observer-relative in a way that undercuts the question. And since humans are physical systems too, the same would go for us.

I would like to keep (1) and (4) if I can, but I suspect that combination is not free. Tell me which step to reject, or whether I have quietly shifted between two senses of what it is for a stance to predict successfully. In particular, if the right move is to distinguish adopting the stance from the underlying pattern being real, I want to know whether that distinction does genuine work or just restates the problem more politely. If I have mischaracterized Dennett, please say so plainly rather than adjusting the argument to make his view come out right.
