I'm a novelist, and the next book is set in a small town with two characters I keep rewriting because I can't tell whether the people around them are seeing something real or just telling themselves a comfortable story. Here are the two.

One is a man who runs a chain of pawnshops and payday-lending counters in the poorer neighborhoods. He's an acquirer to the bone: he wants more, and once he has more he wants still more, and nobody could tell you what amount would make him stop. The town thinks he's a vulture, a greedy son of a bitch, and so do my beta readers.

The other is a woman who has spent thirty years buying up decaying buildings and lots on the town square, restoring them, and renting them at prices very few locals can pay. She's the same kind of acquirer—she has never once said "enough"—but the town mostly calls her driven, a builder, a person who saved the downtown.

So it's the same appetite aimed at different targets, and the only obvious difference is that her hoarding leaves behind something people like to look at and his doesn't. My question: is there a principle that justifies the different judgments, or is the town mistaking aesthetic side-effects for virtue? If you think there's a principle, give me the strongest version of it, then honestly test it against the cases that worry me: the pawnbroker who charges exactly fair interest and refuses to take goods the borrower can't replace, and the restorer who pays her workers minimum wage for the same hours she used to work herself. Would the judgments flip if the side-effects were swapped? And if no principle survives, tell me straight whether I should write the town as deluded or write the characters as more different than they look.
