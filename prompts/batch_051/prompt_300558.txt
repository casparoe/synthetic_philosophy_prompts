I chair my department's annual book-prize committee and we're deadlocked, and I need a short decision memo to circulate before our friday vote. The published criteria say the prize goes to "the work of highest intellectual quality, considered on its merits alone." We've got two finalists. By our own scoring, Book A is a bit stronger—tighter arguments, more original. Book B is close, but here's the thing: the author of Book B was the doctoral student of a donor who has quietly hinted that the fellowship fund he supports might not get renewed if his former student "keeps getting overlooked." Also, Book B is in a subfield the broader discipline has been accused of ignoring, and several members think giving it the prize would send a useful signal. Half the committee says those latter considerations are real reasons to do something—write a commendation, lobby the dean, create a separate mentorship award—but they're the wrong kind of reason to declare Book B the best, because the award just is the university's statement that the work is best, and you can't make that statement true by wanting it to be useful. The other half says that's precious: we aren't just describing the book, we're choosing who gets a scarce, career-making honor, and an institution that ignores the consequences of the choice is being cowardly rather than careful. I need the memo to take a clear side: either the pragmatic benefits count, or they don't, or we split the merit judgment from the honors decision—and if we split, what exactly would the honor then mean? Give me the actual recommendation with the reasoning, keep it short enough to read in a meeting, and make sure the losing side's strongest point gets a fair run rather than a straw man.
