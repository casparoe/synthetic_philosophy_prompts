I write about science and ideas for a living, mostly empirical fights — whether an intervention replicates, whether a null result is real, whether a meta-analysis actually settles anything — and I've settle into a habit I'd like someone to stress-test. My rule is that I don't sweat the prior. I start from a maximally spread-out, vague prior and update on whatever evidence I have, because I was taught that priors wash out with enough data. The way I see it, spending an afternoon constructing a careful subjective prior is a worse use of my deadline time than gathering one more piece of evidence, and my vague default keeps me from fooling myself with a prior that just encodes yesterday's opinion in formal clothing.

A colleague keeps pushing back. He says the wash-out result only works under conditions: the prior has to give nonzero probability to the thing that's actually true, the model has to be at least roughly right, and the sample has to be large enough. He points out that a vague prior is still a specific prior, and in high-dimensional settings or with small samples different vague priors can lead to meaningfully different posteriors. He's also caught me a few times applying the rule when the data was basically never going to settle the question at hand — underidentified models, noisy studies, that kind of thing.

So my question is whether the shortcut is defensible as a working journalist's approximation to rational updating, or whether I'm dressing up a bias in the language of a convergence theorem. Is there a principled way to know, in advance or from the inside, whether my prior is likely to wash out fast enough that my conclusions would be stable under reasonable alternatives? And is there a version of the rule — maybe start vague but run a few sensitivity checks against weakly informative priors before I publish — that keeps the speed without the self-deception?
