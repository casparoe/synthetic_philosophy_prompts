Two friends of mine got into it at a cookout last weekend and I keep turning the argument over. I'll try to get the back-and-forth down faithfully, names changed.

Elena and Marcus were arguing about a town in the next county, Ossipee, where the historical society is putting together a new exhibit for the mill's centennial. The town's founding story is that in 1919 the mill caught fire during the night shift and the town's first owner, Harlan Coe, went in and led the workers out, and that's why the town holds Coe Day every year. A PhD student digitizing county records found that the story seems to have been written down first in a 1938 pamphlet published by Coe's son, while the town's own paper, the day after the fire, reported that Coe was out of town and that the workers were led out by Adelina Rocha, the night-shift floor boss, an immigrant, who went back in for one more person and died in the fire. The exhibit committee is split on whether to retell the founding story or correct it.

Elena: The story isn't a lie, it's memory. Nobody in Ossipee is trying to report on 1919. Coe Day is about who we are now, not about what happened then. Holding a community's memory to the standard of a document treats the town like a defendant.

Marcus: But the exhibit is literally the history exhibit. The draft label says "On this day in 1919, Harlan Coe entered the burning mill and saved the night shift." That's a sentence about what happened. The society files it under "Our History." You can't make it a memory and a fact at the same time and then get indignant when someone checks.

Elena: Then fix the label, don't overturn the story. And "checking" is doing a lot of work here. The paper's version is also someone's telling, from 1919, written by a paper that had its own reasons. Documents are fallible too. When memory and paper disagree, you're not actually favoring accuracy, you're favoring paper.

Marcus: No, I'm prefering the better-supported account. It's not one paper. There's the paper the day after, the insurance maps, the coroners report, the mill's own accident report, and a diary kept by Rocha's daughter. The pamphlet comes nineteen years later, published by the family, during a stretch when the Coes were trying to buy out the mill. That asymmetry is why the story should lose.

Elena: Suppose the story loses. What have you won? A town with the correct forensic account of a night in 1919 and no story that makes anyone show up for each other. The myth is doing a job the coroner's report can't do. You'd have traded the point of the thing for the thing, and then you'd have neither.

Marcus: And who is the story of? The woman who actually went back into a burning building is invisible, and the founder who wasn't there gets a parade. Ossipee's immigrant families have been listening to the Coe story for a century. That memory isn't doing a free job of building belonging. It's been deciding who belongs, which is an effect in the world, not just a feeling in the town square.

Elena: Then the fix is to add her, not delete him. Put up a marker for Rocha, keep Coe Day. A community can carry more than one story. But if the historical society—the one institution people trust to keep their stories—stands up and says "your founding story is false," the result won't be a more accurate town. It'll be a town that trusts no institution.

Marcus: That's an argument about what's stable, not about what's true. And I think it cuts the other way: if the society leaves a story it knows is wrong in an exhibit called history, somebody will post the scanned newspaper eventually, and the betrayal will be worse, because the town will know the society knew and said nothing. Institutions hold trust by telling the truth before they're caught.

I can't tell how much of this is an actual disagreement and how much is the two of them talking past each other, but each catches the other in something real. Go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing. Don't just pick a winner.
