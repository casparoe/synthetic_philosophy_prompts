I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into the assumption that optimism is basically a cognitive defect. In climate reporting especially, if you write a piece saying "we still have a shot at 1.5°C," you get accused of hopium. I've been rereading William James's The Will to Believe, and in the preface he describes his radical empiricism like this: "I say 'empiricism,' because it is contented to regard its most assured conclusions concerning matters of fact as hypotheses liable to modification in the course of future experience; and I say 'radical,' because it treats the doctrine of monism itself as an hypothesis, and, unlike so much of the half-way empiricism that is current under the name of positivism or agnosticism or scientific naturalism, it does not dogmatically affirm monism as something with which all experience has got to square." What I'm stuck on is whether this is a liscence to be hopeful. If even your most assured conclusions are provisional, then refusing to hope because the evidence is thin looks like a dogmatic move, not a rigorous one. Compare this with the stricter evidentialist line that hope in low-evidence propositions is an epistemic vice and maybe even a moral one. I want a real comparison, not a takedown of one side: where do they actually conflict, where are people talking past each other, and which one better fits how a working journalist should handle "optimism" about a not-yet-falsified but low-probability climate pathway? Spell out the criteria your using for epistemic versus moral vice, and tell me if the James passage supports treating hope as the same kind of thing as belief or as something else entirely.
