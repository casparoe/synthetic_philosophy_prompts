I'm a philosophy PhD student working on metametaphysics, and I've been trying to get clear about which philosophical disagreements are merely verbal and which are about something real. I've got a set of gut calls on particular cases, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind them — or, if there isn't, which of my verdicts I've got to give up.

The cases, as they strike me:

1. Two flatmates at the pub arguing about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. They agree on every fact about the bun, the sausage, how it all fits together. I think they're not realy disagreeing about anything.

2. Two metaphysicians disagreeing about a bronze statue and the lump of bronze it's made from. One says the statue and the lump are two distinct objects located in the same place; the other says there's just one object there, which is both a statue and a lump. They agree about every molecule. I still think their disagreement is genuine — one of them thinks the world contains two things in that region and the other thinks it contains one.

3. Two people arguing about whether a virus is alive. They agree on all the biology — how viruses replicate through host cells, that they don't have metabolic machinery of their own, the lot. One says 'alive' and the other says 'not alive'. I feel that's a line-drawing choice, not a dispute about the world.

4. Two people arguing whether someone's choice was free. They agree on the entire causal history of the decision and on the neuroscience. One says the choice was free because it flowed from the person's settled values; the other says it wasn't free because those values were themselves the product of an upbringing she didn't choose. I want to say this one is substantive — they're disagreeing about what freedom requires. But I can see this is in tension with what I said about the virus case.

5. Two people arguing whether a tomato is a fruit or a vegetable. They agree that it's a fruit botanically and a vegetable culinarily. I think the dispute is really about which classification we ought to use in the kitchen, which is a decision rather than a discovery — so not a substantive factual disagreement.

6. The one I keep going back and forth on: a nihilist who says there are no tables, just particles arranged tablewise, versus someone ordinary who says tables obviously exist. They agree on every particle-level fact. Sometimes I think the nihilist is simply refusing to count composite objects, which is a choice of shorthand; other times I think their disagreement is as real as the statue one.

My half-formed hunch is that a dispute is merely verbal just in case both sides would say the same things about the world if you gave them a richer vocabulary with a seperate word for each sense of the contested term. But when I apply that to the free-will case, it threatens to make that dispute look verbal too — I'd just say the two sides mean different things by 'free' — and I don't think I want to say that.

So: is there a single principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, stated precisely? What does it say about the tables case, and which of my verdicts does it force me to change? And could you throw a couple of new cases at it that I haven't listed, to show where it would break down?
