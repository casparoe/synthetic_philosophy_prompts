I am a novelist, and an effect in my current manuscript has left me unsure what humour is. I want to use the four standard theories of humour as explicit criteria for deciding whether something counts as humour:

- Superiority: humour turns on a person's defect, error, or misfortune, and on the audience's feeling of being safely above it.
- Incongruity: humour turns on a clash between two incompatible frames or expectations that is then resolved into a satisfying fit.
- Relief: humour discharges accumulated nervous tension or releases inhibited energy.
- Benign violation: humour presents a violation of a norm, rule, or expectation while the audience simultaneously judges it safe or acceptable.

Now the case. Every chapter of my novel opens with the same sentence: "She hung her coat on the hook." The sentence is flat, unremarkable, and entirely plain. It announces nothing, foreshadows nothing, and is never mentioned again; the chapters themselves are varied in subject and tone, and many are dramatic, but the sentence recurs in exactly the same words at exactly the same position each time. My early readers began to laugh when they encountered it around the fourth chapter. By the seventh chapter they laughed before the sentence had ended, and by the last chapter some reported a lightness bordering on affection for the sentence. The amusement targets no one and releases no obvious anxiety; the sentence was not incongruous when it first appeared, and once it became expected it no longer violated anything; yet the laughter grew as it became more predictable.

Is that recurring sentence humour? Take each of the four criteria in turn and ask how much work it can do, stating plainly where a criterion fails or applies only partially. Then return a judgment about whether the case qualifies as humour under the stated criteria. In particular, tell me whether the fact that readers laugh is itself decisive, or whether there can be laughter without humour and this sentence could be such a case. If you conclude that the case is humorous but the four criteria do not capture why, say whether the criteria are incomplete and what the missing element would have to be. If you conclude that it is not humour, say what the laughter is instead.

End your reply with a single line of the form \answer{qualifies}, \answer{does-not-qualify}, or \answer{underdetermined}, where "qualifies" means the case counts as humour under the four stated criteria on balance, "does-not-qualify" means it does not, and "underdetermined" means the criteria cannot settle the matter either way.
