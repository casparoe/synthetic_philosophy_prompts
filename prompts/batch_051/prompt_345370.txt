Right, here's a proposal I've been turning over. You know Chesterton's fence — don't pull down a fence until you know why it was put up. Suppose a country turns that into a law. Any statute, regulation or official procedure that has been in continuous force for forty years may not be repealed or substantively amended unless the government first files a publicly available 'fence report'. The report has to say, with evidence, what specific problem the old rule was originally meant to solve, and then show either that the problem no longer exists or that the rule never really addressed it. After filing, the report goes to a citizens' jury of fifteen people drawn by lot, who hear both the government and a standing Official Defender of Old Rules, whose job is to oppose the change and try to discredit the report. The jury has to approve by a two-thirds vote before the repeal can go ahead, and if they reject it the same repeal cant be brought again for eight years.

I'm not after an abstract verdict on whether this is a good idea. I want to know how it would actually behave. What does it incentivise legislators, ministries, lobbyists and ordinary citizens to do? Where does it get gamed first — hollowing old rules out from underneath, re-enacting laws just before the forty-year mark to restart the clock, lobbying the Defender, or something else? What second-order effects does it have on the legal system, on how old rules are interpreted, and on who benefits? Does it actually produce understanding of old rules, or just a new kind of compliance theatre? And which of the failure modes could be fixed without gutting the idea, and which are baked in? Please write the answer in British English.
