As a novelist, I spend a fair amount of time trying to inhabit inner lives unlike my own, and I recently found myself attempting a passage written from the point of view of a bee. That led me to the scale argument about insect welfare: even if the probability that any individual insect is sentient is low, the staggering number of insects means insect suffering could still dominate in expected-value terms. But I cannot see what stops this reasoning from applying far more broadly. If expected value is just probability multiplied by the number of affected beings, then trillions of plants, bacteria, or even inanimate systems ought to matter enormously as long as I do not assign their sentience a probability of exactly zero. Does the scale argument prove too much? Where should the line between a meaningful possibility and a merely logical one be drawn, and why does that line not collapse into fanaticism?
