I got into a long argument last night with a friend about whether a first-order state can be conscious all by itself, and I ended up defending a higher-order view: roughly, a mental state is conscious only if there's a higher-order state (a thought or perception) that represents you as being in that first-order state. So the redness I see is conscious because I'm aware of seeing it, not because the red representation is some special first-order kind; and states I'm not aware of are just unconscious. I'm not sure if I actually beleive this or if I just hated my friend's "consciousness is intrinsic to certain first-order states" line, which felt like it gave up on a real explanation.

I want to know how exposed that higher-order position is. What would count against it? I'm not just asking for the standard objections I already half-know (the worry that higher-order states could misfire and make you conscious of a state you're not in, or the infinite regress of needing a third-order state to make the second-order state conscious). I want a map of the view's exposure: which empirical findings, dissociations, or hypothetical cases would have to be true (or false) for it to fail, which commitments is it really staking itself on, and which of the counterarguments would a committed higher-order theorist actually concede count against them versus dismiss as theory-saving tape. Distinguish fatal from merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a theory of this sort. I'd also like to know where it makes predictions its rivals don't, and whether the animal/infant cases—where the view seems to say there's tons of unconscious perception until some higher-order capacity shows up—are a feature or a cost.

Whichever way you lean, tell me how confident you are in your assessment and single out the specific point you're least sure of.
