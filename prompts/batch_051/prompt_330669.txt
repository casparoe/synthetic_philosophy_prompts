Read the following passage from David Hume, "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757):

"The great variety of Tastes, as well as of opinions, which prevail in the world, is too obvious not to have fallen under every one's observation. Men of the most confined knowledge are able to remark a difference in the narrow circle of their acquaintance, even where the persons have been educated under the same government, and have early imbibed the same prejudices. But those who can enlarge their view to contemplate distant nations and remote ages, are still more surprised at the great inconsistence and contradiction. We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us."

This passage motivates Hume's search for a standard of taste. Later in the essay he proposes that the standard is the joint verdict of true judges, and he lists five conditions for a true judge: strong sense, delicacy of sentiment, practice in a given art, comparison across works, and freedom from prejudice.

Now consider this case. A distinguished calligrapher has spent forty years evaluating Chinese calligraphy. She has extraordinary perceptual delicacy—she can detect minute differences in brush pressure, ink viscosity, and stroke sequence—and her judgments within the tradition are widely trusted. She has examined thousands of works from the Tang through the Qing dynasties. But she refuses to study Japanese, Korean, or Western lettering, on the ground that only Chinese calligraphy is true calligraphy; she regards Japanese works as derivative and European scripts as not art at all. Within her tradition, she is exacting and articulate; outside it, she is dismissive and unwilling to compare.

Apply Hume's five conditions for a true judge to this case. Work through each condition, note where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether this case shows that Hume's criteria need refinement—or merely that a true judge is harder to find than the essay suggests.
