I'm writing a magazine piece about whether physics forces a picture in which the future is already as real as the past, and I got stuck trying to referee a back-and-forth between two physicists I'd interviewed separately and then brought together. I'll set the scene briefly, because I want a ruling on the argument itself, not a primer on the physics (I'm fine on the simultaneity facts).

The first physicist defended what she called the standard route to the block universe. Two people walking past each other in opposite directions will, by the usual special-relativistic rule for assigning simultaneity, disagree by days about which events are happening "now" in the Andromeda galaxy. Since neither walker's reference frame is physically privileged, and since reality shouldn't be relative to a frame, an event can't be already real for one walker and not yet real for the other. So the future is already there; the moving "now" is the thing that needs explaining. The second physicist answered that this is a slide from coordinates to ontology: relativity of simultaneity tells you how inertial frames assign time labels, not what exists, and "simultaneous in my frame" doesn't have to mean "real for me." She came back that this is evasive — the walkers are at the same place at the same time, so if their two "nows" are equally legitimate, there is no frame-independent fact about what is happening now anywhere, and the only way to avoid arbitrarily privileging one walker's slice is to treat all the slices as already real. He replied that this is too quick: the lesson might be only that there is no frame-independent global present, which is not the same as saying the future already exists. And he added that a genuine notion of becoming would more plausibly be tied to what can causally affect you — your past light cone — which is frame-independent and doesn't tilt when you change velocity, whereas the argument leans entirely on coordinate simultaneity. She said he was now smuggling his own metaphysics through the back door, because picking the light cone as the seat of "the real" is just as philosophical a choice as the block, and physics alone doesn't mandate it. I honestly can't tell who has the better of it.

Could you lay out a verdict for me? Go through there exchange — the five moves in order — and say who is right about which specific point. In the last item, also give the overall state of play, including anything both of them are missing. Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among philosophers of physics, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
