At a dinner party last week, two of my friends got into an argument that I would like you to adjudicate step by step. The game under discussion was this: a dealer puts two sealed envelopes on a table and tells a player only that one contains twice as much money as the other. In one variant the player must decide whether to switch without opening either envelope; in the other, the player opens one envelope, sees the amount, and then decides whether to switch.

The first friend defended the standard line. Once you open an envelope and see X, he said, the other envelope contains either 2X or X/2, and, with no further information about how the dealer chose the amounts, those two possibilities are equally likely; the expected value of switching is therefore (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, which exceeds X, so you should switch whatever you see. The second friend objected that this cannot be right, because before anything is opened the two envelopes are completely symmetric, and a policy of always switching is just a muddled way of taking the other envelope. He added that the 50/50 step quietly assumes something about the distribution of possible amounts, that no dealer can choose uniformly from zero to infinity, and that once an ordinary finite distribution is specified, seeing X changes the probability that you are holding the smaller versus the larger envelope. The first friend replied that the symmetry point applies only to the unopened variant: after seeing X you are conditioning on new information, the conditional probabilities need not be 50/50 under a fixed dealer rule, and the defensible remainder is that you should switch when the observed amount is low relative to what the dealer could plausibly have chosen and keep when it is high. The second friend answered that this is a much weaker claim, that under any ordinary distribution with finite expected values the unconditional expected gain of always switching is exactly zero, and that while a conditional policy can be profitable on the whole, it cannot make every possible observed amount an argument for switching; the 5X/4 result, he said, is what you get if you try to assign equal probability to the pair (X, 2X) for every positive X at once, which is not a normalizable distribution.

I could not tell how much either of them had actually landed a blow. Please go through the exchange in order and say who is right about each specific point. In particular, distinguish the opened from the unopened variant; spell out exactly what would have to be true about the dealer's rule for the 50/50 step to be justified; say whether the second friend is right that the 5X/4 formula must fail under any finite real-world rule, and what remains of the first friend's "switch low, keep high" intuition; and then say whether the residual disagreement is best understood as a disagreement about a mathematical fact, about how to model the dealer, or about how to act under ignorance of the dealer's rule.
