I've been going down a rabbit hole about calendar reform lately, partly because a friend keeps bringing up the Holocene calendar at dinner, and partly because I started reading Isaac Newton's The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended for fun. In the introduction Newton basically says the traditional Greek chronology is a mess and the only solid way to fix dates is to anchor them to astronomical events recorded in ancient sources — equinoxes, solstices, star positions — and then compute forward from there; he uses that to push the Argonautic expedition and a bunch of other events later than the received dates. That got me thinking: he was arguing about what an epoch should be anchored to, and a calendar's year zero is just a special case of that argument.

I'm planning a short seminar on the philosophy of measurement, and I'd like to hand my students a list of the desiderata that any proposed epoch for a calendar should meet before we even start debating specific candidates. Please write that list. For each requirement give the consideration or motivating case behind it, and say whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of some camp (astronomers, historians, religious communities, software engineers, and so on). The part I most care about is wheter some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, cultural neutrality vs. continuity with existing practice, or precision vs. simplicity — and which requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates like the birth of Jesus, the founding of Rome, the Big Bang, the Unix epoch, or the Holocene calendar. Skip the scorecard of contenders; I'd rather work through that with the group. The criteria are the real deliverable.
