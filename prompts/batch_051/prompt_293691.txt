I work as a software engineer on reliability for a large distributed system, and much of my job consists of deciding whether two anomalous events are instances of the same underlying fault or of different faults. I have developed a policy I have never fully examined. My rule is that I only group two anomalies under a single mechanism when I can already sketch the mechanism that would produce both — a race condition in a shared queue, a bad configuration flag, a poisoned cache — and I file them as unrelated one-offs otherwise. The rationale is that grouping anomalies without a causal story is how teams waste days chasing phantom correlations; most co-occurring anomalies are coincidences. But the rule now seems to have a built-in blind spot: it makes it impossible for any set of anomalies to count as the first evidence of a mechanism I don't already know, because grouping is only allowed after the mechanism has been stated. Yet without some gate of this kind, I would be buried in spurious tickets. Is my rule a defensible epistemic policy about when to treat anomalies as sharing a mechanism, or is it a bias disguised as triage? Under what conditions on base rates, error costs, and the ways mechanisms actually fail would the rule be rational, and is there a revised version that keeps the efficiency without making novel failure modes permanently invisible?
