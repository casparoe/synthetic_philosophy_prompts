Suppose that in 2035 a mid-sized wealthy democracy passes a law requiring every major social platform to run an automated "standing check" on any public post that accuses a named person of wrongdoing. The system scans the accuser's entire visible history—posts, photos, transactions it can legally buy, court records—for conduct "relevantly similar" to the accusation. If it finds a match—say a person denounces an embezzler and their own history shows padded expense reports—the platform attaches the label "blame without standing: accuser has done substantially the same kind of thing" and de-ranks the post into near-invisibility. Within a few years the label is picked up by mainstream media and treated by ordinary readers as a serious reason to discount any accusation.

The law is meant to enforce a widely held intuition: a hypocrite's blame is defective even when the accusation is true, because you can't uphold a standard you've flouted yourself. Fine. Now give me your best prediction of what blame looks like after forty years in this society. Don't tell a story; lay out the mechanisms and incentives. Who becomes the publicly effective blamers, and why? Does overall norm enforcement get stronger or weaker? What new strategies and genres of blame emerge—clean-record proxies, anonymous backchannels, laundering accusations through people with no visible history? Does hypocrisy decline, or wether does it just migrate somewhere the scanner can't see? Does the truth of an accusation start to matter less than the record of the accuser, and what does that do to public discourse?

Be explicit about your confidence at each link in the chain: which effects follow robustly from the incentives, and which are guesses. Also tell me what assumptions about the social function of blame—or about how people respond to being monitored—would have to be false for your main prediction to flip.
