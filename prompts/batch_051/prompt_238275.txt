I'm a novelist by trade—these days I mostly teach a small evening salon for adults, twelve people, some writers and a couple of retired engineers, and I've been asked to run a 90-minute session on what a gamble is worth. I'd like to sneak the Pasadena paradox in on them before they know it's a paradox at all. The version I mean is the coin-flip game where the payoff flips between win and loss and grows fast enough that the expected value is a conditionally convergent series: the same list of possible outcomes, added in one order, prices the game around 69 cents; added in another order, it prices it at $2, or at $500, or it just refuses to settle. I want the people in the room to feel that instability before I name it, not nod along while I explain it after the fact.

So: design me a game where the participants actually run small gambles and have to set fair prices for them, with two or three groups using different but equally reasonable ways of summing the same ledger of outcomes, so they come out with incompatible prices and have to argue it out. Points instead of money, maybe 45 minutes of play and a half-hour debrief, and the word 'Pasadena' shouldn't appear until the end. I'd like rules, a payoff table with numbers small enough that people can actually add them and orderings that visibly diverge, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto conditional convergence without me having to jargon them to death.

And be straight with me: what's the most predictable way this falls apart the first time I run it, and which feature of the real paradox a classroom version can't reproduce—what will I have to just come out and say? The group likes props, so slips of paper or little ledgers would help, and keep the tone light; some of them will freeze if they think their being tested on arithmetic.
