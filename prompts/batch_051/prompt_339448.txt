I have been trying to reconcile two conversations that both use the word "happiness" but seem to be talking past each other. On one side are the international happiness rankings—the World Happiness Report and similar league tables—which order countries by their citizens' average answers to a ladder question that asks people to rate their lives from 0 to 10, from the worst possible life to the best possible life. On the other side are ordinary judgments about whether a particular person is happy. Before I can decide whether those rankings are measuring the same thing ordinary speakers mean, and before I can decide whether a government should make it its goal to move up the table, I want a working definition of happiness itself. If you think the word is doing two or three distinct jobs, give me those two or three definitions explicitly, and say which of them the rankings are actually after.

Please test your definition against these cases, and tell me where it misclassifies, where it goes silent, and whether the right response is to revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. (a) A person with close friendships, engaging work, and ordinary daily enjoyments, who rates their life an 8 on the ladder. (b) A person in a major depressive episode, who takes no pleasure in anything, judges their life to be worthless, and can barely get out of bed. (c) A person who rates their life a 9 but weeps most days, suffers frequent panic, and would describe their typical morning in thoroughly miserable terms. (d) A person with a neurological condition that leaves their affect almost always bright and cheerful, though they have no close relationships, no projects, and no accomplishments. (e) A laborer in appalling conditions who rates their life a 9 because, long ago, they concluded that nothing better was possible and no longer compare their life with any alternative. (f) A political prisoner who rates their life a 3 and lives in fear and discomfort, but who regards their life as more meaningful and purposeful than ever before. (g) A new parent who is exhausted, anxious, and tearful, yet says on reflection that they are deeply happy. (h) A person inside an experience machine, whose pleasant moods and high life satisfaction are produced by an illusion.

Then turn the definition back on the rankings. Given that the rankings aggregate self-reported life evaluation across populations whose expectations, reference points, and constraints differ enormously, do they measure happiness as you have defined it? If not, what exactly do they measure? And what follows for policy: should a country try to top the rankings, or is there a better target that falls out of your definition?
