Something's been bugging me as I prep for my philosophy of math comps. Mathematicians today define continuity between topological spaces by saying that a function f is continuous iff the preimage of every open set is open. In metric spaces this is equivalent to the epsilon-delta definition, which is way more intuitive — small changes in the input produce small changes in the output. But the open-set definition is the one that gets treated as the real definition, and epsilon-delta becomes a special case or a teaching crutch. Same thing with groups: the modern definition is just a set with a binary operation satisfying four axioms, but groups originally showed up as collections of symmetries of a fixed object, and now the symmetry story is demoted to motivation. The more abstract, more general definition keeps winning. I'm trying to get clear on what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes about what a definition is for. Does it presuppose that the right definition is the one that generalizes farthest? That a definition's job is to make the important theorems — composition of continuous functions is continuous, quotient groups exist — come out cleanly? That mathematical objects are really structures up to isomorphism, so the symmetry definition and the axiom definition are just two presentations of the same thing? Or is the practice compatible with a bunch of different philosophies of mathematics, and am I overreading it?

I'd like the answer in these sections, in this order:

1. The practice — what mathematicians actually do when they choose the open-set and abstract-group definitions over the more concrete ones (~150 words).
2. What it strictly presupposes — the commitments you'd have to give up if you rejected the practice (~200 words).
3. What it merely fits with — views that sit nicely alongside the practice but aren't forced by it (~200 words).
4. Where it's underdetermined — places where the practice doesn't settle the philosophical question (~200 words).
5. Your verdict — wether the choice of definition carries real philosophical weight (~150 words).
