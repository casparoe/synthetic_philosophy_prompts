I am a novelist, and I have been reading a well-regarded book on grammar and style intended for professional writers. One of its chapters attempts to summarize the linguistic distinction between inflection and derivation, and, because I am naturally curious about what counts as the boundary of a word, I read it closely. The chapter's account runs roughly as follows.

Inflection, the author explains, is the set of endings a word takes on to fit the grammatical requirements of the sentence in which it appears: the plural -s on nouns, the past-tense -ed on verbs, the comparative -er on adjectives. Inflection never changes a word's part of speech, and it leaves the basic lexical meaning intact, contributing only grammatical information. Derivation, by contrast, constructs new words from existing material — runner from run, happiness from happy, redo from do — and typically changes the meaning, the part of speech, or both. The author then offers what he calls a straightforward test: an affix is derivational exactly when it changes the word's part of speech, and inflectional when it leaves the part of speech untouched. He concedes that the adverbial -ly, as in quickly, appears to create adverbs from adjectives and would therefore look derivational on this test; but since -ly is perfectly regular and predictable, he concludes that regularity, not part of speech, is the more reliable criterion in such cases. On that basis the chapter ends by advising writers to regard quickly not as a separate word from quick but as the same word inflected for adverbial use, just as walked is the same word as walk.

I have no training in linguistics, so none of this set off alarms on a first reading, but I have been burned before by writerly books that blur technical distinctions to make things digestible. I also have a practical reason to care: I am constantly told to cut -ly adverbs from my prose, and I would like to know whether I am deleting words or merely forms of words. Is the chapter's summary of the linguistics fair? I would be grateful for a claim-by-claim audit: which parts a morphologist would accept, which parts are common simplifications or outright mistakes, and whether the final advice about quickly actually follows from anything true in the account.
