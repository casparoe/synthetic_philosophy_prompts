I am a novelist working out a plot idea, and I want the philosophical structure to be sound. In the novel, a device exists that does not simulate a pleasant world. When activated — once, and irreversibly — it induces a durable state of Stoic-style apatheia: the user remains awake, cognitively intact, and in contact with the real world, but no longer experiences fear, anger, grief, strong craving, or intense attachment, and along with those loses much of the urgency that drives people to create, to love fiercely, and to take dangerous risks. The effects are not hidden: both before and after activating it, a person knows exactly which capacities will be lost and remembers them afterward.

Nozick's experience machine is usually refused partly because we want real contact with reality rather than agreeable illusion. This device appears designed to dodge that objection while keeping much of the trade-off. Work through how that change alters the analysis. Which of the standard reasons for refusing the experience machine still apply to an equanimity machine, and where do the two cases come apart? If the machine must be chosen once and cannot be undone, does the verdict change compared with a reversible trial period? And does the device deliver the Stoic ideal, or only an outward imitation of it?
