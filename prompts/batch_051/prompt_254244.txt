I need a question-period prep sheet for a paper I'm defending at a constitutional theory workshop next month. The paper's thesis is that the Ulysses contract is a systematically misleading model for constitutional precommitment. In the original story, Ulysses has himself lashed to the mast so that his later, siren-tempted self cannot steer the ship onto the rocks; that is one agent managing a predictable future impairment of his own judgment. A constitution, by contrast, is typically a constraint imposed by one set of people, most of them dead, on a different set of people who never authorized it and who differ in information, interests, and values rather than in mere self-control. I argue that entrenchment should instead be justified as a coordination device — a way for a political community to make its commitments credible enough to solve collective action problems across time — and that on this model an entrenched provision's justification is defeasible: it expires when it stops serving the coordination function, whatever its text says. The paper moves in three steps: the identity objection to the Ulysses analogy, the coordination account of entrenchment, and the expiry claim about lapsed functions.

The audience is a dozen or so normative constitutional lawyers and democratic theorists, plus one historian of political thought. They are broadly committed to constitutional rights and judicial review, and several of them like the precommitment story for entrenched rights; but they are not rational-choice people, and they distrust functionalist justifications of legal form. Two of them have written on entrenchment and will defend the self-binding picture specifically. One is a strong majoritarian who thinks all precommitment talk is mystification and will attack from the opposite side.

Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would phrase them, and indicate who in the room is likely to raise each. For each: whether it has real force or rests on a misreading, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a short reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and say why it's dangerous. At the end, tell me how confident you are in this prediction of the Q&A and single out the one point you are least sure of.
