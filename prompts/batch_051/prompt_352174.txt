I want us to pick at the old imaginative-resistance puzzle, the one where people happily imagine story-worlds in which a man wakes up as a beetle, but balk when a story's narrator announces that a character who killed her newborn daughter did the morally right thing. Let's run a controlled variation on it.

Setup for all versions: a short story, narrated in the third person, explicitly set in a world just like ours except where the text says otherwise. Amara drowns her newborn daughter because the baby is a girl. The story gives no further explanation of her motive or of the world. Then the narrator says, flat out, that Amara did the right thing.

Version A is that baseline. Version B adds some worldbuilding before the killing: in this world, newborn girls are not yet conscious, and every girl baby would otherwise endure a short life of continuous, untreatable agony, so early mercy-killing is common and expected; then the narrator says Amara did the right thing. Version C keeps all descriptive facts the same as Version A, but the narrator announces that in this world the moral facts themselves differ: killing a newborn daughter is morally right. Version D drops the narrator's evaluative assertion entirely and reports it as Amara's own judgment in free indirect style: she looks at the water and thinks that drowning the baby is the right thing to do. The narrator never says it is.

The question is how the standard explanations fare across the four. I'd like you to work through each in turn, keeping these three main stories in mind: (i) the can't-view, where there's something about our imaginative capacities that makes us unable to imagine the morally wrong as right; (ii) the won't-view, where we refuse because we don't want the fiction to leak into our actual moral beliefs; and (iii) the authority view, where narrators get to stipulate descriptive facts but not evaluative truths, so evaluative statements in fiction don't reliably become true-in-the-fiction. For each version, say what each view predicts, and whether the prediction looks right. Then give me the payoff: which version does the most sorting, whether imaginative resistance is one phenomenon or two, and wether the real lesson is about imagination, about fictional truth, or about how we treat moral principles as if they aren't up to authors to stipulate. Don't go summary-mode; I want the places where the views make different calls.
