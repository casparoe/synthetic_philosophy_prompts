Since I retired from a career in the natural sciences and began reading philosophy in earnest, one problem has come to trouble me more than its popular treatment usually admits: petitionary prayer addressed to a God who is both omniscient and immutable. If God already knows what I need before I ask, and if God cannot be changed by anything creatures do, then asking for a particular outcome appears either redundant or futile. When I put this point to a neighbor recently, he answered with the familiar saying "Prayer changes things," spoken as though that settled the matter.

I suspect that sentence is carrying more weight than he realizes, and I would like to know precisely what work it would have to do in order to meet my objection. Could you lay out the distinct claims that "Prayer changes things" might be making? For each reading, specify what it is that is said to change, in what respect it changes, and through what kind of mechanism: is prayer offered as an efficient cause of some outcome, as a condition on which an outcome depends, as a transformation of the one who prays, as an episode in a larger plan that itself never changes, or as something else? My training compels me to ask which variable is supposed to move, and by what pathway.

Then assess each reading against divine omniscience and immutability: which readings are compatible with those attributes, which collapse into one another under analysis, which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or merely evade the difficulty. Finally, tell me which reading or readings my neighbor would have to intend if his reply is to be more than a slogan, and whether the best such reading leaves petitionary prayer an intelligible practice rather than a psychological exercise in changing ourselves.
