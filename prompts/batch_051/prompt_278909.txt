I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been turning over something a panelist said at a conference on fiction and race, because it's messing with a manuscript. Her summary of the standard view went roughly like this: what makes a statement racist isn't what the speaker meant, it's what the statement does. A sentence can be racist even if the speaker didn't intend harm, because racism lives in how a line lands — in effects and uptake, not in private intentions. This is clearest with dog whistles: someone says something that sounds ordinary, like a phrase about law and order, and the in-group hears the coded message, so the real content of the line is whatever the target audience receives. So the practical test is whether it causes foreseeable offense or harm to the group its about; intention only matters for how angry to be at the speaker, not for whether the statement itself is racist.

That has the clean sound of a rule I could actually use. I have a scene where a white editor tells a Black subordinate she's "suprisingly articulate" and later calls a neighborhood "full of character now," and I keep trying to figure out whether the prose is just depicting the man or quietly endorsing him. Before I let the panelist's rule settle the page, I'd like an accuracy audit. Which parts of her summary are the actual consensus in the philosophical literature, which parts are shorthand a careful effect-based account would qualify, and where would a defender of that account say she got it subtly wrong? Does the practical takeaway — judge a line by its foreseeable effect on the group it points at — survive the corrections, or does it start to fall apart once meaning and effect are kept apart? And if treating lines I invented as if they were real utterances is itself a confusion, say so plainly rather than playing along. Also flag which of your corrections you're least sure of; I'd rather have a map of the dispute than a tidy verdict.
