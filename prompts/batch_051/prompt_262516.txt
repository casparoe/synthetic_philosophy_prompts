I am writing an essay that reads Villeneuve's film Arrival — and, where useful, Ted Chiang's novella "Story of Your Life" behind it — through the language of thought hypothesis. The film is usually read as a fable of strong linguistic relativity: Louise Banks learns the heptapods' written language and seems to acquire a genuinely new way of experiencing time. But the language of thought hypothesis, in its broadly Fodorian form, pushes in the opposite direction. It holds that learning a public language presupposes a pre-existing internal representational system into which the new language is translated, so a public language cannot by itself hand a thinker new powers of thought; whatever changes in Louise must be a change in that internal system rather than in the script she learns. Work through the film against this tension. Which scenes or details does a language-of-thought reading illuminate, and where does the film blur the very distinction between public language and an internal medium of thought? Does the non-linear structure, the mistranslated object the humans call a weapon, or Louise's alteration over the course of learning dramatize anything like a language of thought — or only the rival Whorfian claim? I want a reading faithful in both directions: do not treat the film as a mere illustration of the hypothesis, and do not distort the hypothesis to fit the film. Close by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Stay close to the plot and scenes as commonly known, and do not rest the essay on verbatim quotation or secondary literature.
