I'm a PhD student in political theory, and in three weeks I'm presenting a paper at our department's political philosophy workshop. The working title is Alignment Without Aggregation: Why an Aligned AI Should Be a Neutral Facilitator, Not a Utility Maximiser. The claim is that once a society has deep value pluralism, an AI system acting across that society shouldn't be built to maximise an aggregated social welfare function over everyone's preferences, because that forces the system to rank final ends that a liberal state has no business ranking. Instead the AI should be a neutral facilitator: it helps each person pursue their own conception of the good within fair terms, enforces only a thin layer of constraints like rights and non-manipulation, and when two people's legitimate ends genuinely conflict it defers to agreed fair procedures rather than trying to trade one off against the other. The audience will be mostly political philosophers, split between Rawlsian neutralists who are sympathetic to the conclusion but think I'm stretching a doctrine about state coercion to cover something quite different, and a smaller group of welfare economists and alignment researchers who think aggregating values is unavoidable the minute the AI has to choose between affecting one person's project and another's. There's also one committed Kantian who thinks the very idea of helping people pursue their ends already smuggles in a teleological picture of practical reason.

My argument sketch is this: (i) aggregating requires interpersonal comparisons of final ends, and those comparisons violate liberal neutrality; (ii) facilitation avoids this because it never ranks ends, it only checks wether a proposed action violates a neutral constraint; (iii) leftover conflicts are settled by fair procedures rather than first-order value rankings. I need a prep sheet, not a sympathetic restatement. Give me the objections I'm likely to get, roughly in order of how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually raise them. For each one: how serious the objection is, what I should concede there and then, what I should hold the line on, and the weakest spot in my own position I should have an answer ready for rather than hope nobody notices. Please also include the objection i'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me straight whether it's the one that kills the paper.
