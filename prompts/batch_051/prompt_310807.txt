A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about meritocracy, and one move she made has been bothering me ever since. The familiar criticism of meritocracy is that the winners do not deserve their rewards because the talents that win them are unearned: intelligence, charm, and the like are products of the natural and social lottery, so no one can take credit for having them. When I pressed this criticism, I asked why the same logic does not apply to effort. A person's capacity for effort, persistence, and conscientiousness is itself shaped by temperament, upbringing, and luck; one does not choose to be the kind of person who tries hard any more than one chooses to be quick-witted. She conceded the factual point but insisted that it made no difference: effort, she said, is still the person's own in a way that talent is not, so rewards that track effort can be deserved even though rewards that track talent cannot. That struck me as a double standard, and when I said so, she replied that any account of desert has to draw the line somewhere, and the line between what you do and what you merely are is the natural one.

I have been trying to work out whether this move has an established name and a serious defense, or whether it is only an ad hoc patch on the meritocratic position. I do not just want a label; I want the carving. What exactly is the move claiming: is it a claim about control, about ownership of one's actions, about a difference between doing and being, or something else? Is there an established position that matches it closely, and if the match is only partial, where exactly does it diverge? If nothing established fits, could you give the move a precise name and definition, and mark its boundaries with cases: would a naturally cheerful temperament that makes persistence easy count as the person's own, and if not, why not? Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
