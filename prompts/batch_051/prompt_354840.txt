I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I want a diagnosis of a debate that has been running in formal epistemology for decades without any visible convergence. The dispute is between two camps I will call precise and imprecise Bayesianism. The precise camp holds that a rational agent's doxastic state can and should be represented by a single probability measure over the relevant propositions: for each proposition, exactly one degree of belief. The imprecise camp holds that this is a distortion whenever the evidence is incomplete, ambiguous, or only loosely constraining; on their view, the agent's state is represented by a set of probability measures, and the width of that set tracks how far the evidence falls short of fixing the probabilities.

What puzzles me is how much the two sides share. Both accept the usual axioms for each individual probability measure. Both can represent an Ellsberg-style choice between a bet on an urn of known composition and a bet on an urn of unknown composition, and both can describe the preferences that ambiguity-averse subjects actually report. Both can describe, in the other's formalism, the phenomenon of dilation, where conditioning on a cell of a partition makes the set of probabilities spread rather than narrow, so that on the set-based picture an agent becomes less opinionated after receiving information. And both agree that a real decision requires some rule for acting: the precise camp maximizes expected utility, while the imprecise camp must add a decision rule for the cases in which the set permits multiple options.

Yet the debate has not moved. The precise side argues that once a decision rule is added, the set formalism either does no further normative work or conceals a precise probability that is doing the real work. The imprecise side replies that forcing a single measure manufactures a determinacy the evidence does not possess, and that rational permissiveness is exactly what sparse evidence warrants. Each side, in effect, accuses the other of answering a different question: the precise side thinks the imprecise side has stopped doing decision theory; the imprecise side thinks the precise side has conflated representing an epistemic state with committing to a betting policy.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is not one disagreement but several fused under a single heading: a disagreement about what a doxastic representation is for—description of a mind versus guidance for action—plus a disagreement about how far idealization may go, plus perhaps a verbal dispute about the word 'credence.' But I do not trust this diagnosis, since each side would deny that its disagreement is merely verbal or merely about exposition.

My question is diagnostic rather than a request for a verdict or a survey. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it verbal, substantive, empirical, normative, or an artifact of asking a single ill-posed question? Where do the two sides genuinely contradict each other, and where do they talk past one another? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, a split of the question into two, or the admission that none should be expected? The label by itself will not satisfy me; I want the structure of the impasse. At the very end, after your analysis, state the diagnosis in a single short phrase inside \answer{...}.
