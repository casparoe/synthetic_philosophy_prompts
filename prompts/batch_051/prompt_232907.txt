I co-lead a reading group whose members mostly come from LessWrong and adjacent rationalist forums. After several months on Bayesian updating, predictive processing, and model selection, they are comfortable saying that models can be useful without being literally true, but they still default to the assumption that the point of language is to represent the world and that a better vocabulary is one that represents more accurately. I want to spend one session on Rorty's antirepresentationalism, understood as the view that vocabularies are tools for coping with the world rather than attempts to represent it as it is in itself, and I need a teaching sequence rather than a content script.

Please specify: (1) what distinction or commitment must be secured first for this audience, if antirepresentationalism is to land as something other than the map/territory platitude they already accept or the idealism they will suspect it of being—and why that item must come first; (2) the order in which the component ideas should then be introduced, with the dependency relations among them; (3) a single worked example that carries the session, and the properties it must have if the discussion is not to lapse back into correspondence talk; (4) the misconceptions this group will predictably bring or form, in the order they are likely to arise, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off—including the objection that Newton's mechanics is not merely more useful than Aristotle's but more accurate, and the slide from denying representation to concluding that truth is just what the community currently agrees on; (5) an end-of-session check that would separate someone who has grasped contingency and antirepresentationalism from someone who has merely learned to repeat that we make truths rather than find them; and (6) what should be deliberately left out at this level, with a reason for the omission.
