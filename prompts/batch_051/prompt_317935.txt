I am a novelist, and I am currently drafting a book in which a provincial magistrate — a person of genuine, even scrupulous conscience — must sentence a young offender for a crime that will define both their lives. The work has forced me into a problem I can no longer treat as mere background, and I want it argued out rather than dramatized. In building the character, I have come to feel the real force of a certain retributive argument about eternal punishment. It runs something like this: the gravity of a wrong is not only a matter of the harm done or the act chosen, but also of the standing of the one wronged; to strike a judge is worse than to strike a stranger, not merely because of consequences but because of what the act reaches. If the one wronged has infinite dignity, then the wrong has a kind of infinite gravity, and a wrong of infinite gravity may warrant — perhaps even require — a punishment without limit. The argument has the virtues that draw me to any theory: it treats proportionality as a real constraint rather than an afterthought, it explains why some wrongs are graver than others along a single scale, and it does not smuggle in mercy by pretending that what is deserved is somehow not deserved.

And yet, as soon as I write the magistrate's sentence, I run into an intuition I cannot shake: a finite creature, in a finite life, cannot do anything that makes it just that she should suffer forever. I do not mean merely that endless suffering would be disproportionate if we have already granted some independent commitment to finitude; I mean that I cannot keep the thought standing — that a punishment without limit ceases to be a punishment and becomes a condition, and that desert, whatever else it is, must be capable of being discharged. So I am caught. I want you to present both the retributive argument and this intuition at their strongest, and then give me a negotiated verdict. Which should yield, and why? Walk me through what each option costs: dropping the intuition, perhaps by diagnosing it as a failure of moral imagination about duration; amending the theory so that infinite gravity permits but does not mandate endless torment, or yields annihilation rather than torment; abandoning the retributive framing in favor of universal reconciliation, in which everyone is ultimately saved; or reinterpreting the punishment so that the contradiction turns out to be apparent rather than real. I would like you to say what would justify each of these moves, and where the disagreement among the three outcomes — eternal torment, annihilation, universal salvation — actually turns, as opposed to where it merely changes the subject. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in a short phrase which element should yield — the intuition, the theory, or neither — or, if you conclude that no yielding is required, says so.
