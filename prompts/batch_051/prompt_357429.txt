Lately I've been trying to get clear on what the old Buridan's ass puzzle actually shows about the difference between choosing and merely ending up with one of two options, and I'd like you to work through a modern version of the puzzle with me. I'll state my criteria for when a selection counts as an agent's choice made out of a preference, then describe a borderline case and have you test it against the criteria.

My working criteria for counting some event as a choice are:

1. There is a system that it is reasonable to model as having a real-valued utility over outcomes, or at least a coherent preference ranking in something like the revealed-preference sense.
2. At the moment of selection, at least two options are open to it.
3. Which option gets selected is produced by the system's evaluation of the options, rather than by a process that is exogenous to that evaluation.
4. Counterfactual sensitivity: if the system's evaluation of the two options were swapped, the selected option would swap as well.
5. Label-independence: whether an option happens to be listed first does not determine the outcome.

Now the borderline case. Consider a small autoregressive language model at a single decoding step. Its softmax distribution over the vocabulary assigns exactly equal probability to two candidate next tokens, call them t1 and t2, and a pseudorandom number generator seeded by the system clock breaks the tie. Does the model choose t1 over t2? Does it prefer t1 over t2? Next, consider t3, whose probability exceeds that of t1 and t2 by a tiny epsilon; the model selects t3 deterministically. Is the selection of t3 a choice while the tie-break was not? If so, how small can the epsilon get before preference and choice disappear, and is that threshold a fact about the model or about my criteria?

I'd like you to go through my five criteria one by one and say whether the model's selections in the tied case and the epsilon case satisfy each criterion, flagging wherever a criterion underdetermines the verdict rather than simply settling it. Then tell me whether either case shows that one of the criteria should be dropped or weakened. In particular, I want to know whether my criteria rule out choice without preference by definitional fiat, so that the Buridan-style case cannot get off the ground for me, and whether the language-model example is best described as choice without preference, as preference too weak for us to detect, or as something non-agential passing through a stochastic process.

I also have a recurring suspicion, inherited from the rationalist line that ties have measure zero and the puzzle is manufactured, that the t1/t2 case is my attempt to construct a real tie. Please say explicitly whether you think a real tie has actually been constructed here, or whether the equal-probability framing hides a sleight of hand.
