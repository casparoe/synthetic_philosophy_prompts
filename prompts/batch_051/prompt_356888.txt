Last month I started working in a behavioral-research lab, and on Tuesday two of the researchers there spent the whole lunch arguing about a study one of them is designing, until neither would budge. The study pays participants to play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with the usual payoff structure, for real money: no communication, no reputation, no repeated play. Its distinctive feature is that each participant first completes a long questionnaire battery about how they reason—risk tolerance, patience, reciprocity norms, long-term planning, and similar dispositions—and a matching algorithm pairs each participant with the person whose profile is most similar to their own; both players are told, truthfully, that they were paired with each other because they make decisions in a very similar way.

The argument went, in compressed form, like this. (1) Amara argued that the rational play is to cooperate. Defection dominates only against an unrelated stranger; here, because your counterpart reasons like you, your own choice is strong evidence about what they will do. If you cooperate, you should expect cooperation with high probability; if you defect, you should expect defection. Conditional on having been matched this way, cooperation has the higher expected value. (2) Ben replied that this confuses evidence with influence. By the time you choose, the other player's decision is fixed by their own brain, and nothing about your button-press can reach across rooms to change it. Cooperating cannot cause a good pairing; it can only supply you with reassuring news about a pairing you were already dealt—the decision-theoretic analogue of buying the newspaper only for the good news it might contain. Since defection pays strictly more than cooperation whatever the other does, defection dominates. (3) Amara answered that the causal structure is not so tidy. The experimenters deliberately made your decision procedure and your counterpart's decision procedure correlated through a common cause, the matching algorithm—just as in Newcomb's problem, where your decision procedure and the predictor's prediction share a common cause. The boxes are already filled, yet many careful decision theorists still one-box; in setups like this, the rational policy is to treat your own choice as diagnostic of the correlated choice, because otherwise you systematically forfeit exactly the cases the design selects for. (4) Ben came back that Newcomb's predictor is stipulated to be highly accurate after studying you in detail, whereas questionnaire matching yields only a weak and crude similarity—likely too weak for cooperation to pay even by Amara's own expected-value arithmetic—and that one-boxing is itself contested. (5) Amara then changed register. Grant that this particular algorithm is crude, she said; in a sufficiently large world there are bound to be enormously many agents whose decision procedures resemble yours closely, and your choice here is evidence about what that whole cohort does in counterpart situations. If cooperating is the policy that cohort adopts, the gains from the many mutually cooperative pairings dwarf the losses from cooperating against an occasional defector, so a single local encounter is the wrong unit of evaluation. (6) Ben said that this makes the rationality of a two-button game hostage to cosmology, and that a convinced defector who believed the universe contained no near-copies of them could, on Amara's principles, reply that defection really would be rational there—which just shows the principle has stopped being about the game in front of you.

They turned to me for a referee's view, and I could not give one; but I need to understand the issue because I'll be helping with the analysis plan for this study. Please go through the exchange move by move: indicate how far each move is right or wrong, who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches, and what both of them are missing. Then give a defended overall verdict on the rational play in the study as actually designed.
