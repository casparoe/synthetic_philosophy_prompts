I've recently begun building Bayesian forecasting models at work, and a colleague walked me through an argument I cannot shake, because its conclusion seems to be that the choice of prior is not a real choice at all.

(1) Two forecasters share the same likelihoods — the same model of how the data would look under each hypothesis — but begin with different priors.
(2) Both update by conditionalization on a common, growing stream of evidence.
(3) For priors that do not rule the true hypothesis out in advance, the standard Bayesian convergence results say that each forecaster's posterior probability for the true hypothesis converges to 1 as evidence accumulates.
(4) So, with probability 1, any two such priors produce posterior distributions that eventually become arbitrarily close to one another and to the truth.
(5) A difference between two starting points that is almost sure to vanish as evidence accumulates is not a difference that matters for rational belief.
(6) Therefore, no admissible prior is rationally significant: the evidence eventually washes out any prior that doesn't flatly exclude the truth.

I cannot accept (6). It seems to prove too much: it would make it rationally fine to start with a prior that is extraordinarily overconfident in a false hypothesis, as long as that hypothesis isn't assigned exactly zero — on the grounds that the data will eventually correct it, even if "eventually" arrives long after every decision I will ever make. The same logic suggests that the likelihood side of the model doesn't matter either, provided the model contains the truth somewhere in its support, since the data will likewise wash out a bad likelihood. Yet each step from (1) through (5) seems hard to resist. I would like to keep the idea that evidence can in principle overwhelm a prior, but I also want which prior I choose, and when I have to act, to matter substantially. Where does the argument go wrong? If I have to reject a step, which one, and what does rejecting it cost me?
