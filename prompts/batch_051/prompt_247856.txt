I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have ended up in a reading group that keeps circling the same question: what actually causes a famine? Two positions have crystallized, and the debate keeps drifting because the two sides are not writing their claims down precisely. I want to produce the document that solves this the way an incident postmortem does in engineering: record what both sides already agree on, make the residual disagreement exact, and list the tests that would settle it.

The two positions are as follows.

The availability camp holds that famines are, at bottom, natural disasters. An environmental shock—drought, blight, pest swarms, flood—destroys the food supply in a region. Policy can make the resulting mortality better or worse, but the primary event is a collapse in the amount of food available, and no administration, however competent, can conjure calories the land did not produce.

The entitlement camp holds that famines are, at bottom, political failures. What kills people is not usually a shortage of food in the aggregate but a collapse in their ability to command food—through wages, land, assets, or transfers. Because those things are set by property rights, markets, and state action, the shape of a famine is political the whole way down. This side, associated with Amartya Sen's approach, notes that severe hunger can occur even while food is available nearby, because some people have lost the entitlement to command it.

I want the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The camps have agreed to take seriously three cases that are often cited—Bengal in the 1940s, Ireland in the 1840s, and the famine associated with the Great Leap Forward—but I want the statement to be a conceptual document, not a trivia contest. Please define technical terms as you go, and do not rely on precise statistics from memory. Structure the response in exactly this order:

1. Agreed Claims. Ten to twelve numbered claims, each specific enough to be non-vacuous and phrased so that both camps would sign it. (About 400 words.)

2. Disagreement Table. A table with one row per live disagreement. Columns: (a) the issue, phrased neutrally; (b) the exact wording the availability camp would accept as the most charitable statement of its own view; (c) the exact wording the entitlement camp would accept as the most charitable statement of its own view; (d) why resolving this row would matter. Aim for six to eight rows. (About 600 words.)

3. Cruxes and Tests. Identify the three to five sub-questions such that a good answer to them would move at least one camp. For each, state the kind of evidence or historical comparison both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction of what that evidence would show. Present this as a table. (About 500 words.)

4. Residual Statement. A paragraph of four to six sentences beginning "We jointly certify" that both camps can sign, stating what remains unsettled and under what conditions each side would change its mind. (About 150 words.)
