I have been rereading Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography, and I keep coming back to the section where he describes his scheme for moral perfection. As you probably remember, he sets out thirteen virtues, keeps a weekly scorecard, and tries to acquire them one by one, only to find that the work is harder than he expected. Then he backs off. He tells a story, more or less from memory, about a man at a grindstone who is offered a completely bright ax and answers that he prefers a speckled one; Franklin extends the image to the moral life and suggests that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, since a character with no specks may be less useful or less agreeable to the people around him.

What interests me is the asymmetry this seems to license. Franklin was, in almost every other part of his life, an extremely exacting improver: he kept records, revised his writing, ran experiments, and treated errors as things to be eliminated. Yet in the domain he himself regarded as most important—his moral character—he not only conceded failure but eventually produced a principled-sounding rationale for leaving himself deliberately imperfect. This is not just Franklin. A programmer who would be ashamed to ship a known bug will still say, “I’m loyal except in that kind of case,” or “I’m just not very patient, and that’s who I am,” and treat the flaw as part of a rich human character. Is there any real justification for this asymmetry, or is it mostly motivated reasoning—polishing what is visible and socially rewarded while sparing the private defects that are expensive to repair? Construct the strongest defense of Franklin’s speckled-ax position you can, then test it against nearby cases: a physician who knowingly leaves a flaw in her diagnostic method because it would take too much effort to fix; a friend who is scrupulously honest except when he is flattered; a scientist who protects a known bias because it makes her bolder in generating hypotheses. Say which of those cases, if any, should get the same leniency Franklin gives himself, and tell me whether you would keep, revise, or reject the asymmetry.
