I have recently taken on the chair of a small citizens' assembly in my borough, and in trying to work out what our assembly is for I have found myself deep in Spinoza's political writings. Spinoza keeps coming out both more democratic and less liberal than I expected, and I want help pressing a scholar on exactly those points. I am moderating a recorded Q&A with a political theorist who has just published a book arguing that Spinoza's account of democracy, drawn mainly from the Theological-Political Treatise and the unfinished Political Treatise, is a live alternative to both Hobbesian and liberal accounts of popular government rather than a historical curiosity.

Write me ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give. The questions should go beyond restating the view and should press the real fault lines. I want at least one question each on the following:

- the equation of right with power, and what it does to the bindingness of democratic decisions once the multitude's power shifts;
- Spinoza's claim that in democracy one transfers one's natural right to the community as a whole, and whether this genuinely preserves equality or merely relabels subjection;
- the defence of freedom of thought and speech in the Theological-Political Treatise, and its limits – where exactly the sovereign may still silence speech;
- the unfinished democracy chapter of the Political Treatise, especially the exclusion of women and the question of who counts as part of the people;
- whether a Spinozist democracy can survive deep religious or philosophical pluralism, given his willingness to let the sovereign shape public religion;
- and how the account handles factions, majority tyranny, and the gap between the assembled people and the day-to-day exercise of power.

For each question, make the follow-up push against the response the guest is likeliest to give – the scholarly distinction they usually deploy, the textual evidence they reach for, or the passage they hedge about. Where you can, build the sequence so that earlier questions strip away the guest's easier replies before the later questions arrive. Do not include any generic questions about the guest's biography or current events; do not ask for a bibliography, page references, or verbatim quotations; paraphrase any doctrine you invoke rather than putting words in Spinoza's mouth. The entire response must be no more than 1,000 words.
