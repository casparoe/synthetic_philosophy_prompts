I've come around, mostly from reading Confucian role ethics, to a view I didn't expect to hold: that a person just is the network of roles they occupy—daughter, teacher, neighbor, friend—and that there's no role-independent self behind them, standing in a neutral spot and deciding which roles to endorse. The norms come with the roles, and the idea of stepping outside every role at once to ask which ones are worth having is itself a fantasy of a self that doesn't exist. I find this pretty attractive, honestly. But I want to know what would count against it, and I don't just mean the standard line that it's conformist or can't explain rebellion. Give me the cases that would put real pressure on someone already committed to the view: a role assigned without consent, like being made the family caregiver; a role that conflicts with another in a way the roles themselves can't settle; someone who throws off all their roles and describes what's left in a way that sounds like a bare self. Sort these for me into decisive, merely awkward, and things a role ethicist should shrug of. And here's the thing I actually worry about: the view might be insulated—every apparent counterexample can be re-described as just another way roles have shaped you, so nothing could count against it. Is the view as I've stated it actually unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a bug in a view about the self, or more like a feature?
