In my comparative economic systems class we just did the socialist calculation debate, and there's something I keep going back and forth on. Everyone in the class (me included, honnestly) has no trouble saying a fully centrally planned economy can't work because planners could never gather the dispersed, tacit knowledge that market prices condense—roughly the Mises and Hayek line. But then we talk about firms and nobody blinks: a big firm like Toyota or Walmart is basically a centrally planned island, where managers allocate labor and capital by command, with only a few internal prices here and there, and we don't treat it as impossible or irrational. If the knowledge problem makes planning without prices unworkable, why doesn't that same argument make firms unworkable? Or if command works fine inside a firm, why does the argument suddenly become fatal once you cross the firm boundary? I'm trying to figure out whether there's a principled justification for this asymmetry—maybe firm size, exit, competition, the discipline of capital markets, something about residual claimancy—or whether we're just drawing an arbitrary line. What's the best way to hold both judgments, or should we stop holding one of them?
