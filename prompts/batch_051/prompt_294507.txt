I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and much of my work sits where behavioral science meets environmental policy. Over the past year I have been collecting my own reactions to stories about people who consume very little and people who try to get other people to consume less. I expected my reactions to line up behind a straightforward instrumental view — that frugality matters only through its downstream consequences. They do not, and I want help extracting the principle I seem to be applying.

Here are five cases with my verdicts.

1. The inert ascetic. A retired engineer lives on roughly a third of his pension. He patches his clothes, keeps his flat cold, and cooks plain meals from bulk staples. He neither gives the surplus away nor advertises his habits; the unspent money sits in a checking account, and his lower use of power and water produces no detectable change in anyone else's consumption. My verdict: his way of living is genuinely admirable, not merely eccentric.

2. The effective spendthrift. A climate advocate flies often, runs a large well-heated house, and replaces her devices frequently. She has also been the decisive voice behind a building-retrofit program that will save far more energy than a thousand households' private economies could. My verdict: her advocacy is very good, and her private wastefulness is a real defect in her — a defect that does not erase the good she has done, but that I cannot treat as irrelevant.

3. The costly bargain. A household buys the cheapest available appliances, which fail every two years. Over a decade the family spends more money, consumes more materials, and discards more goods than a neighbor who bought one well-made set. My verdict: I do not call this frugality, and I do not admire it, even though it involves constant self-denial and small outlays.

4. The effective giver. A software engineer lives on two-thirds of her salary and gives the rest to a well-evidenced malaria-prevention charity. My verdict: her thrift is about as clearly good as anything I can name, and here the value of the frugality seems inseparable from what it buys.

5. The two laundry-hangers. Two neighbors with identical houses hang their laundry to dry all year. The first does it because she hates waste; the second does it only because it plays well on her lifestyle blog and otherwise would use the dryer. Their environmental effects are the same. My verdict: I admire the first more, and I am reluctant to say the second has practiced frugality at all, though I cannot point to any difference in outcomes.

I would like to know whether there is one principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures all five verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can see which case motivates each clause. If no clean principle gets all five, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn and why. Then apply the principle you favor to a case I have not settled: a city pays residents for each kilowatt-hour they save, and a woman who signs up cuts her use by a fifth only because the payments make it worth her while. Is she frugal, merely responding to a price, or something else? And does it matter that the program's designers are promoting frugality rather than practicing it?
