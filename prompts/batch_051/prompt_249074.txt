I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and for the past year I have been reporting a long-form piece on the ethics and rationality of bringing children into existence. One idea has come to structure nearly every interview I do: the procreation asymmetry. As I understand it, the view has two faces. The first is a claim about reasons: a future child's likely suffering is a strong moral reason not to create that child, while a future child's likely happiness is no moral reason, or at most a very weak one, to create that child. The second is a claim about the value of nonexistence: the absence of pain in a life never lived is good, while the absence of pleasure in a life never lived is not bad.

I have become genuinely convinced of this, and I have started to worry that I can no longer see what would un-convince me. Before I let the asymmetry do so much work in the piece, I want to know what would count against it.

What would have to be true for the procreation asymmetry to be false, or at least misdescribed, overextended, or reducible to a more familiar principle? I am asking for a map of the position's exposure rather than a survey of the debate. In particular:

Which precise judgments or commitments does the asymmetry actually stand or fall with? I suspect the two faces can come apart, and I want to know which one is load-bearing.

What cases, arguments, or results would count against each face — for example, cases where the reason to create a happy life seems to survive reflection, or where the value of nonexistence begins to look like a disguised valuation of the welfare of actual people?

Which defeaters would be decisive, showing that the asymmetry collapses into something like a general priority of harm-avoidance, a person-affecting principle, or a debunking explanation in terms of status quo bias or loss aversion?

Which defeaters would merely be awkward — pressure points that a defender of the asymmetry would concede count against it but would try to absorb?

How insulated is the view, and is the insulation a problem? Does it make risky predictions, or has it been protected by qualifications?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish those that committed defenders would themselves admit as counting against the asymmetry from those that only its critics emphasize. If the honest assessment is that the position is well insulated, say whether that insulation is a defect or a virtue. The single most persuasive thing for me would be a demonstration that the asymmetry is an artifact of how the cases are framed — that it thins out or reverses once the comparison is posed differently — but I do not want that possibility to drive the ranking.
