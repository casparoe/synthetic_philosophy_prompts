Clinical genetics is built on a tension between two commitments. On one side, the field treats informed consent as foundational: patients and research participants are entitled to clear and comprehensive information about tests, risks, and results, and a decision made without that information does not count as valid consent. On the other side, the field has institutionalized a right not to know. A person at risk for a serious, incurable, late-onset condition such as Huntington's disease may decline predictive testing. A research participant may opt out of receiving incidental findings even when sequencing has already revealed a pathogenic variant. Some clinics keep standing instructions about which results a given patient does not want to receive. These refusals are honored as legitimate exercises of autonomy rather than corrected as mistakes.

In the background sits Good's theorem on the value of information. Roughly, it says that an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization cannot be made worse off in expectation by cost-free information: the agent can always ignore the information, and having the option to act on it cannot reduce the best attainable expected outcome. On that picture, refusing free information looks like an error unless the information itself carries costs.

What theory of the value of information does the practice of honoring such refusals presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. In particular, does the practice presuppose that information can have negative value even when it is free, because it carries psychological or social costs? Does it presuppose that people are not, or ought not to be treated as, expected-utility maximizers in this domain? Does it presuppose a distinction between information that bears on an external decision and information that bears on a person's self-understanding or identity? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the places where the practice is underdetermined among rival theories. Finally, explain what would have to change in the practice — in consent procedures, in how clinicians frame refusals, in the handling of incidental findings — if the field adopted the Good's-theorem view straightforwardly, treating free, decision-relevant information as something a rational agent should not decline.
