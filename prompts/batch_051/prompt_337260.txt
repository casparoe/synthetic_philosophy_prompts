I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if this is old hat. I've started to suspect that the heart of my work isn't inventing anything new but adjusting a small set of paramaters on a pattern I already have—shift the register, stretch the timeframe, swap the social role of one character, replay the same scene in three different emotional keys. The 'new' scenes that impress readers are almost always the ones where I did the least wholesale invention and the most of this fine-grained variation. The same seems true of the art I admire: a jazz solo on a standard, variations on a folk tune, genre writers ringing changes on a formula. I don't just mean the generic point that creativity combines old elements. I mean something narrower: that variation on an already-articulated structure is the core act, and the 'original' theme is mostly an excuse to start adjusting dials. Is there an actually established name for this view, and how close is it to what I'm describing? I'm less intersted in a label than in the carving—what this idea is, what it's not, and where it stops being true (say, when a variation becomes a genuinely new theme). If nothing established fits, give it a name and define the boundary cases. Keep your answer to one paragraph, 250 words at most.
