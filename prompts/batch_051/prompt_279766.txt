I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last month I sat in on a talk by a philosopher who works in something called epistemic utility theory. Her pitch, as best I could follow it, was that there is exactly one fundamental standard for how confident a person ought to be: their credences should be as accurate as possible — on average, as close to the truth as possible — where closeness is measured by a proper scoring rule. (A forecaster who says 70% chance of rain and it rains is penalized less than one who says 99% on that rule, assuming you check enough days.) She claimed every other epistemic virtue — coherence, responding to evidence, humility, not being overconfident — either follows from this single accuracy norm or is just a rough practical shortcut to it. I've been turning it over since, and I honestly cant tell whether this is a deep claim about the mind or a definitional trick.

So here's what I want to work out. Suppose I ran a large forecasting tournament: people assign probabilities to a hundred real geopolitical and weather events over a year, and I score them with a proper scoring rule. Could that tournament, or some other observation, actually test whether accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic norm? Design the strongest test you can. Say what outcomes would favor the accuracy-first picture and what outcomes would undermine it. And be honest about what even a clean positive result would leave unsettled — for example, whether we have shown that accuracy is the only norm or just built that conclusion into the score.
