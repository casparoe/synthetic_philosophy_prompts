While writing a novel in which an entomologist loses her nerve in a dusted lettuce field, I worked out an argument for her and have been unable to find the bad step. Set the exact numbers aside as illustrative; what worries me is the reasoning. It goes:

(1) Suppose, as a deliberately low estimate, that the probability a given insect is sentient is one in a billion.
(2) If an organism has probability p of being sentient, then, other things equal, destroying that organism has an expected moral cost equal to p times the cost of destroying a sentient being.
(3) From (1) and (2), destroying a trillion such organisms has the expected moral cost of destroying a thousand sentient beings.
(4) An ordinary industrial farm destroys at least a trillion insects in a season.
(5) A practice whose expected moral cost is a thousand sentient deaths is a serious evil unless it prevents an evil of comparable or greater size.
(6) The crop being protected does not prevent an evil comparable to a thousand sentient deaths; it merely supplies ordinary food at ordinary margins.
(7) Therefore ordinary agricultural insecticide use is a serious evil, despite the low probability that any particular insect killed is sentient.

Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise would you reject first? If it is invalid, state the exact step at which it fails and the kind of mistake involved.
