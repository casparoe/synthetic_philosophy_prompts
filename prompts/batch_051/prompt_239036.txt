Next month I'm giving a paper to our department's political philosophy workshop, working title "Equalising capability resilience: why the equality-of-what debate has been indexing the wrong object". I've read enough LessWrong/ACX over the years to know the actual risk is pattern-matching to generic seminar pushback insted of prepping for the specific people who'll be in the room.

The thesis, in one paragraph. The equality-of-what debate (welfare vs resources vs capabilities) mostly assumes the equalisandum is something a person holds at a time. I claim the thing we should be equalising is a property of a person's capability set over time, namely its resilience: how quickly and how fully someone's set of effective freedoms recovers after a standard battery of shocks (redundancy, illness, bereavement, divorce, onset of disability). Equality of capability resilience means two people can have quite different current menus of options and still be equally well off for purposes of distributive justice, provided their option sets are equally robust to the same shocks. The more policy-facing claim is that when you measure equality this way, a lot of policies that look merely sufficientarian (income floors, insurance mandates, public health capacity) turn out to be egalitarian in the relevant sense, and a fair amount of cross-sectional equalising turns out to be beside the point. My argument sketch: (1) each standard equalisandum was introduced partly to cope with uncertainty and adaptation, but none of them can track both without collapsing into ex ante expected welfare; (2) resilience is what those moves were really gesturing at; (3) resilience can be compared, at least partially, via recovery-time and option-density measures without requiring a complete ranking of capability sets.

The room. Our department is analytic political philosophy. There are two capability people (one very Nussbaumian about central capabilities and thresholds, one more Senian and sceptical of any fixed list), two or three luck egalitarians who think Dworkin and Arneson basically won the equality-of-what debate in the 90s, one relational egalitarian who keeps asking why everyone ignores Elizabeth Anderson, and an economist visitor who is a prioritarian and will want to know why this isnt just expected utility with extra steps. Nobody in the room doubts that equality matters; they'll disagree hard about what the metric should even be tracking and how much precision a metric needs.
