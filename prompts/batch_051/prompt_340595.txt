I'm a science journalist and I have a rule I have been enforcing on myself for years, and my editor is convinced it's just a tic: I wont put a single number on anything deeply uncertain. No '37 percent,' no 'more likely than not' if I can help it, no point estimate at all. If a modeler gives me a range, I print the range. If a scientist says a new variant is 'probably more transmissible,' I push them until I get a bracket — 'somewhere between 10 and 40 percent,' for instance — and if they won't give one, I write that the group would not put a number on it. My editor says readers need a number in the headline and that my intervals are really just me refusing to commit, and she has a point. I do think almost every point estimate in this beat is false precision, and I think a crisp number makes readers overconfident no matter how many caveats I heap on it.

The thing I actually want to be told is whether the rule survives its own hard cases. When two sources give me different intervals — one says 10 to 25 percent, another says 20 to 40 — my instinct is to report the union, 10 to 40, never to average them or split the difference, because averaging two guesses seems to invent information. But then I end up with a range so wide it tells a reader almost nothing, and I'm not sure I can justify treating 39 percent and 11 percent as equally live just because they both fall somewhere between the brackets. And when my own editor forces a call — is it safe to fly in a bad season, should the lede use a high or low figure — I catch myself using the midpoint, which is exactly the thing I just told the modeler was meaningless.

So, for a column on how reporters ought to handle uncertainty: is my no-point-estimate policy defensible as a matter of epistemology, or is it a bias wearing a rule's clothes? If a cleaned-up version is defensible, what should that version say about combining intervals from different sources, and about what to do when a decision or a headline forces a single number out of me? Please answer in American English.
