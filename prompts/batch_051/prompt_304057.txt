I run a discussion group whose members mostly come from the rationalist and effective-altruism end of the internet, and I want an instrument that sorts people into positions in the moral-demandingness debate on the strength of their verdicts about cases, not on the labels they reach for. Please write me a decision tree to that end, in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as one would write an email or a letter, and with no citations or further reading; I want the instrument itself, not a syllabus.

The question the tree should peg itself to is this: how much, and in what shape, may morality demand of an ordinary affluent adult in giving up resources and life choices to improve the lives of distant strangers? I want the tree built from three distinctions rather than from the standard positions. The first distinction concerns whether a person is ever required to choose the best available act, full stop, or whether requirement runs out short of the optimum, at a threshold or at a set of rules. The second concerns whether there is a permission to weight one's own projects, relationships, and tastes above the weight one gives to equivalent interests of strangers, and whether that permission is bounded or open-ended. The third concerns whether acts below the standard of the best are wrong in the same way as one another, or merely worse on a scale with no bright line.

Turn those distinctions into between seven and ten branch questions, each a concrete case or claim an intelligent reader can answer without knowing any of the labels. The first node should be a drained-down variant of the drowning child in which the cost is something with ongoing consequences, such as a missed connection that costs a job interview, so the answer cannot be a reflex. The leaves must include straightforward maximizing act-consequentialism of the Singer sort, Scheffler-style act-consequentialism with an agent-centered prerogative, satisficing consequentialism, rule consequentialism, scalar utilitarianism of the sort that replaces right and wrong with better and worse, and a moderate deontological position on which negative duties are strict but positive duties are imperfect and limited. If a further leaf earns its place by being a genuinely distinct pattern of case verdicts rather than a social identity, add it; otherwise leave the tree lean.

Where the cross-product of the answers leaves a cell empty, say so, and say whether you think nobody lives there because the combination is incoherent, because it is too unattractive for people to describe themselves that way, or because it is simply unexplored. And be candid about which leaves the tree cannot separate. My prior is that scalar utilitarianism is hard to distinguish from maximizing consequentialism on concrete cases and may diverge only on a question about whether the word wrong properly applies below the optimum; if that is right, the tree should flag the pair rather than force a clean split.

The readers know the drowning child and the basic machinery of expected-value reasoning, but I would not assume they know the academic positions by name, so phrase every branch question in plain language and give each leaf a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment next to its name. Keep the whole thing short enough to work through in one sitting, and write it as a single piece of prose I could put in a blog post without editing.
