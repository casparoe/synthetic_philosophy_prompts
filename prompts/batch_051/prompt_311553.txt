I am a philosopher of language, and I have been studying the settled, case-by-case verdicts of my colleague Professor Lindgren, a comparative linguist, about what she is willing to call a language. Her record is as follows. She classifies as languages: American Sign Language; Nicaraguan Sign Language, which emerged across successive cohorts of deaf children brought together in Nicaraguan schools; and the mature sign language of a village with a high incidence of hereditary deafness, used by both deaf and hearing residents and transmitted across at least three generations. She refuses to call a language: the home sign of an isolated deaf child whose gestures are understood only by the child's immediate family; fingerspelling, in which hand configurations spell out the words of a written language; Morse code; and the honeybee waggle dance.

The new case is this. Two profoundly deaf siblings, raised together in a rural area with no access to an established sign language or to a Deaf community, have by adolescence built a shared manual system. It has a stable lexicon of several thousand signs, regular word order, productive ways of forming questions and negation, and a spatial device for marking who did what to whom; they use it to tell new stories, lie, joke, and plan. No one outside the pair has ever learned the system, and it has not been transmitted to a younger generation.

I want you to predict how she will classify the sibling system, but I care about the prediction mainly as a test of the underlying rule. Please lay out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts, say how each rule would classify the sibling system, and give your best guess as to which rule she is actually applying and why you prefer it. Finally, specify one further case that would most cleanly separate the live candidate rules.
