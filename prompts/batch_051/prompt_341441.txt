I keep coming back to the Volkswagen diesel scandal from 2015 and want to work through it using a philosophy idea I half-remember from an intro class. The way I remember it, in Plato's Republic a guy named Glaucon argues that people only act justly because they're afraid of being caught or punished. To make the point he tells the story of Gyges, a shepherd who finds a ring that turns him invisible. As soon as Gyges has that power, he seduces the queen, murders the king, and takes over the kingdom. Glaucon's claim is roughly that there's no reason to be just if nobody will ever know, and that what we call justice is just a mutual agreement not to harm each other so that we can each avoid being harmed.

The VW scandal seems like a real-world version of the ring. As I understand it: engineers wrote engine-control software that could detect when the car was being tested in a lab versus driven normally, and it only ran the full emissions controls during the test. So the cars passed in front of the regulators, but on actual roads they were emitting far more NOx than permitted. This went on across millions of cars. When it became public, the usual response was to treat the people involved as clearly guilty, and most of them scrambeled to deny they knew or to say it was a few bad apples.

What I'm curious about is whether Glaucon's story actually explains what happened there, and what the case does back to the theory. The defeat device made the wrongdoing invisible to the relevant observers, so Glaucon would seem to predict exactly this: give people an invisible ring and they'll cheat. But the case doesn't feel like one shepherd suddenly free of consequences. The deception was spread across teams, managers, suppliers maybe; it probably looked inside the company like meeting difficult goals under cost and regulatory pressure, not like being unobserved in a vacuum. Some engineers probably thought they were solving a legitimate problem—keeping diesels cheap and performant while satisfying CO2 rules—rather than indulging the desire to get away with something.

So walk me through it. Where does the VW case fit Glaucon's challenge and where does it resist it? What does the story of the ring predict about the engineers and managers, and which parts of their behavior does it miss or misdescribe? And what should the VW case make us think about the idea that people are only honest while they are being watched—does it confirm it, complicate it, or show that the real story is about organizations and legibility rather than individual impunity?

And one more specific thing, because I write code for a living. There's a difference between writing a routine whose explicit purpose is to fool an external tester (like the defeat device, which is an invisible ring by construction) versus discovering a bug or a gap in an interface and just not drawing attention to it. Does Glaucon's challenge treat those as the same kind of choice, or does the distinction matter for whether the case actually supports his picture? I want the analysis, not a reading list.
