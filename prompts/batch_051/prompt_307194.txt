The other day I came up with an argument about the experience machine, and I cannot tell whether it is airtight or quietly cheating. The thought experiment, as I understand it, is this: you can be plugged into a machine that gives you a perfectly realistic-feeling life, and once you are inside you cannot tell from the inside whether you are living the real version of that life or the simulated one. My argument goes like this:

(1) Suppose you plug into the machine and get exactly the experiences of someone whose real life is going well.
(2) From the inside, your situation is indistinguishable from that person's situation.
(3) Indistinguishable situations are supported by exactly the same evidence.
(4) So if contact with reality added anything to the value of your life, it would have to be a difference you could not notice from the inside.
(5) A difference you could not notice from the inside cannot make your life better or worse.
(6) Therefore, contact with reality adds nothing to the value of your life, and the machine life is just as good for you.

I suspect step 5 slides from the fact that I cannot tell the difference to the claim that there is no difference that matters, but I cannot make the objection precise, and I keep getting tied up in whether step 4 already assumes what I am trying to prove. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise is false, and on what reading of it? If it is not valid, which step fails exactly — the move from step 2 to step 3, from step 3 to step 4, or from steps 4 and 5 to step 6? Please be specific about the step and the reading of it.
