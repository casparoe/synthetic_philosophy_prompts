In preparing a lecture for my philosophy of language seminar on what writing systems reveal about our implicit metaphysics of language, I keep returning to a puzzle that strikes me as philosophical rather than merely historical. The story we are all taught is that an alphabet is a notation for speech: its virtue is supposed to be that a small inventory of letters maps onto the phonemes of a language, so that the written word records how it sounds. Yet actual writing systems, especially ones with centuries of continuous use, are far more conservative than that story allows.

English writes the same plural marker in cats and dogs even though the sound it corresponds to differs, preserves a silent gh in night, and insists on distinct spellings for sight, site, and cite despite their identical pronunciation. French fills its written sentences with plural and gender agreement endings that speech largely leaves silent. Chinese characters track morphemes at least as much as they track sound. And spelling reform, tellingly, tends to be resisted even when it would make a script more faithfully phonemic.

What theory of what a language is does this conservative, standardizing practice presuppose? Where, according to such a script, does the identity of a word reside — in its sound, its sense, its ancestry, its written form, or some combination the standard phonemic story cannot express? Reconstruct the implicit theory embodied in the practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what is mere historical accident or tolerated inconsistency, and say whether several rival theories of language would license the same orthography. Finally, spell out what a thoroughgoing spelling reform — one that made the script track surface phonemes — would implicitly be affirming or denying about the nature of language.
