I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in philosophy of cognitive science — sixteen students, one 90-minute meeting — and I want a runnable activity built around Bongard problems. By a Bongard problem I mean the two-box format: six simple line drawings on the left, six on the right, where every drawing on the left obeys a single rule that every drawing on the right violates, and the task is to state the rule. I want the students to feel three things, in this order: first, that twelve examples vastly underdetermine the intended concept; second, that the rule they find obvious is being selected by tacit preferences for simplicity, naturalness, or familiar vocabulary rather than being forced by the examples; third, that even when a learner can actively probe the concept with new test cases, a finite budget of probes still does not pin the concept down. The words Bongard problem, underdetermination, and induction should not be spoken until the debrief.

I have been sketching a designer/solver game and I want you to turn it into something tight, or replace it if it is flawed. The sketch is this. Pairs design a hidden rule over a restricted vocabulary of simple shapes — size, fill, number, orientation, containment, and the like; they choose six in-rule and six out-rule examples and seal four new cases in an envelope as holdouts. A solver pair may ask the designers a fixed number of yes/no classification questions about new cases before committing to their guessed rule; scoring rewards the solvers for classifying the sealed holdouts and rewards the designers when the solvers fail. After the reveal, the solvers may issue a challenge by stating an alternative rule consistent with everything they saw and heard; the designers must refute it with a case that separates the two rules, or lose points. If that mechanic is workable, develop it; if not, say what is wrong with it and give a better one.

Please give me the full plan: exact rules and materials — paper, pens, pre-printed shape sheets, nothing that needs software or money — a scoring scheme using points, a timeline for 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the three ideas above. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it on the spot, and be honest about which feature of real concept acquisition from a few examples the activity cannot reproduce.
