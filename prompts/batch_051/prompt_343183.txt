So I've been going in circles trying to understand how we number years, and the conventions seem to contradict each other. The traditional BC/AD scheme doesnt have a year 0: it goes straight from 1 BC to AD 1. A kid born on Jan 1 of 1 BC turns one on Jan 1 of AD 1, but the year labels make it look like two years went by. That missing zero is also the reason people fought over whether the new millennium started Jan 1 2000 or Jan 1 2001. Astronomers dodge the problem by inserting a year 0 and calling 1 BC 'year 0', 2 BC 'year -1', and so on, so the numbers finally subtract cleanly. Meanwhile Unix clocks count seconds since Jan 1 1970 UTC, treating time like one big integer counter with an arbitrary origin, and they still pretend every day is exactly 86,400 seconds even on dates that get a leap second added. Excel does its own thing too: its serial-date system counts from around Jan 1 1900 but includes a fictitious Feb 29 1900 that it keeps for compatibility with old Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheets. And when people renamed BC/AD to BCE/CE to get rid of the religious reference, they kept the exact same pivot year. I'm trying to figure out what these practices presuppose about time: is a date a label for a position in an ordered list (so zero doesn't apply), or a number counting elapsed time since an origin (so zero matters), and can the origin be arbitrary or does it need some justification? Which of these conventions are merely arbitrary choices, which are internally inconsistent, and is 'what should year zero be?' a real question or a confused one?
