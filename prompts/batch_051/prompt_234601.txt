The serious-incident review committee I currently chair at a regional hospital system has spent two months arguing about how to fix a recurring ambiguity problem in our reports. Writers lean heavily on pronouns and bare indexicals — "she told him it had already happened here yesterday," "the patient in bed 12 said the same thing again" — and because reports circulate outside the ward where they were written, reviewers often cannot recover who did what, where, or when. A subgroup has now proposed a rule: every narrative section of an incident report must be written in a controlled register containing no pronouns (I, you, we, she, he, they), no indexicals (here, there, now, then, today, yesterday), and no standing demonstratives (this, that). Persons would be identified by role and shift; places by ward, room, and nearest staff station; times by clock time and date. So one could not write that a nurse "was told the patient was not here"; one would have to write something like "the night-shift charge nurse in Ward C was told at 03:12 that the patient in bed 12 was not in Ward C at 03:10."

The committee is split. The proposers argue that indexicals are merely shorthand for definite descriptions and that incident reports are quasi-legal records, so the loss of naturalness is a small price for referential clarity. Two objections have surfaced. One is practical: busy staff may under-report or fall back on boilerplate if writing becomes that laborious. The other is more philosophical: a committee member with a background in clinical ethics argues that some indexical information cannot be paraphrased away without remainder — that a complete clock-and-place description of a scene can still omit exactly what a person knows when she thinks "I am the one who is here," and that such self-locating facts matter for reconstructing who could have acted otherwise. I am not trained in philosophy of language, and I do not want our debate to collapse into the abstract question of whether a language could lack pronouns or indexicals; I need an analysis we can actually use at next month's meeting.

Please walk me through how this register would actually behave if adopted: what would improve and what would degrade, how report writers would adapt, where it would be gamed or evaded, what second-order effects would follow, and which variants, if any, would keep the intended gains without the foreseen costs. If the request is itself confused — for instance, if the boundary between indexicals and other context-dependent terms such as "local," "current," or "nearby" is too fuzzy to regulate, or if the proposal misdiagnoses our ambiguity problem as a problem about indexicals — say so plainly instead of writing around it. Structure your response as a written analysis I can circulate, with these sections in this order and roughly these lengths: "The rule as it would actually operate" (~250 words); "What improves and what degrades" (~350 words); "Evasion, gaming, and second-order effects" (~300 words); "The indexicality question underneath" (~350 words); "Variants that might preserve the gains" (~250 words); and "Bottom line" (~100 words).
