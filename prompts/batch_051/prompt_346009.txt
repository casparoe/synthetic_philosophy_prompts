Im taking a decision theory seminar and we did counterfactual mugging last week, and I cant stop going back and forth on it. The setup we used: Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it lands heads, Omega gives you $10,000 if and only if it predicted you would have paid the $100 in the tails branch. So when you find yourself being asked for the $100, the coin has already landed tails, and your paying can't causally bring about the $10,000—that benefit lives in a heads branch you're not in. The policy-level argument for paying feels nearly watertight: payers collect $10,000 in heads branches where non-payers get nothing, so over both branches the paying policy comes out massively ahead, even though it costs $100 in the tails branch. But I also have a stubborn intuition that paying is just handing Omega $100 for a payoff I never causally receive, and that a decision rule shouldn't care about what would have happened in a branch that didn't happen. I get that if I could precommit before the flip, paying is obviously right; the fight is about what to do once the coin has landed and I'm actually being asked. I don't want someone to just tell me which side wins. Steelman both sides, then tell me: if you keep the intuition, what exactly has to give in the policy-level argument; if you keep the policy-level argument, what exactly has to give in the intuition; and is there a reading of 'the right decision' on which both survive?
