I have no academic background in economics, game theory, or international relations, but for the last few years I have spent much of my free time thinking about whether the small number of firms and states now racing to build very powerful AI systems could strike a bargain before any single one of them succeeds, in order to lock in part of the post-AGI world while they still need one another. I want you to bring proper analytical tools to an arrangement I keep returning to.

Adopt the role of an expert in institutional mechanism design, and think slowly. Before you praise or attack the arrangement, start by sketching the baseline: the parties' incentives, what each of them most fears, and what a workable bargain would have to do.

Suppose that, at a time when no party has yet built a system that could substitute for an entire human research group, the five leading AI labs, each headquartered in a different country and acting with the explicit backing of its home government, sign the following agreement.

Each lab deposits with a neutral custodian a complete copy of the weights, training code, records of data provenance, and safety logs for every model it trains above an agreed floating compute threshold. Each lab also places twenty per cent of the equity held by its founders and key technical staff into escrow. The custodian is a foundation created by three small non-signatory states; its only significant asset is the escrow itself, and its charter forbids it from ever inspecting the deposited material.

The escrow is governed by a single conditional. There is a standing panel of thirteen: two retired judges appointed by each signatory state, plus three technical experts drawn by lot from a list that the signatories compile and may amend only unanimously. If this panel finds by majority vote that a lab has done either of two things — (i) deployed a system above the agreed threshold without the panel's prior written approval, or (ii) refused an adversarial audit that the panel had requested by majority vote — then the custodian must, within thirty days, release that lab's entire escrowed archive to every other signatory lab, and that lab's escrowed equity vests permanently in a public trust whose trustees the other signatories appoint. If no such finding is made within fifty years, the escrow is dissolved and everything is returned pro rata.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral stance. Walk through how it would actually behave: what it would incentivise inside each lab, between rival labs, between each lab and its home government, and among the panel and custodians; how each party would try to game it; where it would break first; and what second-order effects it would have on who enters the field, on how the capability threshold is defined and tested, and on what gets built or hidden. In particular, tell me what you expect to happen the first time a lab crosses one of the red lines in a way that is plausible but not provable, and what the arrangement is worth once one lab is clearly capable of making the threat of reciprocal disclosure either irrelevant or dangerous to the others. Finally, say which of these failure modes could be patched without gutting the underlying bargain, and which cannot.
