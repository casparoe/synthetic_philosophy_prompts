In his essay The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity, Daniel Dennett argues that the self is not a substance or a thing but an abstraction, like a center of gravity in physics. A center of gravity is not a material part of an object; it is a useful simplification that lets us predict the object’s behavior. Dennett proposes that the self is similarly a center of narrative gravity: something we posit as we tell a unified story about a person’s life, choices, and experiences. A self, on this view, is not found in the brain but attributed in the practice of interpreting someone as an agent with a coherent biography.

I want a working definition of ‘self’ that captures this proposal and can be applied case by case. Test your definition against the following cases:

1. A healthy adult with a coherent autobiographical memory.
2. A person in the late stages of Alzheimer’s disease who no longer recognizes family members and cannot sustain a life narrative.
3. An infant.
4. A corporation.
5. A fictional character in a novel.
6. A large language model fine-tuned to maintain a consistent persona across separate sessions.
7. A person with anosognosia who denies their paralysis and confabulates reasons when questioned.

I suspect the ordinary word ‘self’ does two jobs: one ontological and one practical, and Dennett’s analogy is an attempt to keep the practical job while dissolving the ontological one. If you agree, give me two definitions and say which cases move between them. Then tell me where your definition gives a surprising or uncomfortable verdict, and whether that should make me revise the definition or revise my confidence in the ordinary concept.
