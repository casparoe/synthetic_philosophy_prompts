I am a data scientist at a hospital network and a practicing Catholic, and a dispute in my parish has recently forced me to revisit an argument I have not thought about since college. A claimed healing is being put forward as a miracle: medical images and laboratory values before and after, no treatment in the interval, and records created in the ordinary course of care, before anyone had an interest in arguing for a miracle. The person advocating for the claim insists that the evidence is no longer the sort of thing Hume had in mind — it is not the testimony of enthusiastic witnesses but a set of independently generated clinical records. I have been trying to work out whether Hume's argument against miracles survives this change. As I remember it, Hume argued that a miracle is a violation of a law of nature; that laws of nature are established by uniform, exceptionless experience; and that testimony is itself a form of evidence whose reliability we know only from experience. When testimony to a miracle conflicts with the uniform experience behind a law, a reasonable person should always judge it more probable that the testimony is false than that the law was violated. Hume also added empirical complaints about witnesses — their credulity, their taste for wonder, their motives. If the witnesses are replaced by a large, independently generated set of clinical records, which premise of the argument is actually doing the work, and which premise is under pressure? Does the argument still go through against this kind of evidence, does it need a repaired form, or does it become inapplicable? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not about what conclusion my parish should draw.
