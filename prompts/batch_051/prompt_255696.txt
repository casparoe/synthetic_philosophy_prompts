I retired a few years ago from a lab that did biophysical chemistry, mostly protein spectroscopy, and I've been using the free time to read philosophy of mind. I keep getting stuck on epiphenomenalism, especially the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and I've got an idea for a short essay. I'd like comments, particularly on the places where I lean on my old scientific habits, because I can't tell whether they're illuminating or just a retired guy decorating an argument with lab equipment.

Here's the sketch. The thesis is that epiphenomenalism about qualia is not a stable resting point: once you take causal inertness seriously, it collapses either into eliminativism/physicalism or back into interactionism.

My notes so far:

1. Epiphenomenalism says qualia are real, they're caused by the brain, but they cause nothing—not a wince, not a memory, not the sentence "I'm in pain."

2. The paradox, as I understand it: if the judgment "I'm in pain" is caused wholly by a neural state, and the pain itself is a free-rider, then the judgment is not caused by the pain. But then the pain does no work in producing my conviction that I'm in pain. A detector whose readout is produced by something other than the target, and merely happens to match the target, is not really a detector for that target.

3. The standard reply seems to be that the judgment and the quale share a common cause, so the correlation isn't a miracle. That's fine for correlation. But knowledge needs more than correlation—or does it? I want to press on that premise, because a natural scientist ought to be relaxed here: lots of instruments only track correlations. Still, there's a difference. In spectroscopy the molecule absorbs or emits the photon; it has a causal footprint. By definition, an epiphenomenalon has none, not even on its own introspective report. So the inside view—the only place qualia show up—would be a measurement of something with zero coupling. In physics we have a phrase for a difference with no possible observable consequence: a gauge degree of freedom, a difference that makes no difference. We normally refuse to treat those as physically real. But here the difference is the pain itself, the very thing the theory was trying to save.

4. So my provisional line is this: epiphenomenalism treats qualia as both the data to be explained and as explanatorily inert, and that isn't a paradox you can live with; it forces a choice. If the pain explains nothing, including my saying "I'm in pain," stop treating it as data. If my saying "I'm in pain" is data for pain, then pain must explain it. Either way you land back in physicalism or interactionism.

I'd like comments on three things. First, wether I'm strawmanning the common-cause reply in point 3—do epiphenomenalists have a story about how a merely correlated judgment can count as knowledge, or do they bite the bullet and say we don't know about our own qualia? Second, is the gauge analogy misleading? Philosophical readers may not care much about physics, but I'd rather not embarrass myself if the analogy is false. Third, does the essay need to handle the objection that if beliefs themselves have phenomenal character, then "judgment" has already smuggled in a mental cause—or is that a separate paper?
