A colleague in my lab defends a version of memetics on which some messages are antimemes in a strong sense: their content itself lowers the probability that they are encoded, retained, recalled, or repeated accurately, an effect she insists is not reducible to the ordinary reasons a message fails to spread, such as being boring, complicated, low-stakes, or socially costly to transmit. Her stock example is the self-undermining sentence Do not remember this sentence, which she claims is forgotten faster and reproduced less faithfully than a sentence matched for length, grammatical form, subject matter, and presentation. I suspect the label antimeme is doing less work than it seems to: every forgotten sentence is forgotten for some reason, the self-undermining cases may simply sit at the tail of a perfectly ordinary distribution of failures to encode and transmit, and describing them as a distinct kind looks like re-describing a feature of the cognitive system as though it were a feature of the message itself.

Design the strongest test you can of the claim that strong antimemes exist as a distinct psychological kind. Specify the manipulation and the matched controls, the predictions under the two readings, and the metric that would actually discriminate between them. Then be explicit about what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, address whether a reliable recall-and-transmission penalty for self-undermining content shows that the content does the suppressing, or only that the content is a reliable trigger for independently existing mechanisms such as directed forgetting, compliance with an embedded instruction, or stress on self-reference — and say whether any experiment could close that gap, or whether the gap is a residue built into the way the concept is being used.
