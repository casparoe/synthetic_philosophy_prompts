Consider the passage in Book II, chapter 11 of Aristotle’s Rhetoric where he distinguishes zēlos (emulation) from phthonos (envy). In that passage, he defines emulation as a kind of pain felt at the apparent presence, in persons similar to oneself, of goods that are honored and possible to attain, a pain felt because one does not yet have those goods rather than because the others possess them; he adds that the emulous person thinks himself worthy of what he lacks, and that emulation is a good feeling felt by good people while envy is a bad feeling felt by bad people. Use this passage, and in particular the contrast between lacking-for-oneself and wishing-the-other-to-lose, as the core text.

Compare Aristotle’s zēlos/phthonos distinction with the modern task-oriented/ego-oriented distinction in achievement motivation, where task orientation is responding to competition by trying to improve one’s own competence and ego orientation is responding by trying to establish or defend relative superiority. Which parts of Aristotle’s distinction survive into that framework, and which parts — especially the role of perceived worthiness and the moralized characterization of the two responses — drop out or get redescribed? Then apply both pairs of concepts to this mechanism: a school implements a public leaderboard for a voluntary reading program, with the explicit aim of encouraging students to emulate the top readers. How would each framework predict the mechanism’s effects, and what failure modes would Aristotle’s distinction alert us to that the task/ego distinction might not?
