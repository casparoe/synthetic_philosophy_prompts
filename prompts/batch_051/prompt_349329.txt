A user in one country writes a post that is hosted on a platform in a second country and read in a third; whose speech law governs it? Suppose a treaty replaces the existing tangle of national claims with this mechanism. Each platform must designate one signatory state as its home speech law, changeable only once a year, and content is presumptively lawful if lawful under that law. Any other state may still order the platform to geoblock or remove content for its own residents, but only for content directed at that state, judged by a published three-prong targeting test: the content is in the state's dominant language, or chiefly concerns its domestic politics, or the platform solicits its residents through local advertising, pricing, or staff. The ordering state must also deposit a bond scaled to the platform's claimed compliance costs. A standing international panel reviews appealed orders; if an order is reversed, the bond is forfeited to the platform and the affected posters, and a state loses expedited-order privileges after repeated reversals. Orders and outcomes are published.

Analyze the incentives, not the abstract merits. Which states do platforms choose as home speech law, and does the system drift toward the most permissive law, the most predictable, or something else? How do states, platforms, and speakers game the targeting test? What does the bond do to which states bother to issue orders, to how platforms claim costs, and to the content of the demands themselves? What happens to the review panel as it becomes a bottleneck or an arena, and where does the scheme fail first? Which failures are fixable without gutting the mechanism?
