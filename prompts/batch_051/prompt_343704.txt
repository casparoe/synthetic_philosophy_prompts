I run a recurring reading group on the physics of information. Our next meeting will have about eleven people, mostly physics and philosophy graduate students, and we will have two hours. We have spent the last two sessions on Maxwell's demon: the setup in which a small agent sorts fast and slow molecules across a partition and would seem able to extract work from a single-temperature gas, and the standard resolution, which says the demon has to acquire and then erase information about the molecules and that erasure carries a minimum thermodynamic cost. A few members find this resolution genuinely explanatory; the rest keep saying it feels circular, as if the second law is being smuggled back in under the heading of information erasure. I would rather have them operate inside the constraint than argue about it in the abstract.

I would like a tabletop activity for that group, two hours start to finish. Design it as a game with roles or teams, cards, tokens, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money, in which participants get to act as the demon and perhaps as the demon's ledger-keeper or the rest of the box. The design goal is for them to run into the trade themselves: sorting can be made profitable only by keeping a record of what has been sorted, the record has finite capacity, and clearing it costs something. By the end they should feel, rather than just report, why a Szilard-engine-style setup turns information into a physically significant quantity, and why information processing is subject to thermodynamic limits.

Please include the complete rules, a materials list, a timed agenda, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto the conceptual pieces: the second law, the demon's memory, and the erasure cost. Include an honest note about what the game cannot reproduce — for instance, the distinction between measurement, which can in principle be done reversibly, and erasure, which cannot, or the fact that the actual bound is a continuous relation rather than whatever discrete price the tokens impose. Also list what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as players finding a loophole that lets them sort without keeping a record or the payoff scheme accidentally making brute-force trial and error rational, and say how to patch each failure mode on the spot.
