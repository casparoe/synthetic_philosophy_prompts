A dispute has arisen in a way that is more personal than I would like. I was meant to look after a friend's houseplants during her two-week trip and forgot to water them; they died. I have been trying to determine whether my inaction really caused their death, and I keep returning to the following argument, which seems tidy but also suspiciously convenient:

(1) My friend was away for fourteen days, and on each of those days I omitted to water the plants.
(2) Had I watered them on exactly one of those days and omitted the other thirteen, they would still have died, since they needed water every other day; so, by the usual counterfactual test, no single one of those omissions caused the death.
(3) If none of the individual omissions caused the death, then the whole pattern of omissions did not cause the death.
(4) Therefore, my overall failure to water the plants did not cause their death.
(5) If my overall failure to water them did not cause their death, then I am not causally responsible for it.
(6) Therefore, I am not causally responsible for the plants' death.

Each step seems acceptable when I read it in isolation, yet the conclusion feels like exactly the kind of thing I would dismiss if someone else offered it. Is the argument valid? If so, do I have to accept the conclusion? If not, state precisely which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be.
