I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for an essay I am planning I have been reading the kalām (dialectical theology) literature on the old dispute between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ariyya about whether acts are good and bad in themselves or only because God commands and forbids them — the Islamic counterpart of the Euthyphro question. The question has been alive in one form or another since at least the ninth century and still has no settled answer, and I have reached the point where I need help diagnosing the impasse rather than another summary of the two positions.

As I understand them, the Mu'tazila held that reason can recognize that some acts are good or bad in themselves, and that God's justice means His commands answer to that standard: God commands veracity and forbids ingratitude because those acts are what they are. The Ash'ariyya held that goodness and badness are whatever God commands and forbids, that there is no independent standard of justice to which God's will must answer, and that reason's role in morality is correspondingly limited. Later writers refined both positions, and some tried to split the difference by granting unaided reason broad moral knowledge while reserving specific obligations to revelation. What puzzles me is that the two sides largely agree on the particular rulings — the same acts end up commanded and forbidden — yet they never converged on the underlying explanation.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is it verbal, with the two schools meaning different things by 'good' and 'reason'? Is it a disagreement about the powers of unaided reason — about moral epistemology rather than moral ontology? Is it, deeper down, a disagreement about what divine oneness, omnipotence, and justice commit one to, with the ethics riding on top of the theology? Or did the dispute change its object across the centuries, so that later participants were answering a different question under the same name? My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that 'good' has been doing at least two jobs — naming a property acts might have and naming what reason is entitled to prescribe — and that the more fundamental disagreement concerns whether any norm can be prior to God without compromising His will. But I want the strongest competing diagnoses and an honest assessment of what survives.

Please structure the response as follows:

1. The two positions restated (~300 words): each school's view in its strongest, most defensible form, including later refinements, without caricature.
2. Where the argument makes contact (~350 words): the points at which the two sides genuinely engaged one another, and the points at which they talked past one another.
3. Diagnosis (~450 words): what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, factual, about standards, about which cases count as data, about bedrock theological intuitions, or ill-posed — and which hidden question, if any, is doing the work.
4. What would move it (~200 words): given your diagnosis, what distinction, reformulation, or change of question would count as progress, or why no progress should be expected.
5. Settled and contested (~200 words): what specialists broadly treat as historically settled, what remains genuinely contested, and where your own diagnosis leans on a contested reading.

Throughout, keep settled material apart from genuinely contested material, and do not present one school's position or the 'rationalism versus voluntarism' framing as the consensus. Paraphrase rather than quote, and do not invent attributions or citations.
