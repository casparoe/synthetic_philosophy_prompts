I'm a health and science journalist, and I run a small professional-development workshop for other reporters — about 15 people, mostly mid-career, who've covered drug approvals and screening controversies but never had to defend the ethics of randomizing an actual patient. We've got 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them feel, not just hear, why clinical equipoise is the thing that makes a randomized trial ethical and at the same time the thing that is always on the verge of dissolving. No lecture up front, and the word equipoise should not be spoken until the debrief.

The shape I'm imagining: first force each of them to commit, privately and then publicly, to which treatment they'd want for themselves or a family member in two or three genuinely contested cases — the kind where expert opinion is honestly split, like surgery versus a sham procedure, or intervening early versus waiting. Then, in small groups, make them design an actual randomized trial comparing those very treatments: inclusion criteria, a consent script, a stopping rule. The catch is that at the end each group has to say which arm they'd put their own child or parent in, and if they can't honestly answer "either," the design has failed and they have to fix it. I want them to run straight into the wall between their private hunch and the disagreement in the expert community, and to notice how much of their first-pass ethical reasoning is really about safety and informed consent rather than about the state of genuine uncertainty.

Give me the full design: the cases to use (plausible but not tied to any specific real trial, so nobody gets stuck litigating facts), the exact instructions, a timeline, the debrief that maps moments in the exercise onto the ideas — in particular the difference between individual (theoretical) equipoise and community (clinical) equipoise, and the therapeutic misconception, the way participants in trials assume every decision is being made for their own benefit like in ordinary care. Maybe end the debrief with a quick thought experiment about a design that randomizes people before they're even asked for consent, and ask why it feels like a violation even when the statistics and the consent form are clean. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess is people retreat to "more data is always better" and struggle to see that it's the community, not just the individual doctor, that has to be uncertain), and be honest about what the exercise can't reproduce — presumably the actual weight of a sick patient in front of you, and the way money and career incentives quietly tilt what counts as genuine disagreement in real medicine. And end the debrief with one concrete change in how they'd report a trial afterwards; there coverage of trials is the thing they'll actually take home.
