I'm a novelist, and a while back I made the mistake of telling two friends about the book I'm working on — a woman who lives to about four hundred, where the whole point of the book is wether she's in any meaningful sense the same person at the end. One friend's a philosophy postdoc, the other teaches comp lit, and they immediately stopped talking about my book and started having it out with each other about time, parts, and identity. I took notes, because which one of them is right actually matters for how I narrate the last third. Here's the back-and-forth, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) M: The 20-year-old and the 400-year-old are the same person, full stop. She persists by being wholly present at every moment of her life. One entity, not a parade of replacements.

(2) R: But then one and the same thing has incompatible properties. At 20 she's reckless and full of energy; at 400 she's brittle and sedentary. If the very same whole object is present at both dates, it's reckless and not-reckless all at once, and that's a flat contradiction. The way out is to say she's stretched through time the way a road is stretched through space — a four-dimensional worm — and the 20-year-old and the 400-year-old are different temporal parts of that worm, like the first mile and the last mile of a road.

(3) M: On that picture she never changes. A road doesn't change; it just has potholes at one end and smooth asphalt at the other. If her life is a worm with different parts at different dates, then nothing ever becomes anything else. Real change requires one and the same thing to have a property and then lose it. You've fixed the contradiction by eliminating change.

(4) R: I haven't eliminated it; I've analyzed it. Change just is the worm's having different temporal parts at different times. And your "one and the same thing" only looks coherent because you quietly relativize properties to times — reckless-at-t1, not-reckless-at-t2. That's doing the same indexing work with the properties instead of the parts, and it's no less weird. Going my way also gives a clean story about why time is structurally so much like space.

(5) M: Fine, but if you're a four-dimensional worm, which thing does "I" pick out? If it's the whole worm, then my present-tense thought "I am old now" is thought by something that's simultaneously 20 and 400. If it's a momentary temporal part, then there's a brand new subject at every instant. Either way you've lost the thing that thinks and says "I."

(6) R: "I" picks out the whole worm. The momentary stage isn't a separate self; it's just the part of the worm that does the thinking at that date, the way my hand does the grabbing but I'm the one who grabs. The worm thinks "I am old now" truly because it has an old stage at that location in its career. No new subject needed.

(7) M: Then what ties the 20-year-old stage and the 400-year-old stage into one worm in the first place, rather than two series of stages parked next to each other? Whatever causal or psychological relation you point to is doing the real work of identity over time — and it's starting to look a lot like the endurance story you set out to replace.

Work through the seven moves one at a time and tell me, for each, whether it's solid, shaky, or outright wrong, and why, in a sentence or two. Then say who's right about which specific point, who's closer to winning overall, and what both of them are missing. The thing I actually need out of the verdict is a defensible way to think about whether my 400-year-old character is one person, so the narration doesn't talk nonsense.
