I co-host a small animal-ethics podcast with my sister, and after a year of mostly rescue workers and vets we finally booked an actual philosopher. The episode is built around a split the guest defends as his reading of Nozick's few pages on animals in Anarchy, State, and Utopia: persons get inviolable side constraints, but nonhuman animals are owed only the kind of consideration a utilitarian calculus gives them — their interests get summed and weighed, with no absolute prohibitions attached. This has become weirdly personal for me lately, because I've been volunteering at a farm sanctuary where we make exactly these trade-off decisions every week, and I keep noticing the difference between how we talk about the pigs and how we talk about the volunteers. I get the broad picture, but I don't want eight questions that just invite him to restate it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that push where the view actually strains? Please include at least: (1) a question about what grounds the side constraints — whatever feature (self-consciousness, the capacity to guide a life by a conception of the good, moral agency) is supposed to explain why persons can't be used as mere means, and why that feature doesn't just come in degrees across species, with a follow-up keyed to his likely answer that it's a threshold and not a gradient; (2) the marginal-humans problem — humans with severe cognitive disabilities who lack the feature — does the view put them on the utilitarian side of the ledger, and if not, what is the non-ad-hoc reason? (3) whether the utilitarian half has any internal constraints of its own: can we sacrifice one animal painfully for a lot of small improvements to other animals, or replace one contented animal with another contented animal? (4) the interaction problem — when an act affects both persons and animals, do benefits to persons (say, cheap food) get to count inside the animal utility sum? If they do, the view seems to license the kind of aggregation the side constraints were meant to rule out; if they don't, why is the animal calculus sealed off? (5) a wild-animal question — if animals are utilitarian patients, is there a duty to intervene in nature to reduce wild suffering even when no persons are harmed either way? And somewhere in the list, a question that asks what case or evidence would actually lead him to abandon the split rather then keep adjusting it.
