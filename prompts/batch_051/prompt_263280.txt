I got into keeping a forecasting journal this past year—just jotting down what I think the chances are of sports and politics stuff, no money involved. A friend of mine who does quant finance saw it and has been needling me that my numbers are "structurally off." In the week before the last election, he pointed to three of my calls: I had candidate X at 30% to win, candidate Y at 40% to win, and "either X or Y wins" at 50%. (There were other candidates on the ballot, so "neither X nor Y" was a live possibility.)

He then proposed a set of bets, and I agreed each one looked fair by itself: I buy a ticket that pays me $1 if X wins, for 30 cents; I buy a ticket that pays $1 if Y wins, for 40 cents; and I sell him a ticket that pays $1 if either X or Y wins, for 50 cents. Net, I hand over 20 cents today. Then no matter who wins, the later payouts cancel out and I never get that 20 back. He says this shows my credences were irrational—that any reasonable way of assigning degrees of belief has to be unexploitable in this way, and mine weren't. His move is clever but I don't buy it yet: I wouldn't of accepted the three bets as a package, and anyway there's a difference between having wierd degrees of belief and having to lose money. Is he actually right? What, if anything, do my three numbers commit me to? I'd rather understand the principle than relitigate my specific calls.
