I teach a third-year PPE seminar on social choice, and we're about to do Sen's liberal paradox. The proof on the board is short, but it never lands: students nod at each step and then still think there must be some trick, or that it only shows majority voting is broken. I want an activity for the seminar — about twelve students, ninety minutes — that makes them run into the impossibility themselves before they hear the label. The idea is that each student has one genuinely personal pair of options they should get to decide alone (read a book or not, paint their bedroom or not, that sort of thing), and there is also a shared option where everyone's preferences point the same way; the personal and shared decisions should end up in a cycle. Design the game or structured role-play with actual roles, a preference sheet for each student, a clear rule for aggregating choices, and a timeline for the ninety minutes. By the end they should come away seeing that rights-protecting personal choices and unanimous welfare improvements can genuinely clash, and that the problem lies in the conditions rather than in a clumsy aggregation rule. The debrief should map what happened onto Sen's minimal liberalism condition, the Pareto condition, and transitivity, say what will predictably go wrong when it's run (e.g. students merging the private and shared decisions into a single vote), and be honest about what the exercise cant capture — in particular that the paradox isn't about any specific voting rule but about the conditions themselves, so its reach is wider than any game with a simple aggregation rule. No lecture up front; they shouldn't hear the term liberal paradox until the debrief.
