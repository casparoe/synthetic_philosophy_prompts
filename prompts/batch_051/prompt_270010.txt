I'm a second-year undergrad doing a philosophy of biology module, and last week we covered the adaptationist programme and Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique. It's colliding with my part-time job at the natural history museum, where I help edit the little interpretive panels next to the specimens. Every fortnight I get a sheaf of draft lables from the science communication team, and I'm meant to check the evolutionary wording before the curator signs off. The stakes are moderately high: about forty thousand visitors a year read these panels, lots of them school groups, and a confidently wrong just-so story about why a trait evolved is exactly what the exhibitions team wants to get away from. But the house style guide basically just says 'be accurate about evolution', which gives me nothing to work with.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure for a single claim on a label, something like 'the okapi's stripes help calves follow their mother through the forest', 'the human chin is an adaptation because we have one', or 'the red colour of blood is there because natural selection made haemoglobin obviouse'. For each claim the procedure should place it into one of: we can say X evolved for that function; we can only say X is a by-product or spandrel of something else; we can only describe it as one hypothesis among several; or we should drop the causal story entirely. It also needs to catch the trap where a trait is present because it is developmentally or physically coupled to something that was selected, rather than because it was itself selected. The person using it will have A-level biology and no phylogenetics, and they'll have maybe ten to fifteen minutes per label. Give me two or three worked examples, including at least one where the procedure says we have to kill a label the curator really likes, and say which cases the procedure can't settle and where a judgement call is unavoidable.
