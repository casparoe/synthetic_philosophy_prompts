I am a senior at a small Reformed Christian secondary school, and I hold the student seat on the Chapel Life Committee, which must approve the form and content of our twice-weekly chapel services. The school's sponsoring denomination adheres to the regulative principle of worship: the elements of public worship are limited to those that Scripture positively commands, and whatever is not commanded is, in effect, forbidden. Accordingly, our chapel services have long included only prayer, the public reading of Scripture, preaching, congregational singing, and an occasional observance of the Lord's Supper.

A student in the drama program has petitioned the committee to add a weekly five-minute dramatic monologue to next term's chapel services. Each week a different student would deliver a prepared first-person speech in character as a biblical figure, after which the chaplain would briefly connect the monologue to the day's sermon text. The petitioner argues that the monologue is not a new element of worship but merely a vivid mode or circumstance of the commanded elements of teaching and exhortation, no different in principle from choosing an illustrative story or adjusting the manner of a sermon's delivery. Several committee members accept this argument; others reply that a regular dramatic performance is an addition to the commanded elements, and that fixing it in the liturgy turns it into a distinct element of worship that the tradition forbids. The committee has asked me to draft the decision memo for our next meeting: approve the petition, reject it, or approve it with specified conditions. The memo must reach a clear and actionable recommendation, give the theological and practical reasons behind it, and engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side, since the committee will discuss it line by line.
