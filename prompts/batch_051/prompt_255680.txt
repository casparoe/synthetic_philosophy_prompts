Most of the standard justifications for statutes of limitations rest on an epistemic claim that goes roughly like this: with time, evidence goes stale, memories fade, witnesses move away or die, and so the risk of wrongful conviction rises while the defendant's ability to answer the charges falls. There's also a separate fairness/repose idea about not living under indefinite threat, but I want to set that aside and focus on the evidence side.

Here's a development that seems like it should undercut the evidence side. For a growing share of ordinary life, evidence no longer decays. Messages, emails, location data, purchase histories, photos, CCTV, biometric logs, cloud backups: these persist for years or decades, and they're often more searchable later than they were at the time. A digital record of an event can be easier to marshal at year seven than at year one, becuase indexing and search got better in between.

So does the epistemic rationale for statutes of limitations survive this? Or was it never really about time, but about the storage media it happened to be built around—human memory and paper?
