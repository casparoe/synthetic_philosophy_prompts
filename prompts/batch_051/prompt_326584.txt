A student in a philosophy of social science course got this question:

"People talk about 'Baby Boomers,' 'Millennials,' and 'Gen Z' as if these labels pick out real groups in the world. Evaluate that way of talking. Are generational categories real kinds, merely conventional groupings, or something in between? Distinguish at least two things someone might mean by 'real kind,' and say which of them, if any, generational categories satisfy."

The student wrote:

"Generational categories are real kinds, but not in the way chemical elements are. A natural kind like gold has a hidden essence—atomic number 79—that explains why all samples of gold behave alike. Generations obviously don't have that kind of essence; nobody thinks everyone born between roughly 1981 and 1996 shares some inner property. But there is a second sense of 'real kind': a social kind is real when a category picks out a group whose members share a cluster of properties that tend to go together and do explanatory work. By that standard Millennials are real enough. They grew up as the internet spread and entered the job market around the 2008 financial crisis, and on average they have measurably different attitudes toward institutions than Boomers do, which means the category is doing real explanatory work. Fuzzy boundaries don't matter; lots of real kinds have fuzzy boundaries, like biological species. The fact that market researchers or journalists drew the lines rather than nature doesn't matter either. Money and marriage are social kinds, and nobody doubts those are real. The only thing that would make Millennials not a real kind would be if the shared properties were entirely made up. But the survey data show they aren't, so the matter is settled. Calling generations 'mere conventions' is just ignoring the evidence."
