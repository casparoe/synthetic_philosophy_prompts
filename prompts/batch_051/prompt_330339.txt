I work in computational linguistics, and I have been arguing with a colleague about whether any machine translation system can be said to understand the sentences it translates. We agree that fluency and accuracy are not the point at issue: modern systems routinely produce serviceable output, and no one denies that their benchmark scores keep improving. The point at issue is the one Douglas Hofstadter has pressed in his writings on translation, which I will put in my own words rather than his. A system can model the statistical regularities that connect two languages across enormous parallel corpora without ever having a sense of what the source text is about, and when the genuinely hard cases arrive, that missing sense of the situation is exactly what shows. I want a working definition of the term 'understanding' as it applies to a translator—human or machine—at the moment of translating. The definition should count a professional literary translator who reads a difficult novel, forms images and expectations about its people and scenes, and re-creates it in another language, and it should not count a tourist who produces a correct sentence by reading entries from a bilingual phrasebook.

Please test your definition against the following cases, and place each one on one side of the line or the other, with no hedging unless you explicitly defend the claim that the case is genuinely indeterminate:

1. A phrase-based statistical machine translation system trained on aligned corpora, with n-gram translation tables, a phrase reordering model, and no representation of meaning beyond those tables.
2. A modern Transformer-based machine translation system trained end-to-end on billions of parallel sentences.
3. A human translator working on a pharmaceutical patent who applies fixed templates and memorized terminological equivalences, and who reports that she produced the translation correctly without visualizing or otherwise experiencing the content.
4. A clerk in a room with an enormous printed rulebook and dictionaries who can convert texts from a language he does not otherwise know into fluent English by following condition–action rules, but who cannot answer questions about what the texts say.
5. A rule-based machine translation system built around an interlingua: it analyzes the source sentence into an explicit semantic representation involving predicates, arguments, tense, and modality, then generates the target sentence from that representation.
6. A retrieval-based system that takes an input sentence, finds the nearest matching human-translated sentence in a large bitext memory, and returns that translation verbatim, flagging a failure when no match is close enough.
7. A simultaneous interpreter who renders an expert's technical talk accurately but, by her own account, is running on pattern recognition and could not reconstruct the argument afterward.

Where your first definition gives a verdict that strikes you as wrong, revise it rather than abandoning it, and tell me which revision cost you the most. If you conclude that the term does two different jobs here—one cognitive and one about outward performance, say—give me both definitions and state which of the above cases move between them. Finally, under the definition you settle on, I would like to know whether correct translation with zero understanding is possible, and whether genuine understanding can coexist with a flatly wrong translation.
