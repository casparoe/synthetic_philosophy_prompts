I'm writing a paper on whether the reasoning behind Pascal's wager can be separated from its theology and turned into a defensible, fully secular rule of rational choice. I need a thought experiment built to specification that isolates the dispute between two decision rules which agree on all the probabilities and all the objective payoffs, but disagree about what to do.

The two rules are:

- The Pascalian rule. An agent maximizes expected utility with an unbounded utility function: for any positive probability p of a payoff, however small, there is some finite payoff large enough that the gamble has positive expected value, and a rational agent should pay any finite cost below that expected value rather than decline.
- The bounded rule. The same agent, but utility is capped: past a point, extra payoff adds nothing of rational significance, because tradeoffs between wildly different scales are treated as fanatical and rejected. This agent declines a gamble whenever the capped expected gain is less than the certain cost.

Construct a one-paragraph scenario in which the two rules, fed exactly the same beliefs and the same finite numbers, deliver opposite verdicts: the Pascalian rule says to take a costly action, and the bounded rule says not to. Constraints: set the case in an ordinary institutional setting — a hospital, a municipal budget, an insurance choice, a lab-safety decision; no gods, no supernatural beings, no perfect predictors, no demons; all probabilities and payoffs as explicit numbers; the payoff enormous but finite; the Pascalian verdict should look defensible rather than absurd, and the bounded refusal should be principled rather than a straw man. The costly action should be a precaution, insurance, or investment, not a payoff to a threatening stranger. I want the case to put the bounded rule under genuine pressure — on reflection the Pascalian verdict should be the more compelling one — without making the bounded rule stupid.

The case is for the central section of a paper aimed at philosophers of decision theory, so the demonstration must be earned through the arithmetic, not stipulated.

Please use exactly the following sections:

1. The scenario (120–180 words). Give the decision, the certain cost C, the probability p, and the finite payoff V as explicit numbers, and keep the setup mundane and concrete.
2. The Pascalian calculation (150–200 words). Show the expected-value arithmetic under the unbounded utility function, and explain why the rule mandates taking the action.
3. The bounded calculation (150–200 words). State the utility cap you are using, show the arithmetic when the payoff is capped, and explain why the rule declines.
4. The load-bearing feature (100–150 words). Say which feature of the case does the work — I expect it to be the unboundedness of the utility function rather than the particular size of p or V — and say how both verdicts shift if that single feature is removed while the numbers otherwise stay fixed.
5. The bounded rule's rejoinder (100–150 words). Give the strongest attack a defender of the bounded rule can make on this case, and the best reply available to the Pascalian.
