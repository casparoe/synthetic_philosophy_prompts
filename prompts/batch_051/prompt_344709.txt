I'm a logic PhD student, and I keep reading truth-theory papers where every fix seems engineered to kill one particular paradox while quietly breaking something else. I've decided I need the criteria sorted before I look at one more proposal. So here's the assignment: write me the desiderata that an adequate theory of truth (or at least of a paradox-prone truth predicate) has to meet, with the motivating case behind each, and a clear mark on which ones are non-negociable and which are just parochial preferences of one camp.

Here's the raw material I care about. Curry's paradox: take a sentence C that says "if C is true then 0=1." Using only the T-biconditional, modus ponens, and contraction, you get 0=1, with no negation involved. So any theory that locates the problem in negation or the law of excluded middle has missed something. Yablo's paradox: the infinite sequence where each sentence S_i says "every later sentence S_j is false" — intuitively no circularity at all, yet it still seems to go bad. A decent theory has to explain either where the circularity sneaks back in or why the derivation fails, and it can't just ban self-reference outright, since harmless cases like "this sentence is true" should come out fine. Throw in the contingent liar too: co-reference can build a liar by accident, so a theory can't only handle carefully constructed diagonal sentences. And the T-scheme itself, which feels completely trivial until it isn't.

Also flag any pairs of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — say, full T-scheme, classical logic, nontriviality, and an unrestricted conditional for Curry — and say which one you'd give up first and why. Then, briefly, score the paracomplete, dialetheist, and substructural approaches against your list. I care more about whether the list is right than about the scorecard, so dont spend too long on that part.
