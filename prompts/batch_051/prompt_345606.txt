Here's a case I keep chewing on, and I want an actual number from you rather than hedging. A person has to decide whether some uncomfortable action is okay—say, voting through a policy that will cost someone their job. They sincerely want the moral question to go right, but they don't trust their own judgment. So they find someone they regard as morally sharp and reliable, hear that person's verdict, and adopt it as their own. Assume the source is in fact correct this time.

My question: what's your credence that this person has gone wrong in a distinctively moral way—not just in the ordinary way you go wrong whenever you trust a speaker who might be mistaken? By distinctively moral I mean a failing tied to what moral judgment is or demands: a failure of understanding, a lapse of autonomy, a refusal to own one's reasons, something like that.

Decompose it for me. Sub-questions worth seperating include: (1) can a moral belief even be acquired that way, or does taking it on testimony stop it from being a full moral judgment? (2) does it matter what kind of content is deferred to—a verdict on this concrete case, a general principle the person then applies themselves, a bare instruction about what to do right now? (3) is the problem, if there is one, epistemic, or is it closer to moral cowardice or self-abandonment? Assign each a rough probability, say how they combine, identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me what kind of evidence or argument would move it most.
