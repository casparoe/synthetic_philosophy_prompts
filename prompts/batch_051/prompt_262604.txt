We keep going in circles reading about whether the big 19th- and 20th-century famines were natural disasters or political crimes, so I tried to write out the argument that they have to be political, and now it looks like it proves too much.

(1) A famine is a natural disaster only if the shortage of food was caused by a natural event — drought, flood, blight, bad weather.
(2) In Bengal in 1943 the harvest was poor but not catastrophic, and the standard account of the mass death runs through wartime procurement, hoarding, and the collapsing purchasing power of rural wage earners, while food kept moving around.
(3) In Ireland in the 1840s the potato crop failed, but the island kept exporting grain and livestock, and the deaths followed from the fact that the landless poor had no other claim on food and relief was organized in a way that didn't protect them.
(4) In the Great Leap Forward, bad weather in some areas is invoked, but the mass mortality followed procurement quotas and the suppression of honest reports, with grain still moving to the cities.
(5) In each case, what turned a bad harvest or a tight food market into mass death was a set of human choices, not the natural event itself.
(6) If what turns a bad harvest into mass death is a set of human choices, then the famine is not a natural disaster but a political crime.
(7) So these famines are political crimes, not natural disasters.

The problem is that (6) seems to apply to virtually any famine once there's a state or a market, since human choices always shape who loses access to food. So either almost no famine is a natural disaster, or something is wrong earlier in the argument. Which premise or step should I give up, and what does each option cost? I'd like to keep (1) and (5), but I can't see how to reject (6) without making natural disasters disappear from the vocabulary. Answer in American English, please.
