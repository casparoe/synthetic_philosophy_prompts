Write a dialogue of roughly 1,300–1,800 words between two equally sophisticated philosophers of social science: one a methodological individualist, the other a methodological holist. Their disagreement should be concrete rather than a survey of authors. They are arguing about a single question: what must a satisfactory explanation of a bank run look like?

The individualist maintains that any acceptable account must bottom out in the beliefs, preferences, and choices of individual agents — depositors, bank officers, and regulators — together with the common-knowledge structure linking those beliefs. On this view, a macro-level claim is legitimate only if it is underwritten by an individual-level mechanism, even when that mechanism is only sketched. The holist maintains that an explanation in terms of individual states is neither necessary nor adequate in the cases that matter: the causal work is done by irreducibly structural facts, such as leverage ratios, the topology of interbank claims, legal priority rules, and the fact that liquidity is a property of the system rather than of any depositor's mind.

Make the two characters genuinely respond to one another. In each round, the replying character should first state the strongest version of the point just made by the other and then explain why it still fails or needs qualification. Do not let either character retreat to slogans such as "there is no such thing as society" or "society is more than the sum of its parts."

Push the debate into the weeds by having the characters confront at least three of the following issues:

- the supervenience of social facts on individual facts, and why supervenience does not by itself give the individualist explanatory priority;
- multiple realizability — the same bank run arising from different individual psychologies — and whether robustness across psychologies is a reason to prefer a structural explanation;
- whether the demand for microfoundations is a demand about the logic of explanation or only about the standards of evidence;
- whether the relevant individuals are only the depositors or also the regulators, auditors, rating agencies, and clearing-house members, and what expanding the cast does to the individualist's proposed explanatory contrast;
- whether structure can be fully redescribed as a set of relations among individuals, which would reconcile the two views, or whether structural facts have causal powers over and above those relations.

After the dialogue, add an assessor's note. Speaking as the professor, state which side of the debate, as your two characters have actually articulated it, has the better case — or identify the precise ambiguity whose resolution would settle the dispute. Take a definite position and give your reasons; do not conclude merely that both sides have insights.
