I've ended up on the board of my housing co-op, and we're fighting about whether one committee should set a single price list for our shared workshop and kitchen spaces or whether people should just trade slots among themselves. Someone quoted this bit of Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945) at me and I have to respond in a few days, but I want to actually understand what it's doing:

"The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess. The economic problem of society is thus not merely a problem of how to allocate 'given' resources—if 'given' is taken to mean given to a single mind which deliberately solves the problem set by these 'data.' It is rather a problem of how to secure the best use of resources known to any of the members of society, for ends whose relative importance only these individuals know. Or, to put it briefly, it is a problem of the utilization of knowledge which is not given to anyone in its totality."

I get the surface point that knowledge is scattered and no single mind has it all. What I cant pin down is how this is supposed to cut against Lange's trial-and-error reply. As I understand Lange, the central planning board doesn't need to know everything: it just announces prices and adjusts them when there are surpluses or shortages, basically like a Walrasian auctioneer. Doesnt Hayek's point about dispersed knowledge leave that option untouched? Or is he making a different argument than Mises's original claim that without real capital markets there's no way to calculate at all? I want to be able to say, at our next meeting, exactly which step in Lange's proposal this passage is meant to break.
