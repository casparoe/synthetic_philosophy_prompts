I have been compiling a taxonomy of the distinct considerations philosophers have offered in favor of rejecting bivalence — that is, in favor of admitting that some declarative sentences are neither true nor false. I have read the usual discussions, and so far my file contains the four standard entries.

(1) Future contingents. Consider a sentence about an event that is not yet causally settled, such as there will be a sea battle tomorrow. On this line of thought, the sentence is neither true nor false now, because there is not yet a determinate fact for it to answer to; making it true or false today would amount to fixing an open future in advance.

(2) Vagueness. For a borderline case of a vague predicate, such as a man who is neither clearly bald nor clearly not bald, the sentence he is bald is held to be neither true nor false. There is no sharp boundary that classification is failing to detect; drawing one would be stipulation rather than discovery.

(3) Presupposition failure. The sentence the present King of France is bald is not assessable as true or false, on this view, because its presupposition — that there is a present King of France — fails. There is no object for the remark to be about, so the question of truth or falsity does not arise.

(4) Semantic paradox. The Liar sentence, this sentence is false, cannot be assigned the value true without implying it is false, and cannot be assigned the value false without implying it is true, at least given the principle that a sentence saying that P is true iff P. Treating the Liar as neither true nor false is therefore a principled way to block the contradiction rather than a local repair.

I want to know whether this taxonomy has a fifth independent member. Give me one further motivation for truth-value gaps that is structurally different from all four — not a special case of vagueness, reference failure, or sensitivity to time or world, and not a combination of them. Say what general feature of the relevant sentences makes bivalence fail, and say whether the standard treatments of the four cases above — supervaluationism for vagueness, a partial or three-valued assignment for the Liar, a Strawsonian presupposition analysis, and true-at-a-time-or-branch accounts of future contingents — would extend naturally to the new case or whether it demands a different kind of treatment. Finally, assess briefly whether the proposed motivation is any good, or whether it can be absorbed by one of the existing entries after all.
