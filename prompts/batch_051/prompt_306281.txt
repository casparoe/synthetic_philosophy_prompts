I have been trying to determine whether my moral judgments are held together by a general principle, or whether I am effectively deciding case by case. To make this concrete, here are the verdicts I find myself with.

(1) I promised my friend I would help him move on Saturday. The night before, my sister has a bad asthma attack while our parents are away, so I stay to drive her to urgent care and back. I do not think I did anything wrong.

(2) I promised the same friend I would help him move on Saturday, but on Friday someone offers me a free ticket to a concert that day. I cancel and go. I think this was wrong.

(3) My grandmother is in the hospital before surgery and keeps asking whether I have finished my college applications. I tell her yes, even though I have not, because the worry seems to be making her recovery harder. I think this was fine, though the lie unsettles me.

(4) A year later, the same grandmother, now healthy, asks the same question because she wants to have the conversation. I tell her yes even though I still have not, just to end the discussion sooner. I think that was not fine.

(5) My eight-year-old cousin has started playing chess. I let him win several games because it keeps him interested and happy. I think that is fine.

(6) My eight-year-old cousin is miserable about an online math quiz. I give him the correct answers so he gets a high score and is happy. I think that is not fine.

(7) At breakfast I take the last pancake while my brother is clearly about to reach for it. I think that was not fine, even if it seems trivial.

(8) A week later I take the last pancake after everyone at the table has said they are full and no one wants it. I think that is obviously fine.

The problem is that I cannot find a principle that tracks all of these. The two promise cases look nearly identical, since in both I break a promise to spend Saturday differently, yet one feels clearly justified and the other clearly wrong. The two grandmother cases involve the same lie, separated only by her condition and my motive. And cases (5) and (6) both involve letting my cousin get a success he did not fully earn, yet I draw a sharp line between them.

The closest I can get to a principle is something like: breaking a commitment, telling a lie, or otherwise departing from the ordinary rules is permitted only when it prevents a foreseeable harm that clearly outweighs the harm done, and not merely to benefit yourself. But this principle seems incomplete. It handles the promise and grandmother cases reasonably, but it does not explain the chess and quiz cases, which involve producing happiness rather than preventing harm. It also makes the pancake case look like a mystery: no serious harm is at stake, yet I find taking the last pancake from my brother not fine.

Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and tell me what it says about a case I keep going back and forth on: telling a friend, right before he goes on stage to give a speech he has practiced badly, that his speech is good, in order to steady his nerves. If no principled account captures the pattern, should I accept that my moral judgment is genuinely case by case, in the way moral particularists say, or is that conclusion too quick?
