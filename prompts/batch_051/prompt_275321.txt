I just got out of a long argument with a friend about art forgeries and now I'm not sure which of us is confused. He had an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I couldn't find the exact step where it goes wrong. You're a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and the metaphysics of value; could you think through this slowly and tell me which step fails, or whether the conclusion is just one I have to swallow? Maybe start by clarifying what each step is actually committing me to, since he built it up fast and I may have nodded along to loosely.

His argument went roughly like this. (1) Imagine technology eventually lets us make a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a painting — same pigments, same craquelure, same dust, same everything currently detectable. (2) If two things are physically identical in every detectable respect, then any reason for preferring one over the other would have to appeal to something undetectable. (3) The only features that distinguish an original from a duplicate like that are relational, historical facts: this canvas was touched by Vermeer, hung in that church, was stolen in that war, and so on. (4) But the value of a thing has to be grounded in the thing itself — in its detectable, intrinsic features — not in facts about its past. (5) So the duplicate has exactly as much value as the original. (6) By the same reasoning, you should be completely indifferent between owning the real painting and owning the duplicate, a museum should be indifferent between displaying the original and displaying the duplicate, and burning the original after making a duplicate would destroy nothing of value. (7) But that's crazy: if we learned the Louvre had secretly replaced the Mona Lisa with a molecule-for-molecule copy, we'd feel that something had been lost, not just that we'd been lied to.

He says the only alternative is admitting that an object's value can change because of facts you'd never notice, which he calls snobbery or fetishism. My instinct is to reject something earlier in the argument rather than accept (7), but every step looked fine as he stated it. Which step do I actually reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I half-remember a distinction my professor made between arts where the particular physical object matters and arts where any accurate copy counts (paintings versus novels, I think), and I suspect that distinction is doing work here, but I can't see exactly how.
