I am an undergraduate in a seminar on video games and the aesthetics of agency, where we have been treating player agency as an aesthetic category rather than merely a design feature. We keep circling the question of what a public arts funder would actually be selecting for if it awarded grants to games, and I want to stress-test a concrete proposal instead of rearguing the are-games-art question.

Here is the scheme. A public arts council creates a Video Games Fund with a two-stage evaluation. At submission, a developer provides a fifteen-minute playable segment, a short artist statement, and a depleted script: a walkthrough of the same segment that uses only the interactions strictly required to advance, ignoring every optional choice, branch, and side system. A three-person jury plays the segment twice, once freely and once following the depleted script, and assigns an agency score from 0 to 10 measuring how much of the work's expressive content is carried by the felt difference between the two runs rather than by its story, image, or sound. The total score weights this agency score at fifty percent, craft at twenty, ambition at fifteen, and accessibility of the intended experience at fifteen.

The second stage is an audit. Three first-time players, recruited by the council and shown nothing from the artist statement, each play the segment once. The council logs how far their playthroughs diverge from each other and from the depleted script in choices taken, systems engaged, and outcomes reached. If the three playthroughs cluster tightly, or all track the depleted script, the agency score is capped at six out of ten, on the theory that agency that ordinary first-time players do not actually exercise is decorative rather than aesthetic.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a policy to be endorsed. Walk through which kinds of games and developers it would select for and against; where it would be gamed first and how; how the fifteen-minute segments themselves would change once studios optimize for the procedure; what the lay audit would do to applicant behavior that the jury stage alone would not; and which of these failure modes could be repaired by changing a single parameter without abandoning the scheme.
