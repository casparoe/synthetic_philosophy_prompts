Next month I'm presenting at a public law / political theory workshop, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I'm defending is that constitutional entrenchment is not a real case of precommitment: unlike Ulysses tying himself to the mast, a constitution binds people who never did the binding, so the one-agent-past-self-constraining-future-self picture gets the agency wrong. My positive line is that we should think of entrenchment as a coordination scheme (or contract-ish arrangement) among overlapping generations, where the binding is something the older generation does to later ones, not something a single "people" does to itself. My argument in one sentence: precommitment needs a single agent whose earlier self has authority over its later self; the intergenerational "people" isn't one; so the precommitment story either silently assumes a corporate agent or fails.

The room is mostly constitutional lawyers and theorists who like the precommitment framing of bills of rights and supermajority rules, plus two or three philosophers of action who work on self-control and think the personal precommitment case is basically settled; I'd rather not have to litigate personal precommitment with them. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way this crowd would put them, each with a short reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and say why it's dangerous. Also, where a reply leans on a contested view of precommitment or collective agency, flag it as contested rather than consensus, and keep track of which parts of the debate are actually settled versus genuinely up for grabs among people who work on this.
