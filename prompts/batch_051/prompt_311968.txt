You are a constitutional lawyer and political philosopher who specializes in self-determination, minority rights, and statutory interpretation. Please work through each interpretive approach carefully, begin by brainstorming the strongest version of each argument, and do not assume the text simply settles the matter.

I serve on the Atheta eligibility committee, and I am myself an Atheta person displaced to the mainland; whether my community may vote on its own future depends on how far Article 4 reaches. Article 4 of our federation's Basic Law reads:

"A people whose traditional homeland lies within the Federation may freely determine its political status, including by secession, but only through a plebiscite of its members habitually resident in that homeland; the plebiscite is valid only if at least half of those resident members cast a ballot and a majority of the votes cast favour the proposal."

The Atheta homeland is a low-lying island that recurrent flooding has made uninhabitable. Over fifteen years the Federation resettled nearly all Atheta to mainland towns; only about 800 voting-age Atheta remain on the island. Last year the Atheta people held two votes. An online plebiscite open to self-identifying Atheta worldwide, in which the diaspora participated heavily, drew over 11,000 ballots and returned 78% in favour of independence. A separate in-person plebiscite for the roughly 800 members still resident on the island returned 61% in favour, but only 34% of those resident members cast a ballot. The Federation says neither vote satisfies Article 4; the Atheta committee says the drafters never imagined the loss of a homeland and that a literal reading would let a natural disaster extinguish a people's right of self-determination.

What does Article 4 require here, and which of the two plebiscites, if either, satisfies it? Organize your answer as follows:

1. "Textual reading" (2–3 sentences): the strongest case from the words of Article 4.
2. "Purposive reading" (2–3 sentences): the strongest case from the evident purpose of Article 4.
3. "What the drafters would have said" (2–3 sentences): how the drafters would most plausibly have handled an uninhabitable homeland had it been put to them.
4. "The reasonable reader" (1–2 sentences): what an informed, reasonable reader of the Basic Law would understand Article 4 to require in this case.
5. "Verdict" (3–4 sentences): which approach should win, and whether either plebiscite counts.
