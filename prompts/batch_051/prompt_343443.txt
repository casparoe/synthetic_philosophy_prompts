I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of aesthetic judgment, and I keep being struck by an asymmetry in how people treat expert testimony about works of art. Suppose an art historian tells you, about a painting you have never seen, that it was produced in a particular workshop, that technical analysis has revealed an earlier composition underneath it, and that it first sold for almost nothing. Most people are happy to accept these claims on her authority, file them away, and repeat them with modest hedging. But if the same art historian adds that the painting is also a beautiful one, or that its composition is a genuine aesthetic achievement, ordinary speakers suddenly refuse to let the testimony settle the matter. It sounds perfectly fine to say "I believe the painting has an earlier composition underneath it, though I've never seen it," but something close to incoherent about "I believe the painting is beautiful, though I've never seen it"—even when the informant is beyond reproach. The same asymmetry shows up across the arts: we accept testimony about dates, provenance, technique, influence, and reception, but resist testimony about whether a work is elegant, profound, subtle, or successful.

I'd like your help deciding whether this asymmetry is justified, or whether it is simply an inconsistency in the way we police aesthetic judgment. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of aesthetics. Start by brainstorming the best candidate justifications for the asymmetry—for instance, appeals to the first-person or experiential character of aesthetic judgment, to the idea that aesthetic properties must be directly "acquainted" with rather than taken on trust, to differences between aesthetic and non-aesthetic concepts, to the pragmatics of assertion, or to norms specific to testimony. Then defend what you take to be the strongest candidate against the obvious objections. In doing so, test your account against nearby cases: a music teacher who tells you before you have heard it that a passage is elegant; a trusted friend who says a film you have not seen is overrated; a critic who reports that a difficult novel is "difficult but rewarding"; an expert who says a work is important but not very good. Finally, say whether the asymmetry should be kept as it is, narrowed to a special class of aesthetic judgments, or given up, and explain why. Think carefully and write as though to a philosophy colleague.
