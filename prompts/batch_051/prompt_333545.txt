You are an expert in political philosophy with a working knowledge of labor economics and the structure of antitrust law. Think carefully, and resist the temptation to settle on the first distinction that sounds plausible: brainstorm several candidate justifications, test each against the variant cases below, and only then commit to a verdict.

Here is an asymmetry in ordinary legal practice that I want you to assess. In many Western legal systems, a group of employees is permitted — indeed encouraged — to coordinate in the sale of their labor: to form a union, bargain collectively for a wage floor, and back the demand with a coordinated withdrawal of labor, a strike. But if a group of independent businesses in the same market coordinates on a minimum price and backs the demand by collectively withholding supply, that is price-fixing, a core antitrust offense carrying fines or imprisonment. In both cases, suppliers of something agree not to sell it below a common price, backed by a collective refusal to deal. Yet the one is called organizing and is protected as a fundamental right; the other is called collusion and is treated as a public wrong.

Your task: say whether this asymmetry is justified, and by what. Work through at least the following candidate distinctions, and say which survive scrutiny and which do not:

- Labor is not an ordinary commodity: a worker's wage engages their livelihood and dignity in a way the price of bread or trucking does not.
- Bargaining-power asymmetry: an individual employee typically faces an employer with far greater bargaining power, whereas a firm joining a cartel need not face any comparable imbalance.
- Who benefits: unions raise the incomes of wage-earners, while cartels can raise the incomes of owners or shareholders, who may already be well off.
- Exit costs: a worker changing jobs usually bears much heavier costs than a customer changing suppliers.
- Workplace voice: collective bargaining gives workers some say in the conditions under which they spend a large part of their waking lives.

Do not stop with these. Add your own candidate justifications, and then test the strongest ones against the hardest cases: a union of already very well-paid workers coordinating to raise wages further; a group of self-employed freelancers coordinating their rates, often treated as price-fixing rather than organizing; and a group of small farmers agreeing on a common minimum price to stay solvent. Does the principle that justifies the asymmetry also settle these cases, or does it break down at the edges? Conclude by saying whether you would keep the asymmetry, extend one side to the other, or replace the bright-line distinction with something finer.
