A republic's written constitution contains the following provision, headed "Vacancy in the Office of President":

"1. The office of President shall become vacant upon the death of the President, upon resignation in writing signed by the President and delivered to the Speaker of the House of Representatives, or upon removal from office following conviction on impeachment.
2. Upon a vacancy in the office of President, the First Deputy President shall assume the office of President for the remainder of the unexpired term.
3. If the offices of President and First Deputy President are vacant at the same time, the Speaker of the House of Representatives shall exercise the powers and perform the duties of the office of President until the House of Representatives elects a President to serve the remainder of the unexpired term."

Now suppose this case arises. At a state ceremony, a poisoning attack leaves both the President and the First Deputy President alive but in a permanent vegetative state, with no prospect, on the unanimous medical evidence, of recovering consciousness or any capacity to communicate or form an intention. The constitution contains no provision governing incapacity short of death. Resignation requires the office-holder's own signed writing, and impeachment is limited to cases of "serious misconduct," with the accused traditionally given an opportunity to appear and answer; neither man can do either. Amid a currency crisis and a border incursion, the Speaker convenes the House, which passes a resolution declaring that the two offices are "vacant" within the meaning of the provision above and directing the Speaker to exercise the powers of the office of President. A coalition led by the President's own party challenges this before the constitutional court, arguing that a living officer cannot be a vacancy, however incapacitated, and that the Speaker is therefore acting without lawful authority.

Interpret and adjudicate this dispute. Specifically, work out what each of the following approaches yields about whether the Speaker lawfully exercises presidential power: (a) the plain words of the clause; (b) its evident purpose, including the prevention of a vacuum in the exercise of executive authority; (c) the most plausible answer of the constitution's drafters if asked about permanent incapacity; and (d) the best reading available to a reasonable interpreter of the constitutional scheme as a whole. Weigh the strengths and weaknesses of these approaches, and state and defend the conclusion you would reach. Do not stop at a survey of approaches; decide the case.
