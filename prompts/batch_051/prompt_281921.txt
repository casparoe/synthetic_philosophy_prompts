I've been stuck for a week on a passage in Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895). It's a short dialogue where the Tortoise accepts the premises (A) and (B) of a little Euclidean argument, and then lets Achilles keep adding the conditional "If ... then Z follows" as yet another premise, without ever drawing the conclusion Z. The part I keep going back to is this:

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

My understanding before reading this was that if you accept A and B, and you also accept that A and B entail Z, then logic just moves you to Z — no extra act of acceptance needed. But here the Tortoise seems to say that each conditional, once written down, can still fail to be "seen" as true, and therefore can always be treated as one more premise that he hasn't granted. I can't tell whether he's discovered a real gap in the foundations of deduction, or whether he's simply confusing a rule of inference with a premise. In my lab days I would never of said that, after accepting that a sample was heated and that heating it would decompose it, I still needed one more belief before concluding it had decomposed — I just did the inference. So what is the Tortoise doing here, exactly? Is Achilles's real mistake agreeing to write the conditionals into the notebook as though they were just more premises, and should he instead have insisted that modus ponens isn't the kind of thing you "accept" as a premise at all? And if that's right, does the regress that worries the Tortoise show anything deep about how deduction is justified, or does it dissolve once you notice the category difference? I'd like you to walk me through the passage as closely as you can, because I suspect its subtler than "the Tortoise is just stubborn."
