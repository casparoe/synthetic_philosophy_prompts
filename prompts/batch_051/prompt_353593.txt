I am a survey methodologist with no training in political philosophy. My working vocabulary is sampling and estimation: sampling frames, coverage and nonresponse error, stratified and clustered designs, post-stratification weights, bias–variance trade-offs, mode effects, and the difference between drawing a sample that estimates a population parameter and running a sample through deliberation that changes its members' views. I have lately been encountering arguments for lottocracy — the proposal that part or all of a legislature should be chosen by lot from the full adult population, often with stratification, rather than by election. The defenders seem to me to be making a statistical argument: election is a selection procedure with systematic bias toward the wealthy, the educated, and the organized, and sortition removes that bias by giving every citizen a known, equal chance of inclusion, yielding a body that descriptively mirrors the public. Restate the lottocrat's positive case, and the strongest contrast with electoral representation, in my sampling-and-estimation vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the lottocrat's claim translate cleanly — for example, to unbiased estimation, stratification, and the removal of selection bias — and which parts translate only with distortion, such as the idea that the assembly's judgment after deliberation is still an estimate of what the public currently thinks? Which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, such as legitimacy, authority, accountability, or the sense of being represented? End by saying whether the residue shows that my vocabulary is missing something essential to the political question, or that lottocracy is, at bottom, a statistical proposal wearing democratic language. Report the residue honestly, and do not smuggle normative premises back in under the cover of sampling terminology.
