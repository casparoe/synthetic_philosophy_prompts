Honestly, this one has us deadlocked. I'm on the grants committee of a small private foundation that funds what we label "speculative-but-serious" work in science and philosophy, and a philosopher and a theoretical physicist have asked for three years of funding to develop what they call "simulation-robust decision theory" — roughly, a systematic account of how we should choose, plan, and assign probabilities if some version of the simulation argument is right. Their application leans hard on a single paragraph from Nick Bostrom's paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", which they quote as:

"Moreover, we need not assume that in order to create a mind on a computer it would be sufficient to program it in such a way that it behaves like a human in all situations, including passing the Turing test etc. We need only the weaker assumption that it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses."

One faction on the committee says the whole thing is unfalsifiable metaphysics dressed up as research, and that Bostrom's argument is no foundation for a three-year project on decision theory. The other side says the interesting question isn't whether we're in a simulation but whether the argument's premises — especially this "weaker assumption" — actually do the work they're being asked to do, and that's proper philosophy, maybe with some formal epistemology mixed in. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it: a concrete recommendation (fund, reject, or fund with conditions and a time frame), with reasons the losing side can genuinely engage, and flag which part of the recommendation I should be most willing to reverse and on what evidence. If you think the committee is framing the problem badly — for instance by treating "unfalsifiable" as an automatic disqualifier for a fund we ourselves called speculative, or by reading Bostrom's passage as a stronger claim than the one he's actually making — say so plainly instead of writing around it. And please write it in American English.
