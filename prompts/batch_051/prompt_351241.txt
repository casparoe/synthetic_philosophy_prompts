OK so I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole on the side of Berkeley that isn't the idealism, namely the money stuff. From what I can reconstruct (I haven't read all of it), he was writing in 1730s Ireland, basicly an economy with a shortage of coin and a lot of idle hands, and his whole position was that gold and silver aren't real wealth — they're just tickets or counters for recording and transferring claims on the actual wealth, which is productive labor applied to land. He wanted a national bank to issue paper notes, treated hoarding as a sort of pathology, and kept asking whether a slip of paper doesn't do the same office as a gold coin as long as people will accept it and it actually circulates. The throughline, as I read it: money is a social technology for mobilizing idle people, circulation beats accumulation, and the commodity value of the token is irrelevant.

So here's the game. Sit Berkeley down in the present and show him two things: first, Bitcoin — a token with zero intrinsic backing and no state, but mostly held rather than spent, produced by burning real resources just to maintain a ledger; second, modern fiat after Bretton Woods, especially the MMT-flavored version where the state spends pure tickets into existence and taxes some of them back, plus the coming central bank digital currencies. Give me your best prediction of what he'd actually say about each, and defend it — no hedged survey, pick a stance. For each one, say which of his actual principles is doing the work, where he'd have to update or where his 1730s model doesn't transfer, and flag the parts of the prediction you're least confident about. I keep going back and forth: on the face of it Bitcoin is the purest possible ticket, which should make it appealing to him, but it also seems to violate basically everything else he thought money was for.
