I have been thinking, mostly on my own, about when it is all right to put other people at risk without asking them. I have a list of cases and my own verdicts on them, reached more by feel than by principle, and I cannot find the rule behind the verdicts. I would like you to find it for me, or tell me that there is no one rule and which verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases.

1. My driving. I drive to the grocery store a few times a week, sober, in a well-maintained car, within the speed limit, with my phone put away. Each trip still imposes a tiny chance of death or injury on pedestrians and other drivers who never agreed to it. My verdict: I am not doing anything wrong.

2. Fireworks. A neighbor sets off powerful fireworks in his backyard in a neighborhood of closely spaced wooden houses, on a dry and windy evening. He does not ask anyone and has no precautions beyond pointing them at the sky. My verdict: wrong.

3. The flu. A person who feels the flu coming on goes to a sold-out indoor concert anyway, without a mask, because he does not want to waste the ticket. My verdict: wrong.

4. The plant. A chemical plant vents a byproduct over a town. The release gives each of the 100,000 people living near it a 1-in-100,000 chance of dying of cancer over the next year, which means that, statistically, one person will die. The plant is profitable, and the neighbors never agreed to the exposure. My verdict: wrong. It stays wrong if the plant later cuts the release a hundredfold, so that the release now kills, statistically, a small fraction of one person each year: less wrong, but still wrong.

5. The flight path. A city approves a new approach path that sends planes over a residential district. About 200,000 people live under it, and each resident's annual chance of being killed by a crash rises by 1 in 2,000,000. That works out to roughly one predicted death every ten years. The route serves the whole region and keeps fares down, but the people underneath did not consent. My verdict: acceptable.

6. The delivery vans. A home bakery operates on a quiet residential street where children play. It sends three delivery vans down the street each day. The vans go slowly, but each child's chance of being hit rises by a very small amount that no one on the street agreed to accept. I cannot decide whether this is like my car trips, which I call fine, or like the plant and the fireworks, which I call wrong.

My closest attempt at a rule is this: imposing a risk on someone without consent is permissible only when it is part of an ordinary practice that the exposed people generally take part in and benefit from, and it is wrong when a distinct group is made to carry a risk for someone else's benefit. But I can already see trouble. The people under the new flight path are a distinct group who did not choose it, and they may not fly any more than anyone else. Yet I still come out accepting the flight path. So the rule probably needs repair or replacement.

Is there one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that gets my five settled verdicts right, or nearly right? State it precisely, test it against every case above, say whether my tentative rule can be repaired or should be thrown away, then apply the best principle you find to the delivery-van case and to one further case of your own choosing that I have not listed. Also tell me which of my settled verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to give up.

Please structure your answer exactly as follows:

- "The best principle" (about 150 words): state one principle, or at most two competing ones, precise enough to deliver a verdict on each case.
- "Testing the cases" (about 250 words): go through my six cases in order and show what the principle says about each, flagging any mismatch with the settled verdicts rather than smoothing it over.
- "The hard pair" (about 200 words): put the plant case and the flight-path case side by side and explain why they are the hardest to hold together; show how your principle deals with the tension or which verdict it overturns.
- "The undecided case" (about 150 words): apply the principle to the home-bakery delivery vans.
- "What I must give up" (about 100 words): name any of my settled verdicts, or parts of verdicts, that cannot survive the principle you recommend, and say whether the principle only saves them by becoming complicated.
