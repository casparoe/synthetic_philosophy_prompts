I’m putting together a discussion worksheet for my philsoophy of religion seminar on the problem of evil. I’d like you to think about what actually makes something a theodicy rather than just a dodge or a restatement of the problem. Here are the criteria I usually give: a theodicy (i) grants that God exists and that at least some evil is real rather than an illusion; (ii) gives a reason for why God permits evil that’s supposed to be compatible with God’s being perfectly good; (iii) doesn’t merely say the evil is “for the best” but actually shows how the evil itself does some explanatory work in the story; and (iv) leaves the traditional attributes of God — omniscience, omnipotence, benevolence — intact, or at worst reinterprets them only slightly.

Now here’s the borderline case I want you to work through. In Part XI of Hume’s Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, Philo offers what he calls four circumstances that account for the misery found among animals and humans. As I remember them — and I want you to work from my summary, not from memory of the text — first, pains and pleasures are the springs of action, so creatures are driven mostly by discomfort and only occasionally by ease; second, the world is run by general laws, not by particular divine volitions, so good and bad weather fall on the just and the unjust alike; third, each animal’s powers and faculties are granted with great frugality, just enough to get by, so that most beings are one bad season or injury away from ruin; and fourth, nature’s workmanship is neither exact nor accurate, full of missteps, waste, and misfires. Philo then suggests these features are less like the work of an infinitely powerful and good creator and more like what you’d expect from a very limited or morally indifferent cause.

So here’s the question: does Philo’s discussion of the four circumstances qualify as a theodicy under my four criteria, or is it a different kind of response to evil? Go through each criterion and say whether his account meets it, where my criteria underdetermine the answer, and finally whether the case shows that my criteria themselves need revision — for example, should a response that concludes God is less than perfectly good count as a theodicy at all?
