I am a high school student currently working through Kant's Critique of Pure Reason for a class, and I would appreciate help with a passage that has been troubling me. Near the beginning of the book, in the Preface (translated by J. M. D. Meiklejohn), Kant writes:

Human reason, in one sphere of its cognition, is called upon to consider questions, which it cannot decline, as they are presented by its own nature, but which it cannot answer, as they transcend every faculty of the mind.

It falls into this difficulty without any fault of its own. It begins with principles, which cannot be dispensed with in the field of experience, and the truth and sufficiency of which are, at the same time, insured by experience. With these principles it rises, in obedience to the laws of its own nature, to ever higher and more remote conditions. But it quickly discovers that, in this way, its labours must remain ever incomplete, because new questions never cease to present themselves; and thus it finds itself compelled to have recourse to principles which transcend the region of experience, while they are regarded by common sense without distrust. It thus falls into confusion and contradictions, from which it conjectures the presence of latent errors, which, however, it is unable to discover, because the principles it employs, transcending the limits of experience, cannot be tested by that criterion. The arena of these endless contests is called Metaphysic.

My difficulty is with how this diagnosis is supposed to show up in the antinomies. I have read the First Antinomy several times, and both the thesis and the antithesis seem like legitimate proofs to me. As I understand them, the thesis argues that if the world had no beginning in time, then an infinite series of successive states would already have elapsed, which is impossible, so the world must have a beginning. The antithesis argues that if the world did have a beginning, then before that beginning there would have been an empty time in which the world did not yet exist, and since nothing can come to be in an empty time, the world could not have arisen, so it cannot have a beginning. I can follow each proof on its own, but I cannot see where either one goes wrong, and I do not understand how the two can both seem so compelling.

Could you walk me through the First Antinomy in detail, explain what each proof is actually doing, and then relate it back to the passage I quoted? In particular, I would like to understand what Kant means when he says that reason falls into contradictions because it uses principles that transcend experience, and how that diagnosis applies to this specific case. Is one of the two proofs supposed to contain a hidden error, or are both of them somehow illegitimate, or is the whole question about the world's beginning in time somehow ill-posed? And if the question is ill-posed, why does reason nevertheless find it unavoidable?
