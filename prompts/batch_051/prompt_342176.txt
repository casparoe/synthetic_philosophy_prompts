Consider the paradox of tragedy: why do people voluntarily and repeatedly seek out films, novels, and plays that arouse sadness, grief, or dread? Below are six candidate explanations, described in neutral terms.

1. Catharsis. The work purges or discharges negative affect, leaving a felt sense of relief or restored equilibrium.
2. Compensation. The displeasure caused by the depicted events is outweighed by, or converted into, pleasure taken in the beauty, craft, or meaningfulness of the work.
3. Meta-emotion. The first-order sadness remains unpleasant, but the viewer takes a higher-order satisfaction in being the kind of person who is moved, or in the very experience of being moved.
4. Control. Fictional contexts allow us to undergo powerful negative emotions in a safe setting, and the safe exercise of these emotional capacities is itself gratifying.
5. Insight. The sadness is not enjoyed at all; it is accepted as the cost of arriving at some understanding the work affords.
6. Dissolution. The paradox is ill-posed because so-called enjoyment of tragedy covers several heterogeneous activities, none of which individually requires enjoying one's own sadness.

Now impose this hard case: a person who steers away from opportunities to feel sad in her own life, reports that the sadness she feels during tragic films is genuinely unpleasant while it lasts, can identify no higher-order satisfaction in being moved, and yet repeatedly chooses and recommends such films.

Sort these six explanations into three tiers — strongest, middling, and weakest — according to how well they account for this hard case. Within each tier, order the explanations from strongest to weakest. For each explanation, provide exactly one sentence justifying its placement. Finally, state which tier you regard as the minimal threshold for an acceptable explanation of this case, and explain why in one or two sentences.
