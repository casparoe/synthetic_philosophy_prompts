Ok here's one I keep going back and forth on. In Parfit's hitchhiker, a man stranded in the desert promises a driver he'll pay, say, $100 once he's been dropped off safely, even though at that point he'll have no self-interested reason to pay: the ride is over and the driver can't punish him. The driver picks him up anyway because he can tell the man is the sort of person who keeps those promises. The standard reading is that this is a case where it's rational to be irrational at the moment of action—to hand over the money later, despite having no local reason to, because the disposition to do so is exactly what got him the ride in the first place.

I'd like you make an argument for a less standard take: that the payment isn't locally irrational either. The error is individuating the act as "handing over $100 to a stranger after the service is complete" rather than as "the final stage of keeping the promise that got him across the desert." If acts are individuated over their whole temporal span, and under the description that makes them the acts they are, then ordinary causal decision theory already says pay, and we don't need to posit rational irrationality. Then take on the obvious objection: what's to stop this from being a cheap relabeling trick, where any apparently altruistic act gets redescribed as the tail end of a longer self-interested scheme? What fixes the boundaries of "the act" here, and is that boundary itself just pragmatic?
