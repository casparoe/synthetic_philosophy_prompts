I've been reading Dennett's Quining Qualia and trying to reconstruct his argument as a numbered chain, because he seems to be doing something sneakier than just denying qualia exist. Here's what I've got:

(1) Qualia are supposed to be ineffable, intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible by the subject. (2) But when we run through the standard thought experiments — inverted spectrum, absent qualia, the neurosurgery patient who notices a shift — our judgements about when qualia change, disappear, or invert are driven by background assumptions and memory, not by a direct comparison with something private. (3) If our judgements about qualia are driven by external factors and not by direct apprehension, then the notion of direct apprehension does no explanatory work. (4) If the notion of direct apprehension does no explanatory work, then the strict definition of qualia fails to pick out any property we can sensibly attribute. (5) So qualia, as strictly defined, are not real properties but artefacts of a bad theory.

I think step 3 is where the sleight of hand is, but I can't tell whether Dennett would actually accept step 3 or whether he thinks the thought experiments only show our reports are unreliable, not that there's no direct apprehension. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why?
