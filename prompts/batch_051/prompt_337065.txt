My friend is on the acquisitions committee at the county historical society, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer their collection policy because their decisions feel principled but I can't quite pin the principle down. Here's their track record as I've gathered it:

- They accepted the personal and business papers of the family that owned the local mill for a century, even though the family insisted some 1910s correspondence stay sealed until 2040.
- They declined a general store's complete account ledgers because the state historical society already holds very similar ledgers from the same county and period.
- They accepted an oral-history project with Black residents of the neighborhood bulldozed for an interstate spur, on the condition that descendants get to review interview transcripts before they're opened.
- They declined a mining company's records after the company asked for a written pledge that the archive would not also collect from the union local.
- They accepted the archive of a street photographer who shot candid photos in the 1930s, including strikers and children, with no consent from anyone in the frame.
- They declined a trove of diaries and love letters between two local women from the 1920s, offered by a great-niece, saying they were too intimate to make public.

Now they're deciding whether to accept the complete membership and sick-benefit ledgers of an immigrant mutual aid society, roughly 1900 to 1935. The ledgers record names, addresses, births, illnesses, deaths, and who owed what, for families that are almost entirely absent from the rest of the archive's holdings. The donor is a private collector who bought them at an estate sale and wants them fully open to genealogists, with no access restriction.

Act as an archivist who works on documentary silences and the ethics of collecting, and think carefully. What should they decide if they want to be consistent with their own precedents? Which prior decision is the closest analog? Lay out the different principles that fit the track record, say what each would do with the ledgers, give your best prediction of what they'll actually do, and name a further case that would most cleanly seperate those principles.
