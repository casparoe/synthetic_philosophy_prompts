I'm an associate provost at a mid-size public university, and every spring I co-chair a program-review committee that has to recommend which small departments to protect, merge, or cut; the provost and trustees mostly care about the budget. Most of the committee are scientists and accountants and none of us are philosophers. Write me a decision procedure we can actually run in a two-hour meeting, grounded in whatever philosophy actually has figured out about the value of 'useless' scholarship (intrinsic vs instrumental value, the can't-predict-spinoffs argument, the difference between a field being useless and merely non-vocational, mission statements) but expressed as an ordered checklist with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a value call. Then run it on three cases: a classics department with six majors a year, a pure-math group doing foundations, and an ethnobotany program thats losing students but sits near strong indigenous communities. Make sure at least one of the outcomes is one I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Start with the procedure itself (the ordered checklist), no preface or commentary.
