I've ended up convinced of something I keep using to size up any field I read about, and I'd like you to tell me how exposed I actually am. The view is this: the difference between fields that build cumulative knowledge and fields that just produce more and more sophisticated commentary is mostly feedback. Specifically, how fast and how unambiguously practitioners find out they're wrong. When the world snaps back quickly and hard—the bridge collapses, the program crashes, tomorrow's forecast is checked against tomorrow—the field accumulates. When feedback is slow, noisy, or easy to reinterpret—a cure takes twenty years, a policy has mixed results, an interpretation is always "fruitful"—the field spins.

I want an honest map of what would count against this. Not just the two obvious objections I get at parties (pure math, maybe string theory), but the whole inventory of things that should genuinely move me. For each one, tell me whether its fatal, seriously weakening, or just awkward—and whether a committed defender would shrug it off by precisifying "feedback" or "progress," and whether that move is legitimate or smuggling. Address the math case head-on: is a proof just extremely fast, unambiguous feedback, in which case math supports the view—or is saying that exactly the kind of insulation I should be worried about? Dont let me off the hook there.

Also throw cases at me where the pattern breaks: a field with tight feedback that still didn't accumulate (I keep thinking about social psychology before the replication crisis—experiments look like feedback, so why did so much of it fail to build?), and a field with almost no feedback that built something real anyway. Tell me which of those, if any, would falsify the strong version of the view versus just force a weaker version.

Finally, I want the meta-question: does the view risk anything at all, or have I made it so elastic that no field could ever count against it? What would the world have to look like for the feedback-first story to be wrong, not just incomplete? If you conclude it's insulated, say whether that's a problem for a view of this kind or just the normal condition of a big-picture claim. I'd personally be most moved by a case where a field gets fast, unmistakable feedback and still fails to progress, especially if the failure looks structural rather than just the field being young.
