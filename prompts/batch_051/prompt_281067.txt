I am a retired natural scientist — most of my working life was spent in experimental physics — and I have spent my retirement working through the philosophy of science. The line of thought I keep returning to is the Popperian idea that a theory earns the title 'scientific' not by being supported but by its proponents specifying in advance what observation would show it to be wrong. That has mostly remained a philosopher's criterion, and it is not obvious to me what would happen if someone tried to build it into machinery for organizing actual research. Here is the machinery I would like you to analyze.

Suppose a public research council adopts the following rules for any proposal whose central deliverable is a general theory rather than a bounded empirical claim. At submission, the applicant must register a single 'refuter': an observation or measurement outcome, specified precisely enough that two competent observers would agree about whether it had occurred, such that if it occurs the applicant has committed in advance to the theory's having failed. The refuter is archived in a public register and cannot be changed silently; any amendment is logged, published under the applicant's name, and counted against the project at renewal. If the registered refuter is later observed, the theory is moved onto a published list of refuted theories: for five years no publicly funded project may rely on it as an accepted premise, and the original group may submit a descendant theory only if the new proposal explains explicitly why the old refuter no longer applies and registers a new one. Journals receiving public funds are required, as a condition of that funding, not to publish work that treats a listed theory as an accepted premise. Grant renewal requires real progress toward either testing the refuter or showing that testing it is feasible on a stated timescale, and a proposal whose refuter is unobservable in practice is graded down rather than up.

Treat this as institutional engineering, not as a referendum on whether Popper was right. I want a committed analysis of how the scheme would actually behave in a live scientific community. What would it incentivize among theorists, experimentalists, journals, and the council's own reviewers? Where would it be gamed first — for example, by choosing refuters that are safe because they sit beyond any feasible experiment, by splitting one theory into many small theories each with its own refuter, by blaming a realized refuter on instruments or auxiliary assumptions rather than on the theory (the familiar point that no hypothesis is tested in isolation), or by letting the registry's adjudications quietly become the new arena for the old demarcation dispute? How would the scheme handle theories whose predictions are statistical rather than binary? Please take a definite position rather than surveying the possibilities: which failure modes would dominate, whether the scheme would on balance discipline theorizing or merely move the hedging to a new layer, and which fixes, if any, would preserve the original idea.

End with a single sentence as the very last line, beginning 'Bottom line:', stating your committed assessment in one sentence.
