I've been working through Skolem's paradox, and I'm having trouble seeing why it doesn't collapse the distinction between countable and uncountable entirely. Here is the puzzle as I understand it. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that if ZFC is consistent, it has a model whose domain contains only countably many elements. ZFC, meanwhile, proves that the real numbers form an uncountable set. So there is a countable model of ZFC that says the real numbers are uncountable; from outside the model, however, its 'real numbers' form part of a domain that can be listed off against the natural numbers, so there are only countably many of them after all. The same collection is countable when viewed from outside the model and uncountable when viewed from within it. It seems to follow that 'countable' and 'uncountable' are relative to the model one is standing in, and that there is no objective fact of the matter about whether the continuum is countable or uncountable. But then Cantor's theorem that the reals are uncountable would be an artifact of a model's internal resources, not a discovery about any fixed mathematical reality. I suspect this inference is too fast, but I can't locate the step where it goes wrong. Doesn't the paradox force us to admit that the question how many real numbers there are has no model-independent answer?
