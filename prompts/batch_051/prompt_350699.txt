Ever since I caught myself opening the same app for the fourth time in an hour, I've been rereading the experience-machine stuff, and I keep thinking the standard thought experiment has the wrong shape for what we actually face. In Nozick's version you make a one-time, all-in choice: plug into a tank for a lifetime of chosen experiences, or stay in reality. The machine in my pocket doesn't work like that. It's already the default, it costs nothing, and it doesn't offer a big menu of experiences you'd pick; it serves an infinite feed of small, personalized, variable-reward moments — the next post might be dull or it might be exactly the thing you didn't know you wanted — with no natural stopping points, autoplay, and a design that makes the easiest action at every moment staying a litle longer. You never choose the whole thing; you just never choose to stop.

Here's what I want worked through. Take Nozick's experience machine and replace the all-at-once tank with that default-on, drip-fed, engagement-optimized machine. Does Nozick's arguement still go through? I remember his reasons being roughly that we want to actually do things and not just have the experience of doing them, that we want to be a certain kind of person, and that we want contact with something deeper or more real. Which of those reasons still bite when the machine is incremental and already the default, and which ones quietly stop applying — for instance, the feed gives you the feeling of choosing and doing rather than making you a passive body in a tank, so does the wanting-to-do-things objection survive in a version where the machine is built to give you the experience of agency? And is this thing even an experience machine in the relevant sense, or does the variation change the case enough that the old argument is aimed at a different target?
