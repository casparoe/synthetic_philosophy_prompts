Palliative-care services in many countries offer continuous deep sedation to patients near the end of life whose pain, breathlessness, or agitation cannot be controlled by other means. Under this practice, a patient who would otherwise spend their last days or weeks in severe distress is kept unconscious, usually until death; assume here that the sedation neither hastens nor postpones death in any way that matters for the comparison. Clinicians, patients, and families typically regard the unconscious state as better for the patient than the waking state of uncontrolled suffering. Yet the same clinicians generally would not sedate a patient whose remaining days contain moderate or intermittent suffering mixed with periods of positive experience, even when a simple calculation over the whole remaining time would make the net hedonic quality slightly negative. The practice therefore seems to take a strong stand on when losing consciousness is an improvement, and an equally firm stand on when it is not.

Reconstruct the theory of value this pattern of practice presupposes about how the value of a state of a sentient person combines two dimensions: degree of consciousness, from none to full, understood to include the richness of experience; and net hedonic quality, meaning pleasantness minus suffering, where suffering counts negatively. In particular, assess the following three candidate combination rules.

An additive rule sets value = consciousness + net hedonic quality, once the two dimensions have been placed on a common scale. A multiplicative rule sets value = consciousness × net hedonic quality, so that zero consciousness forces value to zero no matter what the hedonic quality would otherwise be, and a state of full consciousness with negative hedonic quality has negative value. A lexical rule makes all differences in one dimension dominate any finite difference in the other, in either direction.

Say which of these rules, or which broader family of rules, the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to merely fitting. Does the decision to sedate severe suffering require the multiplicative rule, or is it also licensed by the additive rule? Does the reluctance to sedate moderate mixed states commit the practice to a lexical priority of consciousness, or is it better explained by independent considerations such as risk, consent, uncertainty, or the value of particular future goods? Are the two limbs of the practice coherent under a single value rule, or does their combination show that the practice is not really attempting to maximize a two-dimensional value function at all? Finally, state what would have to change in the practice if an institution explicitly adopted the additive rule, and separately if it adopted a lexical priority of consciousness.
