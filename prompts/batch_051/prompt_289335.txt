I'm making a decision-tree handout for a discussion group of people who know absolutely nothing about algorithmic information theory but keep having the same argument about wether simpler explanations are actually better. Build it from the logical space of possible commitments, not from a list of named views. Use three concrete cases as the splitting questions wherever they genuinely split the space: (1) a friend who always cancels plans—"he's flaky" versus a complicated story about his schedule, his girlfriend, and a secret project; (2) a coin that lands heads eight times in a row—fair coin lucky streak versus a biased coin; (3) two curve fits to the same data, a straight line versus a 20-parameter wiggle that fits a bit better. Where named positions actually exist and are held—like simplicity as overfitting control, simplicity as a language-dependent prior, or simplicity only as a tie-breaker—put them at leaves; define Solomonoff and Kolmogorov in one clause at the leaf where they genuinely matter, and don't assume anyone knows the names. Flag any combination of answers that has no plausible occupant and say in one sentence whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and note any positions the tree can't separate. Commit to one definite tree instead of laying out options, and wherever a split is close, say in one sentence why you cut it where you did. Then give the tree as a JSON object with: 'questions' (array of {id, text, option_left, option_right}), 'nodes' (array of {id, question_id, left_child_id, right_child_id}), 'leaves' (array of {id, position_name, one_sentence_commitment, occupancy, diagnosis_if_unoccupied}), 'hardest_calls' (array of {question_id, decision, reason}), and a 'separability_note' field listing positions that end up on the same leaf and why the tree can't split them.
