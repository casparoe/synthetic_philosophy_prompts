I keep seeing arguments about whether AI systems should reason like utilitarians or deontologists, and it reminds me of a distinction I deal with constantly at work, so I'm trying to figure out whether the resemblance is real or just two fields using similar words.

In my day job, pretty much every system has an objective you're minimizing or maximizing—latency, cost, throughput, whatever—and then a set of constraints around it. Some of those constraints are hard: the planner/solver/controller is never allowed to violate them, and if it does, that's a bug or a safety incident. Some are soft: we stuff them into the objective as a penalty with a big coefficient and then twidle the coefficient until the behavior looks right. I've worked with engineers who would never accept a hard memory-safety violation in code, but who will happily treat a "do not overload this database" rule as a -1000000 penalty term, and then wonder why it still gets violated when the other side of the objective is big enough.

The way I've seen utilitarianism described, it's the unconstrained version: every action gets a total score from a single function over consequences, and you pick the best-scoring action. Deontology, especially the version where some acts are just forbidden, looks like the same setup but with hard constraints: don't kill, don't lie, don't break a promise, and no amount of good consequences is allowed to override them. Under that mapping, an AI being deontological could mean at least three different things: (a) shove the rules into the reward function as huge penalties, (b) give the optimizer hard constraints it isn't allowed to violate, or (c) restrict the action space so the forbidden actions are never generated in the first place. Option (a) looks to me like it collapses back into utilitarianism with extra terms in the objective. But then I get stuck, because someone had to pick the hard constraints in (b) or the restricted action space in (c), and the way we pick safety constraints is usually by asking which rules, if we enforced them, would let the optimizer loose while keeping expected harm below some level. That's just utilitarianism one level up, deciding rules by their consequences.

So my question: is this a genuine structural identity, or am I doing the intellectual equivalent of variable-name collision? If the mapping is real, does the engineering distinction between hard constraints and big penalties transfer over to debates between deontologists and utilitarians—for example, is someone who says "never lie, except when the consequences would be catastrophic" actually just running utilitarianism with a large-but-finite penalty on lying? If the mapping breaks, I want to know exactly where, and my vague suspicion is that a constraint in an optimizer is something I impose from outside for safety, while a deontological rule is supposed to be part of what makes the act wrong in the first place, not a guardrail around a separate objective. But I can't make that precise, and maybe it's not even a real difference.
