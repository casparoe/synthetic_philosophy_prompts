I keep hearing a particular argument in conversations about whether God exists, and I want to know how much weight it can carry. The argument works by comparing religious experience to the senses. When I see a mug on the table, I take myself to have good reason to believe a mug is there, even though I cannot prove I am not dreaming or hallucinating; I would need some special reason to distrust my eyes. People then extend this to God: if someone has a direct, overwhelming awareness of God's presence — as vivid and attention-commanding, they say, as seeing another person in the room — then that person has at least some reason to believe God exists. To dismiss the experience as mere emotion would, on this view, be the same kind of inconsistency as trusting one's senses everywhere except when the content is unwelcome. Some versions go further and say that since we treat sense experience as a basic source of evidence, consistency demands that we treat religious experience as a basic source of evidence too. Some people put the point only about the person who had the experience; others extend it to the rest of us, saying we should credit reports of religious experience in the way we credit reports of ordinary observation.

I am not asking whether God exists. I want to know how far the analogy between sensory experience and religious experience actually holds. Please address both uses: the experience as evidence for the person who had it, and the report of the experience as evidence for everyone else. If the analogy fails as an argument but still has a useful core, tell me what a better comparison would be.
