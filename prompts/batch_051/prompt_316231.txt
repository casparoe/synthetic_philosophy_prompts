P. F. Strawson’s essay *Freedom and Resentment* contains a passage in which he contrasts two ways of looking at another person. In the ordinary, participant way, we hold people to moral expectations and feel resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, anger, and similar attitudes toward them. In the objective way, we suspend those attitudes and see the person as something to be handled or managed — as an object of social policy rather than as a participant in a shared moral relationship. Strawson says the objective stance cannot include the reactive feelings that belong to interpersonal involvement; it sets aside, among other things, resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, and the kind of love two adults can feel for each other.

Now consider an actual institutional practice. Criminal courts normally treat defendants as responsible agents whom it is appropriate to blame and punish. But when a defendant is found to have been severely mentally disordered at the time of the act, the system shifts: the person is excused from criminal responsibility, yet often not released. Instead the person may be involuntarily committed to a psychiatric facility, medicated, or placed under supervision — treated, that is, less as a participant in the moral community and more as a person who must be managed and contained.

What philosophical commitments about moral responsibility, blame, and the boundaries of the moral community does this two-track practice presuppose? In particular: does it presuppose that the capacity for the reactive attitudes is what makes someone answerable, and that when that capacity is absent or impaired the appropriate stance is an objective one? Or can the practice be defended while holding that the offender is still a full participant in the moral community, just excused on a narrower basis? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, explain what would have to change in our criminal and mental-health institutions if we accepted Strawson’s underlying picture, or if we rejected it in favor of a purely objective policy of social protection.
