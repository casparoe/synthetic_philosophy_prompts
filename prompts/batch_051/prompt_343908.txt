I've recently been helping a fact-checking project draft a scoring guide for short general-knowledge claims, and the category that keeps breaking our rules is generic statements. We know how to handle explicit quantifiers—"all," "most," "some"—by checking proportions among the relevant individuals, but a sentence like "ducks lay eggs" resists that treatment. It is clearly true, yet read as "all ducks lay eggs" it is false, since only mature females lay eggs and only during part of the year. The companion case is stranger: "mosquitoes carry malaria" is a true sentence, although only the females of certain mosquito species can transmit the parasite and only a tiny fraction of individual mosquitoes ever do. The usual frequency moves do not help, because "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true and "mosquitoes do not carry malaria" is false, even though the first sentence describes a property possessed by almost no individual mosquitoes and the second describes a property possessed by almost all of them.

This matters because our guide keeps oscillating between two paraphrases. If we score a generic as "at least one member of the kind," we correctly keep "mosquitoes carry malaria" true but wrongly keep "birds are flightless" true; if we score it as "a majority of members of the kind," we correctly reject "birds are flightless" but wrongly reject "mosquitoes carry malaria." I had been assuming that the truth of a generic must supervene on the individual-level facts—that some statistical fact about the members of the kind is what fixes whether the generic is true. But I no longer see which statistical fact could do the work. What is the correct frequency rule, or the correct threshold among members of the kind, that our scoring guide should encode for generic statements?
