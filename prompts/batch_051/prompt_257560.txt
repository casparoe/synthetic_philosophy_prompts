I am a graduate student working through Bayesian epistemology, and I keep meeting a kind of belief change for which I have no proper heading. Standard conditionalization handles the case where I become certain of an evidence proposition: lightning flashes, and now I am certain that the room was momentarily illuminated, so I replace P(H) with P(H | E). But much of my learning is not like that. A clinician reads a murky X-ray and moves her probability of a fracture from .15 to .65 without becoming certain of any clean proposition—the image itself is noisy, and any exact description of the smudge would be at least as uncertain as the diagnosis. I hear a muffled sound at the window and move from .1 to .55 that someone is there, though I could not formulate any precise description of the sound that I would treat as certain. The probabilities of a target proposition seem to reorganize directly in response to messy perceptual input, with no proposition attaining probability one along the way.

What I cannot settle is what the update rule for such cases actually is, or whether it is rationally mandatory. One suggestion I have met is that you take a mutually exclusive and exhaustive set of alternatives for the target proposition, revise the probabilities of those alternatives to their new values, and then hold fixed all conditional probabilities given each alternative. But I do not see why rationality requires holding those conditional probabilities fixed: could not the same murky experience also teach me something about what a fracture, if present, would imply for the patient's likely range of motion? And does the result depend on which partition I choose, or on the order in which several such partial shifts occur? Is this best understood as a species of conditionalization after all, a rival to it, or a limiting case from which ordinary conditionalization can be recovered when an evidence proposition attains probability one?

Does this form of updating have an established name, and if so, what exactly is the rule alleged to be, what assumptions does it rest on, which of those assumptions are considered controversial, and how does it relate to standard conditionalization? I care less about the label than about the carving: which features are essential to the phenomenon, which variants of the rule are genuinely different, and at what point the thing stops being well-defined.
