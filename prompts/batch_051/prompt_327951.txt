Act as a professional philosopher of logic and language. I have been going back and forth with a colleague about truth-value gap solutions to the Liar, and I want to know whether a familiar objection to those solutions is as decisive as it sounds. I can state the objection myself, but I cannot tell whether it genuinely destroys the gap approach or whether it quietly smuggles in a piece of classical reasoning that the gap theorist is already entitled to reject.

Let L be the simple Liar sentence, "L is false." A truth-value gap theorist says that L is neither true nor false: it falls into a gap. Now consider the strengthened Liar S, namely "S is not true." The objector argues as follows:

(1) Suppose S is true. Then things are the way S says they are, so S is not true. Thus, on the supposition that S is true, S is both true and not true. Since no one here wants to accept that a sentence can be both, the supposition fails; therefore S is not true.
(2) But "S is not true" is exactly what S says. Since things are the way S says they are, S is true.
(3) Therefore S is both not true and true.
(4) This cannot be defused by assigning S the same gap-status the theory assigns to L. The point of (1) was to show that S has the very status S ascribes to itself—not being true—and (2) then converts that fact back into truth. Any further status the theorist introduces, such as "gappy" or "indeterminate," just licenses a new sentence that says of itself that it is not in the privileged status, and the same two-step reasoning returns.
(5) So the gap solution is unstable. The only stable options are to ban self-referential truth talk altogether or to accept the contradiction as true, as the dialetheist does.

Is this argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the correct version of that step would be. If you think the argument is valid through (3) but that the real weakness lies in (4) or (5), then give the strongest coherent reply available to the gap theorist: what must they say about the predicate "true," about the biconditional in the T-schema, or about the difference between the object language and the metalanguage in order to avoid the conclusion? I suspect the objection may confuse "it is not the case that S is true" with "S is not true," or may treat the metalanguage verdict as if it were an object-language fact, but I cannot make that suspicion rigorous. Please reason it through carefully; begin by writing down the logical form of S under the unrestricted T-schema, and then check whether the gap theorist's best reply merely postpones the same problem to a higher level.
