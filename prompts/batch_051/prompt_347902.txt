It struck me that Augustine's problem about measuring time in Confessions XI has the same shape as a foundational puzzle in calculus. Augustine treats the present as an unextended instant, since any extended now would divide into past and future; but then no duration can be assembled from durationless instants, and he ends up locating the measure of time in the mind's distension among memory, attention, and expectation. Calculus faces the mirror image: an instant has zero length, yet instantaneous velocity is defined not by summing zero-length moments but as a limit of average rates over intervals shrinking around the point. Is this a genuine identity of structure or only a superficial analogy? Could Augustine have accepted the limit construction as a resolution of his measuring problem, or does it quietly presuppose the objective continuous timeline he found puzzling? What would the parallel predict—for instance, about experienced time behaving like a differentiable quantity—that turns out false?
