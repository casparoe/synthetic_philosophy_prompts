Me and my friend just spent an hour argueing about our acquaintance Priya, who got asked point-blank 'did you finish the proposal?' and answered 'I sent the draft to Sam this morning.' Strictly true, and also a pretty transparent way of leaving the asker with the belief that the proposal was done when it wasn't. My friend's line is that Priya didn't lie, so calling her dishonest would be wrong. My line is that the only thing she's clean on is the literal text, and that shouldn't matter morally, since the literal wording is irrelevent to what actually got communicated. The weird part is that the same friend does not accept the mirror case: if someone says 'all the students passed' while knowing only some passed, she calls it a lie and plenty bad, but if someone says 'some of the students passed' while knowing all passed and lets the hearer draw the false conclusion that not all did, she calls it 'just being careful with words.' So she's running a standing asymmetry: saying something false gets condemned much harder than deliberately implicating something false, even when the speaker's intent and the hearer's uptake are identical.

Then give me the strongest Gricean defense of the asymmetry you can build (assertion being a stronger commitment, implicatures being cancellable, that sort of thing), test it against those two cases and at least one harder case of your own devising, and tell me honestly whether ordinary practice is tracking a real distinction or just a superstition about the literal words.
