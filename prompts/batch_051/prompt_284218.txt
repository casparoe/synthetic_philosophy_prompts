I'm a PhD student in political theory, and in a seminar on non-democratic rule we discussed China and Singapore. Two of my classmates ended up in a long argument about performance legitimacy, and I want to report the exchange faithfully because it seemed substantive and I can't cleanly call it. Their disagreement, tidied up, went roughly as follows:

(1) A said: a state that reliably delivers physical security, basic order, and rising material well-being to its subjects satisfies the central condition that justifies any state in the first place. So competence can generate genuine legitimacy even without multi-party elections; a system along Singaporean lines should count as legitimate so long as its record holds.

(2) B replied: that conflates good outcomes with a right to rule. Legitimacy attaches to directives, not to services. A regime that feeds you and keeps the streets safe may give you reasons to be glad it exists, but it has not thereby shown that its commands have authority over you. Authority needs a procedural source, such as accountability or consent, because commands are claims made over persons, not merely benefits delivered to them.

(3) A came back: that procedural requirement is question-begging against cases like these. If the point of procedures is to protect people from bad outcomes, then insisting on procedures when the outcomes are already secured by other means is fetishizing the mechanism. Moreover, obligations can be content-dependent: if a directive is generally beneficial and not unjust, that fact is itself a reason to treat the directive as binding, independently of who issued it.

(4) B answered: the outcomes can't do the work by themselves. To call an outcome "good" you need a standard, and a non-democratic regime is largely the one that defines and measures its own success — growth, stability, order, satisfaction. Without some authorization independent of the regime, applying the standard is circular. And performance is hostage to reversal: a legitimation that evaporates the moment growth stalls is only provisional acquiescence, not legitimacy.

(5) A replied: every basis of legitimacy is conditional, including democratic ones, which also lapse under sustained failure. The deeper issue is whether substantive performance can suffice without procedure. If a regime meets a content-based test over decades, the burden shifts to the proceduralist to name what value is missing. The only candidate is that being able to authorize one's own rulers is intrinsically, not merely instrumentally, valuable — and that is a much stronger and more contested claim than B has defended.

(6) B said: that "candidate" is not an afterthought; it is the entire issue. What separates justified authority from beneficent domination is that the governed can regard the arrangements as theirs in some sense. Prosperity and order make you well-governed; they do not make rulers answerable, and answerability is exactly what is at stake.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say who is right about what overall, what both parties are missing, and whether they are ultimately engaging the same question or partly talking past each other about the definition of legitimacy itself.
