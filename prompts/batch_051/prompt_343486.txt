You are to draft the joint statement that would be produced by an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over how major moral reversals happen. The first camp — call it the honor and social-meaning account — holds that norms such as the aristocratic duel, Chinese footbinding, and chattel slavery ended primarily because the practices underwent a change in social meaning: what had been honorable, refined, or respectable came to be seen as absurd, degrading, or beneath a person of status, and this change in the economy of honor and shame did the main causal work. The second camp — the reasons account — holds that these reversals were driven primarily by the force of moral arguments and reasons that were in the relevant sense available long before the reversal, so that shifts in honor and status are best understood as downstream symptoms of a change in what people found genuinely justifying rather than as the engine of change. Both camps accept that a bare material-interest story is insufficient; their disagreement is over which non-material mechanism does the primary explanatory work.

Draft the document itself: a short preamble, a set of numbered agreed claims that are non-vacuous and about the phenomenon rather than about the methodology, a section stating each camp's positive view in wording the other camp would accept as accurate, a disagreements table that separates genuine residual differences from points that turn out to be verbal, and a crux section. For each crux — a sub-question such that resolving it would move at least one camp — state what evidence or argument both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts.

Use the decline of the aristocratic duel, the abandonment of footbinding, and the abolition of chattel slavery as your shared test cases, but treat them as evidence rather than decoration: say where the two accounts read the same sequence differently. In particular, attend to the sequencing problem (did argument uptake precede or follow the collapse of status?), the problem of salience (a reason must become salient to a group before it can move them, and the camps may locate the cause of salience differently), and the problem of acceleration (why reversals that appeared impossible for generations became fast and near-total once underway). Pay close attention to whether your final formulation of the honor account has secretly absorbed the reasons account, or vice versa, and say explicitly what each camp would count as evidence against its own view.
