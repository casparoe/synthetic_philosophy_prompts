So at work we've started doing this thing where five of us have to guess which of two suppliers will be better, we go around in a fixed order, and everyone hears the earlier guesses. I'm fourth, and I have pretty good private info, but the two people who go first are basically going off gossip. It hit me that every account of rational herding I've seen assumes everyone's private signal is equally reliable, so I want to know what actually changes when that's broken, because this is literally my tuesday now.

Set up the standard cascade first. Two urns, call them R and B, are chosen by a fair coin. A private draw from the chosen urn tells you the truth with probability 2/3 (the usual mostly-red/mostly-blue urn). Players go in sequence; each sees only her own draw, then publicly announces her guess of which urn was chosen, having observed all earlier guesses. Everyone is a Bayesian, no one cares about reputation or looking consistent, and everyone knows all this. The familiar result is that once two consecutive guesses match, the third person rationally follows them even with a contrary private draw, and then everyone after piles on, sometimes on the wrong urn.

Now the variation I want you to actually compute rather than hand-wave. Suppose the first two players get a weaker private hint: it names the true urn with probability only 0.6 (and is wrong with probability 0.4). Everyone from player 3 onward gets a sharp private hint that names the true urn with probability 0.9. These probabilities are common knowledge. Players still announce in order and hear every earlier guess. Ties are broken by following one's own hint.

Work through the Bayesian updates for players 1, 2, and 3 in this 0.6/0.9 version. Does the standard result still hold after two matching early guesses, or can the third person rationally break the crowd when her hint points the other way? Then find the exact cutoff: if players 1 and 2 each have accuracy a (and players 3 onward have accuracy 0.9), for which values of a does the third player, after seeing two matching early guesses, still go with those guesses even when her own sharp hint says the opposite? I keep wanting it to be around three-quarters, but I might be mishandling the tie case.

Finally, for a = 0.6, give the probability that by the end of the third player's turn a cascade is underway toward the wrong urn (meaning all later players will rationaly choose the wrong urn whatever private hints they see), and compare that with the uniform 2/3-accuracy baseline. Then say in a sentence or two whether the usual early-match-and-the-herd-takes-off story survives this change and where it gets misleading.
