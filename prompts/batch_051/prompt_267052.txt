A trial judge with a long mixed civil and criminal docket has produced a series of rulings on what she calls naked statistical evidence: evidence that places the defendant inside a class very likely to have contained the person who did the act, without any evidence tying this particular defendant to the act beyond class membership. Her prior rulings include the following.

In the original blue-bus civil case, a cyclist was struck by a bus and no witness could say which company operated it. It was stipulated that 80 percent of the buses on the route belonged to the Blue Bus Company and 20 percent to the Red Bus Company. The judge entered judgment in favor of the Blue Bus Company, ruling that a market-share statistic cannot by itself support a civil verdict, even when it makes the defendant more likely than not to be the responsible party.

In the reverse-gatecrasher civil case, one thousand people attended a lecture at which only one ticket had been sold. The organizer's records proved that exactly one attendee had paid and the other nine hundred ninety-nine had entered through an unmonitored door without paying; the organizer could not identify the paying attendee and sued one attendee chosen at random for the price of admission. The judge entered judgment in favor of the attendee, ruling that a 99.9 percent probability generated entirely by the ratio of payers to non-payers is a probability about the class, not proof against this defendant.

In the prison-yard criminal case, one hundred prisoners were in an exercise yard when ninety-nine took part in a fatal assault on a guard and exactly one stood apart and took no part. The footage made that ratio certain but could not distinguish the one from the ninety-nine. The prosecution charged one prisoner on the resulting 99 percent chance alone. The judge dismissed the charge, holding that proof beyond a reasonable doubt requires evidence that picks out the particular accused rather than a near-certain statistical likelihood centered on the group.

In the DNA cold-hit criminal case, a stranger rape left semen at the scene, and years later a database search produced a match to a defendant with no other connection to the crime; the laboratory's random-match probability was one in ten million. The judge ruled that the match could by itself sustain a conviction, reasoning that a DNA profile match is a case-specific comparison of trace evidence with a sample from the defendant, not a naked statistic, even though its evidential weight is expressed as a probability.

A new civil case now comes before her. A pedestrian is struck at dusk on the same route as the original blue-bus case. A single witness saw the bus for a split second and says it was blue. The stipulated facts about the witness are these: when the bus is blue, the witness in those conditions would say it is blue 60 percent of the time and red 40 percent of the time; when the bus is red, the witness would say it is blue 40 percent of the time and red 60 percent of the time. The route is unchanged: 80 percent of its buses belong to the Blue Bus Company. No other evidence distinguishes the companies. If these figures are combined in the ordinary probabilistic way, the probability that the responsible bus belonged to the Blue Bus Company is roughly 86 percent. The plaintiff argues that the split-second identification supplies the case-specific element missing from the original blue-bus case, and that 86 percent is comfortably above the civil standard. The company replies that a glimpse with a 40 percent error rate is too flimsy to count as an individualizing link, and that the 86 percent figure is just the forbidden market-share statistic wearing an eyewitness disguise.
