I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist is a committed value hedonist: he holds that pleasure is the only thing good in itself and pain the only intrinsic evil, and he has arranged his life around that thesis. Across the novel he is made to defend it against grief, illness, demanding aesthetic experience, and his own capacity for cruelty, and I don't want him to be a straw man or a mouthpiece for a vague slogan. Before I write the scenes in which he states and defends the view, I need the specification of what a theory of pleasure has to deliver if it is to carry the weight his hedonism puts on it.

Please write the requirements list. The things we call pleasures are strikingly heterogeneous — a warm bath, the relief of a toothache, malicious delight, absorption in a proof, the satisfaction of a long-deferred desire — and it is not obvious what, if anything, they share; a minimally adequate theory must say what unifies them without pretending the differences don't exist. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are insisted on only by particular camps. Then identify the single requirement that most embarrasses, or outright eliminates, the familiar identifications of pleasure with a distinctive felt quality, with preference or desire satisfaction, or with an attitude of liking. I care more about the criteria and their motivating cases than about a thorough scorecard for the existing views.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the list as a whole and name the one requirement you are least sure of.
