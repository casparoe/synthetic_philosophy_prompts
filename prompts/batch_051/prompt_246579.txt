I am preparing a short talk for an interdisciplinary reading group on why voting systems so often seem gameable, and a friend who took a game theory course some years ago sent me his recollection of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem to use as a framing device. He tends to compress technical results into sweeping takeaways, which is why I want it checked carefully. He summarized it as follows:

"Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved that when there are three or more candidates, every non-dictatorial voting rule can be manipulated: there will be some election in which at least one voter gets a better result by submitting a ranking that misrepresents their true preferences. So no ordinary election with more than two candidates can be safe from strategic voting — in practice someone will usually have an incentive to lie. The only way to make a voting rule completely immune to strategic voting is to make it dictatorial, so that one voter's top choice always wins. That is why every real-world election with three or more candidates is riddled with strategic voting, and why no voting system can be both fair and honest."

I have read enough to know that the theorem is an impossibility result, but not enough to tell where this gloss crosses from simplification into distortion, and I do not want to build slides around a claim I could not defend in discussion. Could you give me an accuracy audit of that summary? Go sentence by sentence and distinguish what a social choice theorist would accept, what they would want to qualify, and what is simply false. Pay particular attention to whether the theorem's assumptions are stated correctly, and to whether the final sentence about real elections follows from anything in the theorem. Where the summary is subtly wrong, say how the claim would need to be restated to become true.
