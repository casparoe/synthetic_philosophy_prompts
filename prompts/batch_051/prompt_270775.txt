I've been trying to write a short paper for a practical ethics seminar on when it's permissible to impose risks on other people without their consent, and I've reached the point where I can't tell wether I have one coherent position or five different committments stapled together. Before I present it, I'd like an audit of the following set. I'm not attached to any particular wording; I just want to know where the load-bearing seams are.

(1) Risking serious harm to someone without their consent is itself a pro tanto wrong, even if the harm never materializes. If you drive drunk and get home without incident, you have still wronged the people on the road; the wrong is in the exposure, not in the outcome.

(2) Yet driving a normal distance to buy groceries is plainly permissible, even though it imposes a small but real risk of death on pedestrians and other drivers, none of whom have consented in any meaningful sense.

(3) When we assess a risky action or policy, the question that matters is ex ante: what harms and benefits could reasonably have been expected at the time of decision, not what actually happened. A public-health measure that unexpectedly kills twenty people was still the right call if the probabilities favored it.

(4) Genuine, informed consent can make enormous self-regarding risks permissible: a competent adult who freely chooses a dangerous job, BASE jumping, or a paid early-phase drug trial may take those risks, and the state should mostly leave him to it.

(5) But I'm also convinced that a lot of what gets called consent to risk is a fiction. The pedestrian walking along a road hasn't consented to the traffic; she simply walks anyway because the alternative is absurd. Likewise a worker who takes a hazardous job because the only other option is no income hasn't consented in any way that should do serious moral work.

So here's what I need. Which of these commitments are actually in tension, and which only seem to be once the right distinctions are drawn? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give to restore consistency and what the resulting view would lose. Along the way, be explicit about what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested, so a contested position doesn't come out looking like the consensus.
