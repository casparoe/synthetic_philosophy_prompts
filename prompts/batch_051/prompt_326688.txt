Question (10 points): In the United States, courts have for decades permitted race-conscious university admissions to pursue student-body diversity while forbidding quotas. India's constitution instead reserves a fixed share of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes in legislatures, government posts, and public higher education. Norway's boardroom quota law requires covered companies to give each gender at least 40 percent of board seats, with sanctions for noncompliance. Compare what these three arrangements are each trying to justify and remedy. Then give at least one reason they should not be lumped together as a single kind of policy.

Student's answer: Although they differ in mechanism, all three are ultimately applications of the same diversity rationale: each uses group membership to improve decision-making by bringing in underrepresented perspectives. The United States is the clearest case, since the Supreme Court has treated diversity as a compelling interest while ruling out quotas, so American universities rely on holistic review. India's reservations implement the same principle — they ensure that historically excluded castes are present in legislatures and universities, which enriches deliberation — except that India sets explicit numerical targets rather than treating caste only as a plus factor. Norway's board quotas are the most direct version: a statutory quota backed by sanctions, justified by the fact that mixed boards make better decisions. The three policies differ in mechanics — holistic review, reserved percentages, and a hard quota — but their underlying justificatory logic is the same.
