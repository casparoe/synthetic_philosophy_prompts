Friend and I spent basically the whole evening arguing about whether presidential systems are more accountable than parliamentary ones, and somewhere around midnight I got convinced the whole debate is missing a distinction. I've been calling it sanction latency versus sanction fidelity. Sanction latency is how long it takes, on average, between a government doing something blameworthy and voters being able to punish it through normal electoral means. Presidential systems have high latency because terms are fixed and removal is genuinely hard; parliamentary systems have low latency because no-confidence votes and snap elections can force a reckoning almost immediately. But heres the part my friend wouldn't grant: parliamentary systems have low sanction fidelity, because by the time the punishment lands its been smeared across whatever coalition fight triggered the election, not the original bad decision. Presidential systems punish a single identifiable actor so the fidelity is high, but the signal arrives so late its basically stale. So I think accountability isnt one axis running from more to less, its two axes, and maybe no system can score well on both. Is this distinction actually doing work, or am I just reinventing something obvious in comparative politics? And where does it break—are there systems that get both high, or both low, and would that count against me?
