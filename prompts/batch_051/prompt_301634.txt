I'm an aesthetics PhD student, and I keep seeing the same move in Q&As. Last week someone gave a talk on aesthetic testimony—roughly, whether you can rationally believe that a movie is good on the say-so of a reliable critic, without watching it yourself—and a commentator shut the whole discussion down with 'de gustibus non est disputandum, there's no accounting for taste,' and the room nodded. I want to figure out what that line could even mean before I respond to it in my paper. Lay out a menu of the distinct precise claims 'there's no accounting for taste' might be making, from the boring to the radical: e.g. that taste disputes can't be settled by argument, that everyone's aesthetic judgements are equally correct, that aesthetic verdicts aren't beliefs at all, that you can't acquire aesthetic knowledge from another person. For each reading, say whether it's banal, defensible, or false, and then—this is the part I actually need—say which of these readings, if any, would actually license using the slogan to dismiss a question about aesthetic testimony. If even the strongest defensible reading doesn't get the commentator her conclusion, say that too. End with \answer{...} giving the label of the reading that comes closest to licensing the move, or \answer{none}.
