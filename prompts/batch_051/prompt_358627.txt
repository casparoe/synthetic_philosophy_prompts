In our upper-level ethics seminar on integrity in public life, we spent a session trying to separate being strictly unbribable, being unmoved by incentives of any kind, being free of conflicts of interest, and retaining one's integrity under sustained pressure. The instructor ended the discussion by shrugging and saying, "Everyone has their price," which she took to show that no one is genuinely incorruptible, and that the very ideal is incoherent or at best self-flattering. I suspect that one sentence is doing much more work than it can bear, but I need to get clearer about what it could actually be claiming before I can push back. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "everyone has their price" might be making — about trade-offs, about the power of sufficiently large incentives, about the limits of steadfastness, about how people describe themselves, or anything else — and give, for each, a verdict on whether it is near-trivial, substantive but plausible, or substantially false? Then identify which reading or readings would actually be needed to support the instructor's conclusion that no one is genuinely incorruptible, and say whether any reading leaves room for a meaningful notion of incorruptibility — and, if so, what that notion amounts to and whether it would always be an admirable quality. I would like the readings laid out as a menu with verdicts, not as a single defended interpretation.
