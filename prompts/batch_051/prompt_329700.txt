I am a theoretical physicist, comfortable with the standard apparatus of classical and statistical mechanics and with field-theoretic ideas such as Lagrangians, symmetries, propagators, and retarded versus advanced solutions. I have no formal training in philosophy, but I keep encountering a philosophical dispute about causation, and I want it translated into the vocabulary I actually work in — because I suspect that, stated properly, the dispute dissolves.

As I understand it, the dispute has three sides. One side treats causation as fundamental: causal powers or dispositions are basic ingredients of the world, and the laws themselves are, in some sense, descriptions of what things do in virtue of those powers. A second side treats causation as real but reducible: causal truths are made true by, and analyzable in terms of, non-causal facts — regularities, counterfactual dependence, probability-raising and screening off, or invariance under interventions. The third side, associated with Bertrand Russell, treats causation as eliminable: Russell held that the notion of cause is a leftover from an earlier, more anthropomorphic stage of thought and that mature physics gets by perfectly well with functional relations among variables, with no need for a distinct causal ingredient.

Restate each of those three positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I use day to day: dynamical laws plus initial or boundary conditions, symmetries and conservation laws, response functions and Green's functions or propagators, conditional independence and screening off in statistical ensembles, the thermodynamic arrow of time, and the difference between a perturbation I impose and a fluctuation that just happens. This is not a request for an analogy; I want a genuine translation, in which you say what each position amounts to when it is expressed as a thesis about physical theory.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which claims did you have to bend or fudge to fit into physical vocabulary? Which claims have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all — for example, an intervention understood as something over and above a boundary condition, an asymmetry between cause and effect when the microphysical laws are time-reversal symmetric, or the claim that causal structure is needed for explanation rather than prediction? For each piece of residue, tell me whether it is residue because physical theory is genuinely missing something it should want, or because the philosophical claim turns out to be less precise than it looked once it is forced into a clear register. I want to know whether my working assumption is right — that causal talk is a high-level convenience layered on top of the dynamical laws and that the eliminativist has effectively won — or whether the things that do not survive the translation are exactly where the real argument lives.
