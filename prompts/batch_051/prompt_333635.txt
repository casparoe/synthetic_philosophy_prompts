I'm a PhD student TAing an undergrad philosophy of religion course, and I've been going back and forth on how many points to give this one, so I'd like a second opinion.

Here's the question the students got:

> Distinguish the logical problem of evil from the evidential problem of evil. Then assess what Plantinga's free will defense and a Hick-style soul-making theodicy each accomplish, and say wether together they give a satisfying response to the problem.

And here's the student's answer:

> The logical problem of evil says that the existence of evil is logically incompatible with an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God. Mackie's version is that a good being would eliminate evil as far as it can, an omnipotent being can eliminate it entirely, and yet evil exists. The evidential problem is different: it grants that God and evil are logically compatible, but says that the amount and distribution of seemingly pointless evil makes God's existence unlikely. Rowe's fawn example, where a fawn suffers and dies alone in a forest fire, is meant to show that some suffering doesn't do any discernible good.
>
> Plantinga's free will defense answers the logical problem by describing a possible state of affairs in which God has a reason to permit moral evil. If God wants creatures with libertarian freedom, and if it's possible that every such creature would go wrong in at least one respect no matter which world God creates, then God might not be able to actualize a world with free creatures and no evil. A defense only needs to be logically possible; it doesn't need to be probable or to be God's actual reason. So the free will defense breaks the logical problem.
>
> Once the logical problem is handled, the evidential problem is really just a weaker version of the same worry: it points to particular evils and says they look pointless. But for any particular evil, we can always imagine some greater good for which it was necessary, and since that good is possible, the existence of apparently pointless evil isn't actually evidence against theism. That's where Hick's soul-making theodicy comes in. Hick says the world is a place for spiritual growth, where suffering is necessary for the development of virtues like courage and compassion. And because Hick's account, like Plantinga's, is offered as a defense, it doesn't have to be plausible or likely to be God's actual reason; it just needs to be possible. Between them, Plantinga and Hick answer both the logical and the evidential problem.
