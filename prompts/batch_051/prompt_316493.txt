I am a retired physicist, and after four decades of working on stochastic dynamical systems — Langevin equations, free-energy landscapes, dissipation, feedback control — I have recently begun reading philosophy of biology and some of the machine-learning literature on optimization. A debate keeps crossing my desk that I only half-recognize: whether natural selection is profitably described as an optimizer, and whether gradient descent and related machine-learning methods have anything to learn from the way evolution searches. I have seen it put uncharitably as the claim that evolution is a stupid optimizer, and I have seen opposite morals drawn from it — that evolution's success vindicates simple local greedy search, or that evolution's pathologies should cure us of storybook optimization.

I would like you to do something more disciplined than explain the debate to me. I want you to restate it in my native vocabulary — the mathematics I actually trust: stochastic dynamical systems, control theory, and non-equilibrium statistical physics — and then tell me what did not survive the translation.

Please proceed in three steps. First, reconstruct the two or three main positions in the debate as you understand them, in plain terms, without tying them to particular authors and without citations; I want the logical geography, not the scholarship. At minimum address the view that natural selection is an optimizing process whose products can be read as good designs; the view that selection is myopic, path-dependent, satisficing, and hedged round by drift and constraint; and the view or views about whether gradient descent can borrow anything from natural selection. If my map of the positions is wrong, correct it.

Second, translate each position systematically into my vocabulary: state variables, fitness as a stationary or time-dependent potential, gradient flow plus noise, Lyapunov functions, local versus global minima, landscape ruggedness, annealed versus quenched disorder (whether the randomness is averaged away or frozen in), dissipation, feedback and control, exploration and exploitation. Be precise enough that I could write down equations if I chose to, but keep formalism in the background.

Third, and most importantly, give me the residue report. For each position, separate the claims into three groups: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then offer an honest verdict: is the residue a limitation of the target vocabulary I am forcing you to use, or is it a sign that some element of the original debate — a teleological idiom, a normative claim, a semantic dispute — was doing less work than it appeared to do?

For epistemic hygiene, please tell me at the end how confident you are in the translation and in the residue report, and single out the one point you are least sure about. Do not pad your confidence and do not hedge beyond what the arguments support.

Act as a professional philosopher of science who is also comfortable with the relevant mathematics. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming passage in which you lay out the main conceptual joints of the debate and flag the risks of translation before committing to your reconstruction.
