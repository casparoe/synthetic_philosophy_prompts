I'm a journalist covering philosophy of mind for a general science magazine. I've been interviewing a philosopher—call her Dr. N—who takes a strongly deflationary line on qualia. She doesn't deny that people seem to have experiences; she denies that there is a coherent extra posit of intrinsic, ineffable, incorrigibly accessible qualitative properties behind the seemings. Her recorded verdicts on a series of cases are:

1. Chase: a coffee taster reports that his coffee tastes exactly as it always has but he no longer enjoys it. Dr. N says there is no further fact about whether an identical taste-quale really persisted; the report is a useful expression of a preference shift.
2. Sanborn: another taster reports that his enjoyment is unchanged but the taste itself has changed. Dr. N says there is no further fact about whether a taste-quale changed; the report tracks shifts in attention and expectation, not a private quality.
3. Inverted spectrum: two people pass every behavioral test involving color, yet each says their red and green experiences are swapped relative to the other. Dr. N says the imagined swap is not a hidden fact about qualia; it is a vivid but empty picture.
4. Mary: a color scientist raised without color vision sees red for the first time after knowing all functional facts about color. Dr. N says no new intrinsic property is accessed; Mary gains recognitional and imaginative abilities.
5. Blindsight: a subject can guess the location of a light under forced choice but denies seeing it. Dr. N says the case shows a difference in access and report, not an extra nonfunctional glow that conscious vision possesses.

I'm writing a closing section and want to anticipate how Dr. N would handle an untidy, less philosophical case. A perfumer has for thirty years described the scent of vetiver as having a cold, gray-green felt quality. After a minor respiratory infection, she passes every standard olfactory test, her blends are as accurate as before, and her preferences are unchanged; yet she reports without prompting that the felt quality of vetiver has become less cold and more silver, while she can identify no change in her behavior or reactions.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the two or three most plausible general rules fitting Dr. N's earlier verdicts; (2) what each rule predicts about the perfumer case; (3) your best-grounded prediction of Dr. N's verdict and why; (4) which earlier case is the closest analog and why; (5) one further test case that would most sharply split the competing rules. Please write in American English.
