I cover international institutions and regional economic law for a magazine that writes about science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on how treaty language fares when it must absorb a case its drafters never pictured. The case I am working with arises under the 1962 Treaty establishing the Meridian Customs Union, whose Article 9 reads: "Any State which accepts the purposes of this Treaty may apply for membership. The applicant shall be admitted upon the unanimous vote of the Member States."

The applicant is the Port Administration of Valcan, a coastal enclave that for more than a decade has run what is, in practical terms, a complete customs service: it levies its own duties on goods entering its port, issues certificates of origin, and polices its own customs frontier. Valcan is not recognized as a sovereign state by any member of the United Nations. It remains, in the constitutional law of the neighboring Republic of Moranne, a province of Moranne, although Moranne has exercised no effective governing authority there since an internationally brokered administration arrangement ended in 2017. Moranne is not a member of the Union and therefore has no vote. Valcan has now formally applied for membership under Article 9. One member state maintains that the application need not be considered at all, because "State" in Article 9 is a term of art meaning a recognized sovereign under international law. Another argues that the Treaty's object—a common external tariff, uniform rules of origin, and a predictable customs frontier—is best served by treating any functioning customs territory as eligible, and that the drafters would not have wanted access to the Union to turn on the vagaries of diplomatic recognition. Moranne has threatened to impose transit restrictions on any member that votes to admit Valcan.

Walk me through the case as the text of Article 9 decides it, as the purpose of the accession mechanism decides it, and as the drafters of a 1962 customs treaty would most plausibly have settled it if asked about an unrecognized but functioning customs territory. Then tell me which interpretive approach should govern here and why, and whether the reasonable-reader reading converges with any of them or calls for something different. I want a ruling on the interpretive question, not merely a list of considerations.
