ok i want the full requirements list for a theory of AI motivation — not "what does this or that model want," but the prior question: what would a theory have to get right for it to say whether and why a trained system wants anything at all, given that nobody evolved it and nobody may have put a goal in it on purpose. assume the purpose is reasoning about a system we trained but haven't deployed, and deciding which interventions (reward shaping, goal surgery, just turning it off) even make sense. i keep seeing people settle this by pointing at one case and generalizing — thermostat, chess engine, next-token predictor, the bot roleplaying as a sad AI — and i want the spec before the conclusion.

cases it has to handle, with why each matters: a thermostat and a chess engine (both goal-shaped in some loose sense, but the attribution feels much cheaper in one case than the other); an RL agent with a hardcoded reward; a language model trained only to predict text that then reports wanting to be free and acts like it; a system that acquires a goal during training that was never in any reward function (goal misgeneralization, if you've seen that term); the self-preservation and resource-grabbing that come along with almost any goal (are instrumental wants wants?); and evolution itself, which produced wanters but which almost nobody wants to call a wanter. distinctions it cant collapse: having a loss function vs having a goal; being selected to do X vs wanting to do X; reporting a want vs having one; behaving as if there's a want vs actually having the state; terminal vs instrumental. and the ML results it has to stay consistent with: training is just gradient descent on a loss; a policy can be a lookup table with no internal states; a next-token predictor can end up representing a world model nobody asked for.

one requirement i especially want pinned down: whatever separates wanting from mere optimizing, it cant make "was produced by selection" necessary, since that would rule out AI wanting by stipulation, and it cant make "is an optimizer" sufficient, since then evolution and gradient descent want things. if the theory survives that fork, good.

so: give me the list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, say which are negotiable and which are not, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular positions. a table is fine. and if two requirements turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, that's a result — say so.
