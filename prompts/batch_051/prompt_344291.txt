Two of my flatmates spent most of last night arguing about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I've been turning it over ever since; I'd like someone to adjudicate the actual exchange rather than just give a verdict.

Ruth's doing a module on free will and thinks the Basic Argument is basically right. Her line was: whatever you do flows from the way you are — your desires, temperament, values, dispositions — and you never chose any of that; it came from genes, parents, peers, accidents. So if your actions are just the working-out of a self you didn't choose, how can you ultimately deserve blame or praise for them? Marco pushed back that this only sounds forceful because it smuggles in the assumption that responsibility for an action requires responsibility for the self behind the action. In ordinary life, he said, we mean something much thinner: the action came from you, you weren't being coerced, you weren't delusional, and it expressed your own values. No one sane thinks you have to be the self-caused cause of yourself. Ruth came back: if you settle for that thin criterion, then a person whose values were beaten into them as a child still counts as fully blameworthy when they later act on those values, and that's exactly the sort of case where ordinary judgement itself wavers — we go easier on people with brutal upbringings, but never all the way, which suggests we're inconsistently applying a standard nobody can meet. Marco said that's just a hard case about degrees of formation, and anyway the regress premise is the weak link: why think being responsible for the way you are means choosing the way you are? A sculptor doesn't choose her block of stone, but the finished statue is still her work; a self can be built over time by its own choices even if the starting materials weren't chosen. Ruth replied that this is exactly the regress Strawson points at — if early choices build the later self, those early choices were made by an even earlier self, and so on until you hit brute facts about how you originally were; calling it "building" doesn't create desert, it just relocates the problem. Marco said the whole argument sets the bar impossibly high and then complains no one clears it: it only refutes some theological idea of being the cause of oneself, and in doing so it changes the subject, because ordinary responsibility was never about ultimate self-creation, it's about being responsive to reasons over a lifetime. Ruth answered that ordinary responsibility survives only because we don't inspect its foundations — resentment and indignation do presuppose that the person could have been otherwise in a robust sense, and once you press that you're led straight to the regress. Marco's last move was that Strawson himself admits giving up "true" responsibility has little practical upshot, since we still restrain people, still blame, still reproach ourselves — so either the argument has real practical consequences (which Strawson plays down) or the phrase "true moral responsibility" is doing no work and the dispute is verbal. Ruth said it does have consequences: you stop feeling entitled to your resentment, blame becomes more like handling a hazard than punishing a sinner, and pride and gratitude get hollowed out too. At that point they both looked at me, and I had no idea what to tell them.

Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I don't just want an overall winner — I want to know how much of the Basic Argument's force survives Marco's thin-responsibility reply, whether Ruth is right that thin responsibility secretly borrows from the ultimacy idea, and whether the "no practical consequences" charge matters for the argument's conclusion. And please write the whole answer in British English.
