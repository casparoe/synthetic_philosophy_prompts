In my graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology, we have been working through the familiar idea that priors wash out as evidence accumulates. The informal argument runs something like this. Two agents share a likelihood but hold different priors over a common space of hypotheses. After conditioning on a sufficiently long common sequence of observations, their posterior distributions should come to agree, because each new observation reweights the hypotheses by a likelihood factor, and repeated multiplication by likelihoods eventually overwhelms any finite initial advantage a hypothesis enjoyed from the prior. From this one is meant to get two further conclusions: that the posterior homes in on the true hypothesis, and that rational agents who honestly update on common evidence must converge in their beliefs, whatever their starting points.

Standard presentations of this argument assume something that is now hard to take for granted in actual scientific and statistical practice: that the model is correctly specified, that is, that the true data-generating process is among the hypotheses under consideration or at least in the support of the agents' priors. The development I want you to think through is the pervasive case of model misspecification. The truth lies outside the space of hypotheses anyone in the dispute is even considering, while the observations continue to arrive from a fixed distribution. Does the washing-out argument survive this changed assumption?

I would like a precise structural answer. Identify exactly which step or premise in the informal chain is under pressure once the model is misspecified. Trace what the two agents' posteriors actually do in this setting: do they still merge with one another, and do they converge on the truth or on something else — the best approximation available within the model class, for example, or different limits depending on their priors? Then tell me what, if anything, can be salvaged from the original conclusion, and state the weaker version as carefully as you can. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want the argument itself taken apart and rebuilt.
