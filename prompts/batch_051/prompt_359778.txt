In discussions of biopics and historical fiction about real people, one of the standard defenses of dramatic licence runs like this: a film about a real person is a portrait, not a photograph. A painter selects, flatters, interprets, and composes; no one accuses a portrait of lying because the sitter never looked exactly like that, and no one mistakes it for the record. So, the argument goes, we should stop treating such dramas as failed documentaries and instead judge them as interpretations, answerable to artistic or emotional truth rather than to factual accuracy; complaints from historians or from the subject's family that a scene was invented or a motivation distorted are therefore mostly beside the point.

I want a careful audit of how far this analogy holds and what it can actually carry. Give me the correspondence table first: what in the film plays the role of the sitter, the painter, the sitting, the likeness, the composition, the frame, and the public that views the result. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion being drawn from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, consider: a painted portrait is usually of someone who knows they are sitting for it, while the dead cannot consent, object, or correct; a portrait is displayed under conventions that flag it as one artist's representation, while a mass-market biopic is often treated by its audience as how it really happened; a real person in a drama is not merely depicted but made to say and do specific things that are then attributed to them; and the dead person's name, image, and story are doing commercial and reputational work for living people. Also identify what the portrait analogy quietly imports from its source domain that the target lacks, and whether those imports are doing the real argumentative work.

Then answer directly: does the conclusion usually drawn from this analogy, that factual-accuracy objections to dramatizing the real dead are mostly misplaced, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If some better analogy captures what a responsible dramatizer of the real dead is doing, state it and say what it would license instead.
