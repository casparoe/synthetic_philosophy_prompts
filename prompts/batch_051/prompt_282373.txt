I have been trying to pin down a puzzle about how the law handles music, because it seems to commit itself to a view of what a song is without ever saying so. Consider the way United States copyright law splits music into two rights: a musical work — the underlying composition, its melody, harmony, and lyrics — and a sound recording, which is the particular fixation produced in the studio. A musician can record a cover of a published song without the composer's permission by paying a statutory royalty, and the new recording is treated as a performance of that same musical work even if the tempo, key, and arrangement are substantially changed. But that same musician cannot copy even a short sample of the original studio recording without first clearing a separate right belonging to whoever owns that recording. The law, in other words, treats the song as the kind of thing that remains identical across indefinitely many different performances, while also treating each captured performance as a distinct piece of property.

I would like to know what philosophical conception of a musical work this practice presupposes. Is it committed to thinking of works as abstract types that can have many instances, with no particular performance or recording identical to the work itself, or is the practice compatible with a nominalist view on which only the performances and recordings really exist? How much of this is strictly presupposed by the legal rules, and how much merely fits with them? The sharpest test seems to be the identity conditions: at what point a changed melody stops being the same musical work, and whether that boundary is a defensible account of sameness or just an economic line drawn for licensing purposes. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we instead held that the work just is its first recording, or that the work is nothing over and above the scattered performances of it.
