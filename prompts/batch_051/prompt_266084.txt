I recently got into philosophy after retiring from experimental physics, and a little reading group of retired lab people has me stuck on the final theory question. We keep going back and forth about whether, supposing there really is a complete and correct final theory, we could ever know we had it — as opposed to merely having a theory that has not been refuted yet. I tried to turn my skeptical worry into a numbered argument so I could see where it might break, and I'd like a careful check of the steps.

(1) Suppose T is a theory that fits every observation we have made so far, with no free parameters tuned after the fact just to save it.
(2) For any finite set of observations, it is always possible to build a rival T* that agrees with T on all of them but yields different predictions for some future experiment — the old trick where a predicate quietly changes its behavior after a certain date.
(3) So no finite body of data can single out T uniquely; T and T* are both consistent with everything we have seen up to now.
(4) To know that T is the final theory, we would have to rule out every such rival, because even one live rival would mean we cannot know that some future experiment won't overturn T.
(5) The only way to rule out an endless supply of these rivals is to perform every possible future experiment, which is impossible, so we could never know that T is final even if it happens to be true.

I can't tell whether the problem is step (2) or step (4), or whether the whole thing is actually sound and I just dislike the conclusion. Which step, if any, fails, and does the conclusion follow? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, one for each step, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: say for each step whether it holds as stated, and if the argument fails, identify the first step that gives way and say how it would need to be rephrased for the argument to become valid (or explain plainly why it cannot be repaired).
