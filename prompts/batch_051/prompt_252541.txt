im running a small applied-ethics discussion group for second-year undergrads and next week we're doing the "my single burger, short-haul flight, or vote doesnt make a difference" problem, the thing people usually call the inefficacy objection. I want a decision tree that sorts each student into the position they actually hold on individual obligations in large collective harms, not the position they would name if I asked them outright.

Build the tree around three running cases — the burger, the short-haul flight, and the vote in a big electorate — and reuse those in the branch questions so nobody needs background reading to answer. Start everyone from the shared starting point "I accept that my single act makes no perceptible difference to the outcome," and let the questions after that do the sorting.

Construct it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first work out the questions and which combinations of answers are possible, then place the actual positions as leaves in that space. Make every branch node a concrete yes/no or forced-choice question about one of the three cases — whether a threshold is known, whether most others are already doing the thing, whether the act is observed or expressive, whether the harm comes through participating in a practice rather than through some causal tipping point — and phrase it so a student can answer without knowing any labels. Keep it to about 8 leaves and no more than 5 levels.

The leaves have to include at least: the no-obligation individualist, the expected-value consequentialist, the threshold or sufficientarian view, the universalizability person, the virtue or integrity person, the complicity person, and the collective-obligation distributivist. If a real position cant be reached as a leaf under any path, name it as an off-tree position and say which additional question would have been needed to reach it.

Flag the pairs your tree cant really separate — i'd bet virtue and complicity usually tell people to do the same thing but for different reasons, and expected-value and threshold views may overlap whenever thresholds are uncertain — and note any branch combinations nobody plausibly occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Use these sections in this order: (1) The tree — the branching structure itself, ~350 words. (2) Why each split goes where it does — ~200 words. (3) The leaves — one sentence naming each position and its most awkward commitment, ~250 words. (4) Empty combinations and why they're empty — ~150 words. (5) Pairs the tree cant separate and how to tell them apart anyway — ~150 words.
