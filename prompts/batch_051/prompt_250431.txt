Next month I'm leading a discussion on Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" for a reading group at my church, and I recently went down a rabbit hole reading David Lewis on convention and how norms persist once they start. So I want to do something more specific than the usual "tradition versus morality" take. Here's the question I want to work through with the group: read the village's lottery as a convention in roughly Lewis's sense — a regularity that is arbitrary (another arrangement could have worked, given a different history), sustained by everyone's expectation that everyone else will go along, and kept in place by precedent more than by anyone actually endorsing it. I remember the plot well enough: the black box, the slips of paper, the children gathering stones, Tessie Hutchinson complaining at the end that the drawing wasn't fair. I don't need you to quote the story, and in fact I'd rather you not try to quote it from memory — just work from the plot and scenes as commonly known.

The thing I'm stuck on is that Lewisian conventions usually solve a coordination problem the parties have a common interest in solving, while the lottery seems to coordinate the town on something that isn't in anyone's individual interest, unless they really believe it keeps the crops from failing or holds the town together. So: is the lottery a Lewis-convention, or is it a different kind of norm wearing a convention's clothes? Where does the Lewis picture illuminate the story — the deference to the black box, the half-remembered ritual, the way rules get dropped over the years while the core stays fixed — and where does the story push back on Lewis by showing that a practice can be arbitrary and precedent-bound without being the kind of thing anyone would rationally stick with? I'd like a reading that doesn't reduce the story to an illustration and doesn't just moralize about it either; say what Jackson dramatizes that Lewis's framework leaves out or can't see, and whether a Lewisian could reply that the villagers are simply failing to notice their convention has become pointless or worse.
