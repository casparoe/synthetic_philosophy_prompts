I teach epistemology, and for years I have been trying to say honestly what remains of the notion of the given once one takes the usual criticisms seriously. When I set out my verdicts on particular cases, though, I cannot tell whether a single principle is behind them, or whether some of the verdicts simply have to go. I would like you to find the principle, or tell me which verdict to abandon.

Here are my verdicts.

(1) When I look at a tomato in good light and judge that it is red, that judgment is not a conclusion I draw from anything more basic. It is straightforwardly observational, and I am entitled to rest on it unless I receive a specific reason not to. I would say the same thing about my judgment that I have a mild headache while writing this.

(2) The two lines in the Müller-Lyer figure still look unequal to me after I have measured them and know that they are not. I regard that lingering appearance as something I cannot revise by an act of judgment, but I do not treat it as giving me any reason at all to believe the lines are unequal. It seems, in one sense, to be exactly the sort of item the given was supposed to be, yet it has no epistemic standing for me.

(3) A radiologist glances at a chest film and says she saw the lesion immediately. She did not consciously compare it with a normal film or work through diagnostic criteria. I count this as observational knowledge, even though it plainly required years of training and a large background of theory. The fact that her seeing is acquired and concept-laden does not undermine its noninferential character for her now.

(4) A novice looking at the same film sees a gray smudge and cannot say what, if anything, it indicates. When asked what is given to him, he says something like "a pattern of shapes and shades." I am not sure that answer picks out a privileged stratum. The smudge is visible to him, but "shape" and "shade" seem to me already as conceptual as "lesion" is for the radiologist.

(5) A subject with blindsight insists he sees nothing, yet when forced to guess the location of a light he is right far above chance. I think he possesses subpersonal information, but I do not think anything is given to him, and I would not call his guesses observational knowledge on his part.

(6) A mathematician tells me that once he understood the proof that the square root of two is irrational, the conclusion became simply obvious to him, and it no longer felt dependent on the proof. I share the feeling, but I do not want to say that mathematical insight is a form of the perceptual given. I would instead say the proof remains in the background as a standing entitlement even when he no longer rehearses it.

(7) I am generally willing to treat a sincere report of a current pain as authoritative in a way that reports of lesions or chest sounds are not. But the pain case mixes two things I keep wanting to separate: the subject's privilege about how things are with her, and the fact that the report uses a shared concept, "pain." The first seems to deserve something like givenness; the second does not.

Is there a single principle that captures all seven of these? If not, where is the best place to draw the line? State the principle precisely, identify which of my verdicts it fits and which it forces me to revise, and explain why you prefer it to the alternatives. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: an experienced birdwatcher who reports seeing a goshawk from a distant raptor shape, cannot say what features she used, makes the report reliably, and could still have been wrong.
