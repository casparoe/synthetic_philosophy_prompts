As a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, I am preparing a short piece on John Stuart Mill’s later engagement with socialist ideas, and I would like help understanding an argument he makes near the beginning of the unfinished essays published after his death as _Socialism_ (1879). The passage occurs just after Mill has observed that manhood suffrage now prevails in the United States, France, and the German Confederation, and that the last Reform Act has given British wage-earners a large share of electoral power. He then writes:

Now these are the very class which, in the vocabulary of the higher ranks, are said to have no stake in the country. Of course they have in reality the greatest stake, since their daily bread depends on its prosperity. But they are not engaged (we may call it bribed) by any peculiar interest of their own, to the support of property as it is, least of all to the support of inequalities of property. So far as their power reaches, or may hereafter reach, the laws of property have to depend for support upon considerations of a public nature, upon the estimate made of their conduciveness to the general welfare, and not upon motives of a mere personal character operating on the minds of those who have control over the Government.

My question concerns how to read the final two sentences. Mill seems to move from an observation about political power to a claim about the justificatory basis of property. Is he saying only that, once working-class voters can act together as a class, property arrangements will have to be defended in terms of the public good rather than by appealing to the self-interest of the propertied? Or is he making a stronger claim that widening democratic power inherently exposes inequalities of property to a different and more demanding kind of justification? I am also uncertain about the parenthetical phrase “we may call it bribed.” Is that merely a rhetorical concession to the vocabulary of his opponents, or does it do real work in his account of the relationship between class interest and support for the existing property system? Finally, I would like to understand how this passage sets up the rest of Mill’s treatment of socialist objections to private property. Could you help me interpret the passage and explain its role in the argument?
