I'm writing an essay on Camus's The Stranger and I want to read the novel against his later writing on capital punishment. I'm not an expert, but from what I've absorbed, his position is roughly that the death penalty is a coldly premeditated murder carried out by the state, that the surrounding legal ceremony gives it a false air of rationality, and that it doesn't deter anyone; it just degrades everyone it touches. The novel feels to me like it's dramatising part of that: the prosecutor is far more interested in Meursault not weeping at his mother's funeral than in the killing of the Arab, and the trial turns him into a monster for reasons that have nothing to do with the shooting. But the book plainly isn't a reformist tract—Meursault really is guilty, the killing is senseless, and by the end he seems to accept his sentence and open himself to the world's indifference. I'm trying to work out how far I can honestly push an anti-death-penalty reading. Which scenes actually support it, which pull against it, and does the novel end up saying something darker and more absurdist that his later essays cant capture? I'd rather have a reading that's fair to both the novel and the philosophy, even if it means admitting the book undercuts its own case.
