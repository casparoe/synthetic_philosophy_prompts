I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I would like you to act as an expert in philosophy of science with a strong background in Thomas Kuhn's account of normal science, anomaly, paradigm change, and incommensurability. Please think carefully, begin by laying out the strongest considerations on each side before committing to a verdict, and do not offer bibliographies or citations; I am interested in the reasoning rather than references.

For several years I have followed a fairly specific epistemic policy when reading about contested results in mature scientific fields. Suppose a precision experiment or an observational program reports a finding that conflicts with the reigning framework—for example, a measurement that disagrees with the Standard Model of particle physics, or a cosmological result that sits badly with the current consensus model. The characteristic first response from scientists inside the paradigm is to treat the anomaly as very likely an instrumental artifact, a calibration error, an unmodeled background, or the product of some special-case auxiliary assumption. My rule is this: I heavily discount such dismissals when they come from researchers whose careers, training, and interpretive skills are invested in the paradigm, and I do so even when I cannot myself evaluate the technical merits of the anomaly or of the proposed error analysis. My rationale is that normal science, on Kuhn's picture, is precisely the enterprise of defending the paradigm's core by accommodating anomalies within the existing framework; its practitioners are structurally disposed to see anomalies as noise, and their expertise is partly expertise in a set of techniques for explaining failure away. I also suspect that the standards for what counts as a plausible source of error are themselves partly paradigm-laden, so that the dismissal and the anomaly claim are not always adjudicable on neutral ground. The rule is not that the anomaly is correct; it is only that the insider dismissal carries very little independent evidential weight for me.

This rule has cost me: I have assigned substantial probability to anomalies that the field later quietly resolved as systematic errors. I have kept it anyway, because the alternative seems to be deferring to the very community whose entrenched assumptions are in question.

Is this policy rationally defensible, or is it a bias dressed up in Kuhnian vocabulary? Under what conditions would a tightened version of the rule be defensible—for instance, should the discount depend on whether the anomaly has been replicated by independent groups, on whether the proposed error source was reverse-engineered to fit this particular result, or on whether there is already a rival framework that predicts the anomaly? And is someone in my position, as a non-specialist in the relevant empirical science, entitled to apply such a discount at all, given that I cannot perform the technical evaluation myself?
