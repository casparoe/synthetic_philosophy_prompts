Some animal ethicists defend a fewer-deaths argument for preferring beef to chicken. The argument runs as follows. If you are going to eat meat and you grant that the life of each farmed animal counts equally as one unit, then the morally relevant comparison is the number of animals deliberately raised and killed per unit of edible protein. By that measure, beef looks far better than chicken: a single steer supplies a very large amount of edible tissue, so a kilogram of beef corresponds to only a tiny fraction of a killed animal, whereas a kilogram of chicken corresponds to a substantial fraction of a killed bird. In headcount terms, chicken is markedly worse.

Now suppose a new development. A credible new life-cycle analysis extends the comparison beyond the animals raised for slaughter, adding incidental deaths caused by producing feed: wild invertebrates and small vertebrates killed during cultivation, harvest, insecticide application, and other field operations, along with animals displaced or killed in pasture management. The analysis concludes that, once these indirect deaths are included, intensively farmed chicken is associated with fewer total deaths per kilogram of edible protein than either grain-finished or grass-based beef, because broilers convert feed into flesh much more efficiently and have a smaller cropland footprint per kilogram of protein. For the purpose of this prompt, accept that finding as reliable.

Does the beef-over-chicken argument survive this development, does it need repair, or is it dead? State exactly which premise of the original argument the new estimates bear on; assess whether the argument can be repaired by replacing the headcount metric with a welfare-adjusted or sentience-weighted metric; and state what, if anything, remains of the argument once incidental deaths are admitted into the comparison. Do not merely report the controversy—defend a verdict.

End with exactly one line of the form \answer{survives}, \answer{needs repair}, or \answer{dead}.
