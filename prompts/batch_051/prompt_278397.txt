I run a small nonprofit that helps people in their late sixties and seventies plan major transitions — retiring from a career, selling a house, giving up driving, agreeing to accept help at home. In a typical week I sit with four to six clients for about fifty minutes each, and each session has to end with a written next step that my board will sometimes review when a family member questions my advice. Some months ago I read a used translation of Cicero's short book On Old Age, the dialogue in which the elderly Cato does most of the talking. What has stayed with me is how Cato answers the four standard complaints about aging — that it takes us out of active affairs, weakens the body, removes pleasures, and brings us close to death. He mostly refuses to accept the complaints on their own terms: the misery, he thinks, comes not from the losses themselves but from the person's continuing to judge strength, enjoyment, and usefulness by a standard that no longer fits his condition, and then mistaking the loss of one kind of activity for the end of activity altogether. I was struck by how closely my clients repeat those four complaints, and by how useless it is when I simply agree that old age is hard.

I want to turn Cato's position into a reusable decision procedure I can actually run inside a fifty-minute session, without mentioning Cicero and without expecting any philosophical background from the client. Give me a one-page checklist: the questions to ask in order; how I can tell that a question has been answered well enough to move on; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to me, a family member, or a clinician because the matter is one of safety or values rather than judgment. The procedure should decide, for a given complaint or proposed change, whether the client's unhappiness is the kind of mistake about standards that Cato's view says should be gently refused, or the kind of loss that even on his view a reasonable person should accept and plan around. Keep the distinctions that do the real work — especially the difference between a loss of strength and a loss of the only activity the client is willing to count, and the difference between losing a capacity and losing the standing or self-respect that had been attached to it.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a retired surgeon who says he has nothing left to do and wants to be left alone. Second, a woman with mild arthritis who says she would rather give up driving than submit to an evaluation she finds humiliating. Third, a client in her late seventies whose children are pressing her to stop organizing a large annual fundraising dinner, even though she still runs it capably. Make one of the three a case in which the procedure gives me an answer I will not like, and say which feature of the case produces that answer. End by saying honestly where the procedure will misfire or where it cannot keep me from having to make a value judgment of my own, and write the whole answer in American English.
