My friend and I were arguing about whether I should just take his word on a moral question, and he sent me this summary of something he read (he wont tell me the author, of course):

"In contemporary ethics there's a pretty robust consensus that moral testimony isn't like ordinary testimony. If someone tells you the restaurant is on Main Street, you can take their word and that's knowledge. But if someone tells you that a certain action or policy is wrong, you can't take their word in the same way; a moral belief has to be formed through your own reflection or experience to count as genuinely yours, or to count as justified at all. That's why we don't defer to moral experts the way we defer to doctors, and why someone who gets their moral beliefs entirely from friends, books, or their community is in an important sense not fully autonomous. So testimonial moral belief—believing something is wrong because someone you trust told you so—is either impossible or at least illegitimate."

I suspect this is overstating things, but I don't know enough to say exactly where. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me (a) what someone working in this area would accept, (b) what's a simplification or a conflation, and (c) whether the final "so" actually follows? In particular, I keep getting stuck on the leap from "deferring to a moral expert feels weird" to "any moral view I didn't form myself is suspect" — surely learning your moral views from parents or a community is normal? And I'd like to know whether a belief can be known through testimony but still be inappropriate to act on, or whether that distinction doesn't matter here. Also, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested, and don't present one side's view as the consensus.
