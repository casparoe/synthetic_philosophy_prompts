I recently retired from experimental physics and have been sitting in on a colleague's philosophy of mathematics reading group. Last week, when we were discussing what counting does, I said I could not find oneness in the objects themselves: it looks to me like something our activity of counting imposes rather than something existing things wear on their own. Another member quoted Euclid's definition at me as though that closed the case: "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one" (Elements, Book VII, definition 1). He took it to mean that unity belongs to every existing thing independently of us and our counting.

I doubt that one line can carry that weight, but I want to be able to say exactly why. What are the distinct precise claims this definition could be making, from the almost wholly verbal to the robustly ontological? Which of those readings, if any, would actually support using it to dismiss my view, and which would merely restate the point in dispute? Lay the readings out as a list with a verdict for each, so I can bring it back to the group.
