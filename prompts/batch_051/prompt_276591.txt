I am a PhD student working on animal ethics with a side interest in agricultural ecology, and I keep hitting the same frustration: the debate over eating animals runs on a handful of stock farm cases while whole classes of affected animals—those killed or harmed in crop cultivation and pest management, wild animals whose lives agriculture changes, and invertebrates—are treated as footnotes. Before I read another beef-versus-chicken or pasture-versus-row-crop comparison, I want the specification of what a theory of the ethics of food production must get right to count as adequate once those categories are included.

Write the desiderata. Include: the cases a theory must handle (beef versus chicken at the margin, high-yield row crops versus extensive grazing, harvesting deaths, pest management, and wild animals in and around cultivated land); the distinctions it may not collapse (e.g., causing versus allowing versus financing deaths, sentience versus degree and balance of welfare, expected versus actual effects, natural versus human-shaped wild animal suffering); and the biological and agricultural constraints it cannot ignore. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of particular camps. Then say whether any subset of the desiderata is likely to be jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, whether a theory that fully counts wild animal deaths can avoid implying large-scale obligations to redesign ecosystems—and which single requirement does the most to exclude prominent positions. A table for the desiderata is fine.
