I keep rereading a comment thread under an old post about moral heuristics, and there's an argument in there I can't shake. It's one of those where every step looks right but the bottom line is to obviously wrong to accept. The target is the standard blogosphere take that virtues like honesty and duties like promise-keeping are derivative: they're heuristics that save a computationally bounded agent from its own motivated, noisy, case-by-case expected value reasoning. Here's the reconstruction, cleaned up a little.

(1) If honesty is a mere heuristic, the entire case for have it is that a policy of following "be honest" outperforms case-by-case EV calculation over the distribution of situations you'll actually meet.

(2) The usual story is that this advantage comes from your case-by-case EV machinery being less reliable than the heuristic exactly in the situations where the two disagree — which are also pretty much the situations where you're tempted to make an exception (you're stressed, the stakes make you rationalize, someone you love is involved, etc.).

(3) But if that's why you adopted the heuristic, you can't rationally override it later on the basis of the very local EV estimate whose unreliability justified adopting it in the first place.

(4) So an honest consequentialist is committed to staying honest even in the particular cases where her on-the-spot consequentialist estimate says lying would do more good.

(5) But "stay honest even when your best estimate says lying does more good" is more or less what it means for honesty to have independent, non-derivative weight.

(6) The alternative is that she overrides the heuristic whenever the local estimate says so, but then honesty never actually constrains anything and collapses back into act-consequentialism.

(7) So the derivative-but-action-guiding middle doesn't exist: virtues are either empty labels for act-consequentialist casuistry, or deontology wearing friendlier vocabulary.

The conclusion feels like it proves too much — not just about honesty, but about any fallible heuristic a bounded agent might use. I'd most like to keep the middle position, and I'd rather give up (3) than (5), but I worry that rejecting (3) just re-imports the bias the heuristic existed to correct. Where does this actually go wrong, if it does, and what do I have to pay to keep the middle?
