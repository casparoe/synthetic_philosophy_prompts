I help run a free-speech reading group that's been chewing on the US-versus-Europe hate speech divide for two years, and I've started to think the disagreement itself is the more interesting object than the hate speech. The American side keeps coming back to content neutrality and the slippery slope: once the state gets to ban viewpoints it calls hateful, the same power gets used on labor organizers, heretics, and whichever minority the majority currently dislikes, so the only stable line is incitement to imminent lawless action. The European side keeps coming back to militant democracy and dignity: Germany criminalizes Volksverhetzung (incitement to hatred) and Holocaust denial, and the European Court of Human Rights has this abuse-of-rights doctrine where you can't invoke the free-speech article to attack the very rights the Convention exists to protect. From that angle the American position looks like it learned exactly the wrong lesson from the 1930s. What's maddening is that both sides accept the same history, and nobody thinks ordinary racist abuse is harmless. Yet nobody has moved in two years.

My working theory is that they're running different cost-benefit analyses: the Americans are optimizing to avoid the worst case where the censor gets permanent power, the Europeans are optimizing to avoid the worst case where organized hatred gets a free pass, and neither side can see why its worst case should have to trade against the other's. But I'm not confident. Is the impasse actually about empirical facts (would American-style protection meaningfully risk a Weimar scenario, and do European-style bans actually suppress or just displace hatred?), about which historical analogy gets to set the default, about a genuine value disagreement over autonomy versus dignity that no fact could settle, or about something like who gets trusted to draw the line? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the two systems, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis — a comparative finding, a doctrinal distinction, a reframing of the question — or whether this is a standoff that should just stay a standoff.
