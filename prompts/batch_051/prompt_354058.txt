I have been reading Thucydides in Richard Crawley's old translation, and I keep returning to the passage in Book One, chapter 22, where he sets out what he thinks his history is for. He writes:

"The absence of romance in my history will, I fear, detract somewhat from its interest; but if it be judged useful by those inquirers who desire an exact knowledge of the past as an aid to the interpretation of the future, which in the course of human things must resemble if it does not reflect it, I shall be content. In fine, I have written my work, not as an essay which is to win the applause of the moment, but as a possession for all time."

What I want to ask about is a particular use to which this passage gets put. At a recent seminar, a strategist argued that since Thucydides himself says the future must resemble, if not reflect, the past, a careful study of the growth of Athenian power before the Peloponnesian War gives us a reliable template for the present. He then described a contemporary rivalry between two naval powers as "Athens and Sparta with aircraft carriers" and concluded that the rising power would, like Athens, sooner or later be drawn into a war it did not strictly need. He cited the sentence I quoted as his warrant, as if Thucydides had licensed exactly this kind of reasoning. I have seen the same move made with Munich and with Vietnam, where a loose resemblance to a familiar prior case is treated as doing the whole predictive work. I want to know whether the passage actually supports any of this. Read carefully as a rule for the use of history—according to its words, according to what Thucydides says he was trying to do, and according to what a reasonable modern reader would take him to mean—does Book One, chapter 22 authorize this strategist's inference from "the present resembles the past" to "the next stage will resemble it too"? What would Thucydides' own standard of an "exact knowledge of the past," arrived at by severe and detailed testing of the evidence, require of someone who wants to use his work in this way? And does the passage, on its most defensible reading, explain why analogies of the Munich, Vietnam, and Thucydides-trap sort so often mislead—or does it instead hand those analogists their favorite premise?
