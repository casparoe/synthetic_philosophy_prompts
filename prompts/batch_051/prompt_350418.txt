Read Euripides' Medea through the question of whether a myth has a canonical version. Assume, as many scholars hold, that the version we now treat as standard — in which Medea deliberately kills her own children — is Euripides' own, perhaps even his invention, and that rival traditions had the Corinthians kill the boys or had Medea kill them by accident while trying to make them immortal. Given that, what did Euripides' version do to displace the alternatives: dramatic economy, psychological depth, moral seriousness, sheer luck, or something else? Does the play itself give any internal reason to regard its own variant as more authentic or authoritative, or does it instead show that a myth is nothing over and above its variants, with the canonical one being merely the variant that happened to survive? I want a reading that is faithful to the play and to the conceptual question, and that ends by saying what Euripides' case adds to the debate that bare argument cannot.
