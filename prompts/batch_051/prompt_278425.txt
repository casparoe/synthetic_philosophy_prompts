I am a philosophy professor. A student in my decision theory course has brought me the following passage from a popular book on rationality. I would like you to give me a careful, structured assessment of how fair it is as an account of coherence arguments for expected utility maximization. Please do not write a general essay on the topic; I want an audit of this particular summary, separating what is accurate from what is overstated or subtly wrong, and saying what a corrected version would and would not license.

Here is the passage:

"Economists have proved that rational agents maximize expected utility. The proof has two parts. First, the representation theorems of von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage show that, provided a person's preferences satisfy a few elementary consistency requirements — every two options are comparable, rankings never cycle, and a condition of 'independence' holds — that person necessarily behaves exactly as if they held degrees of belief and utilities and always chose the option with the greatest expected utility. Second, coherence arguments show why these requirements are not optional: anyone who violates any of them is vulnerable to a Dutch book or a money pump, a sequence of trades each of which they find advantageous on its own but which jointly guarantee a worse outcome for certain. Since no rational agent can coherently choose a combination of acts that is certainly worse than another available combination, a person who violates the axioms is inconsistent in much the way a person who believes a contradiction is. This is why the Allais and Ellsberg 'paradoxes' are not genuine counterexamples to expected utility theory: the preferences they elicit violate the independence axiom and are therefore simply mistakes. The axioms of expected utility theory accordingly have the same status as the principles of logic — non-negotiable constraints on rational thought."

4. What survives once corrected (2 paragraphs). State what representation theorems and Dutch-book/money-pump arguments actually establish, and what normative conclusions do and do not follow from them.

5. Verdict (1 short paragraph). Tell me whether you judge the passage fair, unfair, or fair in places but misleading overall, and on what your judgment turns.
