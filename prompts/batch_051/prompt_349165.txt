Question: Frank Jackson's Mary has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room, has never seen color, but has learned every physical fact about color and color vision. She leaves the room and sees a red apple for the first time. Explain what Jackson takes this to show about physicalism, and be precise about what Mary learns. In your answer, distinguish the claim that Mary acquires a new ability from the claim that she acquires knowledge of a new fact, and say which of these claims the argument needs if it is to work against physicalism.

Student answer: Mary is a neuroscientist who grows up in a room with no colored things and no way to see color, but she reads all the science and knows every physical fact about what happens in the brain and the world when someone sees color. When she leaves and sees a red apple, she learns what it is like to see red.

Jackson thinks this is a problem for physicalism. If physicalism were true, then knowing every physical fact would amount to knowing everything, but Mary learns something new. So there must be some nonphysical fact that she was missing. The new thing Mary learns is a set of abilities: she can now imagine red, recognize red things by sight, and remember the experience. Those are know-how rather than know-that, and since abilities are not facts, they cannot refute physicalism. This is the ability hypothesis, and it is exactly what Jackson takes the example to establish: the case shows that the knowledge Mary gains is know-how, not knowledge of a new fact. I agree with Jackson. The one leftover is that Mary seems to gain knowledge that red looks this way, but that is just another description of her new abilities, not a new fact, so physicalism is untouched.
