Last evening a friend and I argued at length about whether something like the Atlantis story can count as a live historical hypothesis at all, and I left unsure whether I had been pressing a passage beyond what it can bear. In Book 1, chapter 22, section 2 of his History of the Peloponnesian War, Thucydides states his method for the narrative of events: "And with reference to the narrative of events, far from permitting myself to derive it from the first source that came to hand, I did not even trust my own impressions, but it rests partly on what I saw myself, partly on what others saw for me, the accuracy of the report being always tried by the most severe and detailed tests possible."

Now consider a case he did not address. A present-day writer reconstructs Atlantis as a real Bronze Age maritime power, resting the argument almost entirely on a chain of written tradition: Plato has the character Critias report that Solon learned the story from Egyptian priests at Sais, who possessed temple records of events nine thousand years earlier. The writer argues that this chain cannot be refuted, that the silence of archaeology proves nothing, and that the burden therefore falls on the sceptic to justify excluding the tradition from serious consideration. My friend insists that Thucydides' rule cannot touch this argument, because it concerns a war in living memory and a historian who had his own eyes and actual witnesses to fall back on; Atlantis lies beyond all possible eyewitness evidence, where traditions testify only to other traditions.

Does the quoted rule, read in its plain letter, apply to the Atlantis case at all? What does it require of the Atlantis writer if read instead as a general principle about how any report of the past earns belief? What would Thucydides most plausibly have said if asked this exact question, and how would a reasonable reader today understand his words? Decide the case under each of four approaches — the words, the purpose, the probable answer of the author if asked, and the reasonable reader's understanding — and then say which approach should govern here and what that verdict reveals about demarcating pseudohistory.
