I have recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and begun reading metaphysics, and I have noticed that discussions of the unity of the proposition produce theories faster than they produce a clear statement of the problem. Before I read another account, I want the desiderata written out: what any adequate theory of what makes a proposition one — and fit to be true or false — must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each. I suspect the list will have to include at least the following, though I expect you to improve on it. It must explain the difference between a proposition and a bare aggregate or ordered list of its constituents, and why the same constituents can yield different propositions when differently related. It must not merely relocate Bradley's regress, by positing a linking relation that then needs further links; it has to say why its unifying device is itself exempt, or why the regress is harmless. It must account for false propositions and propositions about the non-existent, rather than making unity depend on a real complex in the world. It must respect the distinction between object and concept, or particular and universal, and explain why two saturated components do not combine into a truth-evaluable whole. And, if propositions are meant to be shareable across sentences and languages, it must locate the unity in the proposition itself rather than in the grammar of one surface form. For each requirement, tell me whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, identify any subsets that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the main contenders (briefly: Frege's unsaturated concepts, Russell's multiple-relation theory, and primitivist or unstructured-proposition views). Keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivation, not on a scorecard. Please hold the response to a hard ceiling of 900 words; a table is acceptable.
