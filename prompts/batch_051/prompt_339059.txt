I am a doctoral student teaching a second-year undergraduate logic course, and I have just taken over a fortnight of tutorials on the transition from traditional syllogistic to modern quantificational logic. Last week a student asked, quite reasonably, why we abandoned Aristotle when syllogisms are so much more readable than quantifier notation, and my answer — that Frege showed subject–predicate analysis cannot cope with relational reasoning — came out as a slogan rather than a felt insight. I want to fix that. What the students should come away seeing is not that Frege happened to invent a new notation, but that subject–predicate analysis has a specific expressive ceiling, and that Frege's function–argument analysis is a response to a problem they have personally run into.

Design a 90-minute classroom activity for a seminar of about sixteen students who can already handle the four categorical forms and simple conversions but have not yet met Frege's function–argument analysis or quantifier–variable notation. The activity should make them run into the expressive limits of subject–predicate logic themselves, before Frege is named or introduced. I imagine something like a translation or regimentation game: groups are given a constrained representational system, such as cards for subjects and predicates or a fixed sentence frame, and a sequence of ordinary-language arguments that become progressively harder to represent, until they hit cases the system cannot distinguish or cannot license. But I am open to a different design if it does the job better.

The cases should be chosen so that the specific failures emerge naturally. At minimum they should encounter: a relational argument, such as something turning on the difference between everyone loves someone and someone is loved by everyone; a case of multiple generality, along the lines of the step from every horse is an animal to every head of a horse is a head of an animal; and the fact that a predicate like is loved by someone can be manufactured from a relation, which is precisely the move that lets syllogistic seem adequate while quietly changing the subject.

Please give me the full rules, roles and materials; a timeline for the 90 minutes; the exact sentences or arguments to hand out, ordered so the difficulty escalates; a debrief that maps what happened at each stage to the technical gap it exposed and that leads into Frege's function–argument analysis without my having to assert it; a note on what will predictably go wrong when I run this and how to handle it; and an honest statement of which features of Frege's actual innovation the activity cannot reproduce and will have to be supplied by me afterwards. If the request rests on a confused picture of what Frege changed, or if an activity of this sort cannot deliver what I want, tell me so plainly rather than giving me a polished lesson plan.
