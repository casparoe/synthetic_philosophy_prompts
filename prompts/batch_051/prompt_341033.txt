I've been going round in circles on Ibn Rushd, so I want you to actually commit to a line. Please argue for the following reading, and then consider the strongest objection to it. On this reading, his harmonisation of religion and philosophy is not the mild claim that both routes lead to the same truth. It's the stronger claim that whenever the apparent sense of scripture conflicts with what demonstrative philosophy has established, scripture has to be read allegorically—and the authority to decide when that is happening belongs to the people capable of demonstrative proof, not to ordinary believers or to theologians who argue from merely probable premises. So the harmony is really a hierarchy: reason doesn't replace revelation, but it does get the last word on what revelation means. Make the strongest case for that reading, both as a reading of Ibn Rushd and as a philosophical position about how a revealed text and a body of demonstrated knowledge can be kept in the same world.
