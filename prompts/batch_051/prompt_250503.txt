I've been trying to get clear on what a theory of the timing of conscious experience would even have to accomplish before I read any more of the literature, and I want the full spec first. So: write me the desiderata — the requirements such a theory has to meet to count as adequate. To seed the list, here are the cases I keep coming back to. In the color phi setup, a red dot is flashed at one location and then a green dot is flashed nearby shortly after; people report a single dot moving from the first spot to the second and changing color around the midpoint, which is odd because the color change seems to be experienced before the second dot actually appears. There are similar cases: the cutaneous rabbit, where taps at the wrist, then elbow, then shoulder feel like a series of taps hopping up the arm; the flash-lag effect, where a flash aligned with a moving object looks like it lags behind the object; and Libet-style results where a skin stimulus is reported as occurring earlier than it physically did, as if its timing gets stamped by the earliest cortical response rather than by the stimulus itself. There's also a classic framing that asks whether these cases involve an Orwellian revision (you had one experience and memory got altered after the fact) or a Stalinesque revision (the experience was edited before it reached consciousness), with the suggestion that no experiment could tell them apart.

For a theory of the timing of experience — in the philosophy of mind/cognitive science sense, not the metaphysics of time — I want the list of requirements: which phenomena it has to handle and whether each is non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp; which distinctions it can't collapse, like the difference between the time of a neural event, the time an experience seems to occur, and the time a subject reports it; which empirical results it can't contradict; and what it has to say about whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories are genuinely different hypotheses or a distinction without a difference. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out existing positions, and flag any subsets of the list you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I'd rather learn that now than halfway through the semester. Keep clear throughout which parts are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, rather than making one camp sound like the consensus. American English, please.
