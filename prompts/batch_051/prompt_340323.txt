A couple of people in my weekly reading group got into an argument about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Here's roughly how it went.

Marta opened by saying she can't take the Epicurean line seriously, because a death can clearly deprive someone of decades of life she otherwise would've had. A death at eight seems worse than a death at eighty-eight, other things equal, and the Epicurean story alone doesn't explain that difference.

Dan said he actually thinks the Epicureans were onto something, but not for the usual slogan-y reason. His worry is with the deprivation account itself: if death is bad because of what it takes away, when is it bad? It can't be before death, because you're still alive and haven't lost anything yet. It can't be after death, because there's no you left for the badness to stick to. And the moment of death is just a boundary. So we're left with a harm that happens at no time at all — he said that should make us suspicious that "deprived of life" is doing hidden work.

Marta said that test for harm is too demanding. Plenty of harms are never felt: being betrayed behind your back, being slandered in a way that never reaches you, a spouse cheating that you never find out about. Those are bad for the person regardless of whether they ever feel a thing, so the lack of a felt moment isn't the issue.

Dan said those cases still involve a living person with standing interests at some point. The question is whether a dead person has any interests left to be set back. He granted that we have interests now in our own futures — he's not a pure Epicurean about that — but he said that just means the thwarting happens to the still-living stages, right up to the last moment of life. Which he admitted is weird: the harm sort of accrues to a time when the person is still fine.

Marta jumped on that. So if I die painlessly in my sleep tonight, she said, your view locates the harm last night, while I'm alive and comfortable, and says nothing bad happens to me after that. You've confused the badness of losing a future with a bad state of some earlier me. At that point she said the timing argument proves less than he thinks, because harm can be a relational fact about a life as a whole — the life is worse, even if no particular moment of the life is worse.

Dan said she was now helping herself to the same whole-life move she'd resisted when he said there's no subject after death. The whole-life view doesn't dodge the subject problem: to compare the "whole" life to a longer one, you need someone whose life got shortened, and after death there's no one for that to be true of. Marta answered that this is like saying a book can't be worse for missing its last chapter, because there's no reader to be disappointed in the nonexistent pages.

They went around once more on whether the book analogy smuggles in an author who intended more chapters, and then someone changed the subject. I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it.

Could you adjudicate this step by step? For each move, say weather it's right, wrong, or half-right, and explain why. Then say what both of them are missing, which specific claims are load-bearing rather than noise, and if you had to call it, who is more right overall.
