I am a novelist, and in the draft I am revising there is a long scene in which a retired patristic scholar argues over dinner with his fundamentalist brother-in-law about how Scripture ought to be read. The scholar reconstructs what he takes to be the Origenist case for allegorical interpretation; the brother-in-law calls it sophistry. I want the scholar's argument to be as strong as it can honestly be made, so that neither man is a straw man, and I need to know precisely where it stands before I finish the scene. As the scholar gives it, the argument runs:

(1) If Scripture is divinely inspired, then no passage of Scripture, correctly interpreted, teaches anything unworthy of God; God cannot be the author of deceit, cruelty, or foolishness.
(2) Several passages, taken in their literal or plain sense, do teach things unworthy of God. For example, God hardens a man's heart so that he will sin and then punishes him for it; God commands the extermination of whole peoples, women and children among them; God prescribes death for a son who is merely stubborn and rebellious.
(3) These passages, therefore, cannot be correctly interpreted in their literal or plain sense.
(4) The only alternative to interpreting a passage literally is interpreting it allegorically or spiritually.
(5) These passages must therefore be interpreted allegorically or spiritually.
(6) And so the allegorical method is not an optional ornament; it is an obligation laid upon the reader by the text itself, at least wherever the plain sense would otherwise impute evil to God.

I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in the words 'literal sense,' which may shift between step (2) and step (4), but I cannot say exactly how. Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step that fails and explain, under the strongest available reading of that step, what goes wrong; say whether the argument can be repaired by distinguishing two senses of 'literal.' If it is valid, tell me which premise does the real work, and how a thoughtful literalist — not a caricature — could most economically resist it.
