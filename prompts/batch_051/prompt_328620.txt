Construct a thought experiment for an upper-level metaphysics seminar that separates a matter-based criterion of artifact identity from a career-based criterion, using a legally embedded variant of the Ship of Theseus. The case should have two candidate vessels at the end: the continuously repaired ship and a ship built from the original discarded timbers. The twist must be legal rather than metaphysical: a maritime lien, salvage claim, or restoration statute singles out one of the two vessels as the ship for certain normative purposes, while the other is singled out for different normative purposes. Constraints: ordinary maritime practice; no instant reassembly; no time travel; no branching in the strict sense; if two ships stand in the same harbor at the same time, they must be structurally distinguishable by inspection. After presenting the scenario, show the verdict that each criterion delivers for each candidate vessel, and state which criterion is doing the work in each legal attribution. Then identify the feature that produces the divergence—for example, whether the original timbers are preserved, whether the repair history is continuous, or whether the law picks out one relation as controlling. Say how a defender of the criterion that loses on one attribution would attack the case, and say what minimal change to the scenario would remove the divergence. Finally, say what would change your own analysis: which argument or consideration would lead you to reverse a verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. American English.
