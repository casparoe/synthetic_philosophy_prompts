I've been reading Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, and one sentence in Chapter 1 keeps bugging me, where he says the machine-school people "look upon a constitution in the same light (difference of scale being allowed for) as they would upon a steam plow, or a threshing machine." I know Mill himself later pushes proportional representation, so he's not simply on the "it grew" side. I want to use the made-vs-grown distinction to analyze New Zealand's 1990s switch from FPTP to MMP. The basic facts as I have them: a Royal Commission proposed MMP, then there was an indicative referendum in 1992, a binding one in 1993, and the country actually switched. On the face of it that's a pretty steam-plow story—a committee diagnosed a problem, tested options in referendums, and installed a new machine. But features of the campaign and the outcome—the Māori seats, the 5% threshold, the two-step referendum design, the way the two main parties nearly got rolled—seem much more like adaptations of an existing political culture than like a clean design choice. Analyze the episode through that passage: where does New Zealand actually look like deliberate constitutional engineering, where does it look like organic growth or path dependence, and does the case maybe show that Mill's two-way contrast in Chapter 1 is to crude to do the real explanatory work? And if Mill's own PR arguments depend on the engineering picture, does this case support or undercut them?
