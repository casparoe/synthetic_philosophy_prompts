For a philosophy class I have been reading a view, associated with Hofstadter, that a self is not a fixed soul-like object but a pattern of thinking, feeling, and reacting, and that such patterns can be copied—imperfectly—into other brains. On this view, when a person dies, fragments of the self can persist in the people who knew them well, in proportion to how much of the pattern still runs in those other minds. I want to stress-test this claim with a hard case. Below I state my own four criteria for saying that a fragment of one person's self genuinely persists in another brain, and then I describe a case that seems to sit on the line.

Here are the criteria. (1) Copy rather than coincidence: the characteristics in the survivor's brain must have been acquired through long interaction with the dead person, not developed independently. (2) Fidelity: the pattern must reproduce the person's distinctive mental style—what they noticed, how they reasoned, what moved them, what they feared—not merely generic traits. (3) Live operation: the pattern must actually run, generating new thoughts, predictions, or decisions in the person's style, rather than serving only as stored memories about them. (4) Experiential signature: the survivor must sometimes experience the process as the other person's thoughts occurring in their own mind, as opposed to their own thoughts merely shaped by the other person.

Daniel was married to Miriam for forty-seven years. Without any deliberate effort, he absorbed much of her way of thinking about money, family conflict, and moral scruples; he could finish her sentences and often knew what she would object to before she said it. After she dies, he finds that, when facing a hard decision, he automatically rehearses what she would have said, and the imagined responses sometimes surprise him and change his course. The internal model is strikingly accurate in familiar domains—it captures her priorities, her cadence, and her intolerance for self-pity—but it is patchy elsewhere: it has little of her humor or her love of gardening, and in situations wholly unlike any she faced, it produces only clichés. Daniel does not experience this as Miriam thinking inside him. He describes it as his own thinking, shaped by her; nevertheless, a few times a month a simulated response feels alien and catches him off guard.

Does a fragment of Miriam's self persist in Daniel, in the sense described above?
