I'm preparing a teaching example for a seminar on mental causation, and I would like a single, everyday scenario that forces a clean split between two ways of handling the causal exclusion argument. Please construct a case in which a person performs a simple intentional action—pressing a button will do—and in which a mental state, such as an intention or desire, and its neural realizer are both candidate causes of the behavior. The case should respect the standard background assumptions behind the exclusion problem: the physical domain is causally closed, the neural realizer is by itself sufficient for the behavior, and the behavior is not systematically overdetermined by two independent causes. But the scenario should be engineered so that an interventionist counterfactual account of causation with a proportionality requirement gives one verdict: the mental state causes the behavior, while the fine-grained neural state does not. A production or sufficiency account of causation, of the kind often taken to drive the exclusion argument, should give the opposite verdict: the neural state does the producing and the mental state is excluded or epiphenomenal. Do not stipulate these verdicts; the structure of the case should generate them.

After presenting the scenario, run through the reasoning in detail. Specify the two candidate causes and the behavior, identify the relevant contrast classes or possible interventions, and explain why each account of causation issues the verdict it does. Then say which structural feature is doing the real work—my guess is multiple realizability, but I want you to confirm or correct that. Test the diagnosis by removing it: explain what happens to the disagreement if the action has only one possible neural realizer in all nearby circumstances, so that no alternative realizer is available to support a proportionality judgment. Finally, defend the case against the natural exclusion-side objection that merely possible alternative realizers cannot make the actually occurring mental state a producing cause, and take a stand on whether the interventionist should concede that the dispute is ultimately about what causation is rather than about the metaphysics of mind. Keep the scenario ordinary and short, with no neuroscience fiction and no stipulation of dualism.
