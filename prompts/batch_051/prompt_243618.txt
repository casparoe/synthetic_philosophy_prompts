For a piece I'm writing on what happens to the idea of proof when no one can read the whole argument, I want to stress-test the definition I've always used. It has three parts: (1) each step follows by a rule a human can check on their own, (2) in principle one person could follow the whole thing end to end, and (3) it explains why the theorem is true, not just that it is. Now take the four-colour theorem, proved in 1976 by Appel and Haken: the strategy was human — reduce to an unavoidable set of configurations and show each one is reducible — but the computer did the endless case-checking, and nobody has every actually followed the full computation. Then take a modern cousin: a SAT solver — a program that checks satisfiability of big boolean formulas — refutes a combinatorial conjecture and emits a certificate thats formally correct but has no lemma structure, just millions of clause derivations, so verifying it means running a separate program that no human reads either. Under my three criteria, is either of these a proof? Walk me through criterion by criterion, say which fail and which hold for each case, and then tell me whether the SAT certificate should make me drop criterion (2), criterion (3), or both — or whether I should just accept that proof has split into two senses.
