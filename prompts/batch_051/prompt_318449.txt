I'm taking a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy, and we recently spent a session on the White Horse dialogue. I tried to restate the argument in my own terms, and the result is a chain of reasoning whose conclusion I find impossible to accept, though I cannot see exactly where it breaks. Here is the reconstruction I have been carrying around in my head:

(1) To say that a white horse is a horse is to say that the thing satisfying the request "bring a horse" and the thing satisfying the request "bring a white horse" are one and the same.
(2) If the two requests concerned one and the same thing, then anything that satisfied the one would also satisfy the other.
(3) A black or bay horse will satisfy "bring a horse," but it will not satisfy "bring a white horse."
(4) So the two requests do not concern one and the same thing.
(5) Therefore a white horse is not a horse.
(6) But by exactly the same form of argument, a black horse is not a horse, a small elephant is not an elephant, an old friend is not a friend, and a left turn is not a turn — since in each case the qualified and unqualified requests pick out different ranges of things.
(7) Since the argument entails the absurd line in (6), something must be given up.

I would like to know which step you reject and what the cost of rejecting it is. I have a strong wish to keep (3), since it seems plainly true as a point about what would satisfy the two requests, and I suspect the trouble lies in (1) or (2), but I cannot say precisely what the mistake is — or whether the only way out is to adopt a different reading of the copula that the original dialogue is deliberately resisting.
