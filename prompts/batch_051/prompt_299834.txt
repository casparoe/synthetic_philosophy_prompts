I am a novelist finishing a novel set in the art world, and I repeatedly have to write scenes in which two characters disagree about whether a particular work is good. I have noticed that I cheat in these scenes: my own preferences, or my partiality toward one character, determine which side the narration treats as perceptive and which side it treats as a foil. I want a reusable procedure to prevent this. I have been reading Hume's discussion of the standard of taste, which argues that taste is a matter of sentiment rather than reasoning, but that some judgments of taste are nevertheless better grounded than others because some judges are better qualified, possessing strong sense, delicacy of sentiment, practice, breadth of comparison, and freedom from prejudice. Turn that idea into a working checklist I can run every time I draft or revise one of these scenes. It must be short enough to run in twenty minutes at a revision pass, it must not assume formal training in aesthetics beyond what I have described, and it must be written to survive my editor, who will catch me if a scene cheats. For each scene, the checklist should tell me, in a fixed order, whether a character's verdict may be written as a competent critical judgment or must be written as mere preference or partiality; what evidence in the character's history or habits would count as satisfying each condition; where the procedure should stop and require me to rewrite the character's qualifications or reframe the verdict as bias; and where it should hand the decision back to me because the criteria run out and a legitimate disagreement remains. Mark every step at which my own taste cannot be eliminated. Then apply the procedure to two scenes I am drafting. In the first, an elderly restorer reveres a painter's late, loose canvases as profound, while a younger dealer who has studied the painter's early work and the period's rivals calls them derivative and lax; I want the restorer's reverence to be the wise voice in the novel, but the procedure may not reach that result. In the second, two critics who are both practiced, comparative, and apparently free of prejudice disagree about whether a young conceptual artist's installation is original or merely fashionable; here the procedure should show what it cannot settle. Be committed throughout: give the checklist a definite sequence, fixed thresholds, and fixed outputs rather than a survey of possibilities, and defend the choices.
