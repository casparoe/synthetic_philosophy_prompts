I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need help with a problem that has become central to the novel I am writing. The book follows a family whose members become entangled, one by one, in a sustained wrong none of them planned or personally carried out. The recurring question is what it takes for a person to be complicit — to share in another person's wrongdoing without being its origin or its principal cause. I have been sketching scenes and recording my own considered verdicts, and I find that my judgments follow a pattern I cannot yet state as a principle.

Please act as a moral philosopher who works on responsibility for the acts of others, and who is willing to tell me where my intuitions are inconsistent. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, test each against the cases below, and only then tell me which candidate or combination survives. I am after the moral relation of complicity, not the legal doctrine of any particular country. I do not need references or citations; I need the reasoning itself, stated precisely enough to be applied to new material.

Here are my considered verdicts on cases I have sketched.

1. Mara drives her cousin to the bank knowing he intends to rob it. He has his own car and is a better driver; he asks her along only for company. If she refused, he would have driven himself and the robbery would have gone ahead in exactly the same way. She drives, waits, and takes him home. Verdict: she is complicit in the robbery.

2. Dev keeps the books for a people-smuggling network. He never recruits anyone, never handles the money, and never makes operational decisions, but the network could not run without someone in his role, and he knows this. Verdict: he is complicit in the smuggling.

3. Ilsa runs a farm-supply shop and sells twenty sacks of fertiliser to a man who later uses them to make a bomb. She had no reason to suspect him. Verdict: she is not complicit.

4. Same shop, same purchaser, except that this time the man announces, half defiantly and half in jest, exactly what he intends to build. Ilsa is appalled. She also knows that if she refuses he will buy identical sacks from the shop fifteen minutes away, and the bomb will be built either way. She sells to him. Verdict: she is complicit, though less so than the man who builds the bomb.

5. Rhea co-founded a charity. She learns that its executive director is laundering money for a political faction. She votes against it, argues against it, loses, and then remains on the board for two years, attending meetings, saying nothing more, and lending the charity the respectability of her continued presence. Verdict: she is complicit in the laundering, though less so than those who run it.

6. Tomás inherits his father's estate and discovers that much of it was acquired through the forced eviction of tenant farmers forty years ago. He condemns what his father did, has never concealed it, and has done nothing since the inheritance except keep the money. Verdict: he is not complicit in the evictions, though he owes some repair to the victims or their descendants.

7. At a dinner, a friend makes a cruel and false claim about a mutual acquaintance. Nina laughs. She does not find it funny and privately regards it as slander; she laughs because she does not want to be seen as difficult. Her laugh changes no one's opinion, but the room takes it as her joining in the reception of the lie. Verdict: she is complicit in the slander, mildly.

8. Jonas watches a colleague pocket money from the till. A single word would have stopped the theft; he says nothing because he dreads the awkwardness. Verdict: he does wrong, but he is not complicit in the theft.

9. Amara buys a chocolate bar from a brand she knows uses trafficked child labour. The bar is already on the shelf, and production volumes were fixed long before her purchase; whether she buys changes nothing about any child's situation. She knows this. Verdict: I cannot shake the sense that she is complicit to a small degree in the standing practice, though not in any particular child's harm. This is the verdict I am least confident about.

I can see the trouble in my list: Tomás and Amara both stand downstream of a wrong without changing it, yet I call one complicit and the other not; Nina and Jonas both do nothing material, yet I call one complicit and the other not. I suspect there is a principle behind the differences, but I cannot state it.

Here is what I want from you.

First, state the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best captures the pattern of these nine verdicts. For each element of the principle, tell me which case or cases forced you to include it.

Second, apply your best candidate to two scenes I have not yet settled. (a) A doctor in a conflict zone treats a wounded fighter from a militia that has just carried out a massacre, knowing he will return to the militia and probably kill again; she knows another hospital would treat him if she refused. Is she complicit in his future killings, in the past massacre, both, or neither? (b) A notary notarises a property transfer she knows is a sham designed to launder money, while knowing the parties would simply use the notary next door if she refused. Is she complicit in the laundering?

Third, tell me plainly which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether that should make me revise the principle or abandon the verdict. I am prepared to be told that one or two of my intuitions do not survive. I am not prepared to accept a principle that has been gerrymandered with a new clause for each case, unless each clause can be shown to earn its keep beyond the case it was invented to save.

Finally, invent two further cases of your own that you expect will stress the principle, and tell me what it says about them and why.
