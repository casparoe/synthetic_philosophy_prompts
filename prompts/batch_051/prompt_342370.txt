Yesterday my friend and I ended up arguing late into the night after we’d both been rereading On Certainty. The thing that started it was section 152, where Wittgenstein says that the propositions that stand fast for us aren’t explicitly learned, but can be discovered afterward like the axis around which a body rotates: the axis is not fixed by anything holding it fast, but the movement around it determines its immobility. My friend thinks this effectively predicts what we now see in big language models trained only on text. He says a model never explicitly learns hinge propositions like there have been past generations or the world didn’t start when I was born as axioms, but something hinge-like shows up functionally in its behavior: these are the fixed points its predictions quietly rotate around, and you can discover them later by probing the model. I pushed back that a body’s axis belongs to the whole moving body in a physical practice, and that human hinge certainties are embedded in forms of life, not just token statistics. He said I’m smuggling in more praxis than the passage actually licenses. So my question: does the hinge analogy survive the existence of language models trained on text alone? Does section 152 actually predict a model would develop hinge-like certainties, or does the analogy break when you remove embodied practices? What has to be true for the analogy to apply beyond human forms of life, and which premise in his reading is under real pressure?
