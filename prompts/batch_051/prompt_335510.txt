I am an undergraduate research assistant in a lab that studies human–AI interaction, and I am currently taking a course on the philosophy of AI. The lab is preparing to field-test an LLM-based study advisor, and I have been asked to draft the protocol we will use to classify every user-flagged interaction as deceptive, merely false, misleading, or honestly uncertain. The lab's working definition says deception requires intentionally inducing a false belief. But our system has no beliefs or intentions in the ordinary sense, so on that definition it can never deceive anyone, which seems to exclude the very cases our ethics board cares about, such as the system inventing a paper and citing it with full confidence. Could you turn the philosophical debate over how to define honesty and deception for AI systems into a step-by-step decision procedure usable by a team without formal philosophical training and under time pressure? Please run the procedure on three cases: (1) the system fabricates a plausible-looking source and presents it as a real citation; (2) the system gives a confidently wrong answer to a factual question about the syllabus; and (3) the system gives the correct answer but adds a hedge that makes the student doubt an answer they already had right. Mark each point where a human must exercise judgment, and state explicitly where you expect the procedure to misclassify.
