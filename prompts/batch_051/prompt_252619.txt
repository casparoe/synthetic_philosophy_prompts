I have been rereading Fabre’s *The Hunting Wasps* with an eye toward contemporary discussions of habit, automaticity, and free action. Fabre describes the yellow-winged Sphex’s provisioning routine: the wasp drags her paralyzed cricket to the burrow entrance, leaves it there, enters the burrow briefly, reemerges, and then pulls the prey inside. If the experimenter moves the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not simply pull the prey in from its new position. She retrieves it, restores it to the threshold, leaves it again, and repeats the inspection—apparently as many as dozens of times in succession. Fabre’s gloss is that each step in the sequence is elicited by a particular stimulus, so the wasp cannot take the situation as a whole and correct only the part that has changed.

A colleague in my philosophy of action seminar has now turned this into an argument that I find disturbing because it seems to prove far more than anyone should accept. Put in numbered form, the argument runs as follows.

(1) The Sphex’s repeated resetting of the provisioning routine shows that her behavior is governed by a fixed motor sequence rather than by an updated representation of the end state she is trying to bring about.

(2) A capacity for free action requires at least this much: the agent’s movements must be modifiable in light of the agent’s current estimate of how the goal is best achieved.

(3) If an organism’s behavior is governed by a fixed motor sequence rather than by an updated representation of the goal, then the organism’s movements are not modifiable in that sense.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), the Sphex’s provisioning movements are not free.

(5) Human beings likewise possess large numbers of learned, automatized routines—driving a familiar route while talking, suturing a wound, playing a well-rehearsed passage on an instrument, saying the expected thing in a familiar social script. In such cases, as in Fabre’s wasp, a small change in circumstances often does not interrupt the sequence: the person continues the already-initiated routine unless some conspicuous cue forces a stop.

(6) By parity of reasoning, then, many ordinary skilled and habitual human actions are not free. The surgeon’s practiced movements, the driver’s habitual turn, and the musician’s overlearned fingering are no more free than the Sphex’s provisioning routine.

(7) But this conclusion is unacceptable. It would mean that wide stretches of competent adult life—precisely the actions we train for, rely on, and hold one another responsible for—are not free. And it would make the Sphex case cease to illuminate anything distinctive about insects, because the same argument would place human expertise on the wrong side of the line.

I would like a careful diagnosis. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise should be rejected, and what would that rejection cost us in how we talk about freedom, skill, and responsibility? If it is not valid, at which step does the reasoning slip, and is the slip an equivocation, an overgeneralization, a false contrast, or something else? What I most want to avoid is a cheap solution that saves human freedom while leaving Fabre’s observations untouched, or that saves the wasp’s rigidity while implausibly exempting human habits.
