I am a high school student in a history course that just finished a unit on whether history moves in cycles or in a straight line, covering Polybius, Ibn Khaldun, Spengler, and Toynbee. Our final assignment is a creative piece, and I have chosen to submit a poem. I would like you to write a villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets rhymed aba and a closing quatrain rhymed abaa, with two refrains that alternate and then come together in the final two lines. The poem should stage the debate between cyclical and linear views of history, and the form should do real conceptual work rather than merely decorate the content.

Please make the two refrains carry the two rival positions: one refrain should voice the cyclical thesis, that what rises falls and rises again; the other should voice the linear thesis, that history is going somewhere and does not simply return. Because the villanelle forces refrains to recur, the returning cyclical line should make the reader feel recurrence, while the tercets in between should themselves advance through the thinkers and examples, so that the reader experiences linear progression and circular return at the same time. In the final quatrain, bring the two refrains together without simply declaring a winner; I would prefer an image or reconciliation that lets the cycle and the arrow coexist, something like a wheel that turns while the cart moves forward.

For content, the poem should pass through, without mere name-dropping, at least three of the following positions. Polybius: the cycle of constitutions running from monarchy through tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, and mob rule and back to monarchy, together with his admiration for the mixed constitution as a brake on that cycle. Ibn Khaldun: dynasties held together by asabiyyah, the group solidarity that is strong in harsh conditions and decays in luxury over a few generations, so that each dynasty is eventually replaced by a fresher one. Spengler: cultures as organisms that are born, ripen, decline, and die, with civilization as their petrified final phase. Toynbee: growth through creative responses to challenges and decline through the failure of a creative minority to keep responding, with the important nuance that Toynbee is not a simple cycle theorist, since he allows for genuine novelty and renewal rather than fixed recurrence. If possible, also touch the linear side through Enlightenment progress, providential or eschatological history, or the idea that later history is not merely more of the same. Do not use quotations; everything should be in your own words.

Act as an expert poet with graduate-level knowledge of the philosophy of history. Before drafting, think carefully about how the villanelle's rhyme scheme and refrain placement can carry the argument; brainstorm two or three possible refrain pairs, and choose the one that lets the form do the most philosophical work. Then write the poem, and after it add a short note of three to five sentences explaining the structural choices you made.
