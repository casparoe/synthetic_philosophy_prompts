I follow a strict rule about what I may count as knowledge: I refuse to treat myself as knowing a proposition unless, at that moment, I am also in a position to know that I know it. In practice this means that before I rely on a claim or assert it to others, I pause and ask not only whether I am sure, but whether I can tell how I know that I am sure. If the second-order answer comes back blank or merely a feeling, I demote the original claim to a suspicion and refuse to act on it. The policy was born of experience. I have too often watched people make false, high-stakes assertions with serene confidence, and in my own worst errors I found the same pattern: I had been sure, and I had never once checked whether my being sure was itself something I was in a position to know.

Recently the rule has begun to fail in a new way. Last month, in a planning meeting, I was asked how sure I was about a migration plan for a legacy records system that I had helped design and test for weeks. I was sure of the plan in the sense that mattered — I could state its failure modes, its data dependencies, and why the alternatives were worse — but when I tried to run my usual second-order audit, the introspective signal refused to resolve. I could not locate a distinct feeling of knowing my own knowledge, as opposed to simply having the reasons and the skill. I qualified myself, the discussion drifted, and the team adopted a plan I still believe was inferior. When I explained my rule to a colleague afterwards, he said I was demanding a guarantee no amount of first-order competence could supply and that I know more than I know I know; worse, he suggested that the very act of auditing my state disturbs it, so that my test manufactures the failures it then punishes. I do not want to become the sort of person who treats every confident hunch as knowledge, but I am starting to suspect the rule may be taking knowledge away from me. Is the rule defensible as a standing policy, or does it rest on a mistaken picture of what it is to know that one knows? Under what conditions should I act on a well-grounded belief even when I cannot reflectively certify that I know it, and what would a repaired version of the rule look like?
