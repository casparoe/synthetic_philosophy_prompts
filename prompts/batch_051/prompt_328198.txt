I've been rereading Hume's Enquiry, Section IV, and trying to connect it to the no-free-lunch theorems in learning theory. Hume writes:

"This proposition, that causes and effects are discoverable, not by reason, but by experience, will readily be admitted with regard to such objects, as we remember to have once been altogether unknown to us; since we must be conscious of the utter inability, which we then lay under, of foretelling, what would arise from them."

Now, when I see how the no-free-lunch theorem gets used in machine learning, it sounds almost like a mathematical restatement of Hume: for any predictor or learning algorithm, if you average over every possible sequence of events, its predictions are no better than chance; there's no universal inductive advantage. But the machine-learning response is equally familiar—every real learner just restricts itself to a class of hypotheses and pays for it with bias. My question is what happens to Hume's argment if we treat the no-free-lunch result as a new development. Does the theorem strengthen Hume by turning his skeptical point into a provable limit, or does it actually weaken him by showing that induction was never supposed to work over all possible worlds? In other words, is Hume's problem of induction still the right way to frame the difficulty, or should we say the difficulty is now about justifying our choice of hypothesis class, which is maybe a different problem? I'm not asking for a survey of the theorems; I want the philosophical upshot.
