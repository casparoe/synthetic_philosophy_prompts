My philosophy club is running a unit on film as philosophy, and I have chosen to write my paper on Pixar's Inside Out read through Gilbert Ryle's critique of the ghost in the machine. As I understand Ryle, he thinks a familiar picture of mental life rests on a category mistake: it treats the mind as a non-physical thing located somewhere inside the body and steering it the way a driver steers a car, when in fact words like believing, remembering, and being angry are not names of hidden inner objects or episodes but ways of describing how a whole person is disposed to act, react, and carry on in a shared world. Inside Out appears to double down on exactly that picture. It shows an eleven-year-old girl, Riley, whose mind is run from a control room called Headquarters, where five personified emotions, Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, and Disgust, sit at a console, choose her actions, review her memories, and maintain the core memories that power her personality islands. When Joy and Sadness are lost in long-term memory and the console goes dark, Riley becomes emotionally flat and takes actions that are unlike her; when Sadness is finally allowed to handle the core memories, they become mixed in color and her personality is rebuilt around something more complicated than a single bright emotion. That looks like the film is knowingly working with, rather than simply asserting, the idea of an inner controller.

I do not want the paper to reduce the film to a cautionary example or to treat the film as philosophically naive. So my question is whether Inside Out is vulnerable to Ryle's objection at all. On one view, the film is a vivid example of the category mistake, because it literalizes the mind as a private place and represents mental goings-on as the doings of little inner agents. On another view, the film is a kind of commentary on those very metaphors, and its plot shows the limits of the control-room picture precisely by having the console fail and forcing the resolution to come through relationships, memory, and the acceptance of sadness rather than through a new master controller. I would like the paper to argue for whichever reading is stronger while honestly marking where the weaker reading still has force. Which scenes would you point me to on each side? And on your preferred reading, what does the film add to the philosophical debate that Ryle's argument alone does not, for instance about why the picture of an inner control room is so hard to give up, or about the role of emotion that a purely dispositional account of the mind might miss?
