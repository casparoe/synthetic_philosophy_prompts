I've been turning over a thought experiment about two coffee tasters, Chase and Sanborn, and I want to understand how a change to the setup affects the usual conclusion. In the original, both men have come to reject a coffee they once liked, but they describe the change differently. Chase says the taste is exactly what it always was, and he has simply stopped liking it. Sanborn says he still likes the same taste as before, but the coffee no longer produces that taste in him, so the experience itself has changed. From the outside the two cases look the same, and the skeptical conclusion is that nothing could settle whether one is reporting a changed judgment and the other a changed experience, since each man only has a current experience plus an unreliable memory to compare it with. That is supposed to make the whole idea of a private taste that can change independently of one's judgments look suspicious.

Now add this twist. Suppose we have a drug whose mechanism is well understood: it temporarily blocks the liking response in the brain while leaving the taste signals and the taster's ability to tell coffees apart intact. A third taster takes it and reports exactly what Chase reports — the taste is the same, but it no longer appeals to him. Here there seems to be evidence from outside the taster's head that only one of the two allegedly inseparable things changed. Does this dissolve the original worry, or does the worry carry over into the drug case? In particular, is the taster's claim that the taste is the same still just another judgment produced by the same machinery, so that the drug's known mechanism adds nothing? And if the drug case does have a fact of the matter the original lacks, what does that show about the original — was the skeptical conclusion only as strong as the lack of independent evidence, or was it aimed at a distinction that was empty all along?
