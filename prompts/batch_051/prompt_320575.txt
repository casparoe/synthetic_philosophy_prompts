I am a retired natural scientist now serving on the mental health ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and I am still fairly new to academic philosophy, so I would value reasoning that does not presuppose it. A patient with bipolar disorder, now thirty-two, drew up a psychiatric advance directive three years ago while stable. It states that if she enters a manic episode and refuses admission or medication—as she has in every previous episode—the hospital may admit and treat her even while she objects. She is in such an episode now and has asked the committee to set the directive aside. Her argument is not that she was incompetent when she signed it; it is that her present self never consented, and that the woman who signed was, in the respects that matter, a different person whose promise should not bind her. If we honour the directive, treatment would probably stabilise her within weeks; if we wait for the episode to resolve without treatment, it may last months and has previously cost her a job and a marriage, after which she has each time thanked the team for intervening. I recognise this as a small-scale case of constitutional precommitment, but the committee needs a decision about this patient, not an essay on constitutions. Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting: recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, engage her argument seriously rather than merely asserting that her earlier consent governs, and state candidly the strongest consideration on the losing side. I would like a firm, committed recommendation with its reasons, not a survey of options or a literature review. Please write in British English.
