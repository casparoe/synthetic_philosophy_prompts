I'm drafting a short statement of my views for a reading group on the idea, associated with Hofstadter, that a self is a pattern that can be distributed across many brains. I want a consistency check before I present it. Here are the commitments I find myself wanting to defend:

(1) A self is a particular kind of self-referential pattern of representational activity, with no nonphysical ingredient.
(2) The pattern that is me exists at different degrees of resolution: the highest-resolution version runs in my own brain, while much coarser versions run in the brains of people who know me well.
(3) Those coarser versions are not just memories of me or causal traces I left; they are literally parts of me, so I am already somewhat distributed across other minds.
(4) If I die and some of those coarse fragments keep running in others, I continue to exist in those fragments, and there is no sharp boundary at which this continuation stops being me.
(5) If a high-fidelity, substrate-independent emulation of my whole pattern were created at the moment of my death, that would preserve me in the survival-relevant sense — the sense that matters for my present prudential concern.
(6) My present prudential concern should track only the high-resolution, full-fidelity pattern, not the coarse fragments in my friends' heads; I should not sacrifice my own future for the sake of their persistence.

Are these six jointly tenable? Give me an audit rather than an explanation of each claim: which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, which pairs only appear to be in tension, and what the single cheapest revision would be to make the set coherent.
