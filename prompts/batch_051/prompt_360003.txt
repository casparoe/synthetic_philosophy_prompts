I keep thinking about a puzzle in the way we handle drugs, and I can't get it to line up. On one side, alcohol is legal, advertised, and sold in grocery stores even though it is tied to an enormous amount of violence and disease. Cannabis, after decades of being treated as a serious crime, is now legal where I live: there are licensed stores, taxes, and testing labels. But heroin and cocaine remain crimes, and my cousin, who has struggled with heroin for years, has been arrested twice for carrying amounts too small to sell. On the other side, as I understand it, Portugal stopped treating possession of any drug for personal use as a crime back in 2001. Someone caught with a small amount is sent to a panel called a dissuasion commission that can refer them to treatment, but selling the same substances is still illegal there. So one country will not punish my cousin for simple possession, yet still treats the substance he uses as contraband, while the country that arrests him lets me buy a different intoxicant at the supermarket.

A friend who volunteers with a needle-exchange program says most of the harm of heroin is a side effect of prohibition itself: adulterated supply, dirty needles, and the need to steal to pay inflated prices. To her, punishing users just adds a criminal record to what is really a medical problem. Another friend says decriminalization is not legalization. Portugal is not saying drug use is fine, he argues; it is saying criminal punishment is the wrong tool, whereas legalizing cannabis says selling and using it can be an ordinary regulated business. I do not understand what principled line runs through these policies. Is the hidden assumption that some substances are categorically more dangerous, or that some users are choosing freely while others are compelled, or that the state may discourage some pleasures but not others? Does the Portugal model presuppose that addiction is a disease rather than a moral failing? Does legal cannabis presuppose that the state has no business protecting adults from themselves, or only that it prefers taxes and regulation to a black market?

I want someone to work out what these arrangements actually presuppose. Reconstruct the implicit theory of freedom, harm, and responsibility behind each one, and be careful to distinguish what the policy strictly needs in order to be coherent from what it merely fits well with. Are these policies built on one consistent view, or are they patchworks of inconsistent ones? And what would have to change in practice if you replaced the underlying view with the idea that the only proper grounds for criminal law are to prevent people from harming others without their consent?
