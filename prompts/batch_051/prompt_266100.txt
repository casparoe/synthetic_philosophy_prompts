Over the past year I have drifted toward a particular view of 'societal collapse', partly from arguing with a flatmate who works on late antique cities and partly from reading around the usual cases—the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui. I have come to think the term does useful analytical work only when it is reserved for a relatively abrupt decline—over, say, a century or two—that is simultaneous across at least two of three registers: the reach and ambition of centralised political authority; the size and/or density of the human population; and the production of complex public goods such as monumental architecture, written administration, or long-distance exchange. If only one register declines, or if the change is slow and consists in power relocating rather than weakening, I now prefer to describe reorganisation, fragmentation, or simplification rather than collapse. On this criterion I am inclined to say that the ninth- and tenth-century CE southern Maya lowlands and the palace societies of the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE really do count as collapses in the relevant, restricted sense, while a good deal of what gets called collapse elsewhere does not.

What I would like from you is a genuine map of this position's exposure, not a rehearsal of the usual disputes. Tell me what would have to be true—in the archaeological record, in historical demography, or in our understanding of these specific cases—for my view to be wrong or unhelpful. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish between (a) evidence that would show my definition has misclassified a canonical case, (b) evidence that would show the three registers do not hang together in the way the definition assumes, and (c) evidence that would show the concept is not tracking anything causal at all but only packaging a narrative judgement. Include, explicitly, what a defender of my view ought to concede counts against it, not merely what a critic would say. I would personally be most moved by a well-documented case in which only one register declined sharply and yet specialists across the board treat it as a textbook collapse, or by evidence that the population register cannot be reconstructed well enough to do the work I have assigned it. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position has been insulated against refutation by its definitions—and if it has, whether that is a problem for a conceptual thesis of this kind rather than an empirical one. Throughout, keep clear which claims are settled among people who work on these cases and which are genuinely contested interpretations, and avoid presenting a contested interpretation as the consensus.
