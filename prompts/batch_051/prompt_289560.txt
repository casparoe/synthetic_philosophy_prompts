I'm a journalist who writes about the ideas baked into everyday institutions — city hall, courts, public notices, the fine print of civic life — and I keep hitting the same wall. Some law is still on the books, nobody can remember the last time it was enforced, and I have to decide before deadline whether to describe it to readers as "the law" (which makes it sound like it could come down on someone tomorrow), as "a dead letter," or as something murkier in between. An adultery statute from 1907. A sidewalk-chalk permit rule that hasn't been enforced in living memory. A fortune-telling ban a new police chief says he might dust off. I want a reusable procedure, not a one-off call, because this comes up every few months and getting it wrong either scares readers over a law that can't touch them or tells them they're safe when they aren't.

You're a philosopher of law who writes for practitioners rather than law-review referees. Brainstorm the relevant considerations first, then give me a decision procedure — a checklist or a short sequence of questions I can run in about fifteen minutes, with no legal training — for deciding how seriously to treat a law that's rarley or never enforced. Ground it in what philosophy of law actually says about desuetude, the rule of law, and the gap between a law being valid and a law being in force, but keep the theory in the background; I need the version I can actually use. Tell me what information I need at each step, what counts as enough of an answer, when the procedure says "stop, call it a dead letter," when it says "treat it as live," and where it hands the decision to me or an editor or a lawyer because it's a judgment call dressed up as a checklist. Then run it on two or three hard cases, including at least one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — say, a charming zombie law I wanted to write off as harmless turns out to be one elected prosecutor away from being used, or a law I assumed was live turns out to be deader than it looks. Be explicit about what the procedure can't settle, especially the point where whether a court may even let an unenforced law die is itself a live legal question. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the principle behind the procedure, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
