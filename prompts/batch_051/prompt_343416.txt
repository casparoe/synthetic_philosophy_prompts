I am a PhD student in decision theory, working on the norms governing repeated risky choice. I have been collecting my own settled judgements about how to size a sequence of favourable gambles, with the aim of discovering whether a single principle captures them. I would be grateful if you would take the lot, work out what principle (if any) unifies them, and tell me honestly which of my judgements it cannot accommodate.

Throughout, consider a coin biased towards heads, where a win pays even money (each £1 staked returns £2, i.e. £1 profit) and a loss loses the stake; the coin lands heads with probability 0.6. My bankroll is £100.

1. If the coin is to be flipped indefinitely and I may stake any fraction of my current bankroll each time, my settled view is that I should stake 20% of current bankroll on every flip. Betting a constant £20 forever, rather than compounding, feels obviously wrong; and I regard staking £40, or the whole £100, as reckless despite the positive expectation.

2. If the very same gamble is offered exactly once, I still want to stake 20% of my current bankroll, no more and no less. I do not accept that a one-off wager should be sized differently merely because there will be no future wagers; nor do I accept that the single opportunity licenses a larger stake to make the most of a favourable expectation.

3. If the same coin is offered exactly ten times and then never again, my verdict is unchanged: 20% of current wealth on each of the ten flips. I have no impulse to stake more on early flips or less on later ones.

4. Now change only the probability and the odds. Suppose a win pays 25p profit for each £1 staked, a loss loses the stake, and the chance of winning is 0.9. Here I notice something in me that I do not fully trust. My actual instinct is to stake about 70% of my bankroll, even though I recognise that the rule I used in the earlier cases, carried over mechanically, would place the stake nearer 50%. I report the instinct as my verdict, because it is what survives when I actually face the choice.

5. If two identical even-money versions of the 0.6 coin are offered on the very same flip, my instinct is that the two tickets do not add: my total exposure should remain 20%, not 40%. If I accept both, I would split the 20% between them, perhaps 10% on each. I see no case for treating two tickets on one outcome as two opportunities.

6. If, instead, two independent 0.6 coins are each available at even money simultaneously, I want each stake to be below my usual 20%, but their sum to remain materially above 20%. My instinct lands at about 15% on each coin, not 20% on each, and I am confident I should not simply treat them as two separate case-1 wagers.

7. Suppose I am told only that a coin is biased towards heads, but not how strongly: the bias is either 0.6 or 0.8, with no information about which, and the odds are even money on each flip. My instinct is to stake far less than I would on a known 0.6 coin: something like 5% of my bankroll for at least the first several flips. I distrust my edge until the data teach me what it is.

8. Last, a longshot. A ticket costs £1 and pays £200 in total (i.e. £199 net profit) with probability 0.01, and otherwise pays nothing. Per pound staked the expectation is strongly positive, and the growth-maximising stake would be about 0.5% of my £100, roughly 50p. My honest verdict is that I would not bother with the 50p, and I do not regard this as a mistake. The amounts are so small relative to my bankroll that the edge does not feel like a real opportunity.

Is there one principle, stated precisely, with what it treats as the quantity to be maximised and how it handles simultaneous bets, that captures all, or nearly all, of these verdicts? For each judgement that the principle cannot capture, say whether you think the judgement should be revised or whether the principle must be made more complex in order to keep it. Finally, use the principle you favour to issue a verdict on a case I have not listed: three independent 0.6 coins, each offered at even money, simultaneously. Please write your answer in British English.
