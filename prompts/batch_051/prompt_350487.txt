Please argue, with a definite and committed position, that the standard moral drawn from Simpson's paradox — that when an overall association and the corresponding within-group associations conflict, the disaggregated numbers are the ones to trust — is not generally correct. The correct lesson, which you should defend, is that no purely statistical rule settles whether the aggregated or the disaggregated table is the right one; the answer depends on the causal structure, and in particular on what would happen under an intervention. To make the argument concrete, include at least one numerical example in which the reversal occurs, and explain the mechanism that produces it. Then show why the advice to always disaggregate fails: discuss a structure in which the grouping variable is a common cause of the other two variables, and a structure in which it is a common effect of them, and say which of the two associations is the better guide in each. Conclude with a single, definite statement of what someone should conclude upon encountering Simpson's paradox in a real dataset, and be explicit about what information beyond the two-way tables would be needed before that conclusion is warranted.
