I'm a novelist working on a book set in a small fishing town, and I keep going back and forth on wether shame can be a genuine moral achievement or is just a cheap substitute for conscience. For months I've had this passage from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, book four, chapter nine, stuck in my head. He says there that shame is not a virtue, but is more like a feeling than a state of character; he calls it a kind of fear of disrepute, and he says that while we praise a young person for having a sense of shame, we would not praise an older person for it, because a mature person should not do the things that occasion shame. So the criteria I've extracted for a virtue are: it has to be a settled state of character rather than a feeling, it has to be chosen for the sake of the noble, and it has to make its possessor good at acting. Shame seems to fail the first because it's a feeling, and the second because its object is avoiding bad reputation.

Now here's my case. In the novel, the matriarch, Rosa, was raised by a father who deliberately used shame as pedagogy—not humiliation, but a constant quiet awareness that every action is seen and will be judged. As an adult, Rosa has a permanent background orientation to visibility. She never waits for an actual audience, because she has internalized one. She doesn't do wrong things, but the reason she doesn't is that wrongdoing would feel like exposure even in private. It isn't a sharp fear in the moment; it's a settled way of being, with the same stability you'd expect from courage or temperance. She is reliable, self-controlled, and her actions are good. Is this still Aristotle's shame, or has it turned into something else? Does it qualify as a virtue on his criteria, or does it just look like one because it's been trained to be quiet and steady?

Please answer in four sections: First, my paraphrase and any place I may be misreading Aristotle (about 100 words); Second, criterion-by-criterion, checking Rosa against each criterion I listed, and saying whether the criterion gives a clear yes, a clear no, or an uncertain verdict (about 400 words); Third, where you think she lands overall and wether she counts as having the virtue or not (about 300 words); Fourth, whether her case shows that the feeling-versus-character-state criterion needs revision, or whether the real issue is the object of the fear, and if so how you would phrase the changed criterion (about 200 words).
