I have been vegan for almost a decade, but I recently moved from a large wealthy city to a more remote town in a developing country for a multi-year work posting, and for the first time the practice of veganism has stopped being an easy daily habit and started being a genuine ethical problem. I would like you to help me work out a definition of what it is for a person to be vegan — or of veganism as a practice, if you find that the more useful object — that can survive contact with situations where the alternatives are scarce, expensive, unreliable, or socially costly. Please do not give me references or suggest further reading, and please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a careful letter.

The background to my question is that most formulations of veganism I know include some caveat about avoiding animal use only to the extent that this is actually possible and practicable in one's circumstances, and I worry that my old working definition quietly assumed the food environment of a wealthy city: abundant plant alternatives, predictable labels, and enough money and time to choose freely. I want you to propose a definition that makes that caveat precise rather than using it as an escape hatch, and then run the definition through the following cases, telling me where it gives an awkward or surprising verdict and either revising it or defending the verdict.

The cases are these. A mother in a food desert buys cow's milk for her young child because no fortified plant milk is stocked within reach and she cannot reliably provide the child's calories, fat, and calcium at a price she can afford; I am inclined to say she is still vegan. A subsistence farming family in a semi-arid region keeps several goats for milk and eventually for meat because the land cannot produce enough plant food to keep the family fed; this strains my intuitions badly. A mostly plant-based person accepts dishes made with fish sauce or egg when visiting relatives, because the food has already been prepared and refusing it would damage the relationship; I am genuinely unsure. Someone buys second-hand leather shoes because locally available synthetic shoes fall apart quickly and importing durable vegan footwear is expensive and unreliable; I think this is vegan, but several old friends of mine do not. A person takes a necessary medication or vaccine that contains animal-derived ingredients or was tested on animals; I think this is clearly compatible with being vegan, but I want the definition to say why without making the prohibition on animal use collapse. Someone eats oysters and mussels on the grounds that they are probably not sentient and that farming them does little harm; this feels to me like a different kind of question than milk or eggs. Someone hunts and eats invasive feral hogs to reduce ecological damage when the only shop-bought protein available is factory-farmed chicken; I do not know how an animal-rights understanding and an ecological understanding of veganism are supposed to be weighed against each other here. And finally, a person in a genuine survival situation eats animal flesh to stay alive; I suspect I want to distinguish the person's commitment from the particular action, but I am not sure the definition can bear that distinction.

Please propose the definition, apply it to each of the eight cases in order, say explicitly which of the verdicts you are least confident in, and tell me whether the definition survives as stated or needs to be revised. If it needs revision, give me the revised version and run it through the cases where the first version failed.
