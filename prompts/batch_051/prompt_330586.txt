I've been trying to get my head around Ian Hacking's looping effect for a piece, and I keep hitting a snag I'd like help with. As I understand it, his claim is that classifying people isn't like classifying rocks: when people learn how they've been classified, they respond to that label—they inhabit it, resist it, perform it, organize around it—and that changes the kind of person the label picks out, which eventually forces the classification itself to shift. That made a lot of sense for mid-century psychiatry, where someone gets a diagnosis, reads about it, talks to their doctor, and the category starts moving.

But here's the snag. A growing share of behavioral and mental-health classification now happens algorithmically and invisibly: a model scores someone's social media or purchase history or movement data, assigns some risk or propensity category, and that category feeds into a decision or an ad or a triage queue without the person ever being told "you have been classified as X." The classified person isn't in the loop in the way Hacking seems to need. So does his loop still happen, or does invisibility break it? If it breaks, what should we call whats left—a classification that acts on people but never gets challenged or inhabited? And if you think the loop survives even here, tell me exactly what does the looping once the person's awareness is out of the picture.
