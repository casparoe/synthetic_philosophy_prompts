Not a philosopher — I write software for a living — but I've been chewing on the existentialist slogan existence precedes essence, mostly as Sartre lays it out. As best I can tell it means: there's no fixed human nature or cosmic job description; you show up existing first, and then you have to make yourself into something through your choices. Thats the part I like.

The part I keep getting stuck on is the charge that the whole thing is incoherent. I've been collecting the standard objections, and here's my list:

1. The self-undermining-essence objection: saying humans have no fixed nature is itself a claim about what humans are like, so the view secretly asserts an essence while denying essences exist.
2. The no-standard objection: if there's no human nature telling you what to choose, no choice is better or worse than any other, so existentialism can't say anything about how you ought to live — but then it turns around and says you should choose authentically and own your freedom, which sneaks a standard back in.
3. The facticity objection: you don't choose your body, your birthplace, your era, your language, your upbringing. A huge amount of what you are is fixed before you ever make a choice, so existence precedes essence is at best only half true.
4. The universality objection: Sartre says choosing for yourself means choosing for all of humanity, and he treats freedom as something we're all stuck with. But if being free is a universal feature of the human condition, that sure sounds like an essence.

I have a feeling these four cover the usual moves. What I want is a genuinely different objection to existence precedes essence — not a reworded version of one of these, not a special case of one of them, and not some mashup of two. Bonus points if it doesn't just amount to "you've got an essence after all." Idealy I'd also like to know whether the objection you come up with is actually any good, or just clever-sounding.
