Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over. If a dog is slowly dying of a treatable infection and its owner lets it be, we prosecute the owner for cruelty or at least for neglect—the owner had a duty to intervene and failed it. But if a hiker comes across a deer dying of the same sort of infection, and just walks on, almost no one thinks the hiker did anything wrong; at most we'd say it would be nice of her to call a ranger. The deer's pain isn't any less real, and the hiker is at least as able to help as the dog's owner is. But the two cases are treated like they're morally different in kind, not just degree. I get that one suffering has a human causal origin and the other doesn't, but I don't see why that alone should mark the line between "culpable failure to help" and "not your business." So: is there a defensible principle that puts these two cases on different sides, and does it survive being pushed? Try candidate principles—the causing/allowing distinction, a duty not to interfere with nature, the idea that we only owe duties to creatures we've taken responsibility for, or something about the value of natural processes—and test them against nearby cases: the hiker who could cheaply cure the deer, a ranger deciding whether to treat one animal or let the infection run, a person who finds a wounded bird in her yard, a wildlife agency that already culls deer but won't treat the sick ones. If no principle holds up, is the original asymmetry something we should try to train ourselves out of, or should we instead revise the dog-case intuition to match the deer-case? I want a verdict on which direction to move, not just a catalog of possibilities.
