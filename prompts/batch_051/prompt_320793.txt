In anthropic reasoning the debate between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption has run for decades without converging, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. SSA says one should reason as if one were randomly sampled from the set of all observers in one's reference class; applied to one's birth rank among humans, this underwrites the Doomsday argument, because one is more likely to be roughly typical among all humans who ever live, which favors the hypothesis that few will live after one. SIA points the other way: one's existence is itself evidence that the world contains more observers rather than fewer, so theories predicting vast numbers of observers get a boost. That same move is what makes SIA look presumptuous to its critics: between two theories that agree on all ordinary evidence and differ only in that one predicts a trillion observers and the other a billion, SIA says one should be about a thousand times more confident in the first, though the difference is unobservable. Careful people on both sides grant the formal moves and yet nobody shifts, so I doubt it is a simple mistake on one side. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about what self-locating evidence may do: whether one's own existence can count as evidence, and—if it can—how to pick the reference class the sampling reasoning presupposes. Is that diagnosis right? Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one with a hidden formal answer, a clash of bedrock intuitions about which thought experiments count as data, or an ill-posed question—and what, if anything, would actually move it?
