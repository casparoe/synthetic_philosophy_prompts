I've been teaching vagueness for a while and I still can't settle what I actually think, so let me put the mess in front of you. I'm genuinely drawn to epistemicism. It keeps logic classical, it keeps bivalence, and it treats vagueness as a kind of ignorance rather than a flaw in the world or in meaning. On that picture there really is a last red tile in the long red-to-orange sequence, and the next one is not red; I can't know where it is, and couldn't in principle, but there is a fact. That's clean. It also explains why every cutoff I try feels arbitrary: the feeling of arbitrariness is just what my ignorance feels like from the inside.

But I cannot make myself beleive it. When I look at tile 50,000, indistinguishable from tile 49,999, I can't get myself to think there's a fact of the matter about whether it's red, let alone that the fact flips exactly between two indistinguishable tiles. I'm a supervaluationist by sympathy exactly because it says: no sharp boundary in the object language. A sentence is true if true on every admissible precisification, false if false on all of them, and otherwise neither. And that seems to track the phenomenon. But then I remember the old worry: define 'definitely red' as true on every precisification. There will be a last tile that is definitely red and a first that isn't, and there's a sharp boundary there. The supervaluationist says that boundary is not in the object language, but it's still in the space of precisifications or in the metalanguage, and I don't see why that's supposed to be less objectionable. Iterate the operator and you either get a hierarchy whose top has a sharp boundary anyway, or a non-terminating hierarchy that never finishes giving truth conditions.

So I have a theory with real systematic virtues that offends my firmest semantic intuition, and a theory that respects the intuition but appears to relocate the sharp boundary rather than eliminate it and asks me to accept a non-classical logic as the price. I don't want you to just tell me which side wins. I want the strongest case for each move: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and would a debunking story for it have to show that it's an artefact of our limited discrimination rather than evidence about meaning; what would it take for me to stick with supervaluationism and accept the hierarchy; and is there a stable reading on which my intuition and classical logic both survive, perhaps by distinguishing semantic from epistemic indeterminacy, or by saying my no-boundary intuition is really an intuition about assertion, about when I'd be entitled to assert the sentence, rather than about truth. Then tell me which way you'd actually lean and why.
