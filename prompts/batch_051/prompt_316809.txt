I keep noticing a pattern in ordinary moral talk that I do not have a word for. Take tipping in the United States. Most people will readily admit that the exact percentage is arbitrary, that the practice is local rather than something that follows from the nature of restaurant work, and that the same percentage would be pointless or even insulting elsewhere. Yet when a visitor tips far below the local rate, people do not describe this as a mere breach of etiquette or a social slip; they describe it as exploiting the server — as morally wrong, not simply unfamiliar. The same seems true of queue-jumping where queuing is strongly felt: what is openly admitted to be a local way of coordinating waiting is met with indignation, blame, and the language of wronging the people in line, not merely embarrassing them. Is there an established name for this transformation, in which a practice acknowledged to be arbitrary, local, and learned comes to be enforced through distinctively moral attitudes while remaining local in scope? I am not mainly after the label; I want the carving. If the nearest concepts are things like etiquette, social norms, or the idea that moral duties are themselves a kind of custom, say precisely how this phenomenon differs from each and which fits best; if no established concept fits, propose a name and define it, with attention to the boundary cases where the moral charge persists and where it dissolves. Please answer in continuous prose only, the way you would write an email or a letter; no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
