Consider a Protestant denomination firmly committed to sola scriptura, understood as the claim that scripture alone is the final and infallible rule of faith and practice, and that no council, creed, bishop, or scholarly guild has authority to bind conscience beyond what scripture itself teaches. The denomination continually faces a familiar difficulty: once scripture is made the sole authority, every disagreement about what scripture means threatens to become a disagreement about who may settle what it means, and any human settlement seems to resemble the very magisterium the principle was meant to exclude. To let the text itself function as the decider, the denomination adopts the following Concordance Process.

1. Trigger. A doctrinal question becomes live when a petition is signed by two percent of the membership or by three local congregations. The petition must pose the question as a single yes-or-no proposition: whether a specified doctrine is sufficiently grounded in scripture that it may be taught as binding doctrine.

2. The Panel. Twelve panelists are drawn by lot from the full membership roll. Excluded are members who have ever held a teaching or preaching office, been employed by the denomination, published any work of biblical or theological interpretation, or earned an advanced degree in theology or biblical studies. Those drawn may refuse only for severe hardship, as determined by an independent overseer. Panelists are forbidden, until after the final vote, to identify themselves publicly as panelists or to say which brief is theirs.

3. Briefs. Each panelist receives the relevant passages in the original languages and in the denomination's single authorized translation, along with grammatical and lexical aids. They are instructed to argue from scripture alone: no commentaries, creeds, councils, decisions of prior panels, or appeals to tradition. During the forty days of drafting they are sequestered from one another and from all outside discussion of the question; their briefs are filed under an anonymous code.

4. Publication and deliberation. Once filed, all twelve briefs are published simultaneously to the entire membership. The panelists then deliberate in closed session for ten days, after which an edited transcript is released. They vote by secret ballot. A doctrine may be declared sufficiently scriptural only if at least ten of the twelve affirm it; each dissenter may attach a short dissenting brief to the published finding.

5. Effect and duration. For twenty-five years, every officer, seminary instructor, and licensed preacher must either affirm the finding when the question arises, or remain silent on it in public teaching and pastoral counseling. Public contradiction is not grounds for loss of membership, but is grounds for removal from any teaching or preaching office. After twenty-five years the finding lapses; reopening the same question requires a new petition signed by six percent of the membership or by nine congregations.

Treat this as a proposed mechanism rather than a theological position. Analyze how it would actually behave. In particular: What would it incentivize among ordinary members, among clergy and candidates for ministry, and among panelists? How would sincere but strategic actors game each stage—the petition, the sortition, the drafting, the deliberation, the vote, and the sunset rule? Where in or around the process does the regress of interpretation reappear: the boundary of the canon, the choice of text and translation, the license to use lexical aids, the rules of exegesis, the framing of the question, the bindingness of a past decision, or elsewhere? Is the regress stopped at any point, or merely relocated? Finally, what second-order effects on the denomination's theology, factional life, and epistemic culture would accumulate over several twenty-five-year cycles, and which of the mechanism's failure modes are fixable without smuggling back in an authoritative interpreter?
