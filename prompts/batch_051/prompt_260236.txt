My partner and I recently began IVF with preimplantation genetic testing because a serious monogenic condition runs in my family, and I have been unable to stop thinking about an asymmetry in how two halves of the same practice are treated. Selecting among our embryos to avoid that condition is presented to us as responsible preventive medicine; almost everyone we tell approves. But the same clinic treats selecting among embryos for any non-disease characteristic — sex, for example, or ranking the possible children by their expected overall quality of life — as a different kind of act altogether, one it will not facilitate, and more than one friend has reached for the word eugenics. I am not asking for advice about what to do with our embryos. I am asking the prior question: is this asymmetry defensible, or does it rest on a distinction we cannot actually draw?

The principle I keep testing it against is procreative beneficence, roughly: prospective parents should select, among the possible children they could have, the one expected to have the best life, or at least a life no worse than the alternatives, based on the relevant available information. The broader view that such selection is permissible rather than objectionable is what I have seen called liberal eugenics. That principle seems to treat selection against disease and selection for traits as continuous — both are choices about which possible child to bring about, made on the basis of expected wellbeing. If the principle is right, the asymmetry in ordinary judgment and clinical practice needs a justification it has not yet received; if the principle is wrong, I need to know exactly where it goes wrong.

Please do the following in prose. First, state the asymmetry precisely. Then find the strongest candidate principle that could justify treating selection against disease differently from selection for non-disease traits; consider at least the distinction between preventing a shortfall and providing an enhancement, the idea that one selection respects a natural baseline while the other is a form of design, the thought that only disease avoidance is constrained by beneficence toward the child itself, obligations to existing people with the condition or with other disabilities, and downstream social or equality grounds. Test each candidate against nearby cases, including one in which a disease and a non-disease trait shift expected wellbeing by comparable amounts, one in which a parent selects for a trait classified as an enhancement partly because it reduces later disease risk, and one in which a parent selects a non-disease trait solely because they judge it likely to give the child the best life. Then give a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and be explicit about whatever remains unresolved.

After the main analysis, add a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- strongest_justification: the best candidate principle you found, named and stated in one or two sentences.
- test_cases: an array of objects, each with the keys case and outcome, where case names the nearby case and outcome says whether the justification handles it.
