I spent thirty years in physics before I retired and only started reading philosophy after. Next month I'm giving a talk at my old department's theory lunch — mostly physicists plus a couple of applied mathematicians, all of them perfectly comfortable using Bayesian statistics as a tool but allergic to the word "epistemology." My thesis is that the rules of probability are not just a convenient calculus for handling uncertainty; they are the logic of plausible reasoning. I'll defend the Jaynes-line reading of Cox's theorem: any scheme for assigning degrees of plausibility to propositions that respects qualitative common sense and isn't blatantly inconsistent has to obey the product and sum rules, so probability is in a precise sense the unique extension of two-valued logic to reasoning under uncertainty. My argument leans hard on the associativity of conjunction — the move that turns "and" into a functional equation whose only sane solutions are the product rule — and I'll claim that's what makes Cox more than a repackaging of Kolmogorov's axioms.

I know I'll get pushback, but I dont have a good feel for what will actually be said in that room, as opposed to the objections I've read in philosophy papers, which these people wont phrase that way. They will not let me get away with "surely this is reasonable." The room is friendly but technically brutal, and I'll have about forty minutes of talk before questions.

Prepare me for the Q&A. What will this audience object to, in their vocabulary? Which objections have real force and which are just disciplinary reflexes? What should I concede up front? Where is my own argument softest? And what is the question I should hope nobody asks but prepare for anyway?
