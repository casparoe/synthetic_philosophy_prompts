Question: Comparative studies often report that residents of the Nordic countries express high interpersonal trust and high trust in government, while residents of the United States express lower interpersonal trust and comparatively persistent suspicion of government. Assess whether the Nordic attitude of trust in government is more reasonable than the American attitude of suspicion.

Student's answer: The distinction between interpersonal trust and institutional trust matters for this question, and the question is about institutional trust rather than trust in strangers. The Nordic attitude is the more reasonable one. In the Nordic countries, corruption is low, the welfare state is comprehensive, and participation in associations, parties, and unions is high, so citizens face a government that delivers concrete benefits and is scrutinized by a dense civil society. Trusting an institution that has repeatedly shown itself to be competent and transparent is rational, just as distrusting an institution that has repeatedly broken its promises is rational. Because Nordic citizens also display high interpersonal trust, they can extend this default stance to the state and thereby avoid the monitoring, auditing, and litigation costs that constant suspicion imposes. American suspicion, by contrast, appears to be largely a holdover from the founding era's fear of concentrated power; it persists independently of how well the current government actually performs, so it operates as a blanket attitude rather than a calibrated judgment. The cross-national pattern in which high-trust countries also have low corruption indicates that trust is the enabling condition rather than merely a byproduct: when citizens let their guard down, officials rise to meet that expectation, and low corruption follows. The Nordic attitude is therefore more reasonable, although it would be unreasonable to keep trusting an institution that had stopped earning that trust.
