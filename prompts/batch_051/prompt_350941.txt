I've ended up on our faculty's course review panel this year, and it's starting to do my head in. For any new module, the proposer has to fill in a form that asks which employers want the graduates, what transferable skills the module builds, and how it prepares students for 'the future of work' – actual tick-boxes. The dean has told us flat out that no proposal gets through without a credible line to the labour market. But at the same time the university quietly keeps a tiny Old Frisian programme going, and every few years when its numbers fall bellow the viability threshold, senate waives the threshold and calls the subject 'heritage' or says preservation is just 'what a university is for'. Nobody on the panel actually believes the employability story behind most of the new modules, but nobody can say what the real criterion is either.

My question isn't really about the paperwork. What view of the value of scholarship does a two-track practice like that presuppose? Are the two tracks presupposing two rival views of what a course is for, or can they be shown to hang together as one coherent view? And if we had to make the whole thing consistent – either every field has to show usefulness, or none does – what would we have to give up? I'd appreciate it if you could answer in British English, and don't worry too much about the administrative details.
