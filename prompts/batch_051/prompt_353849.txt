I'm a science and ideas journalist drafting a long-form essay about how we classify famines, and I'd like a rigorous critical read of my working draft before I develop it further. The essay's target is the familiar question, Are famines natural disasters or political crimes? I want to argue that this question is misleading because it fuses two distinct judgments—what caused the starvation and who, if anyone, is blameworthy—and that clearer thinking comes from pulling those judgments apart. My concern is that this move won't be worth making unless it changes how we read the standard cases. Below is the skeleton of the piece. I'd be grateful for comments on the argument and on the historical claims, section by section.

Working thesis: The natural-or-political framing confuses causal origin with culpability. A famine can be natural in origin and political in aggravation; it can be political in origin without being criminal in the sense the polemical phrase intends. Ireland in 1845–52, Bengal in 1943, and the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–61 are useful because they spread across exactly this space.

Section 1, the picture from Sen: Amartya Sen's entitlement approach is usually read as showing that famine can occur without an aggregate decline in food availability, with Bengal in 1943 as the showcase case. On this reading, wartime inflation moved rice out of reach of landless rural laborers whose incomes did not keep pace, so the crisis ran through purchasing power and legal access rather than through an absolute shortage of grain. I want to draw a further moral out of this: the causal trigger and the moral trigger begin to come apart here, because the starvation mechanism was embedded in the market and administrative order even though no one simply withheld a harvest.

Section 2, Ireland: The potato blight was a natural trigger, but no serious account treats the blight alone as sufficient for the death toll. Food continued to leave the country during the disaster, and relief was constrained by a laissez-faire ideology and a Malthusian sense of the proper limits of state action. The complication I want to keep visible is that an effective counterfactual rescue would have required not just the will to intervene but an administrative capacity to override property and trade arrangements that the mid-nineteenth-century British state could not easily improvise. That does not excuse the policy; it moves the charge from simple crime toward something like institutionalized negligence shaped by ideology.

Section 3, the Great Leap Forward: Here the causal story is overtly political from early on. Inflated reporting of harvests produced impossibly high procurement targets, labor was diverted to backyard steel production, and rural communities were left without reserves. But the deepest puzzle is epistemic: the information system that could have revealed the catastrophe was broken in ways the leadership's own commitments required, since bad news could not travel upward. So we have political causation at maximum strength while deliberate, individual killing is diffuse. Calling it a crime is right but incomplete; the more precise charge is that a state can kill through its categories, targets, and reporting channels as efficiently as through orders.

Section 4, the two axes: The causal axis runs from natural trigger through entitlement collapse and administrative failure to deliberate extraction. The culpability axis runs through foreseeability, available alternatives, knowledge, and intent. A famine earns the label natural disaster only when the causal chain bottoms out in physical events and no institution could reasonably have prevented the deaths. It earns political crime when the deaths were foreseeable and avoidable through choices the state could have made. Bengal is mostly a market and administrative breakdown, with culpability located in neglect and indifference rather than design. Ireland is natural in origin, with culpability running through ideology and omission. The Great Leap Forward is political in both origin and amplification, with culpability spread across a system. Ordering them on the old binary makes no sense; placing them on the two axes makes the differences explicit.
