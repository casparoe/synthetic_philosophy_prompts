I've been going down a rabbit hole on the Fermi paradox and the great filter, and I think the whole debate is set up wrong. People talk about the filter as if its one thing with one probability you can stick into the Drake equation. But I think there are two kinds of steps, which I've been calling hard filters and soft filters. A hard filter is a step that fails at roughly the same rate everywhere because the physics or chemistry makes it genuinely improbable — like the jump from non-life to life, if it turns out to need one very specific self-copying molecule. A soft filter is a step whose failure rate depends on the local history of each planet, so it doesnt have a single probability at all. Intelligence feels like a soft filter to me: on Earth it seems to have needed a pile of flukey accidents (snowball Earth episodes, the right body plan, the asteroid), and a planet with the same starting chemistry could go a totally different way. My claim is that only hard filters can explain the silence. Soft filters don't multiply into a clean number of civilizations, so the Drake equation is asking for a number that isn't there — you get a smear of possibilities instead of an answer. And if most of the filters people argue about (intelligence, technology, longevity) are soft, then the whole "where does the great filter sit" debate is misframed. Is the hard/soft distinction real, or am I just smuggling in something like free will for planets? Has someone made this point before under a better name? And the thing I can't get around: even if a soft filter has no single probability, couldn't you just average over all the possible histories of all planets and get an expected number of civilizations anyway, which is all the Drake equation ever wanted? Tell me honestly where this breaks.
