I am a philosophy professor, but political philosophy is not my primary area, and I have been asked to give an undergraduate lecture on distributive ethics. While preparing, I came across the following summary of prioritarianism in a colleague’s lecture notes, and I would like a careful assessment of whether it is fair. The notes say:

‘Prioritarianism is the view that, although inequality is not bad in itself, the strength of our reason to benefit a person depends on how well off that person is in absolute terms: the worse off someone is, the more moral weight a given benefit to them has. The view is meant to capture the appealing thought that a hundred pounds matters more to a poor person than to a rich person, while avoiding the notorious levelling-down objection to egalitarianism. Since the prioritarian’s concern is with absolute levels of well-being rather than comparative standing, the view implies that taking resources from the better off without improving anyone’s condition—levelling down—is not an improvement. But neither is it, on balance, a worsening, since the only person made worse off is someone whose well-being already has comparatively little moral weight.’

Most of this strikes me as plausible, but the final clause unsettles me: it appears to slide from the claim that the better off count for less to the claim that they count for nothing. I would be grateful if you would go through the summary sentence by sentence, separating what a careful prioritarian would accept, what requires qualification, and what is simply wrong. Does the conclusion about levelling down follow from the accurate parts? And does prioritarianism genuinely escape the levelling-down objection, or does it merely relocate the problem? I would appreciate it if the answer were written in British English.
