I'm a novelist, working on a near-future book about a woman who sits on a military autonomy review board — the panel that signs off on whether lethal AI systems can be fielded. I've got a file of cases she's voted on, and I need her to rule on a new one for a hearing scene. I want the decision to be the kind of thing a smart, careful person in her position could actually defend, not just a posture.

Her record so far:

- A loitering munition that orbits a broad contested area and automatically attacks anything matching an enemy air-defense radar signature. No human in the loop per target. She voted no.
- A shipboard close-in weapon system that auto-engages inbound anti-ship missiles, on the grounds that a human physically can't react fast enough. She voted yes.
- A ground drone with a remote weapon station where a human operator picks and authorizes every target; the autonomy is basically just driving and stabilization. She voted yes.
- A counter-battery system that detects incoming rockets, computes their point of origin, and automatically returns fire at that grid square. A human supervisor gets a veto window of a few seconds, but in practice it's usually to late. She voted no.

The new proposal is a small loitering munition for a specific stretch of desert road with no civilian traffic expected, declared a daytime kill box. It's fully autonomous: it picks out and strikes military supply trucks matching a particular thermal and visual signature, with no per-target human sign-off. A human commander authorizes the whole operation for a two-hour window inside a geofenced box and can abort the mission at any point, but isn't on the loop target by target.

What should she decide if she wants to stay consistant with those four votes? Work through the candidate rules that could explain her record, say which earlier case is the closest analog and why, and be honest about whether the record actually forces one answer. If it's genuinely underdetermined, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each possible ruling on the new case, and describe the further case that would cleanly separate the two. I don't need citations or sources here — I need the underlying distinctions (timing, per-target vs. mission-level authorization, accountability, predictability, whether anti-materiel matters, whatever they turn out to be) to actually do the work, not just the phrase meaningful human control.
