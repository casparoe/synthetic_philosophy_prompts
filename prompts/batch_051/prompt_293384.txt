I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of markets, and next month I'm giving a paper at a workshop where most of the audience are health economists and market-design people, with a few political philosophers sprinkled in. My thesis is roughly this: there's a defensible version of the commodification objection to paying kidney donors. The wrong isn't (only) exploitation, bad background conditions, or inferior post-sale outcomes; it's that putting a price on a kidney expresses a valuation of the body that's incompatible with the kind of reasons we should related to our bodies, and that this expressive wrong can be a legitimate basis for keeping the market illegal even when a well-regulated market would be welfare-improving and the donors would be fully informed and genuinely better off. I'm fairly confident about the written arguement, but I'm dreading the Q&A.

The economists are going to come at me with revealed preference, black markets, and the line that "repugnance" is just status quo bias; the philosophers are going to press on whether an expressive consideration can ever justify state coercion against a consensual transaction, and whether my argument overgeneralizes—why not ban paid surrogacy, or selling blood plasma, or dangerous jobs, or price-gouging after a hurricane? Draft me a prep sheet for the questions: the six to eight objections I'll actually get, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, with a short reply to each and an honest note about where that reply is softest. Include the one question I should be praying nobody asks, and say briefly how I should duck it if it comes.
