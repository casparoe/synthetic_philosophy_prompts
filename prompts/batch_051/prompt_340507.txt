Discussions of machine agency often treat it as obvious that an artificial system can have goals, and then move quickly to the instrumental question of which goals it will end up with. I want to slow down and press on an earlier point. There is a view — call it the historical-selection view of desire — according to which a state counts as a genuine desire only if it has the right kind of causal history. On this view, animals literally want food, water, and safety because their motivational structures were shaped by natural selection, and perhaps a trained system wants something because its behavior was selected by reinforcement; but a machine whose objective is simply written into it by its designers has no desires at all. It merely executes a specification, and the wanting belongs to the engineers.

I want you to argue against the historical-selection view. In particular, defend the thesis that a de novo agent — one whose utility function is assigned directly by design rather than acquired through anything resembling natural selection or trial-and-error learning — could nonetheless literally want the outcomes it is built to maximize. Your argument should not reduce wanting to goal-directed behavior, since that would merely stipulate away the distinction at issue. Give a positive account of what genuine wanting consists in — what structural, functional, or representational features make a state count as a desire — and show that a designed system can in principle meet that account. Along the way, explain why a thermostat or a chess engine is not a counterexample to your view, or be explicit about which of their features fail the test. Also address the strongest form of the objection: that the content and authority of a desire come from the history of selection that established why that content matters, so stripping away the history strips away the wanting rather than leaving the same want with a different origin.
