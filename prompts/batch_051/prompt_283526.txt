Give me a working definition of interpersonal utility comparison: what, if anything, I am claiming when I say that a change makes one person better off than it makes another, and under what conditions such a comparison is determinately true, false, or neither. Test your definition against these cases:

(1) Two employees lose identical jobs; one has ample savings and another offer, the other has neither.
(2) A $500 windfall goes to a billionaire rather than to a family behind on rent.
(3) A health system must choose between funding a treatment that relieves one person's moderate chronic back pain and an equally costly treatment that relieves another person's intermittent but severe migraines.
(4) A woman who has spent her life in poverty reports that she is content; an aid official judges that her well-being is much lower than her self-report suggests. I suspect this involves adaptive preferences.
(5) A person with advanced dementia is moved into a somewhat better care home, while a devoted collector loses a lifelong stamp collection.
(6) A regulation produces a very slight benefit for each of one million people and a catastrophic loss for one person.
(7) A climate policy imposes real costs on people alive now to benefit people who will not be born for two centuries.

I am confident about the direction of the comparison only in (1) and (2). I lean toward thinking (4) has no determinate answer, and I do not know how any definition should handle (7). Propose a definition, apply it to each case, note where it gives the wrong answer or falls silent, and then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts it forces on you.
