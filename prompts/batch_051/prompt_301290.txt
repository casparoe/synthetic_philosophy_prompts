I was re-reading Utilitarianism for a course and I've got stuck on what feels like an real tension inside the text. On one side, Mill invokes Bentham's maxim that each person must count for one, and no one for more than one, and he treats this as the core of impartiality in the felicific calculus. On the other side, again in the same book, in his discussion of higher pleasures, he says pleasures differ in quality, not just in quantity, and that the only people able to judge which pleasures are higher are those who have experienced both. He then says a dissatisfied human is to be preferred to a satisfied pig, and a dissatisfied Socrates to a satisfied fool.

The two moves seem to collide. Bentham's maxim seems to tell me to enter each person's pleasures into the sum without excluding or discounting anyone's prefered kind of happiness. The quality doctrine seems to tell me that some pleasures are categorically higher, and that the judgement belongs to a class of competent judges who may not include the person whose happiness is being assessed. So I can count the fool for one and yet let somebody else decide that his pleasures are low-grade and deserve less weight. Is this a contradiction, or are the two claims doing different jobs in Mill's theory? I'd like you to spell out the strongest reconciliations and then say which one requires the least special pleading, and whether the quality doctrine ultimately makes Mill's version of the calculus depend on exactly the sort of interpersonal comparison that Bentham's counting rule was meant to avoid.
