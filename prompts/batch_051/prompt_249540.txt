So in my world history class we spent one class period on Wang Yangming, the Chinese philosopher whose big idea was that knowing and acting are basically one thing, not two separate steps. We read a short excerpt where he basically says if you think you know something but you don't act on it, you don't actually know it, you just know a slogan. My friends Marcus and Dana got into this huge argument after class about our friend Luke, who can list every reason vaping is bad and will tell you he knows it's bad, but vapes constantly anyway.

Marcus said that on Wang Yangming's view Luke doesn't actually know vaping is bad. If he really knew, he would of stopped, so the fact that he keeps doing it shows the knowledge was never real. Dana said that's just redefining the word to win the argument: Luke obviously knows the health facts, he can recite them, so saying he doesn't know is changing "know" into "care about enough to act on," which is a different concept.

Marcus came back: okay, but there's a difference between reciting facts and knowing something the way you know not to touch a hot stove. A doctor who smokes can pass a lung cancer exam but he doesn't know smoking is dangerous in the way that actually matters, the way that shows up in his hands. Dana said no, that's just two senses of the word, and the factual sense still counts as knowledge. People know things and fail to act on them all the time: she knows she shouldn't eat a whole bag of chips, and she does it anyway, and she really did know the whole time. If knowing required acting, akrasia would be impossible, and it's obviously real.

Then Marcus said Wang Yangming would call that self-deception. In the moment she's eating the chips, her real view isn't "this is bad," it's something like "this once is fine." The action shows which knowledge is the actual one. Dana said that's unfalsifiable, because any example of someone knowing something and not acting gets relabeled as them not truly knowing, and it also sounds dangerously like you're allowed to tell people they never really knew their own mind.

Marcus tried a different angle: think of a little kid who says "I know I should share" and then grabs the toy. Nobody says the kid's knowledge is fine and he just needs willpower; they say he doesn't really understand yet. That's the sense Yangming means. Dana replied that sharing is a skill or an understanding maybe, but factual knowledge is different: she knows the capital of France is Paris, and there's no action that proves or disproves that. So Yangming might be right about a special practical kind of knowing, but wrong to act like all knowing works that way.

They both looked at me to referee and I honestly couldn't pick. Can you go through their back and forth and say who's right about which specific move, whether they're mostly disagreeing about Wang Yangming or just using "know" differently, and who's more right overall?
