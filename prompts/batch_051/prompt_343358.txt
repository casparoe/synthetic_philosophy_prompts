I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am working on a long piece about an undertreated question in public debate: whether a legal system acts unjustly when it punishes people under criminal rules whose content they could not reasonably have discovered. I have drafted a compact argument I want to use in the piece, and before I let it near print I should like it examined by a professional philosopher of criminal law. Please think through it slowly, and before you commit to a verdict, try the argument under two or three different readings of the key phrases rather than settling on the first interpretation that comes to mind.

Here is the argument. (1) A person may justly be punished for violating a criminal law only if, at the time of acting, they had a fair opportunity to avoid the violation. (2) A person has a fair opportunity to avoid violating a law only if the content of that law was reasonably knowable to them at the time. (3) For much modern criminal regulation, such as environmental, financial and administrative offences whose requirements sit partly in statutes, partly in agency guidance and partly in unpublished enforcement practice, the content is not reasonably knowable to an ordinary person, because there is no single accessible statement of the rule and the conduct is not wrong independently of the rule. (4) So when ordinary people break such laws, they acted without a fair opportunity to avoid the violation. (5) So punishing ordinary people for those violations is unjust. (6) Yet courts regularly convict people under exactly such laws, relying on the maxim that ignorance of the law excuses no one. (7) Therefore, the convictions courts actually enter in these cases are unjust.

I cannot settle two worries. The first is that the argument appears to slide from a claim about a certain class of hypothetical offenders, namely ordinary people who could not find the rule, to a conclusion about the actual people courts convict; but perhaps that is harmless if the courts themselves do not distinguish the two classes. The second is that a lawyer I consulted said a defender of the practice would reject step (5), insisting that a system of punishment need not answer only to blameworthiness, rather than rejecting any earlier step. I do not know whether either worry is the real one, or whether the argument simply goes through and the premises must be given up.

Your task is to assess the argument as a philosopher of criminal law would. First say whether it is valid as written. If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which it fails and explain why, and then give the strongest repair that would make it valid. If it is valid, then the dispute must be over premises: identify which premise or premises a defender of current practice should reject, on what grounds, and whether any of them looks stronger than it is. Do not soften the conclusion; if the argument is valid and unsound, say plainly which premise must go.

Write your answer in continuous prose, as a letter or email: no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text or tables.
