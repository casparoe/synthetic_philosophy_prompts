I am giving a workshop paper next month and want you to prepare me for the question period rather than write a defense of the paper. The paper argues that the void-for-vagueness doctrine is best understood not as a fair-notice requirement directed at the ordinary citizen, but as a structural constraint on the delegation of lawmaking power: a criminal statute is unconstitutionally vague when it licenses police and prosecutors to decide, case by case, what conduct falls within the prohibition, not merely when the text fails to warn a particular defendant. On my view, fair notice is a real but downstream value, and it cannot explain the doctrine's actual contours—vague laws tend to be struck down where notice matters least, while some quite clear laws give no realistic notice to anyone.

The argument runs in three steps. First, notice-based accounts cannot explain why the doctrine has been used against loitering, disorderly conduct, and gang-loitering ordinances—laws addressed more to police discretion than to any person trying to conform her conduct—while it tolerates vagueness in laws governing sophisticated parties who rarely read statutes before acting. Second, the doctrine is better explained by a principle drawn from the separation of powers and the rule of law: a legislature may not outsource the definition of criminal conduct to enforcement discretion, and the constitutional defect is the authorization of ad hoc, discriminatory enforcement rather than the absence of warning. Third—and this is the move I expect to be attacked—if vagueness review is about delegation rather than notice, it should reach sentencing enhancements, supervised-release conditions, and other measures that shape liability and punishment without criminalizing primary conduct, and it may require invalidating statutes even where the defendant was plainly on notice that his conduct was covered.

The audience is a law school workshop with three groups. Most are criminal law scholars, including former prosecutors and public defenders, who think about the doctrine through its effects on policing, plea bargaining, and mass incarceration. Two are analytic philosophers of language who work on vagueness and will want to know whether my account quietly assumes a solution to the sorites, or treats vagueness as a merely epistemic phenomenon. One is a federal district judge who cares less about theory than about what instruction to give a jury on Monday morning. I would like a Q&A prep sheet: the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that member of the audience would put it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objection has the most force, what I should concede up front, where my own position is weakest and must be defended rather than hoped away, and the question I should be praying nobody asks.
