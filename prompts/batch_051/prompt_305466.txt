In the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus, Spinoza claims that interpreting Scripture should proceed the same way as interpreting nature: first assemble a faithful history of the text—its language, historical setting, authorship, and transmission—and then infer the meaning of passages from that data alone, without importing one’s own doctrinal commitments. I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, but I keep reading this as a claim about the convergence of a procedure: if his method is followed correctly, interpreters with different prior beliefs should converge on the same meaning, just as independent measurements should converge on the same physical constant.

How would one test whether Spinoza’s claim about the method is actually true, rather than merely a methodological ideal? I want a concrete design: what target text, what interpreters, what kinds of preexisting disagreement, and what measured outcome would distinguish a method that genuinely extracts meaning from textual data from one that merely formalizes the interpreter’s prior commitments? Say exactly what result would favor each reading, and then say what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, even if trained interpreters converge, that would seem to show intersubjective agreement but not necessarily that they have recovered what the text really meant or that the resulting reading can function as revelation for a faith community. Is that residue a limitation of the test, or does it expose a deeper flaw in Spinoza’s analogy between interpreting Scripture and interpreting nature?
