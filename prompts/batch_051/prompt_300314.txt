An argument has been going around my study group, and it seems to prove more than anyone involved believes — namely, that a language with no pronouns or indexicals could express everything a language with them can, yet it runs into what looks like an undeniable fact about self-locating belief. I cannot find the step that should be blamed.

(1) Take any assertion made with an indexical such as 'I', 'now', or 'here'. Relative to the context in which it is uttered, there is an indexical-free sentence with the very same truth-conditions. If Ada says 'I am in the main reading room', the sentence 'Ada is in the main reading room' is true in exactly the same possible circumstances. If she says 'The lecture starts now', the sentence 'The lecture starts at 15:00 on 6 June 2025' does the same work. Names, dates, and place-terms are stipulated not to be indexicals.

(2) If two sentences are true in exactly the same possible circumstances, they say the same thing. A person who sincerely accepts one believes exactly what a person who sincerely accepts the other believes; any difference between them is only verbal. Therefore the indexical contributes nothing beyond its indexical-free replacement.

(3) So a language with no pronouns and no indexical expressions — built only from names, predicates, dates, and place-terms — could express every proposition and every belief that English can express. It would be unwieldy, but not poorer in what it can say.

(4) But this conclusion cannot be right, on pain of contradicting a plain fact. Consider an amnesiac alone in a large library. He has read a detailed and fully accurate file about a certain man, 'A.', and he comes to believe, and can sincerely assert, 'A. is in the main reading room'. But the file never tells him that he himself is A., and he does not know it. So he remains lost, still scanning the rooms and unable to settle. Later he realizes the truth he would express as 'I am in the main reading room', and his behavior changes at once: he stops searching, looks for the exit, or simply relaxes.

(5) If step (2) were correct, the belief behind 'A. is in the main reading room' and the belief behind 'I am in the main reading room' would be the very same belief, since the two sentences are true in the same possible circumstances. They would then have to have the same consequences for how he acts. But his conduct sharply distinguishes them.

(6) So indexicals are not redundant after all, and the language described in (3) does leave something out. Yet (1), (2), and (3) looked tantamount to a proof. Where exactly does the argument break down? Is the mistake in one of the numbered steps, or in my inference from the amnesiac's case to the falsity of (3)? Which claim should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I would be least willing to give up (4), since the difference in the amnesiac's behavior seems like straightforward evidence; but I do not see how that can be squared with (2) as stated. Give me the least costly resolution, and tell me what I have to pay for it.
