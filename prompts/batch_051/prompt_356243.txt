I’ve been trying to work out, on my own, what people mean when they say a society should make people equal. Equal in what, exactly? I don’t have any academic background in this; I’ve just been testing my gut against a series of concrete situations, and my judgments don’t all fit together. I’d like you to work out whether there is a single principle behind them, what it says about a further case, and which of my judgments would have to be abandoned.

Here are my cases and reactions.

Ana and Bruno earn the same wage. Ana has a chronic illness whose treatment and home adaptations consume much of her income; Bruno is healthy and has no special expenses. Equal pay is not enough here: I think Ana is owed extra so that her illness doesn’t leave her worse off through no fault of her own.

Carla and David also earn the same. Carla is easily satisfied; David can only enjoy things when they are costly, and with equal pay he ends up much less happy. I don’t think David is owed more. His problem is his expensive tastes, and he should learn to be satisfied with less rather than be compensated for being hard to please.

Esther inherited enough to live on, so she works twenty hours a week as a painter and earns little. Frank works overtime as a nurse and earns a good deal more. At year’s end, Esther has far less money. I don’t think the state should make their year-end totals equal: Esther chose more leisure, and it would be unfair to take from Frank to fund that choice.

Greta was born with a condition that makes it harder for her to work and harder for her to enjoy ordinary activities. I think she is owed substantial support so she can participate in life on something like equal terms, but I would not require that she end up as happy as anyone else, and I don’t think the point of the support is to equalize her subjective satisfaction.

Jack, healthy and able, was given a sum equal to Inez’s. He gambled his away; she saved hers. He is now much poorer. I don’t think he is owed a top-up to restore equality, though I support a minimal floor that keeps him from destitution. Inez should not have to pay for his reckless choice.

The parts that bother me are these. Ana and Greta, in one sense, are like David: all three get less out of the same resources—the first two because of their bodies, David because of his tastes. I compensate the first two but not the third, and I can’t say exactly what the difference is; “need” doesn’t do the work, because David experiences his tastes as needs. And choices seem to matter in Esther’s and Jack’s cases, but Ana didn’t choose her illness either. Finally, I keep going back and forth on this further case: Leila has a profound cognitive disability, so extra money improves her life only a little by any standard reckoning. A rule aimed at equal happiness would spend almost nothing on her. I feel that is wrong, but I need a principle that explains why without collapsing back into my other judgments.

What general account of what should be equalized captures my reactions? Which of my judgments does it force me to change, and does it give a defensible answer to Leila’s case?

Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
