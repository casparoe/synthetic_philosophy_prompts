Immanuel Kant's famous objection to the ontological argument is often paraphrased this way: existence is not a predicate that adds a further determination to the concept of a thing, so the existence of a supremely perfect being cannot be extracted analytically from that concept. On his account, the modal notions of possibility, actuality, and necessity do not enlarge the content of a concept; they express only the relation in which the object stands to our cognitive faculty. Now suppose Kant were shown a faithful, self-contained reconstruction of Gödel's modal ontological argument and were asked to evaluate it without any commentary. The reconstruction runs as follows. Properties are divided into positive and non-positive by the condition that a property is positive just in case its negation is not positive, so exactly one member of each property/complement pair is positive. Positive properties are also subject to a closure condition: anything entailed by a positive property is itself positive. From these two conditions one can prove that every positive property is possibly instantiated. God-likeness is defined as the property of having every positive property, and God-likeness is itself stipulated to be a positive property; hence God-likeness is possibly instantiated. One then derives that God-likeness is an essence of any God-like being. A further stipulation says that necessary existence—understood as the property a being has when every essence of it is necessarily instantiated—is a positive property. Any God-like being therefore has necessary existence, which yields a possible world in which a God-like being exists necessarily; by the S5 principle that what is possibly necessary is necessary, a God-like being exists in every possible world, and so exists actually.
