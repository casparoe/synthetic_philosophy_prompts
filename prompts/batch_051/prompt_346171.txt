In three weeks I am giving a paper at the quarterly clinical ethics seminar of a large teaching hospital, and I want to prepare for the question period rather than improvise on the day. My thesis is that an advance directive refusing life-sustaining treatment retains full authority over a patient who now has moderate dementia and is settled and content, even when she now says, repeatedly and with apparent sincerity, that she wants to go on living. I defend a version of the view that the earlier, competent self has authority over the later self, but I defend it on a specific ground: the later self's present preference to continue living is not a genuinely rival exercise of autonomy when that preference is itself produced by the very condition whose progression the directive was written to refuse, and when the earlier self anticipated that she would later form just such preferences. My argument has three load-bearing steps. First, an advance directive is an exercise of authority by the person over her own future, not merely a record of what she once predicted she would want. Second, the badness of the condition the directive refuses is not exhausted by how that condition feels from the inside at the time; it also includes what the condition means in the biography of the person, such as the loss of a capacity for independence, for long-standing projects, and for a certain kind of self-respect. Third, a contented preference for life formed under that condition lacks the stability and the fit with the person's deeper values that would be required for it to override the earlier exercise of authority.

The audience is not a philosophy audience. It will be roughly thirty clinicians and ethicists: old-age psychiatrists, palliative care consultants, geriatricians, and hospital clinical ethicists. They know the clinical course of dementia intimately, they are sympathetic to the thought that compassionate care should answer to the needs of the person in front of them rather than to a document, and only a few of them have read the philosophical literature on the authority of the earlier self or on personal identity; those who have read it regard it with suspicion. The one philosopher of psychiatry who will be there holds that the self is not the sort of thing that persists robustly across severe dementia, and he will press me hardest.

Please write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I shall actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get from a room of metaphysicians, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are; each phrased the way one of these clinicians would put it; a two-minute reply to each and a note on where that reply is weakest; a statement of what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to concede even if it costs me sympathy; and, at the end, the question I am hoping nobody asks. Please write the prep sheet in British English.
