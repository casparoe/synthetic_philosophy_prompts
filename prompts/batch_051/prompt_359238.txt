I have been working my way through the question of when an infinite regress is vicious, and I have settled on a distinction I want checked before I lean on it anywhere. I call the two shapes of regress 'cascades' and 'staircases.'

A cascade is a regress in which each item yields the next: the later items are consequences of the earlier ones, and the chain runs downstream from what is already secured. A staircase is a regress in which each item owes its standing to the next: the chain runs upstream, and each earlier item is only as secure as the later one is.

My thesis is that the infinity is a red herring. What makes a regress vicious is not that it fails to terminate but that it is a staircase whose relation defers the relevant standing instead of delivering it. Cascades are always benign: the later items depend on the earlier ones, and the earlier ones do not wait on them. Staircases are vicious only when the later item does not actually confer the standing in question but only promises it, conditional on a still later item conferring it first. Justification is the clean case: each later belief is supposed to justify the earlier one, but it can do so only if it is itself justified, so the justification is deferred at every step and never arrives. An infinite causal chain, read backward from a present event, is also a staircase—each event's occurrence is owed to the next cause in the chain—but it need not be vicious, because each cause actually confers occurrence on its effect; nothing is postponed. I suspect the underlying difference is that a cause confers occurrence whether or not it is itself caused, whereas a justifying belief confers justification only if it is itself justified; the second relation transmits a status its relata must already have, and the first does not. But I am not sure that sentence does more than restate the problem.

I test the proposal on two standard cases. The truth regress—from p to the claim that p is true, to the claim that that claim is true, and so on—comes out as a cascade and harmless, which seems right. Bradley's regress, the one in which instantiation seems to require a further relation to tie the universal to the particular, comes out—against a common reading—as a cascade rather than a vicious staircase: each further relation is generated as a consequence of treating instantiation as itself a relation in need of instantiation, and the original instantiation never actually waits on those further relations. That result is attractive to me, but it may beg the question against people who take Bradley's regress to show that relational instantiation is unstable.

I would like an honest assessment. Please reconstruct the distinction in its strongest form and tell me whether it collapses into the familiar idea that viciousness tracks explanatory or justificatory dependence rather than mere entailment, or whether it adds something. Then give me the strongest counterexamples you can: a cascade that is nonetheless vicious, or a staircase my deferral test misclassifies. And say where the causal-chain case leaves the account—whether the gap I admitted between causation's bestowal and justification's deferral is fatal or merely unfinished. Throughout, keep clear the line between what is settled among philosophers who work on regress arguments and what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
