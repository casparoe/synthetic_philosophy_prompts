I’m in a wellbeing and value-theory seminar, and we recently read Sidgwick’s Methods of Ethics. He has this passage where he says, roughly, that the impulse toward pleasure, if it becomes too dominant, tends to defeat its own aim. That stuck with me and I’ve been trying to turn it into a proper argument, but now I think the argument proves too much and I can’t find the flaw.

(1) If hedonism is true, then happiness is the only thing worth pursuing for its own sake.
(2) If something is the only thing worth pursuing for its own sake, then a rational agent should aim at it directly.
(3) But aiming directly at happiness tends to make you get less of it, not more, as Sidgwick seems to say.
(4) So hedonism tells a rational agent to adopt an aim that predictably gives her less of the only thing worth pursuing.
(5) A theory of rational action that tells you to pursue the only final good in a way that predictably gives you less of it is self-undermining.
(6) Therefore hedonism is self-undermining.

I don’t love the conclusion, but I also can’t tell where it goes wrong. My best guess is that premise (2) is doing too much, and that maybe hedonism just says happiness is the standard of value, not that you should aim at it directly. But if I go that way, I’m not sure what it means for happiness to be the “only thing worth pursuing for its own sake” if you shouldn’t, in general, pursue it directly. Also, if I reject (3), I need to explain why the empirical pattern Sidgwick describes either isn’t real or doesn’t matter here.

Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I keep hedonism, what exactly is the relation between the end and the aim?
