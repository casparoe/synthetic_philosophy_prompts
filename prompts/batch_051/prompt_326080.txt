I've been reading a book on symmetry in mathematics, and it summarizes the whole business about roots and labelling roughly like this. In the field Q(√2), the two roots of x² − 2, usually called √2 and −√2, are structurally indistinguishable: there's an automorphism of the field that swaps them, and no polynomial equation with rational coefficients is satisfied by one but not the other. So, considered just as elements of that abstract field, there is no fact of the matter which one is really the positive root; positivity is extra structure the real numbers supply, not a property of the field itself. That is why any definition that seems to single out one root has to be checked for well-definedness: it must not secretly depend on how you label them. But the book then concludes that, since the two roots are interchangeable, the labels √2 and −√2 don't really name distinct objects, and therefore the whole worry about well-definedness is only a worry about notation. I'm a philosopher, not an algebraist, and I suspect that last move is too quick, but I can't quite say why. How much of this summary should I trust? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what an algebraist or philosopher of mathematics would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the final inference — that well-definedness is just about notation — actually follows from the accurate parts.
