I'm giving a workshop paper next month on statues of limitations and the moral relevance of time, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a pep talk. My thesis is that the standard epistemic story — evidence decays, witnesses die, memories get contaminated — can't justify a hard temporal cutoff, because it justifies being more careful or raising the burden, not saying that after N years the wrong is simply off the table. I'm defending the stronger claim that the passage of time itself does moral work: after enough time, even a perfectly documented wrong is no longer the kind of thing the state is entitled to punish, because the wrongdoer's relation to the act and the community's standing to call them to account have both changed. I'm explicitly not saying victims must forgive or that the wrong becomes right; I'm saying the entitlement to punish has something like a sell-by date.

My argument, roughly, is three moves. (1) The epistemic rationale underdetermines the cutoff and overdetermines caution. (2) Our personal reactive practices already treat time as morally relevant: if I learn that a close friend lied to me twenty years ago, I can't reasonably treat that discovery the way I'd treat learning they lied last week, and the difference isn't just that my evidence is older. (3) Punishment is a relationship-constituted practice, so it inherits the temporality of relationships, and a community that waited twenty years has in some cases lost the standing to punish even if it kept the evidence. The audience: a law school workshop, mostly criminal law theorists and moral philosophers. Two committed retributivists who think deserved punishment doesn't decay with time; a restorative justice person who'll think my view is state-centered and forgets victims; a law-and-economics type who thinks limitation periods are just optimal tradeoffs between deterrence and repose and will call the moral weight of time handwaving; and a philosopher who works on diachronic agency and will attack the idea that the twenty-years-later person is answerable in the same way. Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weak point in my argument I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence — where am I most exposed, in one sentence — and then go objection by objection, no preamble and no restating of the question. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
