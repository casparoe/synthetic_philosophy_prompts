I'm in a philosophy of mind / cognitive science course and I've ended up defending a position I don't fully trust, so I want to know what would actually defeat it. The position, roughly: the access/phenomenal distinction is useful for carving up lab tasks, but phenomenal consciousness is a label, not an extra thing. Global workspace theory, as its actually constructed and tested, is a theory of access consciousness — information being broadcast to various consumer systems and becoming available for report. On my view, once you have that global availability plus the self-monitoring and reporting machinery, there's nothing left over for the subjective feel of the thing to refer to; the feeling that there's a residue is itself just one more access-mediated representation, an artifact of the system checking its own outputs. So the hard problem isn't unsolved, it's dissolved — it was built around a phantom.

I don't need the standard objections again (zombies, Mary, the usual hard-problem table-pounding). I want a serious map of what would count against this access-only position: which experimental results, which clinical or stimulation data, which conceptual commitments would a defender of it have to admit are evidence against them — and which of those are fatal rather than merely awkward. Be honest about whether the position, as I've stated it, has been insulated so much that nothing could count against it, and whether that insulation is itself a problem. To seed things: my friend keeps pushing the tip-of-the-tongue state at me — you have a vivid, frustrating sense of the word being right there while the word itself isn't accessible to report. Doesn't that look like phenomenal consciousness outrunning access, and if it does, what exactly has to be true for that not to sink my view?
