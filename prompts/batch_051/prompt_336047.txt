I am applying for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in the philosophy of the social and decision sciences, and I would like help turning a half-formed intuition into a two-page research proposal. The topic has become personally relevant over the past year: after moving to a new city on a fixed-term contract and making several consequential decisions about where to live and which projects to take on, I noticed that I was repeatedly choosing the first option that cleared some threshold rather than comparing all the alternatives, and I could not tell whether this was a defensible procedure or simply fatigue. Reading about satisficing and bounded rationality has clarified part of this but has not settled it, and I now suspect there is a researchable question underneath my unease.

My half-formed idea is this. The familiar debate asks whether satisficing is a genuine rival to expected-utility maximisation or merely optimisation under search costs. Both sides, as I read them, tend to treat the agent's aspiration level as either exogenous or as itself chosen by some higher-order act of optimisation. That framing suppresses the case I find most interesting: agents whose aspiration levels adapt through experience—rising after easy successes, falling after costly failures—without any explicit choice of a threshold. My hunch is that aspiration adaptation of this sort embodies a distinctively bounded-rational norm that is not equivalent to any optimal stopping rule, and that vindicating it requires separating three questions that the literature tends to run together: what fixes an aspiration level, what fixes the stopping rule once it is fixed, and what makes the whole practice rational over time rather than action-by-action.

I would like the proposal to state a single determinate research question, explain why the existing positions leave it open (you may refer to positions and arguments by their familiar names; I will add citations myself, so please do not provide a bibliography or a citation-heavy literature review), describe what I would actually do month by month across the year and why that approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives—in particular, formal modelling of optimal stopping or purely historical reconstruction of Herbert Simon's actual view—and say both what a positive result would look like and what would count as clear failure. Please write it in my voice, as a first-person proposal, and keep the framing honest about what might make the project too large for one year.
