I've been trying to put together a lecture on antimemes for my social epistemology class—ideas whose content makes them resist being remembered, understood, or passed on—and I keep finding the go-to examples are all to sci-fi for the point I want to make. Memetic kill agents, strings of digits that dissolve when you read them, secret bureaus erasing records: it all makes the phenomenon look like a science-fiction premise rather than something that might be going on in perfectly ordinary information ecologies. So I want you to build me a case that's fully mundane. The antimeme has to be an idea a normal person can hold, write down, explain to a friend, and pass a comprehension check on; it should be at least plausibly true; and its failure to spread should not come from censorship, low relevance, or just being boring. I want the resistance to propagation to come from the content of the idea interacting with something garden-variety about the audience—incentives, identity, attention, how people manage the impression they're making—not from anyone suppressing it.

Here are the specs. (i) The scenario should be one paragraph, set in an ordinary place: a workplace, a school, a neighborhood, a family group chat. (ii) The idea must be stateable and, once stated, understandable and memorable; it can't be a self-erasing trick sentence. (iii) There has to be a control: a minimally different variant of the same idea that spreads fine, so we can isolate the exact feature doing the antimemetic work—change one clause, one frame, or one social implication and it should become contagious. After the scenario, walk me through why it satisfies each spec, name the single feature that's doing the work, and say what would happen if you removed it. Then finish with the strongest objection a skeptic could raise—that this is just an unpopular or inconvenient idea and not a real antimeme—and tell me where your construction is most likely to fail on reflection, what would make you scrap it. Start with the scenario itself, no preamble, no commentary; put the analysis after it. And no citations or reading lists, please—I just want the case.
