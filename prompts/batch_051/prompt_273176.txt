So I was reading Daniel Ellsberg's 1961 paper "Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms" the other week, and one bit near the end has been bothering me. He describes what he calls the "two-color" and "three-color" urn problems. In the two-color problem you have an urn with, say, a hundred balls, and you know half are red and half are black but you can't tell which is which when you draw. You're asked to bet on a color and win if it comes up. People don't much care which color they bet on, which makes sense because the odds feel symmetric. In the three-color problem you have ninety balls, thirty red, and the other sixty are some mix of black and yellow, but you're not told the mix. Now people are asked to choose between betting on red versus betting on black, and later between betting on "not-red" versus "not-black." And what he reports is that most people prefer betting on red over black, but also prefer betting on "not-red" over "not-black." Strictly speaking that pattern violates Savage's sure-thing principle, and Ellsberg says it comes from an aversion to ambiguity, not just from ignorance of the odds. In particular he seems to treat the ambiguity-averse choices as perfectly reasonable, not as a mistake to be corrected.

Here's my problem, and I think I'm missing something. A lot of the later literature, especially the behavioral economics stuff, talks about ambiguity aversion as if it's a bias, like a glitch in our reasoning that we should debias. If that's right, then the people in Ellsberg's three-color urn should train themselves out of it, maybe by forcing themselves to assign a subjective distribution to the black-yellow mix and stick with it. But if Ellsberg is right that it's reasonable, then the opposite seems true too: being unbothered by the black-yellow ambiguity should itself be a kind of overconfidence about your own subjective probabilities. So which is it? Is ambiguity aversion a bias I should try to correct in my own decision-making, or is indifference to ambiguity the real mistake? I get that Ellsberg's paper is famous precisely because it doesn't settle this, but I feel like one of these framings has to be the right one for somebody trying to make decisions, and I can't tell which. Also, if I start correcting for ambiguity aversion in everyday life, do I end up just taking on a lot of ambiguous gambles that a reasonable person would walk away from? I'd like the answer to argue for the reading it finds best, say how confident it is in that reading, and at the end single out the point it is least sure about.
