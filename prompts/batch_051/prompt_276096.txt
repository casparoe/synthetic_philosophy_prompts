I have been seconded to a health service that is about to adopt, for its catastrophic-care fund, an allocation procedure modeled on a Scanlon-style contractualist reading of the aggregation problem. The service can afford to fund, in full, only a few patient cohorts each year out of a much larger set of candidates; everyone else receives standard care. The proposed institution works as follows. In each annual cycle the fund publishes a call for proposed coverage rules—standing rules of the form "fund therapy T for patients meeting condition C." Any affected patient, family member, or treating clinician may then file an objection to a proposed rule. But an objection is admissible only if it names a specific individual and argues that this individual's own complaint under the proposed rule is more severe than the most severe individual complaint any single identifiable person would have under the best rival rule, where severity is scored on a common schedule covering pain, loss of function, and expected life-years lost. The tribunal is forbidden to count how many people share a given complaint; a challenge that rests on the accumulation of many small or moderate harms is thrown out as irrelevant. Once objections have eliminated the rules they eliminate, the tribunal chooses among the remaining rules the one that minimizes the worst individual complaint left on the table, and if two surviving rules tie by that test, a public lottery decides.

Treat this as a mechanism-design exercise, not a question about whether Scanlon is right. I want a committed account of how this tribunal will actually behave by year five. Predict which therapies and which sorts of patients the procedure favors, how the behavior of patient groups, clinicians, and manufacturers adapts, the first place a sophisticated actor figures out how to game it, and the first serious failure—the moment the procedure plainly does something its designers would hate. Then tell me whether that failure is fixable with a narrow patch that does not quietly restore aggregation, and if so, which single patch you would adopt, what it would cost, and why you would pick that one rather than the alternatives.
