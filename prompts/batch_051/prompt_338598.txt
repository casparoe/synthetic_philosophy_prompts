I sit on my university's animal research ethics board. A genetics lab has put in a proposal that will, as part of a standard mutant screen, kill roughly 50,000 fruit flies a month. Under our written rules invertebrates are exempt from review, so normally this would never even reach us. But one new member won't let it go. His line is: even if you set the chance that a single fly can consciously suffer at one in ten thousand, 50,000 flies a month over a three-year project gives you an expected 180 suffering flies, and if we would balk at knowingly causing that, we can't just record it as zero. Another member says this is a cheap Pascal-style trick: take a tiny uncertainty you can't resolve, multiply it by a big enough number, and you can manufacture a reason to ban almost anything, so the board should stick to animals we have actual evidence about rather than paralyze whole departments over maybe-suffering in a bug. A third says that even if a fly can feel something, the range of a fly's experience is so thin that a fly-hour of pain isn't the same currency as a mouse-hour of pain, so the arithmatic quietly assumes an equivalence nobody believes. I need to circulate a short written opinion before next month's meeting: should we require the lab to anesthetize or otherwise reduce fly deaths anyway, or let the standard exemption stand, and on what reasoning? Please draft it with an actionable bottom line and a serious engagement with the strongest point on the losing side.
