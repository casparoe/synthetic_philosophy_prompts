I have been teaching Nozick's entitlement theory and the Wilt Chamberlain example for several years, and I find that my settled judgments about concrete cases do not line up neatly with any single theory I know. I would like you to help me locate the principle behind these judgments, if one exists, and to tell me where I have to give something up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. The athlete. A basketball player signs a contract by which a small surcharge on each ticket goes to him. Very many people buy tickets voluntarily. He becomes vastly richer than anyone else. Verdict: the resulting distribution is just. The inequality is striking, but I see no injustice in it.

2. The mineral strike. A farmer purchases land at a fair market price and later discovers a rich mineral deposit beneath it. She sells extraction rights and becomes wealthy. Verdict: just. She did not create the mineral, but the wealth flows from a valid acquisition and a valid transfer.

3. The drought and the wells. During a severe drought, a speculator buys up all the potable-water rights in a region through a sequence of ordinary, uncoerced purchases. He then charges neighboring households ruinous prices for drinking water. Verdict: not just, even though each individual purchase was consensual and the sellers had full title.

4. The polluted river. A factory discharges waste into a river used by downstream fishers, without their consent, and later pays court-ordered damages. Verdict: the discharge was unjust. Paying damages afterward does not make the original transfer of risk consensual.

5. The data platform. A company collects detailed behavioral data from users under a clickwrap agreement that almost no one reads, then sells the data at profit while providing a genuinely useful free service. Verdict: I am disposed to call this unjust, though the arrangement is nominally voluntary and the users receive something real. My confidence here is lower than in the other cases.

6. The stolen farm. A family is defrauded of farmland two generations ago. The present owner bought the land in good faith from the fraudster's heirs. Verdict: the present owner has some claim to the land as a good-faith purchaser, but the original invalid transfer still requires rectification for the original family's descendants.

7. The heir. A woman inherits a large fortune from a parent who acquired it justly. She does no work and lives off the capital. Verdict: this is just, strictly speaking, but I find it less just than the athlete's earnings. I cannot state the difference in principled terms, and I suspect no purely historical principle of just transfer can, either, yet I am reluctant to abandon the judgment.

My tentative principle, which I have been calling the proviso-constrained entitlement principle, says that a holding is just if and only if: it descends from acquisitions that leave no one worse off than they would have been in the absence of the acquisition; it descends from transfers that are genuinely informed, uncoerced, and do not worsen non-consenting third parties relative to the pre-transfer baseline; and any past violations in the chain have been rectified.

Please test this principle against my verdicts. Where does it succeed, where does it fail, and which clauses need to be revised, added, or abandoned? If my verdicts cannot all be captured by one defensible principle, identify which of them I should overturn and explain why. Finally, apply the repaired principle to a further case I have not listed, and show what it predicts.
