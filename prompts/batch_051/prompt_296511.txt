I run a second-year undergrad seminar called Limits of Explanation — 18 students, 90 minutes, mostly non-math majors. I want a hands-on session that makes them feel computational irreducibility before I ever say the phrase, and ideally before I lecture at all. The thing I want them to run into on thier own is this: you can have a system that is completely deterministic, with a dead-simple local rule, and yet there is no shortcut from initial condition to later state — the only way to know step 30 is to do steps 1 through 29, or something just as slow. That's a limit on explanation, not just on our patience.

Build it around elementary cellular automata on a one-row strip, Rule 30. So you don't have to guess the rule from memory, here it is: neighborhoods of left-center-right map 111→0, 110→0, 101→0, 100→1, 011→1, 010→1, 001→1, 000→0. Start with a single 1 in the middle of a row of zeros; don't wrap the edges, treat anything past the ends as 0.

Give me rules for a game with a payoff scheme. Teams earn points by predicting particular later cells — say the center cell at step 20, or the full row at step 15 — without grinding through every intermediate row. They can spend points to peek at an intermediate row, and wrong predictions lose points. I want the incentives set up so they are pushed to hunt for shortcuts, find near-misses, and then feel the moment the shortcut fails. Include a second rule where a shortcut actually exists as a control for the debrief — Rule 90 works (new cell = left XOR right, so 111→0, 110→1, 101→0, 100→1, 011→1, 010→0, 001→1, 000→0) — so they don't leave thinking every cellular automaton is just too hard for them.

Then write the debrief so it maps what happened in the game onto the idea: which moment corresponds to irreducibility, what it would even mean to explain step 30 without exhibiting all the steps, and why prediction and explanation might come apart in exactly this spot. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — someone will miscount and invent a wrap-around I clearly said didn't exist, someone will mistake a local repeating pattern for a global shortcut, one group will brute-force the whole thing anyway and clean up — and say how I should handle each.

Be honest about what the activity can't show. An hour of failing to find a shortcut is evidence of difficulty, not a proof that no shortcut exists; the strong claim about Rule 30 is not a settled theorem. In the debrief notes, please seperate what's settled (the rule fixes every row, the calculation is mechanical, some CA rules do have known closed forms) from what's genuinely contested (whether Rule 30 is computationally irreducible in a strong sense, and how far the lesson actually generalizes to science). Don't present the strong irreducibility-is-everywhere line as if it were consensus.
