I've been stuck on the tautology objection in my philosophy of biology class, and I think I finally see what bothers me about it. The charge is that "survival of the fittest" says nothing real, because the only way we have to identify the fittest organisms is by the fact that they survived and reproduced—so it's just bookkeeping. My professor counters that, under the propensity interpretation, fitness is more like a biased coin's disposition to come up heads, or sugar's solubility: a real property an organism has before any particular outcome. Okay, but that's just an assertion unless the property can be shown to do independent work. If fitness is a real propensity, I should be able to catch it predicting something, not just summarizing it.

So here's what I want: design the strongest, most concrete test you can to distinguish the claim "fitness is a real propensity of organisms" from the claim "fitness is just bookkeeping about actual survival and reproduction." Use a real experimental setup—flour beetles, E. coli, annual plants, whatever you think would actually work. Tell me what result would make you think the propensity talk is earning its keep, what result would make you think the tautology charge still has teeth, and then be brutal about the residue: even if the test comes out looking like fitness is a real propensity, what exactly does a positive result still fail to show? Is there a version of the tautology worry that survives any experiment of this kind?
