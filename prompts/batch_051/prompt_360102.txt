I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been collecting my own verdicts about when pain is bad, but I've never managed to turn them into a single view. I'd like you to act as an expert in the philosophy of pain and help me reverse-engineer the principle. Take your time — start by brainstorming a few candidate principles, then test each one against my cases before you settle on anything.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

A standard kidney stone: the person is writhing, says it's a 9/10, and would do anything to stop it. Verdict: this pain is bad in itself, not just through its consequences, and its badness feels baked into what the experience is like.

A patient with a rare syndrome called pain asymbolia: she can tell you the stimulus is sharp, located in her left hand, and about a 7 in intensity, but she doesn't withdraw the hand, doesn't rub it, and says it doesn't bother her; she may even smile while describing it. Verdict: I want to say her pain isn't bad for her in the ordinary way — maybe not bad at all.

A post-surgical patient on morphine who says the pain is still there at roughly the same intensity but that he just doesn't care about it. Verdict: the badness has mostly drained out of it, even though the pain's location and intensity remain.

A marathoner with burning cramps in the final two miles. He calls the cramping pain and also says he wouldn't trade the experience; he signed up for exactly this. Verdict: I still want to say the cramp is bad while it's happening, even if the whole package is worth it.

A person in a consensual sexual context who seeks out and enjoys painful sensations, reporting them as pain but wanting more. Verdict: I don't want to say the pain is bad for them, or at most bad in a thin sensory sense that doesn't matter in the usual way.

A needle-phobic child getting a flu shot. The tissue damage is trivial and the pain is maybe a 2/10, but the child is screaming and fighting. Verdict: the pain is genuinely bad for the child in a way that far outruns its intensity.

A chronic arthritis patient whose pain has been a steady 5/10 for years. She says she's used to it and it doesn't bother her the way it used to, but her doctors note it wears her down, ruins her sleep, and narrows her life. Verdict: I think the pain is still bad, but I'm not sure whether it's bad because of the felt quality, the fatigue, or the lost agency.

The pair I can't reconcile is the asymbolia patient and the needle-phobic child. In the first I treat badness as seperable from intensity and tied to whether the person minds the pain; in the second I treat a trivial but intensely minded pinprick as seriously bad. So my gut principle is something like: pain is bad to the extent that, and because, the subject is averse to it or suffers it. But then it follows that a 9/10 asymbolic pain is less bad than a 2/10 phobic pinprick, and I balk at that. And the arthritis case seems to say intensity and duration count for something even after the aversion has faded.

Is there a coherent account of the badness of pain behind all this? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Also tell me what your principle says about a couple of further cases I haven't considered. And tell me honestly what would change your mind about the account you give — which further case or consideration is most likely to break your reasoning?
