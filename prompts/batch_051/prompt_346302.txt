I've been reading Hannah Arendt on political lying, and its made me notice something odd about the way newsrooms handle falsehoods. The same outlet that says a footballer lied about betting on matches will say a cabinet minister made a false claim, or that his statement was not accurate, even when the minister repeated it after being corrected. The editors reason that they can't know his mental state; but they seem to have no trouble attributing mental states to the footballer, a witness, or a fraudster. What theory of lying, or of politics, does that asymmetry commit them to? One story is that it presupposes deception needs proven intent, that intent is knowable in private life but not in public, and that political speech is therefore closer to performance than assertion. But could it instead just be libel-law caution and a general rule about not mind-reading, with no particular view of political speech at all? And the first story's last step sounds worryingly like what Arendt thought the political liar is trying to bring about—she thought organised political lying works to wear down our shared factual world until fact and opinion blur. So is the practice a sensible epistemic modesty, or does it quietly adopt the liar's own presuppositions? What would editorial practice have to look like if it took Arendt's worry seriously? I'm less interested in this outlet than in the commitments.
