Write a pantoum (the form where the second and fourth lines of each quatrain come back as the first and third lines of the next quatrain, and the last quatrain loops back to the opening lines) about David Lewis's modal realism. Aim for 16–20 lines.

The poem has to get the view right, not just gesture at it. Work in these commitments: possible worlds are concrete, full-sized universes just as real as this one — not abstract ways things could be; no world is metaphysically privileged; 'actual' is an indexical, like 'here' or 'now,' so 'the actual world' just means whichever world I happen to be in; and when we say I could have been a plumber, that isn't one and the same me showing up in several worlds — it's a counterpart, a separate individual in another world who is similar enough to stand in for me there.

Don't just write about the view in a fixed form; the pantoum should do some real work. Each verbatim return of a line is meant to read as a counterpart of the earlier occurrance: same line-type, different line-token, back again in a new stanza with a new job, the way a counterpart is a distinct individual standing in for someone in a distinct world rather than literally the same thing. Let the form perform the idea instead of merely decorating it.

At some point the poem should face the incredulous-stare objection, that gut feeling that this is an absurdly bloated ontology — and push back, maybe by noting that the other worlds aren't an extra item tacked on next to this one, since this one is just one of them. Try to end on the thought that 'actual' is something a world can only call itself from the inside, never a property some worlds have and others lack.

Someone who knows the Lewisian machinery should finish it thinking, yes, that's exactly what the indexical/counterpart picture does; someone who doesn't should still get a good poem rather than a versified lecture. Write in American English.
