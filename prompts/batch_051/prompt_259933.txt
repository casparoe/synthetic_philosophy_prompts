I help run a small high school club on emerging technologies, and for our next meeting I want a single, carefully controlled example of how a proposed AI-safety technique could break down. As I understand it, the technique works like this: two AI systems are told to argue opposite sides of a claim in front of a human judge, and the judge decides which side has argued better; the bet is that false arguments tend to fall apart under cross-examination, so a competent judge can identify the truth without being an expert. I want a case where that bet fails, but fails for structural reasons rather than because anyone was lazy or dishonest.

The case should be realistic: a false but well-argued side should defeat a true but plainly argued side, subject to four constraints. First, the false side never states a falsehood and never hides evidence the judge explicitly asks about; it misleads only through selection, emphasis, framing, or the order in which considerations are presented. Second, the judge is competent, motivated, and allowed to ask questions. Third, neither debater is more skilled than a strong human debater; there is no superhuman persuasion. Fourth, the underlying claim could be checked afterward, so the wrong verdict is detectable in principle rather than being a case where the truth is simply unknowable. After presenting the scenario, walk through the actual moves each side makes, explain why the judge ends up preferring the false side without any constraint being violated, and identify the single feature of the setup that lets persuasion and accuracy come apart in this way. Then say whether a defender of the technique could close this gap by adding a rule to the debate protocol, and whether that added rule would merely create a new opening for obfuscation. The case should produce the wrong verdict from the structure of the debate itself, not from stipulating that the judge is foolish or overwhelmed.
