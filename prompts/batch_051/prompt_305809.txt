A friend in my metaphysics seminar walked me through an argument on Thursday and I cant find the exit. Every step feels forced, but the conclusion is something I refuse to believe.

(1) I come from a particular sperm and a particular egg. If a different sperm had fertilized that egg, or if my parents had conceived a month later when a different egg was released, the child wouldn't have been me — it would've been a different person, maybe my sibling, but not me. (2) Which sperm wins is a matter of microscopic timing and chance; an hour's difference, a minor stress spike, probably a different sperm gets there first. (3) So my existence is like a coin standing on edge: for almost any small change in the run-up to my conception, I never exist. (4) But our moral judgments dont treat conception timing that way. Suppose one couple postpones conception by a month because they're exhausted from moving apartments; nobody thinks the child who is eventually born was wronged by the delay. Now suppose another couple, both carriers for a severe recessive disorder, know that any natural conception has a one-in-four chance of producing an affected child, and they conceive anyway. The child is born with the disease, and most of us want to say that child was wronged by the decision to conceive when they did — that the parents owed it to that child to choose differently. (5) But on (1), the "different choice" wouldn't have produced a healthy version of that child; it would've produced a different child entirely. So the sick child has no alternative in which they exist without the disease; their alternative to this life isn't a better life, it's no life for them at all. (6) Then "you harmed me by conceiving me" turns out to have no comparative content, and it becomes mysterious how the parents' decision wronged the child, rather than just making the world worse in some impersonal way. (7) So origin essentialism seems to prove there is no such thing as wronging someone by conceiving them carelessly — the two couples' timing decisions collapse into the same moral kind. That cannot be right, or at least I refuse to live with it.

Where does this go wrong? I'd like to keep both the identity claim in (1) and the wronged-child judgment in the second case, but the argument seems to force me to drop one. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? My friend says (5) and (6) are just what "worse off" means, and I'm not sure I can escape them without giving up the identity claim.
