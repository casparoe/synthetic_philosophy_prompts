Two people in my theology reading group got stuck on sola scriptura last night, and they asked me to referee but I honestly couldn't. One of them, Daniel, argued that the whole point of sola scriptura is that the text itself, not a human office, is the final authority. He said hard passages just call for better exegesis, not for handing authority over to some church. Ruth fired back that "better exegesis" still has to be done by somebody, and when two readers reach opposite conclusions about the same passage, the text can't step in and say which one has it right. So private judgment doesn't remove an authority; it multiplies authorities, one per reader.

Daniel said an official interpreter has the same problem, because you'd then have to interpret the council's ruling too—you get the same regress one level up. Ruth said not exactly: a ruling settles what we do next, like who may teach and what counts as the church's public teaching, whereas private interpretation leaves everyone in their own reading with no way to close a dispute. Daniel said she was trading truth for a tiebreaker—a tiebreaker can be wrong, and then the regress just becomes organized error. Ruth said he was trading any actual resolution for the illusion that everyone is just reading the text, because in practice someone's reading always wins; the only question is whether it's an accountable body or whichever charismatic interpreter gathers the most followers.

Daniel half-conceded that disputes create a practical problem, but said that doesn't make the office's reading correct—tying is not proving. Ruth said sure, but the text alone underdetermines what counts as following it, and any Protestant appeal to "the clear meaning" just smuggles in their own tradition as if it were obvious.

Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move? For each move, say whether it's right, wrong, or partly right and why, and say what both of them are missing. And please end by saying what would change your mind about the overall verdict—what argument or consideration would flip you, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
