In Bertrand Russell’s essay *On the Notion of Cause* (reprinted in *Mysticism and Logic*), there is a passage in which he contrasts the philosopher’s demand for a cause with the way the law of gravitation relates successive states of a system, and he argues that, in the advanced sciences, the notion of cause is simply not needed. I have been trying to use that passage in a paper, and a fellow student offered me the following summary of Russell’s view:

“Russell’s essay argues that causation is a relic of a pre-scientific worldview. According to him, the real content of a causal claim, if any, is just that the first event is always followed by the second; beyond that observed regularity, there is no extra necessary connection to be found, and the idea that causes somehow produce their effects is just anthropomorphism. Physics replaces causal talk with differential equations, and since physics is our best account of reality, causal statements are not merely redundant but strictly false. Russell concludes that the word ‘cause’ should be eliminated from philosophy and from daily life, and that anyone who still asks for causes is doing mythology rather than science.”

I am not sure how much of this I should rely on. Is this a fair summary of Russell’s essay, and especially of the passage about gravitation? Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and separate what Russell actually argues, what is a common simplification, and what he explicitly or implicitly denies. I especially want to know whether the final claim—that ordinary causal statements are strictly false—follows from his point about the role of cause in physics, or whether it conflates something Russell keeps apart. At the very end, give a final line beginning “Verdict:” followed by a short phrase stating whether the summary is fair, mostly fair, or unfair, and in one clause the main distortion.
