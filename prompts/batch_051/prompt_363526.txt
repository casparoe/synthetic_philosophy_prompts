I keep trying to get clear on the complicity debate—the one about what it takes to be complicit in someone else's wrongdoing when you didn't actually cause it—and I've realized I don't want a verdict so much as a diagnosis of why the debate feels stuck. The two camps have been at it for a long time without converging, and I want to know what kind of disagreement it really is.

On one side are the causalists, who say complicity is at bottom a matter of making a causal difference, even a small or probabilistic one: you encourage, enable, lower the costs of the wrong, signal support. On the other side are the participation people, who say what matters is that you joined the venture or shared in the plan, even if your contribution was redundant. The standard examples are the extra lookout whose warning never mattered, the getaway driver who drives fine but the robbery would have come off exactly the same without him, the tenth person who throws their token weight behind a decision that's already locked in. Both camps agree almost all of these people are complicit, so the real fight is over the stranger cases: the person who does nothing but welcome the wrongdoer home, the person who would have helped but was never asked, the person whose 'help' was known to be useless at the time and was offered anyway. The causalists say those aren't complicity—they're condoning, or bad character, or wrongful association—while the participation people say no, they're still complicity.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions: the causalists are asking when blame can be attributed to a person, and the participation people are asking what it is to be the subject of a collective act. But I dont fully trust that, because they also seem to disagree about what the weird cases show, not just how to label them. Is this a verbal dispute? A disagreement about which cases count as data? A hidden empirical question about how people actually think about accomplices? And given whatever diagnosis seems right, is there anything that would move it—a distinction, a formal result about redundant causation, attention to how accomplice liability actually works—or should everyone just stop expecting the two camps to converge?
