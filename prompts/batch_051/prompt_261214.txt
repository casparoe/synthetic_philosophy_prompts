I have been trying to locate the principle behind a very common asymmetry in our legal practice, and I would like you to reason it through with me rather than settle for the usual talking points. In the United States and similar systems, we treat the public courtroom as a kind of civic achievement: a party gets an open hearing, an impartial judge, a jury in serious cases, written reasons, and at least one level of appeal, and we would be scandalized if the state simply denied all of this to a class of people by passing a statute. Yet we accept something close to that denial when it arrives through private contract. Employers, credit card companies, phone carriers, nursing homes, and rental companies routinely insert clauses requiring that any dispute, from wage theft to a defective product to an eviction or an injury, go to binding arbitration instead: no jury, no public record, an arbitrator often drawn from a panel maintained by an industry body, extremely limited review on appeal, and commonly a waiver of the right to bring or join a class action. The underlying legal claim is identical either way; what changes is the route, and the route largely depends on whether a signature was collected at the start.

Here is the asymmetry I want tested. If a state legislature passed a statute tomorrow saying that all disputes with landlords, or all employment claims, must be resolved through exactly such a mechanism, most of us would call it a denial of due process and a privatization of justice. If the same mechanism is adopted as a term in a take-it-or-leave-it contract that almost nobody reads and that nobody could realistically negotiate, the courts mostly enforce it and the public mostly shrugs, and the practice is defended in the language of freedom of contract. The only differences seem to be who imposes the rule and whether a piece of paper was signed under conditions of no real choice. Is there a principle that can justify this asymmetry, something about consent, efficiency, expertise, finality, or party autonomy, or is this an inconsistency we have decided to live with because it is convenient to powerful parties? I would like you to build the strongest defense you can, and then test it against hard cases: a clause that selects an arbitrator financially dependent on one of the parties; a class-action waiver where each individual claim is worth less than the arbitration filing fee; a statute that reproduced a company's arbitration clause word for word; and the same clause agreed to by two sophisticated commercial parties who genuinely negotiated it. The question is not whether arbitration ever works well, but whether the difference in route alone can carry the moral and legal weight we let it carry. Finally, say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up, and where you would draw the line if you tried to keep it. Please answer in continuous prose, as in a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
