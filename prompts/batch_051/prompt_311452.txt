In my philosophy of language course we just did deflationism about truth, and I cant stop turning it over. The line we got is that "is true" is not a real property — it's just an expressive device for a few jobs: endorsing a sentence without repeating it, generalizing over a bunch of claims (as in "everything she said is true"), and the anaphora thing where "that's true" points back at an earlier claim. The deflationist's conclusion is that once you've listed those jobs, there's no leftover fact about truth itself.

I keep wanting to treat this as an empirical claim about how the word is actually used rather than a conceptual stipulation. Is there a way to test it? Design the strongest test you can that would distinguish the deflationist picture from an inflationary one where truth is a substantive property doing real explanatory work. Say concretely what data you would collect, exactly which result would count for which side, and then be honest about what even a clean result would not settle. And if you think my whole framing is confused — like "is true is just a device" and "truth is a real property" arent actually rival claims because they answer different questions — tell me that plainly rather than playing along.
