I chair the academic-integrity panel at a mid-sized university, and we are about to hear the appeal in a case that has split the panel. The facts are uncontested except on the one point that matters: a graduate teaching assistant proctoring a large lecture-hall final reports seeing a student use a phone during a closed-book examination; the student acknowledges holding the phone but says she was silencing an alarm, not consulting notes, and that the proctor, who was at the front of the room, could not have seen which it was. No one else saw the incident and the paper itself is inconclusive. Our rules require a finding of misconduct only on "clear and convincing evidence," but they also allow a finding to rest on the testimony of a single witness if the panel regards the witness as credible. Half the panel takes this to mean that a single credible witness can suffice in principle; the other half argues that one person's uncorroborated word can never satisfy a demanding standard, because testimony carries weight only insofar as it is supported by something else the panel can verify. What started as a disagreement about one examination has become a proxy for the old quarrel between reductionist and anti-reductionist accounts of testimonial justification, and the two sides are now talking past each other.

The deadlock sharpened when one member circulated this passage from David Hume, An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I ("Of Miracles"): "There is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses." And a little later he adds: "Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony." The member took Hume to show that trust in testimony is an empirical achievement, earned through observed regularities and transferable only so far as the witness's own record reaches; a colleague replied that Hume is explaining why testimony is generally reliable, not prescribing that a decision-maker may never credit a single otherwise plausible report. I need the panel to move past competing glosses of Hume and decide the case.

I want to circulate a written opinion before our meeting on Thursday that actually decides the appeal rather than merely summarizing the doctrine. Please draft the decision memo: a clear recommendation to uphold the finding, overturn it, or remand for further fact-finding; the reasoning a member of the panel could defend; and a serious engagement with the strongest argument on the losing side, which I expect to be the argument that uncorroborated testimony cannot meet a "clear and convincing" standard. Write it as continuous prose, the way one colleague would write to another in a memo, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Please do not include references or case law; I will supply whatever citations are needed myself.
