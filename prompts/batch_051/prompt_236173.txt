Spent a long time arguing with a friend about whether soldiers fighting for a clearly unjust cause are still permitted to kill soldiers on the other side, and he left me with this argument I can't shake.

(1) On the standard just-war view, an unjust-side soldier who kills an enemy soldier does not commit murder; combatants on both sides are morally equal. (2) This permission can't come from the justice of the cause, since by hypothesis the cause is unjust, and it can't come from the individual enemy soldier's guilt or dangerousness, since any enemy soldier in the same role gets the same treatment. (3) So it must come from the role itself: the uniform, the rank, membership in an army at war, each side accepting the same risk. (4) But if the role is what does the work, the permission should show up wherever the role shows up. Two drug cartels that declared themselves at war, adopted uniforms, ranks, and standing rules of engagement, would thereby make it permissable for their members to kill each other. (5) That's absurd — a cartel gunman in a uniform is still a murderer. (6) So the standard view is false: a soldier on the unjust side kills wrongly after all, and the moral equality of combatants is a cover story.

I'd rather keep (1) than accept (4)-(5), but I can't say which step goes wrong or what I'd be paying. Where does this argument slip, if it does? And if it doesn't slip, which premise is least costly to give up?
