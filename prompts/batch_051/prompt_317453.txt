I am a retired atmospheric scientist, and after a career spent with climate models and observational uncertainty, I have lately been reading philosophy of probability and decision. A puzzle has crystallized for me around an institutional practice that is now common in and beyond the sciences: prediction tournaments and online forecasting platforms that require each participant to state a single numerical probability for each event—say, 0.61 for a particular election outcome, 0.44 for a particular volcanic eruption within a given year—and that score the forecast with a quadratic penalty: (1−p)² if the event occurs, p² if it does not. The designers emphasize that the rule is proper, meaning that a forecaster with a definite subjective probability for the event can do no better in expectation than to report exactly that number; hedging, rounding, or shading can only lower the expected score.

What I want to understand is what this practice presupposes about rational opinion. It appears to take for granted that, for any well-posed event whose outcome will be known, a competent rational inquirer has—or should be able to produce—a unique real number that represents their degree of belief. Yet much of my working life involved deep uncertainty in which that assumption seemed false. When the models diverged, when a parameter was only weakly constrained, or when the evidence was thin, my colleagues and I would often have been willing to say only that a probability lay somewhere in an interval, perhaps between 0.3 and 0.6, and would have treated any particular point inside that interval as an artifact of being forced to choose rather than as a statement of belief. The scoring mechanism has no place for that kind of response; it can only score a point.

So my question is this. Does a proper scoring rule, built into an institution in this way, strictly presuppose the view that rational degree of belief is always precise and single-valued? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined—consistent with the view that rational belief may be represented by a set or interval of probabilities, so long as some point is selected for the bookkeeping? If the latter, can the usual argument for the rule's optimality even be formulated, given that it depends on maximizing a single expectation under one probability distribution? And if the institution were redesigned to permit interval-valued forecasts, what would a principled scoring system look like: would there be a natural analogue of propriety for intervals, and would such a system reward accuracy, calibration, informativeness, or some further virtue? I would be grateful for a reconstruction of the implicit theory, with care taken to separate what the mechanism strictly presupposes from what it merely assumes for administrative convenience.
