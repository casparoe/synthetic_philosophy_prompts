I've more or less come round to a version of the competitiveness-constraint view over the last year, and I want to stress-test it properly. The position, roughly, is this: capability is what wins customers, compute, and hires, so if a lab voluntarily pays a meaningful alignment tax — extra staff on interpretability that never ships, refusing to deploy something profitable because the risk story is unresolved, slowing a training run for evals and red-teaming — it gets undercut by labs that dont. Over time the selection pressure pushes the industry toward whichever actor is most willing to skip the safety spend, and the only things that can hold that back are binding regulation, genuine coordination, or safety that more than pays for itself in the product. I take the argument to be strongest where the tax is real and visible (added latency, delayed launches, higher cost per token) and weakest where safety and capability are entangled, like a model that's more commercially useful precisely because it refuses certain harmful requests without being asked.

I want you to play it straight and tell me what would count against this view. Not the cheap stuff — the fact that labs have safety teams or publish papers on alignment — but the observations, formal results, historical analogies from other industries, and changes in market structure that should genuinely lower my confidence. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which are live rather than merely logically possible. I also want an honest verdict on whether the view is falsifiable at all. Have I left myself an escape hatch — for instance, by being able to reinterpret any observed voluntary safety spending as really being capability spending under another name, or by saying the constraint hasn't had time to bite yet — such that no actual market behaviour could disconfirm it? If so, is that a problem for a claim like this, or is that just the normal situation for claims about long-run selection pressures? For what it's worth, I'd find it most persuasive if the counterevidence came from a real industry where a costly restraint with diffuse or delayed benefits survived under competition for a long time without regulation, so I'd especially appreciate cases like that.
