I've been arguing with my friend for years about who actually deserves out-of-work benefits, and I've started writing down her calls. Her record so far: a 58-year-old steelworker laid off when the plant closed, thirty years of contributions — yes, full benefits, no conditions. A 24-year-old who has never worked, lives with his parents, and applies for benefits while sending no applications anywhere — no. A single mother of two doing under-the-table cleaning while claiming — should lose everything. A homeless ex-prisoner with a heroin habit, never had a steady job, says he couldn't hold one — no, at least until he's clean. A woman whose husband just left her, has barely worked since university, $20k in savings — no, until the savings are mostly gone. A 49-year-old with eleven years of contributions and poorly documented chronic back pain — yes, but only with regular reassessments to confirm he still can't work.

What would she say about a 40-year-old with twenty-two years of full contributions who quit her job a year ago because she wanted a break, lived off her savings, and is now claiming while refusing the only job she's been offered, part-time café work, because she says it's beneath her? I'm less interested in the yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her record about as well, and where they come apart on this case. Then give me your best prediction, how confident you'd really be, and the one further case you'd ask her about to pin the rule down. And keep two things separate: what's just your inference about her from what's actually a defensible position on benefits, and the settled facts from the genuinely contested principles — I don't want one contested theory of deservingness or conditionality presented as if everyone who works on this agrees on it.
