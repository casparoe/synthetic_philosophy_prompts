Two people in my reading group got into it about AI interpretability and I genuinely cant tell who's right. The argument started when Dan said the whole field has a recursion problem: to explain a model you need an interpreter at least as smart as the model, because a dumber interpreter won't be able to represent parts of what the bigger model is doing. But then the interpreter is just as opaque as the thing it's explaining, so you've moved the problem up a level, not solved it. Priya said that's like saying you need a mathematician as good as a theorem prover to check its proofs, but a tiny proof checker verifies proofs found by a much stronger system — checking is easier than generating. Dan came back: a proof checker only works because there's a formal spec of what counts as a proof, and it never has to say where the proof came from or why it works. A neural net has no such spec, so there's nothing small and formal to check an explanation against. Priya said fine, but you can still intervene: propose a local hypothesis about what some circuit or attention head does, knock it out or patch it, and see if the behavior changes the way the hypothesis predicts. That's checking, not generating. Dan said but the interpreter has to generate the candidate hypotheses in the first place, and a weaker interpreter can only generate hypotheses from it's own limited vocabulary — it'll find the human-shaped patterns it already knows and miss the weird strategies a bigger model actually uses, so it'll produce plausible stories rather than mechanisms. Priya said that's a worry about science in general, not a recursion limit: a physicist is dumber than the universe and still explains plenty of it, piece by piece. Dan said the universe doesn't try to outsmart you, but a smarter model can be adversarially opaque to a weaker one. Priya said he'd just assumed the model is trying to hide, which is exactly the thing in question. Then Dan said: suppose you do train an interpreter as capable as the original. Now you need to interpret the interpreter, and if you keep going you get a tower of explainers each as opaque as the last, so you never bottom out. Priya said you could design each level to be more interpretable than the last, so opacity decays instead of being preserved. Dan said designing for interpretability usually trades away capability, so then you're not explaining the original model anymore, you're explaining a crippled stand-in.

I want a point-by-point verdict: which of these moves are actually right, which are wrong, and what are they both missing? Don't just tell me who wins — adjudicate each step of the argument as it was actually made.
