A friend of mine has lately been defending a strongly reductionist reading of the Buddhist no-self doctrine, and last night, after a long argument, he pressed the chariot analogy on me. He argued that the word chariot designates nothing real over and above the axle, wheels, pole, yoke, and other parts in a certain arrangement, and that the word self works the same way: it is nothing but a convenient label for the five aggregates — bodily form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — in a certain causal interplay. From this he concluded that there is no self that persists through time, owns experiences, or is responsible for actions in any ultimate sense; there is only a conventionally designated person assembled from impersonal parts.

I do not want to settle the underlying debate yet. I want to know how much work this analogy can actually do. Audit it for me. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what between the chariot and the person, and then identify where the mapping weakens or breaks. In particular, which features of a chariot does the argument quietly import into the case of the self — that a chariot is an artifact made by an external maker for a purpose, that its parts are already complete objects, that it has no first-person perspective, that its persistence is nothing but the continued arrangement of those parts — and which of these disanalogies matter for the move from chariot being a mere designation to self being a mere designation? Which are distractions? Then distinguish the strongest and the weakest no-self conclusions the analogy can bear, and tell me whether my friend's stronger conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the chariot is ultimately the wrong analogy, say what a better one would be and what it would change.
