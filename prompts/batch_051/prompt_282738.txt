I work as a case investigator for a canonical tribunal that reviews alleged miraculous healings submitted in support of beatification causes. The job involves receiving testimony from physicians, family members, and sometimes the person healed, weighing the records against what medicine can explain, and recommending whether the claim can be forwarded for further examination. A few days ago I had a long argument with a philosopher friend about whether the tribunal’s work is rationally defensible at all. She pressed the usual Humean point: a miracle is a violation of a law of nature, and the evidence for the law of nature is itself prior uniform experience. But then she quoted a passage I had not paid much attention to, from David Hume’s An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I: “In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence.” She said this is not merely an anti-miracle argument; it is a description of how any careful examiner should weigh testimony against background experience, and it could be used as an operational method.

I am not sure she is right, but I have been wondering whether this maxim could be turned into a workable procedure for my tribunal. Write me a reusable procedure for evaluating a claim of miraculous healing, grounded in this passage and the broader principle behind it: a checklist or ordered sequence of tests that walks an investigator through balancing the evidence for the claim against the prior experience that makes the healing seem impossible. Include worked applications to two or three cases, one of which should yield a recommendation the tribunal will not like, and be explicit about the points at which the procedure stops being mechanical and a human judgment has to take over.
