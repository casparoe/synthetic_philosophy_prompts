He says numbers cant literally be sets. His argument, as I've reconstructed it:

(1) If the number 2 just is some particular set, then the question "Is 2 an element of 4?" has a determinate answer — true or false, full stop.
(2) In the usual von Neumann construction, 0 = ∅, 1 = {∅}, 2 = {∅, {∅}}, and generally each number is the set of all smaller numbers. So 4 = {0, 1, 2, 3}, and 2 ∈ 4 comes out true.
(3) In Zermelo's construction, 0 = ∅, 1 = {∅}, 2 = {{∅}}, 3 = {{{∅}}}, and so on. Here 4 = {3}, so 2 ∈ 4 comes out false.
(4) Both constructions recover the ordinary facts of arithmetic — successor, addition, multiplication, all of it.
(5) Arithmetic, and arguably nothing else in ordinary mathematical practice, picks one construction over the other.
(6) So there is no fact of the matter whether 2 is an element of 4; it is just an artefact of how we choose to encode numbers.
(7) But that contradicts (1).
(8) Therefore the supposition in (1) fails: numbers are not really sets.

He then smuggles in the positive view that numbers are places in a structure, whatever that means exactly. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and why? If it is valid, which premise is doing the real work, and do I have to accept it? Keep the answer in British English.
