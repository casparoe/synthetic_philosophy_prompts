I teach an undergrad philosophy seminar on concepts and rationality—about 14 students, one 75-minute session. I want an activity built around Bongard-style problems where the main thing students feel, before anyone names it, is that a small set of positive and negative examples radically underdetermines the concept, and that what feels like the obvious rule is mostly our simplicity bias doing invisible work. Design a card game with two or three students as rule-makers and the rest as inducers: the rule-makers pick a hidden rule, lay out six yes-cards and six no-cards, and the inducers have to classify new cards and then commit to a stated rule before the reveal. The twist I want is that the same twelve cards are chosen so that at least two quite diffrent rules fit them and diverge on the new cards, and you should include one pair of example rules at different levels of naturalness (maybe one rule that's time-indexed or grue-like, but don't use the word until the debrief). Give me materials I can print or hand-draw, the rules and scoring (no real money), a timeline for the 75 minutes, what the rule-makers should and shouldn't reveal, the debrief that maps moments of the game onto underdetermination and the role of simplicity, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of real concept induction the game cannot reproduce.
