Me and my flatmate have been going back and forth about moral particularism, and he's got an argument that seems to show Dancy-style particularism collapses into generalism, which he finds embarassing because he's the one who talked me into particularism last term. It goes:

(1) The particularist says there are no true moral principles because reasons are holistic: the same feature can count in favour of an act here and against it there, so no finite set of features is ever enough, all by itself, to settle what ought to be done.
(2) But everyone in this debate, particularists included, accepts supervenience: no moral difference without some non-moral difference.
(3) Take any actual situation in which someone ought to φ, and let F be the complete set of non-moral features of that situation (including all the contextual, relational, historical stuff).
(4) By supervenience, in any situation with exactly F, one ought to φ. So the universal claim 'in any situation with F, φ-ing is required' is true.
(5) And that universal claim just is a moral principle: it's exceptionless, it's universal, and it runs from non-moral features to a moral verdict.
(6) So particularism is committed to there being true moral principles after all — one for each situation, maybe infinitely many — and the view either refutes itself or it's just generalism with gerrymandered case-specific principles.

He says he wants to keep both holism and supervenience, so he thinks the 'no principles' bit has to go, but then he can't say what particularism is supposed to be claiming anymore. I don't have a good reply, and I'm not sure whether that means the argument is sound and particularism is dead or whether something's being smuggled in.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If you'd reject a premise, tell me which one and what rejecting it costs, and whether there's a way of keeping holism and supervenience without ending up as a generalist.
