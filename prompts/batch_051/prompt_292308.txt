Something I've never gotten to the bottom of: in everyday moral argument, "what if everyone did that?" is treated as a knockout, but the philosophers who have actually worked on universalizability have been circling each other for decades without moving. Kant saw it as a contradiction test on maxims (you can't even conceive of a world where everyone lies to get what they want, because the practice of promising — or trust — would dissolve). Hare says universalizability is built into the logic of moral language itself: to prescribe an act for myself here and now commits me to prescribing it for anyone in the same circumstances, and that alone gives you almost no substantive ethics, since a fanatic can sincerely say "let everyone act on my principle, including when it ruins me." Singer reads it as a demand of impartiality — if my interests count, your like interests must count equally — which does substantive work. Rule-consequentialists treat the question as a rough empirical probe: what would actually happen if enough people did it? And a lot of ordinary users mean something about fairness: you'd be free-riding on everyone else's restraint.

Each camp can state the others' view, and the arguments haven't changed much in a long time. My hunch is that the phrase is doing several jobs at once and that the debate stalls because the camps are answering different questions under one slogan — mostly it isnt clear whether "everyone doing it" is a logical possibility, a causal scenario, or a question about who your principle treats as equally real.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, are the sides' starting examples loading the answer, or is "what if everyone did that?" just an ill-posed question? Use the edge cases — the fanatic, the person who says "let everyone do it, I dont care," the free-rider who says "I only want to do it because most others won't" — to show what the different readings commit someone to. And say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the dispute, a version of the test that has real content, or an argument that we should stop expecting one question to do all this work.
