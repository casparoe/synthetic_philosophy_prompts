I organize a reading group in comparative cognition, and for several weeks we have been stuck on the same argument. We use the phrase "intelligent behavior" about nonhuman animals constantly, and we are confident about many particular verdicts, but we cannot state the rule behind those verdicts. We also all accept something like Morgan's canon when it is paraphrased as a methodological constraint: one should not explain an action in terms of a higher psychological faculty when a lower one explains it just as well. But "explains it just as well" and "lower" are doing all the real work, and our instructor keeps insisting that Morgan's canon is a constraint on a definition, not a substitute for one.

We use the following cases as a fixed test set. (1) A digger wasp, returning to its nest with a paralyzed cricket, leaves the prey at the entrance, goes inside to inspect, comes back for the prey, and then drags it inside. If the prey is moved a few centimeters while the wasp is inspecting, the wasp repeats the leave-inspect-return sequence, sometimes dozens of times, and never brings the prey inside; we all want this excluded as a rigid routine. (2) A rat on a variable-interval reinforcement schedule presses a lever at a steady, schedule-typical rate even when the contingencies shift; we think this is excluded. (3) A New Caledonian crow, with no prior training on wire-bending, bends a straight piece of wire into a hook and uses it to retrieve a bucket of food from a narrow tube; we want this included. (4) A western scrub jay caches food while a conspecific watches, and later, when alone, re-caches that food in a new location, but only if it has itself stolen caches in the past; the group splits on this one. (5) A slime mold assembles its body into a network connecting dispersed food sources, producing routes that resemble an efficient planned rail network; most of us think this must be excluded, but doing so makes us uncomfortable. (6) A honeybee performs a waggle dance whose angle and duration covary with the direction and distance of a food source, and nestmates that follow the dance reach the food; we are divided.

Please supply a working definition of "intelligent behavior" — or argue for a better term — that draws the line where it should be drawn. Run the definition against each of the six cases, say where it conflicts with the intuitive verdict and why we should follow the definition rather than the intuition, and state which of your verdicts is least secure. We are not looking for a literature review or a bibliography; we want a definition we can actually use when reading a new behavioral study, one that respects Morgan's canon without collapsing into the rule "when in doubt, call it a reflex."
