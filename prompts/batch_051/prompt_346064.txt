I've been reading a decision theory textbook that has a sidebar on the Allais paradox, and there's something about the gloss that doesn't sit right with me, though I can't put my finger on exactly what. The sidebar says, roughly:

Most people, asked to choose between (A) a guaranteed $1 million and (B) an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, take the sure million, even though the gamble has a higher expected value. Asked separately to choose between (C) an 11% chance of $1 million and (D) a 10% chance of $5 million with nothing otherwise, the same people mostly take the five-million gamble. This is the Allais paradox. The two pairs of gambles differ only by an 89% chance of $1 million that is common to both options in the first pair; the independence axiom of expected utility theory says such a common branch should not matter, so your choice between C and D should match your choice between A and B. Since the observed pattern violates independence, no expected-utility representation can fit it, no matter what utility function you use. The standard psychological explanation is that people put a special premium on certainty itself, treating the sure million as though it were worth more than its probability-weighted value. And because there is no rational reason to value certainty as such, the Allais pattern is a textbook example of a systematic and irrational preference reversal.

I followed the numbers and I'm pretty sure the arithmetic checks out, but I don't trust the slide from "this violates an axiom" to "therefore it's irrational." Is that actually the right lesson, or is the sidebar smuggling in a contested claim about what rationality requires? Also the bit about certainty being the explanation feels overconfident to me, since If the effect had to be about certainty, wouldn't it disappear in gambles with no sure option? Anyway, I'd like a committed read: go through the summary and say which parts are accurate, which are subtly off or overclaimed, what a defender of expected utility theory (or a critic of the textbook's inference) would push back on, and whether the final "therefore people are being irrational" conclusion survives the corrections. Don't just lay out the options—tell me where you think the summary goes wrong and what the fair version says.
