I'm preparing a worked example for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I want it to isolate a specific, under-appreciated pressure point in Jeffrey conditionalization: its dependence on the partition of the outcome space over which uncertain evidence is represented. Please construct a realistic thought experiment in which a single uncertain observation — one brief experience, not a sequence of updates — can be represented over two different but equally natural partitions of the agent's possibility space, call them P1 and P2, such that Jeffrey conditionalization yields posteriors for a single proposition H that differ by at least 0.10. Keep the case ordinary and non-science-fictional (a clinician looking at an ambiguous scan, a birder in bad light, a mechanic listening to an engine, or something else in that register), and make H practically load-bearing: it should be the proposition the agent actually needs in order to make a decision, so the divergence has stakes. I'd also like the two partitions to be on seriously equal footing — not one obviously correct fine partition and one obviously gerrymandered coarse partition, but two ways of carving up the evidence that a reasonable person could actually have settled on.

After the scenario, walk me through the mechanics as you would for a student. State the relevant prior; state P1 and P2 and the new input probabilities (the q-values) the observation supplies over each partition; say exactly what rigidity requires in each case; and compute the posterior for H both ways, showing the arithmetic. Then diagnose the case: tell me which structural feature is doing the real work — I suspect it is the choice of partition conjoined with the rigidity assumption, but I want to know why this particular case produces a large divergence rather than a small one. Next, say what happens if that feature is removed: for instance, if the agent were constrained to conditionalize on a faithful description of the sensory experience itself rather than on a propositional gloss of it, or if the partition were refined to the finest grain the agent can articulate, would the divergence collapse, and how? Finally, defend the formalism the way a serious Jeffrey-style probabilist would: would she deny that the two partitions really encode the same evidence, deny that rigidity holds for one of them, or accept the partition-dependence and argue that it is not a defect? Be concrete about which move is strongest for your case, and tell me how much of that defense you actually buy.

One more thing: I'd rather have an honest failure than a forced success. If you conclude that the task as I've described it rests on a confusion — for example, that no two genuinely natural partitions of a single observation can produce that divergence without smuggling different information into the q-values — then say so plainly and explain, instead of turning in a case that only works because one of the partitions is contrived.
