Hey—I'm the chief risk officer at a small commercial property and casualty insurer, and I chair the underwriting committee that decides which new lines we take on and at what price. We meet twice a month, the commitments usually run from half a million to several million in annualized premium, and anything over a million needs my sign-off and the CFO's. The committee is actuaries and underwriters—good with expected loss and a risk load when the claim probabilities come out of a long claims history, but shaky when the probabilities are genuinely unknown, like a new cyber product or a business-interruption line for outages we've never seen. I know there's a decision-theory literature on the split between risk and ambiguity, the Ellsberg finding that people pay to avoid unknown probabilities not just bad ones, and the idea that under ambiguity you sometimes maximize against the worst member of a set of defensible probabilities rather than against one point estimate. But I don't know how to turn that into a procedure my committee can actually run in a ninety-minute meeting, and I don't want it to become "when in doubt, price it higher"—alot of my underwriters already do that and I'm pretty sure its costing us good business.

Please write a reusable decision procedure an underwriter with no philosophy background can run on any proposed cover. It should: (1) give the questions to ask, in order, to decide whether the risk is actuarially well-behaved, ambiguous, or a mix; (2) say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (3) show how to get from a set of defensible probability estimates to a price band or a decline, using whatever the decision theory actually supports—if maxmin expected utility is the right tool, say how you'd actually run it; (4) stop with a no when it should and hand the call to a person when the remaining issue is values rather than facts. Be honest about the failure modes: where the procedure will misfire, and the cases where ambiguity aversion as a rule is just status-quo bias in a nicer suit.

Then run it on three covers: (a) fire coverage for warehouses, where we have decades of loss data; (b) cyber extortion coverage for a small city, where the loss distribution is basically guesswork; and (c) a parametric hail product for solar farms, where the only loss history is one vendor's claims study—it shows wonderfully low loss ratios, but the sample is small, the vendor stands to gain from us launching, and our actuaries disagree about whether the study's assumptions transfer to the regions we'd write. For at least one of these, give me an answer the committee won't like and explain why the procedure insists on it. American English throughout, please.
