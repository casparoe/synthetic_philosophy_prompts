I'm a software engineer (zero formal philosophy, so translate anything into terms I can give my team) on an ML infrastructure team. We've started using one model to inspect and explain another model — e.g. a small "interpreter" model tells us why our fraud classifier flagged a transaction, or why the moderation model overblocked a batch of posts. The writeups are often plausible and useful, but we've been burned: the interpreter sometimes gives a confident story about what the target is doing internally that we can't check, and the story later turns out to be wrong. Other times the interpreter only describes behavior we could've gotten from the logs, and tells us nothing about why.

I need a reusable decision procedure my on-call engineers can follow when one of these interpreter reports lands, to decide how much weight to give it before we act (patch, roll back, retrain, or sign off). Constraints: one engineer, about an hour, no formal interpretability training, and the decision has to either get accepted, get downgraded to "treat as a hypothesis only," or get escalated to a human. This happens a few times a month, and the stakes are high enough that wrong trust is worse than no explanation at all.

Some worries I want the procedure to force us to face: (1) there's a difference between an explanation that predicts what the model does and one that describes what it actually is inside — we usually need the second kind but only get the first. (2) The interpreter is itself an opaque model, so isn't this just moving the opacity up a level? If the rule is "trust an interpretation only if you've verified it," and verifying would require interpreting the interpreter, wether does it stop? (3) When target and interpreter share architecture or training data, their agreement might just be correlated blindness, not independent confirmation. (4) A story can be coherent, consistent with everything we see, and still be false — so what counts as an actual check, not just a plausible rewrite of the logs?

Then run the procedure on these three:
- A moderation model's false positives get blamed on a single shortcut (the word "gun") which the logs easily confirm; the interpreter also claims it knows why the model learned this. We can check the correlation tonight; we cannot do weight inspection without a bigger project.
- An interpreter produces a detailed mechanistic story about two internal components that collude to make the target overconfident — same architecture, mostly the same training data. The only checks available tonight are probes written by the same interpreter, and they agree with its story.
- Legal wants to use these interpreter reports as an official audit artifact for a model-risk review because the reports are readable and reassuring. Assume the interpreter is opaque and no human has independently verified the mechanism it describes.

For the first two, tell me what the on-call should conclude and why. For the third, tell me what the procedure says even if it's not what we want to hear, and mark which step forces the no. Also tell me what this procedure still cannot settle, where it's a values or risk-tolerance call rather than a factual one.
