I've been thinking about this for a while now, not for school or anything, just on my own. I read Macaulay's History of England from the Accession of James II — well, parts of it anyway — and I got a bit obsessed with how he writes the whole thing backward. Like, everything in the 17th century is judged by whether it helped bring about the Glorious Revolution and the nice constitutional settlement afterward. Magna Carta, the Reformation, the Civil War, the Exclusion Crisis: all of them get sorted into "steps toward us" or "roadblocks to us." I started an essay about this, and my draft says this way of writing history is really tempting because it makes the present feel inevitable and satisfying, but it's also a bit of a lie. It turns the past into a long training montage for a future nobody back then could have imagined, and it makes the winners of history look like they was always going to win.

I'd like comments on my notes and draft. Specifically, am I being unfair to Macaulay? Is there a defense of his approach that I'm missing? Also, I have a section where I almost sound like I'm saying "thankfully we now know better," which would be pretty ironic given my whole argument, and I don't know if I should keep that or cut it.
