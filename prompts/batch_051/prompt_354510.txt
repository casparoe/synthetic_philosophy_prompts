For the past several years I have belonged to a reading group that meets to discuss what classical Chinese texts about moral cultivation might teach a novelist about character. One dispute has recurred so often, and so unproductively, that I want a diagnosis rather than another round of it: the Mencius–Xunzi debate about human nature. The Mencian side maintains that human nature is already disposed toward goodness, the way a sprout is disposed to grow into a plant, and that virtue is principally a matter of protecting and nurturing those dispositions; cruelty and vice are what happen when the sprouts are starved or crushed. The Xunzian side maintains that our raw material is unruly, envious, and acquisitive, and that decency is an achievement of deliberate artifice—ritual, education, and institutions—laid over that material. The strange thing, for someone who builds characters for a living, is that both positions can absorb the same case without strain. When a child shows spontaneous concern for another person, the Mencian sees the sprout breaking ground; the Xunzian sees the early effects of training, or an impulse that is not yet moral until it has been reshaped. When a person behaves badly after social constraints collapse, the Xunzian sees nature revealed; the Mencian sees a sprout that was never watered. The facts never arbitrate, because each side reads them through a prior decision about what counts as nature once nurture is subtracted.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute is not an empirical one about child development but a boundary dispute about the notion of nature itself—specifically, whether there is any coherent sense in which we can subtract cultivation from a creature that is socialized from its first hours, and which side bears the burden of proof about the baseline. But I am not confident. I would like a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, in the terms that matter: whether it is verbal or substantive, whether a hidden factual issue about early development would resolve it, whether the two sides are answering different questions under one word, or whether it rests on starting intuitions neither side can argue for. Then tell me what, given the diagnosis, would actually count as progress—a distinction, an empirical finding, a different question, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
