In Derek Parfit's hitchhiker example, a traveller obtains a ride by promising to pay the driver at the destination; on arrival, paying is against the traveller's self-interest, so a consistently self-interested traveller would not pay, and a driver who knew this would not stop in the first place. That much is familiar. I have been using a distinction I cannot find drawn explicitly in the literature, and I want it checked. I call the two sides instrumental rational irrationality and constitutive rational irrationality. In the instrumental case, an agent at an earlier time deliberately arranges, or would endorse arranging, that at a later time she will reason or act in ways that are locally irrational, because that disposition makes her promises credible and thereby produces a benefit that cannot be obtained by locally rational action alone. In the constitutive case, the agent simply has a standing disposition to keep promises even when doing so is contrary to her interests; this disposition makes her trustworthy, but it was not selected or installed as a strategy by an earlier rational self. My claim is that only the instrumental case is genuinely rational irrationality. The constitutive case is better described as rational action by an agent whose preferences already include promise-keeping, and calling it irrational smuggles in the assumption that the only real preferences are self-interested ones. I think the distinction tracks a real difference in how the dispositions are maintained under reflection. Is it already present in Parfit or elsewhere under another name? What is the strongest argument that the distinction collapses, and what is the clearest example of a case that falls outside both categories or moves between them? Give me the three strongest objections to the distinction itself and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
