Mathematicians routinely reason probabilistically about facts that are not random at all. To estimate the number of twin primes up to x, for instance, one treats the events "n is prime" and "n + 2 is prime" as though they were independent, each with probability about 1/log n, even though the primality of any given integer is fully determined and the joint pattern is fixed by arithmetic. The same presumption of independence underlies heuristics for Goldbach's problem, for counts of prime k-tuples, for gaps between primes, and for a large part of conjectural number theory. Sometimes it is strikingly accurate, constants included. Sometimes it is badly wrong: the same reasoning applied to pairs (n, n + 1) predicts on the order of x/(log x)^2 prime pairs up to x, when in fact there is exactly one such pair, namely 2 and 3. Congruence conditions among a tuple of numbers create systematic dependencies, and the successful heuristics correct for them with multiplicative constants. A theory of the presumption of independence therefore has to explain how a modeling assumption that is literally false in one setting can be a reliable instrument in another.

Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of the presumption of independence in mathematical heuristics must meet. The theory may be formal or partly informal, but it must theorize this practice: when independence may be presumed, how the presumption must be qualified, and what licenses the resulting quantitative predictions. The list must handle at least these phenomena: the accuracy of simple independence heuristics in the settings where they succeed; their outright failure where a structural constraint (such as parity) makes the posited configurations impossible; the role of local correction factors; the difference between independence as a deliberate approximation and independence as a claim about the true distribution; and the requirement that any heuristic stay consistent with unconditionally proven theorems.
