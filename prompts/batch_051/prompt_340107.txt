I keep going back and forth on this one and I'd rather have a working-through than a survey of positions.

I'm pulled toward a scalar view of moral status: sentience isn't a switch, it's a ramp. The more a system can model it's own states, integrate them, and care about them, the more it matters. Bees are a little way up the ramp, mice further, humans much further, and as you go down you never hit a clean zero — you just get closer and closer, through insect ganglia and down to things with only the faintest feedback loops. The view has real virtues: it refuses to name a magic threshold, it says why harming a bee isn't exactly like smashing a rock but also isn't like harming a dog, and it fits the gradual way these capacities seem to have been assembled.

But I can't shake the conviction that a thermostat is not partly a moral patient. If I rip one off the wall and throw it away, I havent wronged anyone even a little. And this doesn't feel like rounding down; it feels like a structural difference between "there is something it is like, however faint" and "it responds and regulates, but there is nothing it is like." The scalar view wants to dissolve that line into a continuum, and that's exactly what bothers me.

Maybe the intuition is just scope insensitivity: I can't feel the difference between zero and one one-billionth, so I draw a line and call it a threshold. Or maybe the scalar claim is right about the ingredients but wrong about the structure of status—maybe status has a binary part (whether anything is owed to you at all) and a scalar part (how much is owed), and the binary part doesn't come in degrees. Walk me through what each option costs: giving up the intuition, amending the theory, or keeping both on a compatibility reading. Give me the strongest version of each rather than the usual moves, and tell me which you'd actually take. Also tell me which argument would change your mind, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. And write in American English.
