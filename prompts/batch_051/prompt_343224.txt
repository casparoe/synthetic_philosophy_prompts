I'm taking a legal theory course and got into a long argument with a friend about desuetude — the doctrine, recognized in some systems, that a criminal statute can lapse through long, open, unexcused non-enforcement. My friend says desuetude is just rule-of-law orthodoxy once you think about notice: the state shouldn't be able to punish somebody under a law that the state's own conduct taught them was a dead letter. I found that convincing until we pushed on it, and now the argument seems to prove too much. We wrote it out like this:

(1) The rule of law forbids officials from enforcing a norm against someone who had good reason to believe the norm wasn't in force.
(2) When a statute has gone unenforced and openly violated for decades, the government's own pattern of non-enforcement is good reason, maybe the best available, for an ordinary person to think the conduct isn't legally off limits.
(3) So punishing someone now under that statute would be a surprise attack, forbidden by (1), whatever the statute's text says.
(4) But the reasons in (2) don't evidently cut off at 'decades': reliance can build up much faster, and the principle in (1) says nothing about how long or how open the non-enforcement needs to be.
(5) So the same reasoning would forbid enforcement after any neglected period that produced reasonable reliance — a few years, a few months, maybe the transition period after a new legislature passes a law that a governor says her administration won't prioritize.
(6) That starts to look like official neglect functioning as a repeal power, and a legal system can't be committed to the rule of law if its also committed to letting neglect write the statute book.

I'd like to say desuetude is a real rule-of-law doctrine and not a machine for erasing any law an official can't be bothered to apply. But the argument seems to force a choice between 'unenforced laws are still fully laws' (no surprise bar at all) and 'practice is what makes law' (neglect self-ratifies). Which step or steps should I reject, what does rejecting them cost, and why did the rejected step look right in the first place? As you answer, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. And lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating the question.
