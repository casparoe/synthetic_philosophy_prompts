In the canonical Ford-in-the-office Gettier case, an office worker has abundant evidence that a colleague, A, owns a Ford: A drives one daily, has shown the worker the registration and insurance, and so on. The worker infers by existential generalization that someone in the office owns a Ford. In the standard version, A has secretly sold the Ford, and the inferred proposition is made true only because a second colleague happens to own a Ford. The standard diagnosis is that the worker has a justified true belief that falls short of knowledge because it rests on a false intermediate premise — that A owns a Ford.

Now vary the case so that the intermediate premise is true, but true only in a Gettierized way. This morning at 9, A sold the Ford she had always driven (call it F1); the sale was real. At 10 she learned that she had inherited a completely different Ford (F2) from a distant relative, and the car was delivered at 11. At noon, the worker — who has been out of the office all morning and knows none of this — forms the belief that A owns a Ford, on the strength of the long-standing pattern involving F1, and immediately infers that someone in the office owns a Ford. The premise is true (A does own a Ford, namely F2), but its truth-maker is unrelated to the worker's evidence, which tracks F1. No one else in the office owns a Ford, so the existential conclusion is true entirely because of F2. To make the modal verdicts clean, suppose the relative had nearly left F2 out of the will, so that a world in which no inheritance arrives is a near one.

Does the worker know that someone in the office owns a Ford? Work through how the main repairs of the justified-true-belief analysis handle this variation, and say where their verdicts come apart. In particular:

(1) The no-false-lemma repair: there is no false intermediate premise. Can the theorist locate a false belief anyway — for instance, a belief about F1 — and is that extension of the repair principled or ad hoc?

(2) The defeasibility approach: the proposition that A sold F1 this morning is true and would defeat the worker's justification for the premise. Does it also defeat the existential conclusion, and does the independent arrival of F2 restore justification in a way that matters?

(3) Sensitivity and safety: give their verdicts carefully, spelling out the nearby worlds in which the conclusion is false or in which the same belief is formed.

(4) Process and virtue-theoretic accounts: the step from premise to conclusion is valid, but the premise is only luckily true. Is the target belief produced by a dependable process or true because of the worker's competence?

Then assess what the variation shows. Does it merely relocate the original problem one step upstream, or does it demonstrate something the false-lemma diagnosis was supposed to rule out — that the Gettier flaw can be transmitted through valid inference from a true premise that falls short of knowledge?

Close by saying where your verdict is least stable: which move by a defender of one of these analyses would most plausibly change your mind, and what consideration is most likely to flip your answer about whether the worker knows.
