The position I want stress-tested is a Deweyan account of democracy as ongoing experimental inquiry, and I want the test to be honest rather than sympathetic. The view, as I understand it, has four commitments:

1. A public is not a pre-existing people, nation, or electorate. A public is constituted when associated activity produces consequences for people not directly party to it, and the task of that public is to notice, articulate, and regulate those consequences.

2. The legitimacy of a collective decision is a function of how it is formed, not of its matching an independently given standard of the common good. A decision is warranted to the degree that it issues from an ongoing experimental process: a problem is formulated, alternative courses of action are proposed as hypotheses, their anticipated and actual consequences are compared, and the problem and the standards of success are themselves revised in light of what the experiment turns up.

3. Expertise and technical science enter this process as instruments within public inquiry, not as an authority over it. The definition of the problem, the judgment of which consequences count as harms or improvements, and the decision to continue or reopen the inquiry belong to the public constituted by those consequences. This is meant to avoid both technocracy and brute majoritarianism: experts propose and test, but the testing itself is public and revisable.

4. There is no criterion of the public interest outside this experimental process by which outcomes could be scored in advance. "Better" and "worse" are given content only within and through the process, much as warranted assertibility in science is fixed by inquiry rather than by a prior definition of truth.

I am asking you to map the view's exposure: what would have to be true about politics, cognition, or social organization for this position to be false, or to collapse into one of its rivals — technocracy, interest-group pluralism, Schumpeterian competitive elitism, or the view that democratic legitimacy just is deference to well-designed epistemic institutions? I do not want the standard dismissals (that it is utopian about citizen competence, too demanding, or merely vague about how to individuate consequences); I want the deeper defeaters. Which of its commitments would defenders themselves concede as vulnerable under pressure, and which would they treat as definitional and therefore not open to refutation? I would find it especially damaging if the view could be shown to be circular in problem-definition: if the public that is supposed to define a problem is itself constituted by consequences whose description already presupposes a contested framing of that problem, so that the experimental process cannot get started without settling in advance what it purports to discover.

Please give me a committed assessment rather than a survey of options. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish evidence that should decisively move a Deweyan from evidence that is only embarrassing, say which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and give a clear verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or simply the price of a non-foundationalist politics.
