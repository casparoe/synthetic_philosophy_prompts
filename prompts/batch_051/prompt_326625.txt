For a while I treated the claim that technologies have politics as a clever slogan and nothing more. I have changed my mind. I now think at least some artifacts can have politics, by which I mean something stronger than the obvious point that technologies are designed, funded, and used inside social arrangements. I mean that a device, a piece of infrastructure, or a technical system can have a form of power built into its material configuration: it can require, or systematically and strongly favor, some distributions of authority, some patterns of access and exclusion, or some scales of organization rather than others. The cases that keep me here are the familiar ones: the much-discussed story of parkway bridges supposedly built too low for buses, the claim that large-scale nuclear electricity tends to demand centralized and hierarchical control while dispersed solar technologies sit more comfortably with decentralized arrangements, and the mechanical tomato harvester, which was not merely adopted by large farms but seems to have changed what kinds of farming could survive.

What I want is not a debate summary or another round of examples. I want an accounting of what would genuinely count against the strong version of this view. Which observations about a technology's history of use, adaptation, or redesign would show that the politics I am attributing to the artifact really lives in the surrounding institutions, laws, and choices, and that the object itself is inert with respect to those outcomes? What would a committed defender of the view concede as a real difficulty or counterexample, as opposed to a criticism they would dismiss as attacking a strawman? Are there conditions under which the view becomes insulated from disconfirmation, and would that be a defect rather than a feature? Give me a map of its exposure: defeaters that would be fatal, ones that would be merely awkward, and the places where defenders could plausibly say the view risks nothing at all, and say what it would take, in each case, for the view to give ground. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
