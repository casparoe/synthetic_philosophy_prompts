I work as a novelist, and I have a fairly precise working vocabulary for my craft: showing and telling, scene and summary, concrete detail, sensory immediacy, filter words such as she saw or he realized, point of view, free indirect style, and the workshop maxim show, don't tell. That maxim has always struck me as a piece of epistemology disguised as technique. It presumes that a stretch of unmediated sensory rendering can put reality before the reader without the intrusive conceptual gloss of a narrator, as though the concrete detail were simply given rather than selected, categorized, and judged. I'm told Wilfrid Sellars attacked a philosophical version of this assumption under the name Myth of the Given: the idea that experience contains a layer — sense-impressions, immediate sensory awareness — that is non-inferential, prior to all concepts, and capable of grounding empirical knowledge.

I want you to restate Sellars's critique of the Given in my craft vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. In particular: (1) Sellars distinguishes the causal order from the justificatory or rational order; render this as the difference between what prose causally does to a reader and what it gives a reader reason to conclude. (2) Sellars claims that talk of how things look or appear is conceptually parasitic on talk of how things are — that one cannot master looks-red without the concept is-red and the ability to treat some circumstances as standard. Render this as a critique of filter words and of the pretense of a neutral, purely perceptual point of view. (3) Sellars's positive picture is that nothing counts as evidence or justification unless it can stand in the space of reasons, answerable to other claims and open to revision. Render this as the difference between a detail that merely triggers a reader's association and a detail that earns a thematic claim.

Then give me the residue report. Which of Sellars's claims translate cleanly into the craft vocabulary, which translate only if they are distorted, and which have no counterpart at all in the way a novelist thinks about prose? Finally, I want your considered judgment on my suspicion: is show, don't tell a local instance of the Myth of the Given, or do the aesthetic doctrine and the epistemological target merely resemble one another and come apart at some crucial point? I am less interested in a verdict than in precision about where the analogy holds and where it fails.
