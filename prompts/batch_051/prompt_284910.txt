I keep going back and forth on this, and I'd rather be walked through the forced choice than get the usual "it's both/and" handwave. For a few years now I've been doing historical-critical work on the Hebrew Bible — source criticism, the documentary hypothesis, deutero-Isaiah, comparisons with Hittite treaty forms, the lot — and as a method it's the most satisfying thing I've ever used. It has one rule: explain the text the way you'd explain any other ancient Near Eastern document. No special pleading, no quietly stopping the inquiry when it gets theologically uncomfortable. And the rule pays off: real, cumulative results, and when scholars disagree there's somewhere to go, namely the evidence. I don't want to give that up.

But I also can't shake something. When I read a passage as revelation — the lawgiving at Sinai, say, or the prophets — it doesn't feel like I'm adding a devotional layer on top of the historical facts, like icing. It feels like the text is addressing me now, and treating it only as an artifact produced by a scribal school is looking past something that's actually there. The method, pushed all the way, seems to require me to bracket that out — to read the text as not-revelation even when it is.

So here's the conflict: I have a method with all the theoretical virtues (one rule, no exceptions, real explanatory power) and a firm intuition it can't coexist with. Which should yield? Should I debunk the intuition as a category error or a scope confusion between "reading as history" and "reading as scripture"? Should I amend the method — historical criticism for the human history, a separate theological reading for faith — and if so, what happens when they conflict? Or is there a reading on which they're compatible without either side going soft? Don't just pick a side; tell me what each move costs and what would justify it. And at the very end, put your bottom line in \answer{...} with a short label — "debunk the intuition," "amend the method," "different levels," or whatever it actually is.
