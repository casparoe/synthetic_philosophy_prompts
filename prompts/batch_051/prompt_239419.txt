I've been prepping a unit on decision-theoretic puzzles, and a textbook one of my colleagues uses glosses the two-envelope paradox like this: "You're shown two envelopes, one containing twice as much money as the other. You pick one and see it contains $X. The other envelope is equally likely to contain $2X or $X/2, so the expected value of switching is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, which is larger than X. So you should always switch; but the same reasoning works for whichever envelope you opened, which can't be right. The standard resolution is that the two cases can't actually be equally likely, since that would require a uniform distribution over all possible dollar amounts, so the paradox shows probability theory breaks down when the amounts are unbounded." I'm suspicious of that last move — or at least I suspect its doing a lot of work — but I can't quite say what else is off. Is this a fair summary? I'd like an accuracy audit: what's right, what's subtly wrong, and whether the "uniform distribution over all amounts" line is the real resolution or a red herring. In particular, can the paradox be dissolved while keeping the amounts finite?
