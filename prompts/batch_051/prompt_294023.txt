Analyze the U.S. housing bubble of the mid-2000s — specifically the markets for mortgage-backed securities, collateralized debt obligations, and credit default swaps — through the lens of the no-trade theorems associated with Milgrom and Stokey. The standard lesson of those theorems is that when rational agents share a prior and each knows the other is willing to trade, the very offer conveys information: each party should update against the trade until purely information-driven gains disappear, so speculation on differences in private information alone should unravel. Yet in the run-up to 2008 these markets sustained enormous trading volume in instruments whose value depended on house-price paths across the whole country. I am not asking for a narrative of the crisis. I want a disciplined mapping: which of the era's trades can be understood as hedging, liquidity provision, or risk transfer that the theorem does not rule out, and which must be understood as speculative bets on house prices; which assumption — common priors, common knowledge of rationality, or the purely informational character of the disagreement — fails in those trades; and what the episode shows about the theorem itself. Does the crisis refute the no-trade result, confirm it by showing how exceptional the prevailing epistemic situation was, or reveal that the theorem was never a description of real speculative markets? Finally, does the episode undercut the broader idea that markets aggregate dispersed information whenever people are free to trade?
