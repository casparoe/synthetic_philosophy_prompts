I'm a science journalist working on a piece about why pain is bad, and I keep running into the following argument from a rare neurological condition. Pain asymbolia is a syndrome in which a patient can tell you where a noxious stimulus is, how intense it is, and whether it is sharp or burning, yet shows none of the usual signs of suffering: they do not wince or withdraw as expected, and they may report the sensation while remaining wholly untroubled by it. The argument goes:

(1) Pain asymbolia patients are genuinely in pain — they detect, localize, and correctly describe the noxious stimulus.
(2) Their pain is not unpleasant or aversive; they are entirely untroubled by it.
(3) Therefore pain can occur without its felt badness.
(4) If pain were intrinsically bad — bad in itself, simply in virtue of being pain — then every episode of pain would be bad for the person in it.
(5) Therefore pain is not intrinsically bad; whatever makes ordinary pain bad must be supplied by something over and above the pain itself, presumably a further evaluative or motivational response.

I half-suspect that the word 'bad' slides between two senses somewhere, but when I try to pin the slide down the argument looks valid to me. Is it valid? If it is valid, is each premise true, and if not, which premise is weakest? If it is not valid, say exactly which step fails, under what reading of 'pain' or 'bad,' and whether the argument can be repaired to reach the same conclusion. Keep the answer under 450 words.
