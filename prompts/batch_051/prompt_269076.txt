I have been arguing with colleagues about what the standard cases of simultaneous invention—Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection—actually show, and the disagreement has made me think the usual lessons drawn from them are much less stable than people assume. One camp treats such cases as evidence that discoveries are largely inevitable once a field reaches a certain state, so individual priority is mostly a lottery and credit practices are somewhat arbitrary. Another camp insists that the famous cases are selected after the fact and that the differences in how each man formulated and justified the idea matter scientifically, not merely historically. I am planning a long essay on this for a general but philosophically literate audience, and I do not want it to become another survey of the two familiar examples. I want the essay to defend a claim about which apparent implications of simultaneity are actually supported once you look past the folklore.

Suppose I were going to build the essay around the two or three implications that, in your view, most deserve attention and are least overrated. Which should they be, and why those rather than the alternatives? The implications I could foreground include the revision of credit and priority norms in science, the case for funding broad problem-driven research over individual genius, the epistemic function of priority disputes, the role of shared conceptual frameworks as distinct from individual creativity, and the limits of counterfactual claims that the discovery would have happened anyway. I want you to rank the candidates for what the essay should emphasize, and defend the ordering. For each implication you recommend, say what would have to be true for it to follow from the standard cases, whether those cases actually supply that condition, what would count against it, and which candidate implications you would explicitly set aside as overrated or insufficiently supported. In your answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
