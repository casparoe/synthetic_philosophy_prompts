You are a philosopher who drafts formal opinions for institutional ethics committees and who works on the ethics of neural technology, mental privacy, and cognitive liberty. Think through the case carefully before settling on a recommendation; in particular, work out the two or three strongest considerations on each side and name them explicitly before committing.

I chair my university's research ethics board. We are reviewing a proposal from the psychology and education departments for a longitudinal study of attention in large lecture courses. Participating undergraduates would wear a consumer-grade dry-electrode EEG headset during class once a week across a semester, while the device streams raw electrical activity from the scalp to a commercial cloud platform. The researchers say they will analyze the recordings only for sustained attention, cognitive load, and mind-wandering. The consent form states that data will be "used to study attention" and may be shared in "de-identified" form with other researchers. Students receive a small amount of extra credit for participating, with a written alternative assignment available.

Several board members are uneasy. Raw EEG recordings are unusually rich: they can be re-analyzed to infer fatigue, emotional valence, and in some cases markers associated with psychiatric vulnerability, and a person's neural response patterns can function as a biometric identifier that, unlike a password, cannot be revoked or rotated. Removing names and timestamps may not amount to genuine de-identification, since the recordings contain a unique physiological signature tied to a known course, term, and seating cohort, and since the cloud platform's terms permit the vendor to use uploaded data to improve its own affect-recognition models. Other members reply that the study is low-risk, that treating EEG as categorically different from voice, face, or heart-rate data would make useful observational research impossible, and that consenting adults in a low-stakes educational setting should be trusted to make their own choices.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve subject to specified conditions — with a clear rationale, conditions the researchers could actually implement, and an honest engagement with the strongest considerations on the losing side. The committee is not a court: ground the recommendation in the ethics of mental privacy and cognitive liberty while keeping it administratively actionable.
