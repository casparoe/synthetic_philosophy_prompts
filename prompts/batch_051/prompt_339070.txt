I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly writing longform pieces about risk, uncertainty, and the ways economists and statisticians model decisions. I've been asked to lead a single two-hour session for a reading group of fellow science writers and editors — people comfortable with quantitative arguments but untrained in economics or probability theory. We will read the following passage from Ole Peters and Murray Gell-Mann, "Evaluating Gambles Using Dynamics":

"Expectation value maximizers are defined as rational in economics, but expectation values are only meaningful in the presence of ensembles or in systems with ergodic properties, whereas decision-makers have no access to ensembles and the variables representing wealth in the usual growth models do not have the relevant ergodic properties."

The passage will be the anchor for the session. I don't want to lecture through it sentence by sentence; I want a teaching plan that gets the group to see why this criticism of expected-value thinking is not a minor technical point about probability but a shift in what rationality means for a single life lived over time. Please design the session's explanatory arc: what concept has to be in place before they can feel the force of the passage; the one repeated-gamble example that should carry the idea and what it must show; the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring — for instance, that utility functions are merely a description of psychological risk aversion, or that the problem is about irrationality or cognitive bias — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and an end-of-session check that would tell me they have genuinely distinguished a time average from an ensemble average rather than just been startled by the example. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and say so. Lead with the bottom line of your teaching plan in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
