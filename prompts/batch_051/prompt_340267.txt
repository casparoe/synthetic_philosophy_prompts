I am a high school student, and I spend a lot of time programming. I have noticed a parallel between two things I think about, and I want help deciding whether it is real or just pattern-matching.

In philosophy there is a family of views called conceptual engineering. Its starting point is that concepts are tools with functions: they classify things, guide inference, and coordinate action. When a concept does this badly—because it is vague, incoherent, or built around a false or harmful picture—an ameliorative analysis asks not what the concept already means but what it should mean in order to do its job well. For example, we might revise the concept of race, or of a planet, or of marriage, so that the revised concept better serves the purposes we actually have for it, even if that departs from ordinary use.

In programming, refactoring is changing a system's structure, or replacing a bad abstraction, while preserving or improving the behavior the system actually needs. Existing code is like current usage: something you study but are not bound by. The specification, or the purpose the code is supposed to serve, is like the function the concept is supposed to perform. And when you deliberately change behavior because the old specification was wrong, that is like fixing a bug: you cannot correct the concept while leaving all of its old behavior intact.

This looks to me like more than a loose analogy. Both treat the current implementation as disposable in light of a function. Both distinguish what something actually does from what it should do. And both face the same continuity problem: when is a reworked concept still the same concept, rather than a new word for something else? But I also worry I am pattern-matching. Concepts are not executed, their specifications are not written down anywhere, and conceptual change has no test suite.

Please give me a committed answer, not a survey of options: is this a genuine structural identity, a superficial analogy, or a confusion? State the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between software engineering and the philosophy of concepts, and identify at least one concrete thing the parallel would predict that is false.
