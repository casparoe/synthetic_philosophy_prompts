A friend and I keep having the same argument about big history, and last night it ran until 2am without either of us moving an inch. He's a Harari-and-Diamond person: he thinks Sapiens and Guns, Germs, and Steel are worth defending because they take an unbelievably messy historical record and make it intelligible, and that critics who just tick off counterexamples are missing the point of what a grand narrative is for. I'm on the Graeber and Wengrow side, more or less: The Dawn of Everything convinced me that these big stories mostly work by deciding on the punchline first and then selecting cases that fit, so they don't really compress history as much as hide the parts that don't fit. The strange thing is we agree on nearly all the object-level stuff when we actually check: Diamond gets specific claims wrong, Harari oversimplifies, and Graeber and Wengrow have there own cherry-picking. Same agreed facts, same books, no movement at all.

That's what I want a diagnosis of, not a verdict on who's right. My hypothesis is that it isn't really about the history: my friend is willing to pay a lot of false detail for a story that compresses and orients you, while I'd rather have a messy catalogue of cases than a clean story whose spine might be false. But maybe that's too charitable to me, and there's a genuine factual disagreement underneath about how much room geography and ecology actually leave for political contingency. So what kind of disagreement is this? Is it about standards of explanation, about how much error a generalization is allowed to buy, or about something else entirely? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually count as progress for one of us, short of the other just giving in?
