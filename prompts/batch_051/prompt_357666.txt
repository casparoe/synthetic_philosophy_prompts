I'm in a high school philosophy elective and we did a unit on science vs. pseudoscience, and we read a couple pages from Paul Feyerabend's *Against Method*. One bit that jumped out at me is when he says "All methodologies have their limitations and the only 'rule' that survives is 'anything goes'." A friend of mine said this is just edgy rhetoric and that Feyerabend is cheating by making "method" mean something too strict. I tried to turn his point into an actual step-by-step argument to see whether it holds up. Here's what I got:

(1) Any proposed universal rule of scientific method—for example, "when an experiment contradicts a theory, reject the theory"—has at least one major historical case where following that rule would have slowed or blocked scientific progress.

(2) If following a rule would have slowed or blocked progress in an actual historical case, then that rule is not something all scientists are rationally required to follow in all cases.

(3) Therefore, no proposed universal rule of scientific method is rationally required for all scientists in all cases.

(4) If there is a real scientific method, it just is a set of rules that all scientists are rationally required to follow in all cases.

(5) So there is no real scientific method, and the only thing left standing is Feyerabend's "anything goes."

I cant tell whether I've actually proved the conclusion or whether step (4) is doing something sneaky, like attacking a strawman version of scientific method. So my question is: is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what is the strongest repair a defender of scientific method could make to that step? Or if it is valid, is the conclusion as radical as it sounds?
