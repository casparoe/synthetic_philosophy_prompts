Please take the role of a moral philosopher who works on the structure and justification of rights. I am a high school student writing an essay for a philosophy competition, and I have built the following argument. Each step felt right to me when I wrote it, but the conclusion -- that the concept of rights should be abandoned -- seems unacceptable, so I suspect there is a mistake somewhere. Please think carefully, brainstorm the possible diagnoses before committing, and then tell me exactly which premise or inference you reject, why it looked correct, and what the least costly repair would be. I am not asking for a survey of positions; I want a diagnosis of this particular chain of reasoning.

Here is the argument.

(1) If rights are real and not merely rhetorical decorations, then any right must be one of three things: a trump (a constraint that ordinary appeals to aggregate welfare cannot override), a side-constraint (a constraint on how I may treat someone whose point is agent-relative, so that I may not violate it even in order to minimize violations of the same constraint overall), or an instrument (a labeled cluster of interests or protections to be weighed against other considerations).

(2) If a right is a trump or a side-constraint, then I am forbidden to violate one person's right even when violating it would prevent five otherwise unavoidable violations of the same right: the trump rules out overriding it for aggregate welfare, and the side-constraint rules out my violating it even as a means of minimizing violations.

(3) But that is a deeply strange result. It treats the one violation I would commit as more important than the five I would prevent, not because any person is treated better, but only because the one would be mine. That looks more like an agent's concern for the purity of his own conduct than like respect for the rights of the five.

(4) If rights are instruments, then saying "that violates my right" adds nothing to saying "that harms or sets back an interest of mine in a way that should matter." The right is just a pointer to the underlying interests and can be balanced away whenever enough good is at stake.

(5) Therefore, any right is either something we should refuse to respect in exactly the cases where respect seems most important, or something that does no independent explanatory work.

(6) A concept that faces this dilemma should be abandoned rather than kept at the center of moral and political thought.

Where does this argument go wrong? If you reject a premise, say which and explain how the resulting account of rights avoids the dilemma. If the argument is valid but rests on a false premise, tell me which premise is least costly to give up. I am especially interested in whether (5) suppresses a third possibility -- for example, a view on which rights can be infringed in extreme circumstances without being the sort of thing that can be balanced away in ordinary cases -- and whether that third possibility is stable or merely relocates the problem. I most want to keep the idea that rights are not just rhetorical decoration, but I do not want to keep it by stipulation.
