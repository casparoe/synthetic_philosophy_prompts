I'm a journalist who covers ideas and public policy, and I've been collecting my own gut verdicts on when the state's taking of money or property amounts to theft. Here they are as I currently find them. A progressive income tax is not theft; neither is a broad sales tax. A fine for speeding is not theft; neither is court-ordered restitution for damage I caused. A customs duty is not theft. A fee for a license to sell alcohol is not theft. But a license "fee" set far above the cost of administration and openly intended to raise revenue feels like theft, even though it is legally a fee. Civil asset forfeiture, where police seize cash from someone who is never charged, feels like theft. A tax that funds a war I believe is unjust is not theft. A poll tax is not theft, though it is plainly wrong in some other way. I go back and forth on an exit tax on renouncing citizenship: it can reach wealth accumulated long before leaving, yet the state claims it is a final contribution for the legal and economic infrastructure one used. I also cannot settle whether a "solidarity surcharge" on high earners, explicitly framed as taking from them to benefit strangers, differs in principle from the war tax. Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or most of them? State it precisely, show what it says about cases I haven't listed—say, an annual wealth tax, an ordinary property tax, seigniorage from central-bank money creation, and a congestion charge on driving in a city center—and tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up.
