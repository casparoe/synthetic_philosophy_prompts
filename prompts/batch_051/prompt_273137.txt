I cover longevity science and its philosophical implications, and I have been turning over a question about Bernard Williams's essay The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality. Williams argued that an unending life would not be desirable, even if it were healthy and pleasant. His argument depends on a distinction between conditional desires and categorical desires: a conditional desire is one that makes sense only on the assumption that I am alive, while a categorical desire gives me a reason to remain alive in the first place. For Williams, a person is held together by a finite set of categorical desires, and if life went on long enough those desires would either be satisfied or fade, leaving only boredom. The only alternative, he thought, is a self that endlessly replaces its deepest projects with new ones, which would raise doubts about whether the later person is really the same person.

Now suppose a near-future memory-editing procedure can reliably erase the affective residue of repetition while preserving episodic memory, skills, and social bonds. A person could reread a favorite novel, revisit a beloved city, or return to a scientific puzzle with something close to first-time intensity, without losing the autobiographical knowledge that they have done these things before. Does Williams's argument survive this development? Which premise does the technology actually bear on, and is there a repaired version of his conclusion that remains important for debates about radical life extension or digital immortality?
