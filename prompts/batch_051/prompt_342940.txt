I'm drafting a short statement of my views on historical contingency and lock-in, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. I've absorbed most of these views from years of reading rationalist-adjacent work on coordination failure and institutional stickiness, so the vocabulary is familiar to me; what I'm less sure about is whether the positions actually form a coherent whole. The claims are as follows.

1. Lock-in. When an option acquires a large installed base, or when a set of complementary practices grows around it, switching costs and network effects can keep it dominant even if a technically superior alternative is available. I take QWERTY and VHS as canonical illustrations, but for me the historical details are not load-bearing; the mechanism is.

2. Sensitivity to early conditions. History does not forget its initial conditions the way an ergodic process would: small early perturbations are amplified by increasing returns into large, durable downstream differences. If the tape were rerun with tiny random changes, the particular institutions, standards, and dominant designs that came to prevail would differ, and the winners would have been ex ante unpredictable.

3. Long-run selection. Over sufficiently long horizons, competition among firms, states, and institutional rules prunes grossly inefficient arrangements. A practice that has survived and spread for centuries is therefore unlikely to sit far from the local efficiency frontier, relative to alternatives actually available at the time.

4. Coarse-grained convergence. Some broad functional packages — states, agriculture, cities, markets, long-distance trade — arose independently enough times that they look overdetermined rather than accidental. At a coarse grain history is more convergent than (2) suggests; contingency operates chiefly on the details.

5. Punctuated change. Locked-in systems can nonetheless come apart quickly once a threshold or tipping point is crossed; long stasis does not guarantee indefinite stasis. The swift abolition of the Atlantic slave trade and the collapse of the Soviet system strike me as routes out of what had looked like very deep locks.

6. Present-tense explanation. An arrangement's persistence should be explained by current incentives, current coordination problems, and current switching costs, not by the historical accident that originated it. Saying that we use QWERTY because of a choice made in the 1870s is at most shorthand for how the installed base arose; the binding explanation is present-day. I read origin stories as usually a disguised status-quo argument unless they are cashed out in present incentives.

I would like an audit of this package: which claims genuinely pull against each other, which conflicts dissolve once the claims are properly scoped, and what would have to be weakened to restore joint tenability. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating every pairwise tension as equally serious, and for each real tension say what the least costly revision is and what it would cost. I have no preferred candidate for revision; I want to know where the deepest stress points are and how much work it takes to hold the whole thing together.
