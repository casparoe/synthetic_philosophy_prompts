I write speculative fiction — mostly secondary-world fantasy and near-future dystopias — and I keep hitting the same snag. My worlds have legal systems, and I want to know whether a given system actually instantiates the rule of law or merely has laws. Not as a matter of my own politics, but as a matter of the ideal that Fuller, Raz, and Waldron have each tried to articulate. I know the rough shape: Fuller's internal morality of law (eight ways a system can fail from the inside), Raz's idea that law is there to guide conduct and the rule of law is whatever makes that guidance actually work, Waldron's emphasis on procedure, courts, and argument as what gives law its dignity. But I don't know how to turn those into a test I can actually run.

So: write me a reusable checklist for deciding whether a fictional legal system is a rule-of-law system by these standards. It has to be usable by a novelist with no legal training, between drafting sessions. I hit this every time I design a new society, and the stakes are whether my worldbuilding quietly says something I didn't mean to say — my editor keeps asking what a given legal system's deal actually is, and I want to be able to answer without handwaving. The questions should come in order, and each should say what counts as a sufficient answer. It should stop with a clear "no" where it can, and it should flag the points where Fuller, Raz, and Waldron would genuinely disagree or where the theory just doesn't settle the matter and I have to decide as the author. Keep the distinctions that do real work; drop the ones that only a specialist would notice, but say which ones you dropped and why. Be honest about what the checklist can't do — for instance, whether it mostly tests how the system looks on paper rather than how it operates in practice.

Then apply it to two societies I'm considering:
(1) A city-state whose council publishes every law, but each law carries a clause letting the council amend or revoke it retroactively "in the interest of civic harmony." Judges are appointed by the council and can be removed at pleasure. The laws themselves are clear, public, and consistant with each other.
(2) A merchant republic with clear, public, prospective commercial codes and genuinely independant arbitrators — but the codes bind only the merchant class. Everyone else lives under the discretionary justice of a governor, and there is no appeal from his rulings.

One of these I'm attached to and want to keep in the book. Tell me straight whether the checklist fails it, and why.
