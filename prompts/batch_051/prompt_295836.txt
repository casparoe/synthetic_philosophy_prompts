Something's been bugging me about how science actually works. We say science progresses because it has clear feedback—errors get caught, predictions fail, someone eventually replicates or doesn't. But a lot of the institutional machinery of science—blinding, pre-registration, independent replication, power analyses, review committees—looks like it exists to stop people from arriving at beliefs too early. That feels like it cuts against an old pragmatist idea I've been reading. Charles Peirce, in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), says: "Thought in action has for its only possible motive the attainment of thought at rest; and whatever does not refer to belief is no part of the thought itself." If thought's whole job is to reach belief and get quiet, why would a knowledge-producing practice build in so much artificial doubt? What theory of belief or error does that practice presuppose—that the mind's natural stopping point is premature belief, and reliable knowledge requires institutions to keep doubt alive longer than feels comfortable? And if that's the implicit theory, does it explain why fields with murky or delayed feedback advance slower: not because their practitioners are less smart or less honest but because they lack the machinary to force doubt to outlive the individual thinker's itch for settlement? Or is that over-reading the practice, and rigorous science is compatible with Peirce's picture without assuming premature belief is the default?
