Act as a professional moral philosopher working on the ethics of memory and historical representation. Think the problem through slowly; before you commit to a diagnosis, brainstorm the distinct places at which the chain below could break, then argue for the one you think is the real culprit.

A colleague who works on the ethics of film and journalism has constructed the following argument and now finds himself unable to escape it. I want your diagnosis.

(1) To wrong a person is to set back that person's interests or to violate that person's rights.
(2) A person can have such interests or rights only while he exists; a dead person no longer exists.
(3) Therefore the dead have no interests and no rights, and no act can wrong them.
(4) The only wrongs at stake in a biographical drama are wrongs against persons: against the person depicted, or against living people affected by the work.
(5) A drama can wrong living people only through some further effect on them — a surviving family made to suffer, or an audience deceived about the historical record.
(6) Now take a case with no such further effect. A nineteenth-century woman with no living descendants helped enslaved people escape, and every serious account agrees that she did. A century and a half later, a film is made in which a character with her name, biography, and role is revealed near the end to have been a paid informer for the slave-catchers, systematically betraying the people she pretended to help. The film is explicitly presented as a fictionalized retelling; no living person is deceived, and no family survives to be injured.
(7) In this case the film has no victim.
(8) An act with no victim cannot be morally wrong, however much it may be crude, tasteless, or bad art.
(9) Therefore the film is not morally wrong, and anyone who says it is has confused a moral failing with an aesthetic one, or has tacitly imagined victims who are not there.

The conclusion strikes me as false, but I cannot yet say which step to give up. I find premise (2) attractive because it keeps the moral universe clean, but I worry that rejecting it commits me to treating the dead as though they were still there. Meanwhile, rejecting (4) or (8) seems to require admitting that some acts are morally wrong without wronging anyone, and I do not yet know how to make sense of that.

Which step is the culprit, and why did that step look so compelling? Then price the alternatives: what does each candidate position have to give up — a theory of posthumous interests, a theory of victimless wrongs, or a willingness to accept (9)? Defend your chosen diagnosis against the others rather than merely surveying them.
