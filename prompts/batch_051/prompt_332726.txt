I've been asked to step in for a colleague and lead one two-hour session in an advanced undergraduate metaphysics seminar. The students have completed a unit on modal logic, so they can manipulate boxes and diamonds and will say that necessarily p means that p is true in every possible world, but they have not yet examined the metaphysical machinery underneath that talk. The aim of the session is to get them to feel the force of the choice between two pictures of what an object is across worlds: roughly, the transworld identity picture associated with Kripke, on which one and the same object literally exists in many worlds with different properties and possible worlds are stipulated ways this world could be, and Lewis's counterpart theory, on which no object exists in more than one world and de re modal truths are made true by similarity relations among distinct counterparts. I want them to leave understanding not only the definitions but also why someone would adopt the second, apparently deflationary, picture, and what the first picture has to buy in order to make cross-world identity respectable.

I do not need the explanation itself; I need a plan for the explanation. In particular, I would like the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, so the distinction does not look like a bare terminological choice. I would also like the one concrete case or example that should carry the whole session, and what that example must have and must avoid; I am wary of leading with the Humphrey objection because students tend to use it to dismiss Lewis before they have seen his motivation, though I could be persuaded to deploy it late. Then I would like a map of the misconceptions I should expect, such as treating a counterpart as literally me in another universe, thinking of possible worlds as distant planets that could be visited, assuming counterpart theory is a semantic claim about the English words might and could rather than a metaphysical thesis about what makes such claims true, or thinking that transworld identity requires an object's parts to be somehow split across worlds; for each, indicate the point in the sequence where it should be headed off. Finally, I would like a check for understanding: a question or small example I can pose near the end whose answer would distinguish someone who has grasped the distinction from someone who has merely learned to repeat the labels. Please also say what to leave out at this level and how to flag it as omitted.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Nothing in the plan should depend on the students having read Lewis or Kripke, and please do not include a reading list.
