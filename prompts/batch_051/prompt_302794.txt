I'm a philosophy of physics student putting together a one-page statement of where I stand on effective field theory and emergence, and I've started to suspect the things I believe don't all fit together. Here's the list:

(1) Effective theories are real theories, not just instruments: phonons, Cooper pairs, pions, and the order parameter in a superconductor are just as real at their scale as electrons and quarks are at theirs.
(2) The renormalization group is the best account of emergence we have. Heavy degrees of freedom decouple from light ones, so low-energy physics is largely indifferent to high-energy detail. That's why condensed matter and chemistry are autonomous from particle physics.
(3) I'm also a realist about the deep UV: there is a true fundamental theory, and our low-energy effective theories are course-grained approximations to it.
(4) But I want to reject Nagelian reduction as a regulative ideal. I don't think the aim of condensed matter physics is to derive phenomena like superconductivity from the underlying microphysics via bridge laws, and I think the word derive is doing a lot of work in that picture.
(5) At the same time, I think the hierarchy problem and the cosmological constant problem are real explanatory problems, not aesthetic ones: the fact that the Higgs is light relative to the Planck scale, and the cosmological constant is tiny, is exactly the sort of thing a UV theory ought to explain.

I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the least costly revision (or pair of revisions) that makes the whole set hang together? Rank the tensions by severity, and don't just tell me everything is fine or everything is broken.
