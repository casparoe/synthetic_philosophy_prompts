I run a small philosophy of mind podcast, and next month I'm interviewing a philosopher know for defending epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that subjective experiences are real, but have no causal powers whatsoever — they don't cause behavior, and in particular they don't cause the very reports and arguments he gives for their existence. I don't want to waste time on introductory stuff; I want the whole interview to work through the spot where the view seems to undercut itself. He has a few standard replies ready (natural selection can preserve an inert byproduct, our reports are caused by the neural correlates of experience rather than by the experience itself, and we have special phenomenal concepts for thinking about our own qualia), so every question should come with a follow-up that anticipates whichever of those moves he's likely to make and pushes past it.

Could you draft eight to ten questions for me? I want the sequence to tighten around the worry people sometimes call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if the experience itself contributes nothing to the production of his judgments and utterances about experience, then a being physically identical to him but with no experiences at all would produce the same arguments, so what exactly does his introspective confidence that he's conscious track, and why should I trust it? I'd like at least one question on the evolutionary objection, one on the self-stultification charge (the act of defending the view seems to make the defender's own beliefs unreliable, by the view's own lights), and one on whether the label epiphenomenal can be kept stable or whether the position quietly slides into eliminativism or property dualism. The tone should be curious rather than gotcha, but I do want him to feel the net closing.
