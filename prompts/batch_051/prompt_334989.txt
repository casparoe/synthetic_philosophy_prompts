I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about whether some action is right, and I dont have a word for it. People seem to run two different standards for how far you have to trace consequences, and they switch between them depending on what they're trying to show. Example: in a conversation about a big aid program, someone says the long-run effects are just unknowable — the money could reshape local markets, change birth rates, alter politics decades out — so we can't be confident it does good. Then the same person, five minutes later, is totally comfortable judging a personal purchase by its immediate effects: I got a coffee, the shop got money, everyone's fine. The "ripples out forever" worry doesn't seem to apply, or at least it doesn't bother them. I've also seen the reverse: someone defending an existing policy points to visible benefits as if that settles it, while insisting a small reform be rejected because we cant know its indirect effects.

Is there a name for this — not for the fact that long-run consequences are hard to know, but for the uneven way that fact gets deployed? And if there isn't a name, how should we describe it so it isn't just "people are inconsistent"? Im not satisfied with a bare label; I want to know the nearest concepts in this area, how the thing I'm describing differs from each, and which fits best. Also, can you flag what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting one contested view as the consensus?
