Since I took over our firm's internal forecasting pool this spring, I keep returning to a lunch argument I witnessed between two colleagues, Dana and Eli, about a third colleague, Raúl, who had posted his personal betting prices on whether our grant proposal would be funded. I now have to draft rules for next year's pool, so the question has become practical for me. Their exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Dana: Raúl says he would pay sixty cents for a ticket that pays one dollar if the proposal is funded, and sixty cents for a ticket that pays one dollar if it is rejected. I can sell him both tickets; he pays $1.20 and receives $1 no matter what. A person whose commitments allow a guaranteed loss is irrational.

(2) Eli: Only if he accepts both. By his own lights, each ticket alone is exactly fair. Rationality governs the choices a person actually makes; it doesn't condemn him for the fact that someone else can arrange those choices into a losing bundle.

(3) Dana: The bundle doesn't force anything extra on him. He holds the two prices simultaneously, so he is disposed to accept each bet. A disposition made of individually acceptable bets that add up to a sure loss is itself a flawed disposition. The defect is in the prices, not in the bookie.

(4) Eli: "Disposed to accept each" is doing the hidden work. Once Raúl has bought the first ticket, his situation has changed; he may reasonably reprice or refuse the second. Unless you assume his prices stay fixed no matter what he has already bought, your bookie can't count on selling both.

(5) Dana: You are making the guarantee depend on whether Raúl is a sucker, but it doesn't. The structural fact is that his stated prices value a guaranteed dollar at $1.20. The sure loss is just a vivid way of saying the prices are inconsistent with themselves.

(6) Eli: It says they are inconsistent with the probability calculus, because probabilities add to one. But the whole Dutch-book setup assumed from the outset that fair betting prices must behave like probabilities. What if a person is allowed to price a bet differently when he holds it alongside another? Then Raúl's two prices are not internally inconsistent; they simply don't aggregate the way you stipulated. You have renamed the disputed assumption rather than defended it.

I honestly cannot tell who is right at each step. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say what each move establishes, where it overreaches or concedes too much, and who is more right overall. Then tell me how confident you are in that final verdict and which single step you are least certain about.
