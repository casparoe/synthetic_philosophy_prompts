I keep coming back to a weird asymmetry in the way we talk about companies, and I can't decide whether it's defensable or just fudge. I was arguing with a friend about whether it ever makes sense to blame a company, as opposed to the people inside it, and I noticed how selective our blame-talk actually is. We'll say a company knew about the defect, deliberately misled regulators, was reckless with user data — and legal systems sometimes treat a firm as having intentions, knowledge, or even a kind of willfulness of its own, since that's how you get fines and fraud verdicts against the entity rather than any named person. But if you asked whether that same company feels bad about what it did, whether it's ashamed or remorseful, almost everyone would say that's a category mistake: companies don't feel anything.

So we're fine attributing the cognitive, culpability-related states — belief, knowledge, intent, recklessness — to a firm, while we refuse to attribute the affective states — guilt, shame, suffering. Is there a principled line between those, or is the mental-state talk just a fiction or shorthand for picking out the humans whose choices mattered? I'm trying to decide whether to keep the practice of blaming the corporate entity itself or give it up. If you keep the asymmetry, tell me what exact feature does the work, and what you'd say about a company that apologizes, pays compensation, reforms its board, and claims it has changed: is that evidence of something like remorse, or just a new set of people doing better marketing?
