I sit on the governing board of a regional transit authority that votes next month on whether to replace its legacy, human-operated train-control system with a fully automated one. The engineering estimates are no longer in dispute, but the board is deadlocked over how to weigh them, and I have been asked to draft the decision memorandum the full board will act on. The memo should make a definite recommendation — approve the new system, reject it, or approve it subject to a specified condition and phase-in — with the reasoning for it, and it should engage the strongest version of the losing position rather than reducing it to an error. I also want a short closing section on what would change your mind: the consideration, if it turned out to be mistaken or the numbers came in differently, that would most plausibly overturn your recommendation, or the point at which your reasoning is most likely to fail.

The current system's actuarial profile, according to our consultants, is an expected 0.6 passenger fatalities per year, produced by many small and uncorrelated incidents — individual operator errors, misread signals — none of which is projected to kill more than five people in a single event. The proposed automated system has a lower total of 0.3 expected passenger fatalities per year. But nearly the entire reduction comes from eliminating routine human error, and the new system introduces a failure mode the current system does not have: the manufacturer models about a 1-in-250 chance per year of a software fault causing a single mass-casualty event of roughly 50 passengers, while the residual small-accident risk contributes the remaining 0.1 expected fatalities per year.

Half the board reads this as settled arithmetic: 0.3 expected deaths per year is better than 0.6, and approving anything short of the fully automated system knowingly chooses more expected deaths. The other half says the annual expected-fatality total is the wrong moral metric, because a 1-in-250 yearly chance of killing 50 people at once is a categorically different burden than the same expectation spread across many separate small accidents, and a public body is entitled to treat rare catastrophic failures as worse than their expected-value contribution alone. A third architecture has also been priced: an automated system that keeps a human override at the most dangerous control points, cutting the catastrophic-failure probability to about 1-in-2000 per year but raising total expected fatalities to 0.45 per year, because the remaining human-in-the-loop errors return at the margin.

Draft the circulation memo as you would put it before the board: a concrete recommendation, with whatever conditions or timeline it requires, and a fair treatment of the strongest argument on the other side. Then close with the short section on what would change your mind, as requested above.
