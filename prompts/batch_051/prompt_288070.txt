I've been worrying at a rule about my own future opinions and I keep ending up convinced it's right, so I want someone to try to knock it down. Roughly: if I expect that tomorrow-me will be, say, 80 percent confident in some claim, then I should already be 80 percent confident now, unless I have a specific reason to think tomorrow-me will be confused, mislead, or irrational. The "unless" is doing a lot of work and I know that. But anything less seems like I'm treating future-me as an idiot while still planning to let future-me make the call, which is incoherent. If I thought there was something wrong with how I'll be thinking tomorrow, I should factor that in now; if I don't think there's anything wrong with it, then why don't I just match them already? This came up with a concrete thing: I have to decide tomorrow whether to accept a job offer after a final interview, and I can already predict that after sleeping on it I'll be pretty confident one way. The question is whether my current uncertainty is just a mistake.

So: what would actually count against this rule? I do want the standard cases — knowing I'll be drunk, or exhausted, or have forgotten the evidence, or be in the grip of some feeling, or be talked into something by someone I already distrust — but I want them placed honestly. Are they real counterexamples, or just cases where the "unless" clause already fires? More importantly, are there pressure points where the rule can't be saved by saying "I had a special reason to distrust future-me"? Sort the problems from "this actually kills the rule as stated" down to "this only makes it awkward around the edges," and be honest about which problems a defender of the rule should themselves accept as real costs rather than things only critics bring up. And tell me whether my version, as I've actually stated it, is so close to a tautology — trust future-you except when you shouldn't — that it stops risking anything at all. If it can't be wrong, is that a problem, or is that just what a guide like this is allowed to be? I want an honest map of how exposed the rule is, not a takedown and not a pep talk.
