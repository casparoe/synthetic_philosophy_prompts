Me and my friend spent most of last night arguing about whether the state should fund literary fiction, and he ended up at a conclusion I hate but couldn't answer. Here's the argument, roughly as he gave it:

(1) The only respectable justification for public funding of literary fiction is the public-value story: novels train empathy, perspective-taking, tolerance for ambiguity, and those spill over to people who never read them.
(2) Soap operas, romance novels, YA fantasy, true-crime podcasts, and some video games produce the same kinds of spillovers, and since they reach way more people they often produce more of them in total.
(3) If two kinds of cultural product produce the same spillovers, then a public-value rationale that funds one must fund the other too, and if there's only so much money it should prefer whichever gives more spillover per dollar.
(4) So the public-value story either commits you to subsidizing all of that stuff, or commits you to moving money away from difficult literary fiction toward bigger-audience media. Maybe both.
(5) Nobody who defends arts funding actually wants either. They'd never subsidize a soap-opera industry, and they'd riot if you cut the literary grants to fund podcasts.
(6) So the public-value story isn't what's actually doing the work. The real reason must be a preference for a certain kind of culture, and the "public value" talk is rationalization.
(7) Since the public-value story was the only respectable defense, funding literary fiction is indefensible except by saying the quiet, snobby part out loud.

I want to reject this. I'd most like to keep (1) and keep the conclusion that literary fiction deserves public money, but after last night I'm not sure they cant be had together.
