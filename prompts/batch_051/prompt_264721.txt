I keep coming back to Whitehead's account of the rhythm of education, and I cannot decide whether it is a deep insight or an elegant false systematization. The view, as I understand it, is that the unit of real learning is a three-stage cycle. First comes romance: the learner must be seized by a phenomenon, stirred by novelty and the half-formed sense that there is something here worth understanding. Only then may precision properly enter — the discipline of exact notation, analysis, drill, and formulated rules. Finally comes generalization: the now-precise idea is thrown into fresh and active use, applied freely to new cases, so that it does not become an inert idea, knowledge the student can state but never utilize. The systematic attraction of the view is genuine. It gives a principled answer to the oldest classroom argument: do not impose precision before romance, because a rule received without first-hand wonder decays into exactly that dead, correct-but-unusable knowledge. And it explains a wide class of educational failures with a single mechanism rather than many.

But I hold a firm intuition that pulls the other way. My own history, and my observation of students, suggests that for many learners romance arrives through precision rather than before it. It was only after I had been made to drill arithmetic or Latin morphology that the subject began to glow; the first taste of real mastery was what stirred curiosity, not the preliminary exposure. Likewise, some students I now teach flounder precisely because they have been kept too long in a well-intentioned romance — shown the beauty of physics or history before they possess the technique to state, test, or even recognize a claim about it — and they end up with vivid enthusiasm and no purchase on the material. The theory says this is an error of order, a distorted cycle; my experience says the theory has the causal direction backwards for a substantial class of learners.

I want to know which ought to yield, and what would justify either move. Should I treat the intuition as a debunkable artifact of how I happened to be schooled — something the theory predicts I would feel if I had first met the subject as forced drill and only later projected the resulting mastery back onto the drill itself? Or should I treat it as evidence that the stages are not a developmental sequence at all, but two concurrent poles of attention, with different learners needing different doses at different moments — and, if so, does that amendment preserve the theory or drain it of its diagnostic content? Give me the strongest case for preserving the romance-first sequence, the strongest case for believing that technique can itself be the source of first wonder, and a verdict on whether there is a reading on which both the theory and my experience survive.
