I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about risky choices, because my decisions do not seem to line up with the rule I have always assumed, namely that you should pick the option with the higher expected value and, if you dislike risk, simply apply a discount to larger sums of money. I am not an economist or a philosopher; I have just been keeping a mental list of my own choices and trying to find the rule behind them. The cases are these. (a) I happily pay £500 a year to insure my £200,000 flat against a fire that has a 1-in-1,000 chance of happening, even though the expected cost of the fire is only £200. (b) I occasionally buy a £2 lottery ticket with a 1-in-14,000,000 chance of £10,000,000, and I do not think this is simply a mistake, even though the expected value is well under £1. (c) Offered a choice between a guaranteed £1,000 and a spin that gives an 89% chance of £1,000, a 10% chance of £5,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, I take the guaranteed £1,000; but offered a choice between an 11% chance of £1,000 and a 10% chance of £5,000, I take the 10% chance at £5,000. (d) I would pay a good deal to move a chance of winning £1,000 from 98% to 99%, but almost nothing to move it from 48% to 49%, even though the prize and the size of the change are identical. (e) I would turn down a single coin toss in which I gain £110 on heads and lose £100 on tails, but I would accept a hundred repetitions of that same gamble.

A friend who studied decision theory once told me this pattern reminds her of something called risk-weighted expected utility: the thought, as I understand it, is that we ought not simply to multiply each probability by how much we value its outcome, but first to adjust the probability in a way that depends on where the outcome sits in the ranking, so that a 1% chance attached to the worst possible result counts for a different amount than a 1% chance attached to the second-best. She never explained how the adjustment is supposed to work, so I am left with only a slogan. I would like you to tell me whether there is any coherent principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, or whether I should be prepared to abandon some of them. Please state the principle precisely, or offer me the two or three most promising candidates, say which of my cases motivates which part of it, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a single coin toss in which I lose £1,000 on tails and gain £1,001 on heads, and say whether your answer changes if that gamble is offered a thousand times. Where the principle is silent, say so rather than forcing it to speak. I am not looking for references or reading suggestions, just for your own reasoning. Please write the whole answer in British English.
