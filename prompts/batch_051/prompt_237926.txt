So I'm on the committee that splits a small annual pot of money between two labs in my institute, and a visiting economist talked everyone into trying a rule she called a Kalai–Smorodinsky split. At the start of each year, each lab files a "ceiling": the most money it could usefully absorb that year, together with a one-page budget. The budgets aren't really audited, and no ceiling can be set above the total pot. To stop people from immediately claiming the whole pot, the rule uses each lab's three-year average ceiling, and any unspent money from the previous year is posted publicly. The actual split is proportional to those averaged ceilings—so a lab whose averaged ceiling is twice the other's gets twice as much—except that if the two averaged ceilings sum to less than the pot, each lab just gets its full ceiling and the surplus goes back to the central administration. The pitch is that, as long as the ceilings sum to at least the pot, this is the Pareto-efficient split (taking both getting zero as the fallback) where both labs get the same fraction of their own ideal amount, so nobody can complain the other got more of what it asked for.

I'm supposed to vote on this in a month and I honestly can't tell whether its clever or insane. Walk me through how it would actually go over, say, five years: what do the labs start doing to their submitted ceilings, what kinds of budgets get written, does the three-year average discipline anyone or just slow down the gaming, and where does the scheme get gamed first? Then stick your neck out: after the gaming shakes out, who ends up with the money—whoever has the most need, whoever writes the most optimistic budget, whoever is loudest—and is there a fix (real audits, an underspend penalty, letting labs challenge each other's budgets, whatever) that saves the fairness property without turning it into a different rule? Don't just lay out options and stop; commit to a prediction about how this plays out and whether the rule is worth keeping or should be killed.
