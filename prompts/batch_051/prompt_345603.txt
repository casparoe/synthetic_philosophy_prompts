I keep running into cases where a hospital will let someone die but won't kill them, and the difference seems to matter legally. For example, a competent patient can refuse a ventilator and the doctor has to honor that—even unplug it—but in most US states the same doctor can't write a prescription for lethal medication to end that same patient's suffering. And a doctor can push morphine to treat pain even if it's pretty likely to suppress breathing and hasten death, as long as the stated aim is pain relief, not death. But if a nurse gave the exact same dose because the patient asked to die, that's murder. Outside hospitals, a random person who sees a kid drowning in a shallow pond has no legal duty to wade in and save them, but if they hold the kid under, that's homicide. What theory of the doing/allowing distinction—or of the relevance of intention—does this patchwork of legal practices actually presuppose? Is it coherent as a single position, or does the law simultaneously assume outcome-based, intention-based, and duty-based theories in different corners? And if you had to pick one philosophical view that makes most of the practices defensible, which would it be, and which practice would you have to reform?
