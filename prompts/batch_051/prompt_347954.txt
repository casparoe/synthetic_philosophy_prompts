I am currently taking a course in the ethics of communication, and I have been developing a position I plan to defend in my term paper. Before I commit to it, I want to know what could actually be wrong with it.

The view is this: sincerity is not exhausted by truthfulness. Beyond a duty not to deceive, we have a standing pro tanto duty to be legible to the people our conduct significantly affects—to make enough of our relevant beliefs, aims, and reasoning available to them, even when nobody has asked, such that they can see how our words and actions hang together and can hold us to account for them. On this picture, systematic opacity toward others is not merely a failure of intimacy or politeness; it is a distinctive way of failing to treat them as responsible agents. My motivation is roughly Kantian: to treat someone as an agent rather than as an obstacle or an instrument, I have to let her locate my conduct within a framework of reasons she could in principle assess, and I cannot do that if I remain illegible to her.

Before I defend this further, I want a map of its exposure. What would have to be true for the view to be false? I am looking for the cases, arguments, intuitions, and results from adjacent debates—privacy, autonomy, power, social epistemology, the ethics of explanation—that would count against it. For each, I would like to know whether it is a decisive defeater or merely an awkward result, and whether a defender of the view should concede that it counts against the view while keeping it, or may dismiss it as an attack on a straw man. I also want an honest answer to a reflexive worry: have I protected the view so thoroughly with the qualifiers "pro tanto," "enough," and "significantly affected" that nothing could now count against it—and if so, is that insulation a flaw in the view or just a sign that the core claim is modest? Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. The objection I currently find most troubling is that a duty of legibility can become a demand that the vulnerable explain themselves to the powerful; I want to know whether that is a deep problem for the view itself or only for the institutional forms transparency often takes.
