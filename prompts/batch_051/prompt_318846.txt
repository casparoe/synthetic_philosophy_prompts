I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on democratic theory, and next session I am introducing lottocracy to students who have so far thought of democracy entirely as voting. Their assignment gave them only the bare idea that sortition fills political office by random selection from the citizenry, and I want the session organized around a harder claim: that elections bundle two distinct jobs — picking officeholders and letting voters retrospectively punish them — and that sortition unbundles them, treating the first as a sampling problem while giving up the second almost entirely. Plan how I should teach the session. Give me the order in which the component ideas must be introduced and what depends on what; the single running example I should use and what it must have and must not include; the misconceptions students will bring or form — I expect at least that sortition is just direct democracy by another name, that a lottery picks amateurs and amateurs cannot govern, and that because no one chose the assembly it is less democratic than an elected one — and where in the sequence each should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end whose answer would show they have understood the proposal rather than merely found it provocative; and what I should explicitly say I am leaving out at this level.
