I run a philosophy club at my school — there's about ten of us and we get one 45-minute lunchtime slot a week. We're doing a session on the Münchhausen trilemma: the idea that if you try to properly justify a belief, the chain of reasons has to either go on forever, loop back on itself, or stop at some point where you just assert something without a reason. We've chatted about it before and everyone nods along, but then they go straight back to treating some justifications as obviously fine, so I don't think it's landed. I want a game or activity where the group actually runs into the three options during the session, and the trilemma only gets named in the debrief.

Can you design the whole thing? Rules simple enough for 16-17 year olds to pick up in a couple of minutes, no materials beyond a whiteboard or scraps of paper, a rough 45-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the three horns (endless regress, circularity, brute stop). Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do when it does, and be straight about which feature of the trilemma the activity can't reproduce so I'll have to supply it in the debrief myself.

What I want them to come away with is a felt sense that the three horns aren't just a tidy diagram — they're what actually happens when you try to finish a justification. And if you think the trilemma is asking too much of justification, or my brief has this wrong, tell me that plainly rather than handing me a session plan that quietly assumes it's right.
