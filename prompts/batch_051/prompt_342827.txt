I'm working on a paper for a course in philosophy of history, and I keep getting stuck on how to use the old firing-squad thought experiment for historical explanation. You know the case: two soldiers shoot at the same time, either bullet alone would have killed the prisoner, and the standard debate is about whether each shooter caused the death or whether the whole setup is best described as overdetermined.

What I want to do is change the case so it has to do real work in history. Take the outbreak of the First World War as the outcome. Suppose someone says the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand and the July crisis were a sufficient trigger, while the pre-1914 alliance system plus mobilization plans were also sufficient by themselves. These aren't two bullets arriving at the same instant, though: the structural chain builds over decades and also shapes what the "trigger" means, while the trigger chain unfolds in weeks and only becomes sufficient because the structural chain is already there. My question: if we modify the firing squad along these lines—two purported sufficient causes that are not simultaneous and where one is part of the conditions for the other—does the standard analysis still say the outcome is overdetermined? What happens to the counterfactual test ("without X, would Y still have happened?") when the two would-be causes have different time scales and different metaphysical shapes? And should a historian conclude that each was equally a cause, or does the time-scale asymmetry change the explanatory story?

Please act as a philosopher working on causation and historical explanation. Start by briefly brainstorming possible ways to model the original firing-squad case, then use that to assess the modified version. Be careful to separate what is settled about the original puzzle from what is genuinely contested in the historical version, and don't present a contested reading as consensus. I'd also like a note on where the analogy itself might be doing illicit work.
