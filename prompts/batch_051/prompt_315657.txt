I spent way too long last night arguing with a friend about whether you can ever legitimately call a movie good just because someone whose taste you trust told you it is. I ended up on the pessimistic side — that an aesthetic judgment has to be yours, grounded in your own encounter with the work, and that testimony can at best tell you what's worth your time, not whether it's good. My friend was the optimist: we defer to critics constantly, she said, and theres no real difference in kind between aesthetic testimony and ordinary testimony, so if the latter can transmit knowledge the former can too. We circled partly because neither of us could say exactly what counted as "testimony" or as "judging" in the first place, and partly because I suspect I'm a weak pessimist (testimony can license practical deference — what to queue for, what to order, what to watch first — but not a judgment you'd assert in your own voice) while she's a moderate optimist (testimony can justify an aesthetic belief, and even a judgment, though it won't give you the feel of the thing). I want to see whether our fight was substantive, so:

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an aesthetic-testimony pessimist and an aesthetic-testimony optimist, assuming exactly those two positions — the weak pessimist and the moderate optimist — as the parties. Write the document they would actually co-sign after a long workshop. Use this format:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Make them precise enough to be non-vacuous but not so loaded that one side walks out. They should cover at least: the felt difference between experiencing a work and being told about it; the fact that people in practice defer to taste-makers; the difference (if any) between forming a belief and arriving at a judgment; and what practical deference to testimony buys even on the pessimistic picture.

2. Disagreements table: three or four rows. For each row give the exact sub-question, the pessimist's position worded so the weak pessimist would own it, the optimist's position worded so the moderate optimist would own it, and a note on whether the disagreement is genuine or at least partly verbal.

3. Cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each, specify a case or argument both sides agree would bear on it — a super-reliable critic, say, or someone who ends up with a stable, well-grounded aesthetic judgment without any firsthand acquaintance — and record each side's prediction in advance.

4. Finish with a note on where your own way of setting up the disagreement is most likely to fail: which part could just be about words, what consideration or argument would make you redraw the map or flip one of the cruxes, and where the whole adversarial-collaboration framing would stop being useful.
