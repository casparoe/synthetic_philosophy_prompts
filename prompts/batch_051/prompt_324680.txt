I am a novelist at work on a book in which a central character becomes convinced that the Darwinian view of culture — the claim that beliefs, stories, tunes, fashions, and rituals vary, are copied with variation, and are selected for how well they spread — has corrosive consequences for the very possibility of knowledge. The character has distilled the conviction into the following argument, which I found plausible as I drafted it and now suspect proves too much, because it ends in a general scepticism I cannot live with either as a novelist or as a person. I want to know where the argument goes wrong, if it does; and if it does not, what I must give up to avoid the conclusion.

(1) Cultural items — words, melodies, rituals, political slogans, beliefs — replicate through populations by being copied, and the variants that persist and spread are the ones best at getting themselves copied.

(2) A cultural variant's fitness is therefore a matter of transmissibility: how readily it is remembered, repeated, performed, or passed on. Transmissibility need not track truth, usefulness, or beauty; a false and harmful rumour can spread more readily than a true and important finding.

(3) The same applies not only to first-order items such as slogans and songs but also to our norms of acceptance: the habits of mind we inherit — trust the old, trust the many, trust what is vivid, mistrust what is complicated — are themselves cultural variants that spread because they are good at being copied.

(4) It follows that the very standards by which we would assess the truth of any claim, including the truth of the Darwinian view of culture itself, are the products of selection for transmissibility rather than for truth-conduciveness.

(5) But if our standards of assessment are selected for transmissibility rather than for truth, we have no non-circular reason to think our assessments track the truth; we trust them only because they replicate well in us.

(6) Yet the reasoning that produced this conclusion relies on those very standards. So the Darwinian view of culture, if accepted, undermines the reliability of the reasoning that led to it; it saws off the branch it sits on.

The character's conclusion is not merely that some beliefs are infected by fashion, but that the very faculty for distinguishing true from false is itself a cultural item selected for copyability, so that believing things for reasons is a piece of bad faith. That seems to me to prove too much: it would make the novel's own attempt at persuasion pointless, along with every act of reflection. Yet I cannot see cleanly which step is the weak one, or what exactly giving it up would cost. I would rather keep (1) and (2) than surrender to scepticism, but I want to know the price of whatever move saves the rest.
