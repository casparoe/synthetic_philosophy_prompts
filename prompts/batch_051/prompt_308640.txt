Write me an Onion-style news article, headline plus about 500 words, reporting that a married couple in an outer suburb of Chicago have announced that they can no longer "agree to disagree," on the grounds that Aumann's agreement theorem says two rational agents with common priors cannot agree to disagree once their actual disagreement becomes common knowledge. I want the jokes to come from getting the theorem right, not from a generic "math makes people weird" angle. So the piece should understand that before they revealed their posterior probabilities to each other, the disagreement was rationally fine; the trouble starts the moment each becomes confident of the other's posterior and its common knowledge between them—maybe they did it in writing, in front of a notary, or over a group chat where each can see that the other has seen it. Once it's common knowledge that he's at 87 percent and she's at 91 percent, the theorem forces a choice: they had different priors all along, one of them miscalculated, or one of them isn't actually a Bayesian. The real target is the way people treat "agreeing to disagree" as a stable, respectable resting place. The piece should be deadpan and include at least one quote from their marriage counselor, who suggests they try relaxing the common-prior assumption or asks whether the disagreement was ever common knowledge to begin with. It should land for someone who has seen Aumann's theorem and still be readable to someone who hasn't. Keep the whole thing in American English.
