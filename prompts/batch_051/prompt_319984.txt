Here's an asymmetry in programming practice that's been bugging me. Programmers treat rebinding a local variable — `x = x + 1` in a loop — as basically harmless, not even really "mutation." But they treat mutating a field through a reference — `node.value += 1`, where `node` was passed in — as one of the classic sources of bugs. Yet in both cases the same name or cell stands for different values at different times, and in both cases you lose the ability to read one line and know what it does out of context. So: is there a real principle that justifies the asymmetry, or is it just that one damge is visible inside the local scope and the other isn't? Give me the strongest principled distinction you can find, then test it against the case where the local variable gets captured by a closure that outlives the function. I want a verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it.
