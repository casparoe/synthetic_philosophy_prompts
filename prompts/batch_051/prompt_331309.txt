I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about what it actually means for a country to have "joined" a bigger political arrangement, as opposed to just signing a deal with it. He kept saying the only thing that matters is whether you have a seat in the room where the rules get made. But I have a bunch of case verdicts and no principle that obviously gets all of them, and I'd like you to find the principle or tell me which verdict too drop.

Here are the cases.

1. A and its neighbors form a full customs union: common external tariff, a court whose trade rulings bind national courts, and an assembly that passes trade regulations by qualified majority. I say A has clearly joined something.

2. B gets market access to a much larger country in exchange for adopting whatever product standards the larger country sets. B has no seat in the standard-setting body and no chance to argue when the standards change. I say B has not joined anything; its a rule-taker.

3. C is part of a confederation. The members keep their own armies and foreign policies, send delegates who are bound by instructions from their home parliaments to a common assembly, and each member can veto common decisions or leave on one year's notice. I say C has joined something, even though the common body is weak and everything runs on consensus.

4. D uses a common currency with several others, and monetary policy is set by a shared central bank for the whole currency area. D has no guaranteed seat on the bank's board, though it is consulted; fiscal policy stays national. I'd say D has joined something, but the no-seat part truly bothers me.

5. E used to be a member of an arrangement and formally left, but it still adopts all of the arrangement's rules—tariffs, standards, migration rules—because otherwise its exporters would lose access. I say E is not a member anymore, obviously. But I notice E has exactly as much say over those rules as B does in case 2 (none) and about as much as D does in case 4 (none, unless you count being consulted). So my verdicts on 2, 4, and 5 seem to need something other than "a seat" to hold together, and I don't know what it is.

6. F is in a common travel area: no border checks, a shared short-stay visa list, and a common refugee-processing standard. There is no court, no assembly, and disputes get handled by meetings of ministers. I want to say F is "associated" rather than a full member, but I can't say what's missing, since it lives under as many common rules as some of the others.

7. G has a seat and an equal vote in a joint body, but the body's decisions can't be enforced against a member except by expelling them. I say G is still a member—just of a weak arrangement. Enforcement doesn't seem to be what makes membership.

So: is there one principle that gets all seven, or close? If not, which verdict should I overturn first? And what does the best principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a member state has a permanent opt-out from a common policy, but it still gets to vote on the rules for that policy as they apply to everyone else. Has it fully joined with respect to that policy, or is "membership" no longer the right word for it?

If the principle can't accommodate one of my verdicts, tell me straight which one is doing the damage and why it has to go.
