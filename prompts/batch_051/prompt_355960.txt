I am a PhD candidate in cognitive science, and in three weeks I will give a colloquium defending the claim that analogical transfer in even the most constrained, seemingly rule-governed microdomain — letter-string analogy problems of the kind used in the Copycat architecture — is best modeled as graded conceptual slippage under interacting pressures rather than as a discrete rule operating on fixed concepts. The evidence, as I will present it: given that abc changes to abd, a rule such as "replace the rightmost item by its successor" handles the transfer to ijk, yielding ijl, but it commits to a single extension and must silently choose among rival rules when the target string is reversed, grouped, or runs into the end of the alphabet (kji, iijjkk, or a string ending in z). I will argue that human answers and the Copycat model instead show graded, context-dependent competition among candidate slippages — successor/predecessor, leftmost/rightmost, letter/letter-group, and identity-related mappings — resolved by bottom-up pressures from perceived structure and top-down pressures from the conceptual network, with no single privileged rule. The stronger claim of the talk is that none of this is a toy artifact: concepts in general are better understood as networks of pressures whose microstructure slips whenever the concepts are put to use, so that concept and analogy are not two distinct pieces of machinery.

The audience is a cognitive-science colloquium and is not all friendly. Two senior faculty work in the structure-mapping tradition; they think analogy is systematic alignment over structured relational representations and care about one-to-one mapping and parallel connectivity, and they will suspect that "slippage" is just unconstrained similarity with a new label. One psychologist works on concepts from prototype, exemplar, and theory-theory perspectives and will press on where stable categories and inductive constraints come from if concepts are as fluid as I claim. One machine-learning faculty member works on program induction and compositionality and will want to know what objective function, if any, the pressure story corresponds to, and whether stochastic codelet-style search is just approximate inference over a space of programs with an unstated prior. A philosopher of cognitive science will ask whether "pressure" is doing real explanatory work or quietly reinstating a homunculus. There will also be graduate students who know enough to ask sharp but only half-formed questions.
