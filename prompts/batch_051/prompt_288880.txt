I serve as vice-chair of the Meridian Climate Intervention Review Panel, which must decide by vote next month whether to grant a limited outdoor deployment license for Project Veil, a stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) research campaign. I need to circulate a written opinion to the other seven members before we vote.

The proposal before us: over 18 months, a high-altitude aircraft would release a total of 150 tonnes of sulfate aerosol precursors across 14 flights at about 19 km altitude, in a narrow midlatitude corridor over open ocean. The aim is not to alter climate but to reduce the uncertainties in aerosol microphysics, plume dispersion, and stratospheric chemistry that currently dominate SAI model disagreement. The plan has three stages: a two-month inert-tracer phase, a four-month phase at 10% of nominal mass, and a final phase that proceeds only if an independent monitoring board certifies that no significant regional perturbation has been observed. A pre-committed wind-down protocol halts flights within 30 days if any trigger threshold is crossed. Because the injected mass is tiny compared with a major volcanic eruption, no one on either side claims this campaign could itself cause a termination shock.

The dispute among us: two members oppose any authorization. Their first objection is mitigation deterrence: even a well-run research campaign lowers the perceived cost of SAI, generates a constituency for deployment, and will be cited by governments and emitters as grounds to defer emissions cuts; governance checkpoints inside the proposal, they say, do not touch that problem. Their second objection is that the real issue is termination shock in the deployment this research enables: a future operational SAI program would have to be maintained indefinitely, and an abrupt halt—whether from political collapse, conflict, or cost—would produce rapid, dangerous catch-up warming. Authorizing this experiment, on their view, makes us complicit in a catastrophic tail risk. A third group of members is more sympathetic but worries about the legitimacy question: the panel's mandate comes from a consortium of national science funders, no global body has authorized outdoor aerosol research, and affected states—including several small-island and least-developed states—have not been consulted. They want the panel to require a broader international process before any aerosol-release phase.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend authorizing the proposal as submitted, authorizing it with modified conditions (for example, no aerosol release until some further condition is met), or declining. Make the recommendation concrete and actionable, include a timeline for any conditions, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than listing risks.
