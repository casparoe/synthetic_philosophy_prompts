I've been going back and forth with some friends about whether someone who edits away a big chunk of their personality—say the anxiety and ambition that made them who they were—has improved themselves or quietly committed a kind of suicide, and one of the group threw out a concrete proposal that I want stress-tested. The idea is a private continuity contract. Here's the mechanism. Before any elective, irreversible neuro-modification (anything that rewrites preferences, values, or personality, not just a mood patch), you can sign a contract specifying a few values, relationships, or projects that the post-modification person must still keep faith with. A company holds a sum in escrow, put up by your pre-modification self, and charges an annual audit fee; the people you named as beneficiaries receive whatever remains in escrow if you fail an audit. After the procedure, the firm sends auditors once a year. They are not allowed to ask about inner states or use affect scans; they can only look at conduct: spending records, messages you opt in to, whether you maintain the specified relationships, whether the named projects get time and money. If you pass, the escrow is released to you in annual chunks.

Obvously the transformed person could just perform the contract for the money, and the pre-modification person could write absurdly burdensome contracts, so I want to know how this would actually play out if it became a common product. Who buys these contracts—are they the people who least need the guardrail? How do post-modification people respond to being held to a contract written by someone they arguably no longer are? What gaming shows up on both the customer and auditor side? And does the scheme actually preserve anything on the self-continuity question, or does it just create a market for performing the old self? Walk me through which failure modes can be fixed, and which can't without turning the contract into either toothless ritual or a kind of posthumous domination.
