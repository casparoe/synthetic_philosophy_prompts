I've been rereading Plato's *Republic* for the first time in ages and got a bit stuck on what exactly Glaucon is claiming in Book 2, around 360c. The ring of Gyges gets trotted out constantly, but when you read Glaucon's actual conclusion it seems less straightforward than the usual "why be moral if you can get away with it?" gloss suggests. The passage I mean is this one:

"And yet this is a great proof, one might argue, that no one is just of his own will but only from constraint, in the belief that justice is not his personal good, inasmuch as every man, when he supposes himself to have the power to do wrong, does wrong."

Now I've seen this cited in rationalist-adjacent discussions of moral motivation and internalism vs externalism, but people usually just take it as a psychological claim about what everyone would do with the ring. What I'm actually unsure about is how the two pieces of the sentence connect. Glaucon says both that no one is just voluntarily but only under constraint, and that justice is not the person's own good. The first seems descriptive, the second evaluative. Is the argument supposed to move from the psychological generalisation to the evaluative conclusion directly? If so, what's the hidden premise? Or is the psychology just meant to undercut a particular defence of justice, rather than establish the evaluative claim on its own?

I'd like an explanation of the conceptual issue here: the difference between Glaucon's claim about how people *would* behave under perfect impunity and the claim that justice isn't good *for the agent*, and which of these Socrates is actually being asked to refute. Also, does the ring thought experiment give us evidence for the evaluative claim at all, or only for a claim about people's beliefs and motives? I think I'm missing a step in the argument's structure, and I'd appreciate it if you could be quite precise about what follows from what. Please write the answer in British English.
