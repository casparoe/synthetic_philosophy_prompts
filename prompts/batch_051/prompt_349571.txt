After a long argument with a friend about whether Pascal’s wager can justify religious commitment, I tried to show him that the wager, if it works at all, overgenerates in a way that undermines its practical use. But I am not confident my argument goes through. Here is the argument, with steps numbered.

(1) For any religious practice P such that, if P’s central doctrine is true, practicing P yields infinite reward, and if P’s central doctrine is false, the net cost of practicing P is finite, the expected value of practicing P is infinite whenever the probability assigned to P’s doctrine is nonzero.  
(2) There are at least two mutually exclusive such practices — call them P and Q — and for each, a reasonable person can assign nonzero probability to its central doctrine.  
(3) If two mutually exclusive options both have infinite expected value, then expected-value reasoning cannot rationally discriminate between them.  
(4) If a form of reasoning cannot rationally discriminate between at least two mutually exclusive practices, it cannot make it rationally required to adopt any particular one of them.  
(5) Therefore, if step (2) is true, Pascal’s wager cannot make any particular religious commitment rationally required, even if its core idea about infinite reward and finite cost is accepted.

My friend thought step (3) was the wrong one, but he could not say what the correct treatment of infinite utilities should be. Which step, if any, fails, and what is the correct version of the step that fails?
