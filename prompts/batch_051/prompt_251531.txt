I was talking to a colleague who's sympathetic to longtermism, and she floated an idea that's been nagging at me ever since. She called it a "future trusteeship": an unelected standing body with a veto over present-day legislation that has long-range consequences — climate, biotech, AI, pandemic preparedness, that sort of thing. Her reasoning was that future people can't vote, so present majorities will systematically shortchange them; a self-binding device would be the democratic equivalent of Ulysses tying himself to the mast. I get the Ulysses analogy, but this sounds suspiciously like handing power to a group that claims to speak for people who don't exist yet. My problem is I can't tell weather my unease is a philosophical objection or just an empirical hunch that the trustees would turn out venal and self-serving. So here's the question: if you accept the core longtermist thought — that the far future ought to dominate our priorities because there are so many future people — is there any principled line between a legitimate democracy precommitting itself and an authoritarian guardianship, or does the view just push you step by step into accepting the second?
