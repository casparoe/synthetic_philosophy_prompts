A government concerned about the collapse of shared visual evidence adopts a scheme to build an epistemic backstop for audio and video. Every newly sold camera, phone, and audio recorder must contain signing hardware that, at the instant of capture, attaches a cryptographic signature binding the file hash to the capture time, the capture location, and the device's unique identifier. Unsigned media are not prohibited: anyone may still record, possess, and share them. But platforms above a statutory size must label all unsigned media unverifiable, display signed media preferentially, and down-rank unsigned media in recommendation and search results. A public registry lists each device's signing key, and a device is moved to a revoked list—after which its files are treated as unverifiable—if it is reported stolen or if its key is shown to have been extracted. A levy on device sales funds a standing bounty for anyone who can produce a file whose signature verifies but whose content is demonstrably fabricated or altered; a successful claimant collects the bounty, and the maker of the signing hardware must revoke the affected key and reimburse the fund.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not a proposal to endorse or reject. Work through what it incentivizes among device manufacturers, platforms, journalists and confidential sources, ordinary users, and forgers. Where does it get gamed first? Which parts of the backstop give way under pressure, and can the failure modes be fixed without hollowing the scheme out? Be as concrete as you can about second-order effects.
