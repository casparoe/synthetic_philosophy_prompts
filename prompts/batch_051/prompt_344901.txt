I've been trying to pin down a dialectical move in the nativism–empiricism debate, and I want a case engineered to spec rather than another survey. Treat me as a longtime reader of the blog-sphere debates on this, so use the usual machinery — priors, hypothesis spaces, inductive bias, a learnability argument — but I want the actual gears, not hand-waving.

In the standard run of the argument, the nativist is the one who gets stuck positing a large amount of built-in cognitive structure, and the empiricist looks parsimonious because everything is learned by a domain-general mechanism from experience. I want the reverse direction: a case in which an empiricist account of how a learner acquires a particular competence is forced to build in more from the outset than a nativist account is.

Hold these definitions fixed so the case cannot win by relabeling:
- For purposes of the case, an empiricist about competence C holds that C is acquired from experience alone by a domain-general learning mechanism, with no representation of C's content in the learner's initial state.
- A nativist about C holds that acquisition depends on a domain-specific innate structure of modest scope, which lets experience select among, or trigger, a pre-specified range of options. Keep the nativist's structure as small as can honestly do the job.
- Measure "built-in structure" as the total apparatus that must be in the initial state for acquisition of C to succeed — not only explicitly domain-specific content, but also priors or hypothesis spaces that are the only reason the learner favors C over rival generalizations left open by the data.

Construct the scenario under these constraints. The learner's input must be genuinely impoverished: a few positive examples, no negative feedback, no corrective signal, and no access to hidden structure. The input must underdetermine the competence badly enough that a generic simplicity prior would pick the wrong generalization; C should be the less obvious rule that the learner nonetheless acquires. The competence should be specified formally enough that the case can be judged from its internal logic — a toy grammar, a perceptual rule, or a motor routine is fine — and it should not rely on disputed facts about how real children acquire natural language. No exotic physics, no handcrafted teachers, no stipulating the verdicts; the inversion has to fall out of the two accounts once they are spelled out.

The case is for a running argument I keep having about whether a lot of empiricist positions are only parsimonious because their domain-specific commitments are hidden inside the learning mechanism. After the case, tell me honestly whether the case actually establishes that, or whether the empiricist has a clean way out.

Give the answer in American English, with these sections in this order and roughly these lengths:
1. Bottom line (3–5 sentences): the scenario in one or two clauses, the verdict on who builds in more, and the feature doing the work.
2. The scenario (150–250 words): the learner, the input, the competence C, and the fact an account must explain. No more than two paragraphs.
