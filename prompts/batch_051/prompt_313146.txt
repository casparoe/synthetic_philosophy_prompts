My friend and I have been going back and forth about Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and I've tried to lay out the argument I think he's actually making, as a numbered list. I can't decide if its sound or if one step is doing something dodgy.

(1) Informal mathematical proofs aren't formal deductions; they always rest on unstated lemmas and background assumptions about what the objects are.

(2) When you pressure-test an informal proof, you are really looking for counterexamples: cases where a hidden lemma fails or where the conclusion comes out false anyway.

(3) The history of Euler's formula shows this. The conjecture that V - E + F = 2 for any polyhedron was treated as proved for a long time, but then awkward cases turned up — a picture-frame-shaped polyhedron, for instance, gives V - E + F = 0. The community didn't throw the whole thing away; it tightened the definition of 'polyhedron' to rule out such monsters, or treated them as exceptions, or folded the refuted lemma back into the theorem as a newly explicit condition.

(4) So the meaning of 'polyhedron' gets changed in response to counterexamples, and the theorem's content isn't fixed until the proof and its refutations have finished arguing with each other.

(5) That cycle — conjecture, attempted proof, counterexample, revised conjecture or revised definition — is structurally the same as the empirical cycle of hypothesis, test, refutation, revision. The counterexamples are doing the job that data do.

(6) So informal mathematics is quasi-empirical. Not that its truths are merely probable, but that its method of justification (not just discovery) is fallible conjectures-and-refutations rather than derivation from fixed definitions.

I half-suspect the trouble is in step 5: a genuine mathematical counterexample refutes a lemma of necessity, whereas data refute hypotheses only fallibly and with auxiliary assumptions, so the analogy may be off. But I also wonder whether the real weakness is in step 4, where the community seems to be changing the subject rather than testing a single fixed conjecture. So: is the argument valid? If the argument is valid, does it really establish Lakatos's quasi-empiricism, or only something weaker about how research feels from the inside?
