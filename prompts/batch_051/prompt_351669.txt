I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a subplot in the book I'm drafting: a woman, Nora, has an emotionally absent father who has a stroke and needs her to take over his bills, insurance, and twice-a-week rides to rehab, and I can't settle what she actually owes him. To anchor my protagonist's choices in a real person's instincts, I've been keeping a mental file on a friend of mine who has firm, unapologetic verdicts on these family-obligation questions. She's the person everyone asks at parties. Somewhere I also picked up the idea that grown children stand to their parents more like friends than like debtors, and it keeps rattling around in my head while I draft.

Her track record: about her late mother, who raised her alone, never missed a track meet, and worked two jobs — she nursed her through cancer for months and says she owed her every minute of it. About a cousin's father, who was abusive and has been gone twenty years — she says the cousin owes him nothing; if he surfaced sick and broke tomorrow, calling an ambulance would be more than enough. About a hypothetical dad who paid child support on time but was otherwise a ghost, not hostile, and turned up in old age, financially comfortable, wanting a weekly phone call — she says he's owed nothing, because cutting checks isn't a relationship. And I once heard her complain that people treat their parents like unpaid care contracts, while a daughter who ignores a warm, once-devoted mother is, in her view, doing something wrong.

The prediction I want: Nora's father was never abusive — he paid for her college and her first car and bailed her out of a bad patch in her twenties — but he was a workaholic who never came to her recitals, never asked her a real question, and now they speak twice a year, cordially but with out affection. After the stroke, he isn't asking to move in; he wants her to manage his bills and drive him to appointments. I'd like your best guess about what this friend would say Nora owes, and especially what rule she seems to be running, because I can see at least two that fit her track record equally well. Please structure your answer in four sections: (1) "Candidate rules," roughly 200 words, laying out the two or three rules that fit her past verdicts and why each fits; (2) "What each predicts for Nora," roughly 250 words, running each rule on the stroke case; (3) "Your best guess," roughly 250 words, saying which rule has her actual allegiance, what verdict it yields, and which of her earlier comments tips you off; (4) "Deciding case," roughly 150 words, giving one further case I could put to her that would cleanly separate the remaining candidates.
