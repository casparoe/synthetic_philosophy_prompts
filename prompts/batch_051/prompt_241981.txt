I am a professor of political philosophy, and I chair the Standing Committee on Student Conduct at my university. Twice each semester, and more often during periods of campus protest, our five-member panel reviews cases of students who have been charged with conduct code violations arising from acts of civil disobedience: building occupations, disruption of administrative meetings, refusal to disperse after a warning, non-destructive defacement of university property, that sort of thing. The panel must issue a written decision and a sanction, ranging from a formal warning to suspension or expulsion, and in some cases it must recommend whether the university should refer the matter to the police. The decisions are appealable, and the university's own regulations tell the panel to take into account whether a violation was "an act of principled civil disobedience rather than ordinary rule-breaking." Three of my four fellow panelists are not philosophers: one is a student, one is a staff member, one is an alumna, and the fourth is a colleague from the law school. Stakes are high: a student can lose a term, a visa, or a career; the university can face litigation, legislative scrutiny, and a campus climate crisis; and the panel's reasoning becomes a public document that shapes what other students take themselves to be permitted to do.

I want you to write me a reusable decision procedure for these cases, derived from the philosophical literature on political obligation and civil disobedience rather than from generic notions of fairness or institutional risk. The tradition I have in mind runs from Plato's Crito — in which Socrates treats his long residence in and acceptance of Athens as generating a defeasible obligation to obey its laws even when they are being applied to him unjustly — through the later idea that civil disobedience is a public, communicative breach of law, addressed to the community's sense of justice, and undertaken by someone who accepts the legal consequences. The procedure must not simply encode one side of that tradition. It should be built around the central tension: that a person can have a real, content-independent obligation to comply with the rules of a cooperative scheme that benefits them, while at the same time that obligation can be overridden, but only by an act that meets strict conditions, not merely by sincere indignation.

The procedure should be usable by five intelligent people, only one of whom has formal philosophical training, in ninety minutes of discussion per case. It should be structured as an ordered series of tests or questions, with instructions about what to do when the answer is uncertain and which judgments cannot be eliminated by the procedure. It should cover, at minimum: whether the actor can offer a minimally serious argument that the rule or policy being protested is unjust in a way that bears directly on the conduct; whether institutional remedies were unavailable or genuinely exhausted or reasonably judged futile; whether the act was public, communicative, and non-evasive rather than concealable and self-protective; whether the act was proportionate in its interference with other people's rights and with the university's ordinary functioning; whether the actor has accepted, rather than attempted to evade or conceal, the consequence of the violation; and whether the actor's claim of conscience is connected to the injustice in a way that a reasonable observer could recognize, rather than being a rationalization for preference or convenience. I want the procedure to mark clearly which of these are factual findings, which are interpretive judgments, and which require the panel to say, honestly, "reasonable people could disagree."

Then apply the procedure to two worked cases, in enough detail that the application exposes the hard parts. One case should be an occupation of the president's office by students demanding fossil-fuel divestment; the other should be a student who disrupts a controversial invited speaker's lecture with sustained noise, claiming the speaker's presence itself is an act of harm to marginalized students. In at least one of the applications, I want you to show how the procedure might require the panel to reach a conclusion that the panel members, myself included, would rather not reach — e.g., a sympathetic cause that fails the conditions, or an unsympathetic cause that passes them. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: above all, whether it can determine that a policy is in fact unjust as opposed to merely the object of a serious argument, and whether it can determine the sincerity of conscience as opposed to the act's public face. I want the procedure to be honest about where it is a tool for structured judgment and where it is a device for exposing the panel's own unargued commitments.
