I've been trying to wrap my head around Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, and I wrote a short piece for my philosophy club's blog. I'd love comments on it — mostly on whether I've got the shape of the argument right, whether my last-paragraph reply is any good, and where a critc should push first.

Here's the draft:

The Basic Argument, Reconstructed

The Basic Argument aims to show that nobody is truly morally responsible for anything, where 'truly' responsible means being deserving of praise or blame in a deeper-than-pragmatic sense. The argument goes like this. Whenever you act, what you do is a product of the way you are at that moment: your beliefs, desires, values, temperament, mood, and so on. So if you are to be truly responsible for an action, you must be truly responsible for the relevant features of yourself that produced it. But to be responsible for the way you are, you would have to have brought it about that you are that way, through some earlier choice or action. And that earlier choice would itself be a product of how you were before you made it. So you would need to be responsible for that earlier self too, and then for the self behind that one, and so on forever. The only way to stop the regress would be to be causa sui — the cause of yourself — and that is impossible. So true moral responsibility seems to require impossible self-creation, and therefore nobody has it.

Then I try out a reply. Maybe responsibility can be acquired gradually, the way a person builds a skill or a reputation. Over the years I make choices that shape my character — I practice patience, I train myself to be more careful about evidence — and as an adult I own the character those choices built. Sure, each choice came from who I already was at the time, but the process as a whole might bootstrap itself without needing an ultimate, self-caused starting point. I want to say this saves ordinary responsibility-talk, but I'm not sure whether it answers Strawson or just quietly swaps his 'ultimate' notion for a weaker one and declares victory.

So my questions:

1. Did I get the Basic Argument right, or did I flatten it too much? Where exactly does my version go wrong, if it does?
2. Which step is the strongest candidate to reject, and why?
3. Is the bootstrapping reply a real objection, or is it just describing the kind of everyday responsibility Strawson already thought was compatible with his conclusion?
4. If someone accepted the Basic Argument, what should that actually change about ordinary practices like praise, blame, guilt, criminal punishment, and resentment?
