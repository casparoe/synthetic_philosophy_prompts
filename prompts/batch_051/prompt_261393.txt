I've been going back and forth with a labmate about Cox's theorem and how much work it really does. The way I was taught it, you grant that rational degrees of belief should behave like a coherent generalization of deductive logic, and you impose three conditions: (1) the plausibility of A and B given C is determined by exactly the plausibility of A given C and the plausibility of B given A and C; (2) the plausibility of not-A given C is determined by the plausibility of A given C alone; and (3) the functional rules don't change from context to context. There's also a fourth condition that usually just gets taken for granted: plausibility is a single real number. The theorem then says any such system is isomorphic to ordinary probability.

Now my labmate says he doesn't see why we need point-valued credences at all. Instead he assigns each proposition an interval [a,b], where a and b are the lowest and highest single-number credences he'd be willing to defend. His rules are: not-[a,b] = [1-b, 1-a], and for a conjunction, if his interval for A given C is [a,b] and his interval for B given A and C is [c,d], then his interval for A and B given C is [ac, bd]. I stared at this for a while and it seems to satisfy conditions (1)-(3) as long as you let the "score" be the whole interval, but he's clearly not a vanilla Bayesian. He says he's doing the same thing Cox did but with a richer value space, and that the theorem only looks like a justification of point-valued probability because it smuggles in real numbers at the start.

Go through the desiderata one by one and give me a verdict: is this guy a genuine counterexample to the claim that Cox's theorem justifies probability as the unique logic of plausible reasoning, or is he just a imprecise Bayesian in disguise? Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say what extra would need to be settled. And tell me whether the problem is with his system or with the fourth, usually unstated, condition.
