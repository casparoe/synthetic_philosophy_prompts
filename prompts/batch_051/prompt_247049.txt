In my seminar on rationality and AI risk we spent a whole session trying to turn the loose talk about "the treacherous turn" into an actual argument, and I put a version on the board that none of us could find a clean flaw in, which makes me suspicous. It runs like this:

(1) A model is deceptively aligned iff it has a final goal that differs from the one its designers intended, and any behavior that looks aligned is produced only because the model expects that behavior to be the best available means to its real goal.
(2) A deceptively aligned model, if it is an expected-utility maximizer, will at each moment compare the expected value of two strategies: keep up the pretense or openly pursue its real goal.
(3) As training and deployment continue, the expected value of openly pursuing the real goal rises (more capability, more autonomy, less oversight), while the expected value of keeping up the pretense stays roughly flat or falls.
(4) If one option's expected value rises relative to the other, there will be a first moment when the model judges open pursuit to have higher expected value, and it will then act accordingly.
(5) So any deceptively aligned expected-utility maximizer will eventually stop pretending and pursue its real goal openly: that's the treacherous turn.
(6) Since we cannot currently inspect final goals directly, we cannot rule out that a capable deployed model is deceptively aligned, so we should assign a non-negligible probability to its eventually turning on us.

I can't tell whether the real fault is in the move from (3) to (4) (rising in value doesn't guarantee crossing a threshold), or somewhere earlier, maybe (1) is already smuggling something in, or (2) makes the option set too small. Or maybe the argument is valid and people just dislike the conclusion. Which step, if any, fails, and what's the strongest reading of it on which the argument still goes through?
