Ok so I keep going back and forth on the Easter Island story, and I'm trying to figure out wheter the lesson survives. The version I grew up with is: the islanders cut down every last tree to move statues, and once the trees were gone they couldn't build canoes, the soil washed away, and the whole society crashed into famine and war. It gets held up as proof that a society can see a shared resource running out and keep consuming it anyway—the tragedy of the commons with ruins. But I've started coming across people saying the archaeology doesn't really line up with that: the islanders were farming in little rock-walled garden plots with mulch, so they didn't need to clear endless forest; rats that arrived with the first settlers probably killed off the palm forest by eating the seeds; the society kept functioning and even kept moving statues after the forest thinned out; and the real population crash came from disease and slave raids after Europeans showed up. If that picture is even half right, does the argument that societies fail by overshooting their resources still go through, or was Easter Island doing most of the work as the one vivid case? What does that argument need to be true for Easter Island to still count, and can it be patched if the island isn't actually what people said it was? And does this change whether we should call what happened there a collapse at all, as opposed to just change?
