You are an economist with a sideline in Greek philosophy, and you've been asked to think through a constitutional counterfactual. Here's the setup. In 2025 a wealthy, middle-sized country amends its constitution to encode what its draftees say is Aristotle's account of money from the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics. The preamble runs through the usual contrast between natural household acquisition and unnatural moneymaking (chrematistike), and it singles out interest (tokos, literally offspring) as the paradigm of money breeding money rather than serving real production.

Stripped of the preamble, the operative rule is this. Money may be used as a medium of exchange and a measure of value, and it may be lent or deposited. But no person or institution, public or private, may charge or receive a fixed return on the lending of money as such. Interest on loans and deposits and coupons on bonds are prohibitted, and so is any fee whose substance is a fixed return on principal. A return may be paid only as a share of the actual profits of an identifiable productive enterprise into which the money was placed — ordinary equity is fine, fixed-income securities are not, and simply relabelling interest as a service fee doesn't fool the courts. There is a five-year transition, after which the clauses bite.

Assume the country has a normal modern financial system: commercial banks, a central bank, mortgages, corporate bonds, pension funds, government debt. Predict, with your reasoning made visible, what its financial system and wider economy look like thirty years on. In particular:

- Which credit relationships quietly turn into equity-like or profit-share arrangements, and which activities simply shrink or vanish?
- What happens to ordinary household saving, retirement accounts, and mortgages?
- How does the government fund deficits once fixed-coupon sovereign debt is unavailable? Could something like GDP-linked bonds fill the gap, and who would buy them?
- How does the central bank run monetary policy when its usual interest-rate lever is ruled out?

I'm less interested in whether you think the whole thing is daft than in the mechanism. Start by brainstorming two or three ways the system could go, then commit to the one you find most likely and argue for it. Be explicit about which links in your chain are robust and which are guesses, and say what would have to be true for the prediction to fail. Oh, and answer in British English please.
