I host a weekly ideas podcast aimed at a lay but rigorous audience, and my next guest is a political philosopher who has spent much of her career defending a neo-Kantian interpretation of Rousseau's general will. In her reading, Rousseau's claim that a citizen who refuses to obey the general will may be compelled by the whole body is not a mandate for authoritarian rule; rather, it is the claim that a legitimate state may coerce me only in ways that I myself, insofar as I reason as a citizen and set aside my purely private interests, can recognize as laws I would have willed. The force of the state, on this account, is what keeps me bound to my own putatively rational commitments when my private appetites pull against them. I want interview questions that probe this position rather than merely ask her to restate it, because her standard reply to critics is that they are confusing the doctrine with a defense of arbitrary power, and I would like the interview to make that reply progressively harder to maintain.

The questions should press the familiar fault lines in the scholarship. For example: the old charge, associated especially with the postwar liberal critics who saw in Rousseau a precursor of totalitarian politics, that the doctrine licenses the state to define each citizen's true will and then silence dissent in its name; the distinction between the general will and the mere sum of private interests, and how a large modern electorate could ever be said to identify the former; the role of the Lawgiver and the civic religion in forming citizens who are capable of willing the general will in the first place; and whether the compulsion-to-be-free logic can be made to fit recognizable contemporary cases, such as mandatory vaccination or seatbelt-style regulation, without generating conclusions the defender herself would want to disown.

Please draft eight to ten questions. I want at least one question each on the basic paradox of using coercion to secure freedom, that is, why a rational will would ever need an external enforcer; the totalitarian objection and the boundary between Rousseau's view and a state that simply declares what your real interests are; how the general will is supposed to be ascertained under conditions of sincere pluralism and disagreement; the formative machinery of the Lawgiver, civil religion, and censorship, and whether a neo-Kantian reading can treat these as inessential; and a worked modern case such as pandemic restrictions. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and order the questions so that the easy deflections get closed off early, ending with a question about what evidence or cases would actually count against her interpretation.
