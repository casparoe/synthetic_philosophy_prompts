In my former life as a systematic biologist, the species problem occupied me for decades: how one draws a boundary through a set of intergrading populations when no single diagnostic character is shared by every member of a species, and variation runs continuously across the group. Having come to philosophy only in retirement, I recently encountered the letter-spirit problem — the question of what makes all the enormously varied hand-drawn instances of the letter 'a' instances of a single letter at all — and I was struck by how strongly it echoes my old problem. Both involve categories whose members shade into one another through overlapping likenesses rather than sharing a common essence; in both, recognition seems to lean on prototypical exemplars and something like family resemblance; and in both there is a residual notion — style in letterforms, local cohesion or shared descent in systematics — that does real work but resists compression into a set of necessary and sufficient features. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or only a loose analogy my old habits are projecting onto newer material? I would like the mapping stated precisely, the points where it breaks identified, and an honest verdict on whether anything transfers: could an adequate computational account of what makes an 'a' an 'a' genuinely illuminate how taxa should be delimited, or does the fact that species reproduce while letterforms do not make the analogy fail at exactly the point that matters?
