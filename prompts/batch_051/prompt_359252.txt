Here's an argument I heard at a confernece a few weeks ago and I've been turning over ever since. It seems to show that single-case probability claims — the kind oncologists, courts, and actuaries make all day — are never straightforwardly true or false. That conclusion can't be right, so one of the steps has to go. The argument:

(1) A claim like "the probability that E happens is p" is only well-defined relative to a reference class: we're saying that E belongs to some class C, and p is the frequency of E-type outcomes within C.
(2) Any event belongs to many reference classes at once, and these classes yield different frequencies. For instance, "the probability that this patient survives the surgery" could be the survival rate among all patients undergoing that surgery, or among women of her age with her lab values, or among patients at this hospital with this particular surgeon; and if you narrow all the way down to her exact physical state at time t, you seem to get either near 0 or near 1 rather than a tidy 0.7.
(3) There is no rule that singles out one of these classes as the objectively correct one. The choice reflects our interests: which comparisons we care about, which data we trust, which factors we think matter.
(4) Therefore, there is no fact of the matter about whether "the probability that she survives is 0.7" is true rather than false. Different classes give different answers, and none of them has authority over the others.
(5) So ordinary probability talk in medicine, law, and insurance is not a matter of stating truths. It is more like recommending a way of sorting cases.
(6) But that's hard to swallow. A second opinion can catch an outright mistake in someone's risk estimate; insurance premiums can be wrong; a judge can be wrong about "more likely than not." Those seem like truth-apt, factual judgments.

So the argument proves too much, and I assume one of (1)–(3) is the culprit or one of the moves between them is bad. I'm most attached to (1), but I could be talked out of any of them if I knew what I'd be paying. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
