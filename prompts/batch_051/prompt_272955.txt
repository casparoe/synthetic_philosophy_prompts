I’ve been working through the doctrine of double effect as Thomas Aquinas lays it out in the *Summa Theologiae* II–II, Q. 64, Art. 7, and I keep tripping on one clause:

> And yet, though proceeding from a good intention, an act may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end.

I’m compiling a list of objections specifically to what I’ll call the proportionality condition—the claim that self-defense (and by extension other double-effect acts) becomes unlawful when the harm caused is out of proportion to the end pursued. So far I have these four:

(1) The condition makes the doctrine too consequentially minded. Once the act’s lawfulness depends on the overall balance of goods and harms, the intended/foreseen distinction seems to lose much of the work it was introduced to do.

(2) It assumes a shared metric for goods and harms, but the very cases where double effect is invoked—killing one to save several, collateral damage to end a war, ending a pregnancy to preserve the mother’s life—involve values that may not be commensurable in any principled way.

(3) It is psychologically unrealistic as a guide to action. A person facing an aggressor cannot realistically compute a proportionality threshold, and the condition offers no determinate procedure for setting one even after the fact.

(4) It is vulnerable to redescription of the end. An agent can fix almost any level of harm as “proportionate” by describing the good end at a conveniently high level of generality, such as “saving my family” or “defending my community.”

Give me another objection that is not on this list and is not merely a variant, special case, or combination of these four. It should target the proportionality clause itself, not the broader intended/foreseen distinction, and it should differ in structure or in what it says goes wrong. After giving the objection, tell me honestly whether you think it is a serious problem for Aquinas’s test or only for modern restatements of it.
