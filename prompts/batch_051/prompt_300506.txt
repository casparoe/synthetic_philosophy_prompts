At a school-board meeting I attended, a member argued against a proposal to reallocate funds toward closing the spread in reading scores. His case, as I reconstruct it, ran as follows. Differences between children in reading ability are substantially heritable, so we should think of a child's genotype as seed and the school environment as soil. Different seeds planted in the same soil do not grow into the same plant; a dandelion seed never becomes an oak no matter how carefully it is tended. Therefore equalizing the soil cannot be expected to equalize the plants. He concluded that the district should stop promising to equalize outcomes and should instead aim to help each child develop the capacities their seed contains.

That analogy is carrying a real policy conclusion, and I would like you to audit it. First, lay out the intended correspondence table: what in the school setting is supposed to map onto seed, soil, plant, and tending. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and in doing so be explicit about which features of seeds and soil the analogy quietly imports — fixed ceilings, independence of seed and soil, additivity of their contributions, neutrality of the same soil across seeds, a seed's being the same seed regardless of the soil it lands in — and which of those features do or do not hold for genotype and environment. Third, separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the board member's conclusion from the ones that are merely loose metaphor. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy (or none) better captures what a heritability estimate does and does not license.
