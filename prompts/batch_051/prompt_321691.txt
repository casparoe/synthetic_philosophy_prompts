Hey, I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion (mainly working on the problem of evil) and I've been reading a popular book on Christianity and evolution to plug some gaps in my background on natural evil. It gives a summary of the debate about animal suffering before the Fall, and I keep going back and forth on whether to trust it. Here's the gist of what the book says:

Christian theology took Genesis's claim that creation was very good to mean that the original world had no death, predation, or disease in it at all, and it read Paul's remark about death entering the world through sin as covering animals as well as humans. Evolution then showed that animals killed, sickened, and died for hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed, which put the doctrine of a good creation under real pressure. According to the book, the debate now reduces to two live responses. The first says animals feel pain but don't suffer in the morally important sense, since genuine suffering requires a sense of oneself as continuing through time, which animals lack; so the pre-human world was painful without being evil. The second says animal death and predation are simply part of how a good, self-sustaining creation works, and were never among the things God's calling creation very good ruled out. The book then dismisses both: the first is special pleading about inner life that no one would accept if the injured party were a human infant, and the second quietly redefines good until the word has no content left. It's conclusion is that the doctrine of a good creation didn't survive Darwin, and theologians who keep defending it are ignoring the fossil record.

My instinct is to push back a little — I have a vague memory that the no-death-before-the-Fall reading was more settled for humans than for animals, and that Paul's text in context says death spread to all people, so bringing animals in is already an interpretive move; and the second response sounds less like redefining good and more like a substantive claim about what a creation's being good consists in. But I haven't read the primary sources and the author is a professional, so I don't want to overcorrect from a half-remembered lecture. Is this a fair summary? I'd like you to separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and which conclusions actually survive once the sketch is corrected.
