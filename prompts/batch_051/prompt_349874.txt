Design a runnable classroom activity for a second-year undergraduate seminar on the problem of other minds. The seminar has about twenty students and one seventy-five-minute session. The activity should make participants feel the asymmetry between first-person access to their own minds and the indirect, behavior-based access they have to other people's minds, and it should stage the "main character / NPC" intuition — the thought that one's own mind is vividly present while everyone else could be behaviorally identical yet lack inner experience — in a way that lets them discover both its pull and its limits before it is named. The idea should emerge from the mechanics of the activity: use role cards, private information, rules of communication, and a task with enough structure that each participant directly knows something about their own current mental states but can only infer everyone else's, so that the question "how do I know they are not NPCs?" becomes live during the exercise rather than in the explanation afterward. Provide complete rules, materials, a schedule, and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the first-person/third-person asymmetry, the argument from analogy, the problem of other minds, the philosophical-zombie possibility, and the solipsistic main-character intuition, indicating where these come apart. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each predictable failure. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: every participant is in fact conscious, so no one can actually be a zombie, and the exercise can only simulate how others look from the outside; the facilitator must supply that point, and should also mark the step from epistemic asymmetry to the practical or moral stance of treating others as if they have minds.
