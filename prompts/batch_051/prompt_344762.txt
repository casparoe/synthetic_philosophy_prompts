I am a novelist, and a life spent attending to sentences has pushed me toward a view about words: that they have no stable meanings of their own, that a word's meaning is entirely constituted by what it does in particular contexts, and that the fixed meaning we attribute to a word is a retrospective abstraction. Before I settle into this view, I want to know what would count against it. Which linguistic phenomena, arguments, or intuitions should most worry someone in my position — for instance, the way words seem to carry content with them into unfamiliar contexts, the apparent usefulness of dictionary definitions, the distinction between ambiguity and mere context-sensitivity, compositionality, or the practical demands of communication and translation? I want the counterconsiderations ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a sophisticated defender of this view would concede as genuine costs rather than explain away. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the position, once qualified in the usual ways, has become so insulated that nothing could count against it — and whether that is a problem for a philosophical account of language of this kind.
