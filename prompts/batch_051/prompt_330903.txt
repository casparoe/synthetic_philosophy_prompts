You are a professional philosopher of language and metaphysics, with special expertise in the semantics of fictional discourse, empty names, and ontology. Think carefully. Before you write the final document, brainstorm the strongest and most charitable formulations each side would accept, and discard any wording that one side could reasonably reject as slanted.

I want you to draft the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two positions about fictional characters and sentences such as "Sherlock Holmes is a detective."

Position A, abstract-object realism: "Sherlock Holmes" is a referring expression. It refers to an abstract object — a fictional character — that Arthur Conan Doyle literally brought into existence through his acts of writing. On this view, "Holmes is a detective" is true only relative to the stories, while outside the fiction, sentences such as "Holmes is an abstract object," "Holmes was created by Conan Doyle," and "There is a fictional character named 'Holmes'" are literally true.

Position B, fictional anti-realism: "Sherlock Holmes" is an empty name; there is no object, abstract or otherwise, that it refers to. On this view, sentences apparently about Holmes are true only when governed by an operator such as "In the stories..." or when understood within a game of make-believe. Thus "Holmes is a detective" is not literally true when taken at face value, while "Holmes does not exist" is literally true.

Draft the document the two camps could jointly sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. It must contain the following.

(1) A numbered agreed-claims section. Include substantive claims both sides accept about the data, including at least these kinds of case: negative existentials ("Holmes does not exist"); statements true in the fiction ("In the stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street"); external statements ("Conan Doyle created the character"); intentional statements ("Sarah admires Holmes"); comparative and cross-fictional statements ("Holmes is more famous than any real detective" and "Holmes is cleverer than Hercule Poirot"); and quantification ("Some fictional characters are more widely recognized than most heads of state"). Where the two sides would sign the same words but intend different readings, do not paper over the difference: either state the shared wording and register the divergent readings in the table, or rephrase until the words themselves do no partisan work.

(2) A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give: the issue; each side's formulation of its own view, worded so that side would sign it; the precise crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and at least one case, argument, or test that both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction for that case recorded in advance. Pay special attention to these candidate cruxes: whether "Holmes does not exist" expresses a singular proposition about Holmes; whether "Sarah admires Holmes" requires an actual object of admiration or can be true through pretense; and whether cross-fictional comparisons such as "Holmes is cleverer than Poirot" have truth values at all, or instead require the two fictions to share a world.

The aim is not to declare a winner. The aim is to produce exact formulations that separate merely verbal disagreement from substantive disagreement, and to specify what would have to change for either side to concede. Do not include citations, footnotes, or a reading list; the document should stand on its own.
