I'm a novelist, and I do a lot of research reading for characters and settings, so I end up reading a fair amount of social science and journalism. Here's a habit I've developed. Whenever I see a claim about a difference between two groups in the aggregate—treatment vs. control, men vs. women, people who married vs. people who didn't—I basically refuse to update my beleifs until I've seen the numbers broken down by at least one more variable. Once I read that Hospital A had the better overall recovery rate, but when you split patients by how sick they were on arrival, Hospital B did better in both the severe group and the mild group. Ever since, I've treated any aggregate comparison as evidence of nothing. My partner, who works in public health, says I'm overcorrecting: sometimes the aggregate is the number you actually care about, and slicing by subgroups can make things look reversed when they aren't, or just give you noisy little slices. Who's right? Is my rule defensible, or is it a one-time trauma dressed up as a principle? And if it's overcorrecting, what would a better version of the rule be—when should a non-statistician accept an aggregate comparison, and when should they demand the splits?
