I'm a retired geologist, and I've spent enough time with measurement error and calibration that probability theory feels like home, but I'm new to the philosophical arguments about what rational degrees of belief have to look like. I came across a little argument that purports to show rational confidence must obey probability theory, and it feels too easy, like I've cheated somehwere but cant see where. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) Suppose a finite collection of propositions. Assignments of degrees of confidence can be scored for inaccuracy by the Brier score: for each proposition, subtract the truth-value (1 if true, 0 if false) from the degree assigned, square that difference, and sum over all propositions; lower is better.
(2) If there is some single alternative assignment that would get a lower Brier score than yours under every way the world could be, then your assignment is irrational.
(3) For any assignment that violates the probability laws, and any fixed way the world could be, there is at least one assignment obeying the probability laws that gets a lower Brier score in that state: the one that puts 1 on every proposition true in that state and 0 on every proposition false in that state.
(4) So for any assignment violating the probability laws, there is a single assignment obeying the probability laws that gets a lower Brier score in every possible state.
(5) Therefore, by (2), any assignment violating the probability laws is irrational.

I keep going back and forth. Something about step (3) looks cheap to me, but I can't tell whether that's because the argument itself is invalid or because I'm still importing habits from probability theory that don't belong. And I also wonder if step (2) is doing more work than it admits. Could you go through the steps and tell me exactly which one, if any, fails, and what the corrected version of the argument would have to prove?
