Social studies has been doing this unit on why different societies handle insults so differently, and my teacher kept using the words honor culture, dignity culture, and victimhood culture, like our school is drifting between them depending on who you ask. Then after practice, my basketball coach told me to shake off some stuff an opponent said, and he literally hit me with "sticks and stones may break my bones but words will never hurt me." It made me realize that proverb is doing way more than it looks like, because half the class would call it obviously true and the other half would call it gaslighting.

I want the different precise things that sentence could be claiming. Like, is it a prediction about your feelings (if you're tough, the words won't bother you), a claim about status (it's just words, nobody has to take them seriously), a claim about what's actually damaged (the insult doesn't remove anything real from you), a norm about how you ought to respond, or something else about who gets to decide what counts as harm? For each reading I want a verdict: trivially true, empirically false, defensible but contested, or just a way to dismiss somebody. And can you connect the readings back to the three culture thing? Which reading would an honor culture reject, and why it treats the insult as a real wound to your standing anyway? Which reading would a victimhood culture reject, and why it says words can injure you even if you say they don't? Basically I want a menu, not just one interpretation, so I can tell wether my coach is making a modest point about resilience or sneaking in a whole stance on what an insult is.
