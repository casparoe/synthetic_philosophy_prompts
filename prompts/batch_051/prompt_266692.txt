For an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of mathematics (about fifteen students, eighty minutes), design a hands-on activity built around Plimpton 322, the Old Babylonian clay tablet usually read as a table of Pythagorean triples. The activity should make participants feel the pull of the view that mathematics does not begin with proof: that systematic tabulation, standardized lists, and rule-governed calculation can already be mathematical. Have the students themselves generate Pythagorean triples from small reciprocal pairs, using the bare fact that if x and 1/x are reciprocals then (x − 1/x)/2, 1, and (x + 1/x)/2 are the sides of a right triangle; at least part of the arithmetic should be carried out in a simplified sexagesimal notation, restricted to pairs of regular numbers whose reciprocals terminate in base 60, so that the work feels like a scribal craft rather than an exercise in algebra. Provide concrete rules, a materials list using only ordinary classroom items, a timeline, and a debrief in which specific moments in the exercise are mapped onto competing accounts of when counting becomes mathematics. Also say what will most likely go wrong the first time the activity is run, and be explicit about which part of the historical question the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the instructor.
