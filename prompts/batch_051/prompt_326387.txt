I'm a backend engineer, and through some admin accident I've ended up sitting on our lab's frontier safety review board — the group that signs off on proposed training runs before they get the green light. I've got a decision in a couple of weeks and I keep going back and forth on what we should do, because I'm not sure our past decisions come down to the rule my colleagues think they do.

The official story is that we run a de facto compute ceiling: nothing above 10^26 FLOP gets approved, and below that we mostly approve, sometimes with conditions. But I don't think that's actually the principle we've been applying, and the new case is the first one that would expose the difference.

Our past decisions, roughly:

1. A 2×10^25 FLOP model, closed weights, trained for code completion. Approved with no conditions.
2. A 4×10^26 FLOP open-weight general-purpose multimodal model with heavy agentic fine-tuning. It would have landed clearly beyond the public frontier. Rejected; our stated reason was that open weights would make the capability impossible to retract.
3. A 9×10^25 FLOP closed-weight general chat model, slightly below our own deployed model on most benchmarks. Approved with conditions: API-only, staged rollout, and an eval suite before wider access.
4. A 3×10^25 FLOP open-weight model that was deliberately narrow — a research maths/theorem-proving thing. Approved, weights released under a standard licence.

New proposal: a 5×10^25 FLOP general-purpose open-weight model, fine-tuned for long-horizon agentic work. It sits roughly at the public frontier, not clearly past it, and the safety evals so far show no obvious bio, cyber, or deception red flags. The team argues that on the compute-ceiling rule it's obviously fine — well under 10^26 — and that agentic fine-tuning is common enough in open source that we wouldn't be crossing any new line.

But here's my worry. I can think of at least three or four rules that would have produced all four of our past decisions, and they give different answers on this new one: it's about raw compute; it's about open weights combined with generality; it's about long-horizon agentic capability; it's about whether you'd be putting something past the public frontier. Our track record doesn't distinguish them because the concerning features have always come bundled together.

So, in as much detail as you can manage:

1. List the principles that fit all four past decisions.
2. Say what each one would do with the new proposal.
3. Give your best guess as to which principle we should treat ourselves as having adopted — the one that best explains and justifies the track record — and what we should therefore decide.
4. Say which past case is the closest precedent for the new one, and whether our precedents genuinely force one answer or just leave us free to pick a principle.
5. Suggest one future case, varying whichever features you like, that would cleanly seperate the candidate principles.
