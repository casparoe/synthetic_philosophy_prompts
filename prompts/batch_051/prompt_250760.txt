I am drafting a short position statement on temperance and self-control for a workshop, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is my current view, set out as seven commitments. I cannot tell where the real fault lines are, and I would rather not bias the answer by guessing. Please go through the commitments, determine which pairs are in genuine tension and which are only apparently in tension and dissolve on inspection, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most with the least collateral damage. Take the deliverable to be an audit, not a lecture: I am not asking for a summary of the standard case for or against each individual commitment. Begin with your overall verdict in your first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

1. I accept the classical distinction between temperance and self-control. The self-controlled person still has appetites that pull against her better judgment and overrides them; the temperate person's appetites have been reordered so that they no longer pull against that judgment in the first place. These are different states, and losing the distinction would mean losing much of what makes temperance a virtue rather than a technique.

2. But I also think self-control is, for nearly everyone, the developmental path to temperance, and I think in ordinary evaluative practice we should mostly give the two equal credit. Someone who is still fighting a craving and wins deserves, in ordinary life, no less admiration than the person whose craving has faded.

3. I hold an endorsement-relative account of free action. An agent acts freely when the desire she acts on is one she reflectively endorses, even if a lower-order desire pulls hard against it. On this view the recovering alcoholic who endorses sobriety and refuses a drink despite craving is a paradigm of free agency, not a slave to appetite.

4. I do not think temperance can be given a purely endorsement-relative account. A woman who reflectively endorses a life of heavy drinking, feels no inner conflict about it, and acts on that endorsement is not thereby temperate, even though she is not weak-willed by her own lights. The appetites themselves can be disordered even when the will is wholly her own.

5. For the same reason, I resist reducing temperance to prudence. A person who moderates her drinking only because she calculates that the health and social costs of excess outweigh the pleasures is prudent but not yet temperate; the appetite is being managed, not reordered.

6. I am suspicious of rigid abstention rules — the drinker's "not one drop," the dieter's "no refined sugar, ever," the productivity regime's "no screens after nine." They tend to treat the desire as an enemy to be suppressed rather than integrated, and the control they produce is often brittle or joyless. They look to me less like temperance than like repression wearing the costume of discipline.

7. But I cannot accept the corresponding slogan that moderation can handle everything. For some agents and some objects — the addict and the substance being the clearest case — a clean break is the only realistic form of temperance, and telling such a person to integrate the desire would be bad advice.
