You are a professional philosopher who specializes in self-knowledge, moral psychology, and the design of adversarial collaborations between research programs that have been talking past one another. You have been brought in as a neutral pen for two camps that have argued for years about self-examination, and your job is to produce the document they should have produced together.

The two camps are as follows.

Reflectionism: the view that self-examination, when competently performed, is on balance a source of self-knowledge, autonomy, and better decision-making, and that the well-known harms associated with introspection are really the harms of its pathological variant, rumination. Reflectionists characterize competent self-examination as structured, time-limited, directed at an answerable question, and sensitive to the risk that attending to a mental state can alter it. They think rumination is real and harmful, but they treat it as a breakdown of the activity, not a natural upshot of it.

Anti-rumination: the view that self-examination and rumination are not sharply separable in practice, that the distinction between them is often drawn retroactively by looking at whether the outcome was good, and that directing attention inward under uncertainty or distress systematically promotes repetitive, passive, self-focused thought. Anti-rumination theorists hold that the safer default is outward-directed, concrete problem-solving, and that the alleged skill of reflecting without ruminating is far less trainable, and far less stable under cognitive load, than Reflectionists suppose.

Both camps are sophisticated and have already conceded the obvious points on the other side. Reflectionists concede that rumination is common and harmful. Anti-rumination theorists concede that some structured self-examination has instrumental benefits in at least some cases. Neither side may be portrayed as a strawman.

Before drafting, spend as much time as you need brainstorming candidate formulations, analogies, and cruxes. Think carefully about which apparent disagreements are substantive and which would dissolve once terms are sharpened. Do not produce a mushy compromise that blurs the disagreement; produce maximum sharpness on both the shared ground and the residual conflict.

Your deliverable is the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should have four sections.

Section 1: Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both sides accept. These must not be vacuous. They should include, at minimum, claims about the following: a working characterization of rumination that neither side rejects; the fact that self-examination can alter its own object, so introspection is not a neutral measurement of a fixed state; the contested status of standard measures of self-knowledge, such as self-report, prediction of one's own behavior, and agreement with close others; and the existence of a regress problem, namely that the act of deciding whether one is currently reflecting or ruminating is itself a self-focused cognitive act that can become a vehicle for rumination.

Section 2: A disagreements table. Use three columns: the residual disagreement stated in neutral wording; the Reflectionist phrasing of their own side; and the Anti-rumination phrasing of their own side. Phrase each side's view so that that side would accept the wording. Include at least these four rows: (a) whether the reflection/rumination distinction tracks a real online psychological difference or is a post hoc evaluative distinction; (b) whether instructing someone under distress to think about why they feel as they do is on balance a beneficial default; (c) whether self-examination has non-instrumental value, such as being partly constitutive of autonomy, that could justify some cost to well-being; and (d) whether a teachable skill can reliably keep self-examination from sliding into rumination outside clinical or explicitly structured settings.

Section 3: Cruxes. For each crux, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side, and describe a concrete piece of evidence or study that both sides would accept as bearing on it and that could in principle be run, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Include at least three cruxes. One must be the process-versus-content crux: if negative content is held fixed, does structuring the self-focused thought remove the harm? One must concern training and transfer: can a skill of question-driven, time-limited self-analysis be taught so that it survives cognitive load and negative affect outside the training context? One must concern the regress problem: when someone asks themselves whether they are currently reflecting or ruminating, does this act typically function as a neutral act of metacognition that can break rumination, or does it typically become another rumination episode? Predictions should be specific enough that at least one side could be surprised by the outcome.

Section 4: Verbal disagreements. Identify any apparent disagreements that turned out to be disputes about words, and state the redescription on which both camps can agree, so that these items do not need to sit in the disagreements table.

The finished document must be one that both camps would actually sign, while leaving the remaining disagreement exact rather than fudged. If a claim cannot be signed by one side without a caveat, move it into the disagreements table rather than silently slanting it. Do not adjudicate which side is right; your task is to draft a constructive shared document, not a verdict.
