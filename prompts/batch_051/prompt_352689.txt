I'm a science journalist preparing a long feature on why humans can learn a concept from a couple of examples while machine-learning systems remain brittle when the task is genuinely novel. For the piece I have been using Bongard problems as a clean case study. A Bongard problem is a set of twelve small panels, six on the left and six on the right, where the solver's job is to state the single rule that all six left panels obey and all six right panels violate. The philosophical difficulty is that any finite left-right split is consistent with infinitely many rules, including gerrymandered, disjunctive, after-the-fact rules that no human would ever produce. Yet people usually converge on the intended rule, sometimes from a single exposure. I have been collecting the standard families of explanation for why one generalization is the right one, and I want to pressure-test my list before I build the article around it.

Here is the list so far. (1) Simplicity in the sense of compression: the winning hypothesis is the shortest program, description, or minimum-description-length account among those consistent with the examples, so gerrymandered rules lose. (2) Natural structure in the world: the brain prefers hypotheses that track real clusters, covariances, or joints in nature, so the intended rule wins because the world really is carved at some places rather than others. (3) Compositionality through a language of thought: a finite stock of primitives and combination rules makes some hypotheses natural and others impossible or monstrous, and the intended Bongard rule is the one assembled from that grammar. (4) Bayesian concept learning under strong sampling: the examples are treated as draws from the target extension rather than as arbitrary labels, and the learner jointly infers the hypothesis and the sampling process, which sharply penalizes oversized or disjunctive enumerations. (5) Communicative intent: the panels were chosen by a teacher to convey a concept to a learner with minimal examples, so the preferred rule is the one a rational teacher would most plausibly have meant a learner to recover.

Please take the role of a cognitive scientist or philosopher of induction who works on concept learning. Start by brainstorming three or four candidate sixth principles before settling on the strongest; briefly do this in your reply. Then give me one more candidate principle that is genuinely different in kind from the five above, not a restatement, special case, or combination of them. State clearly what this sixth principle adds that the others do not, how it relates to the list, and then assess it honestly: does it survive scrutiny, or does it collapse back into something already on the list? If you conclude that no sixth principle exists, say so and tell me what that would imply for my article.
