An acquaintance who works in computational neuroscience keeps leaning on the old software/hardware analogy to settle disputes about mental causation. On her telling, the mind is to the brain as a program is to a computer: the symbol-level description — beliefs, desires, decisions — is a real causal level, but it does not violate the underlying physics any more than a word processor's delete-paragraph command reaches down and pushes individual transistors around. She then ties this to Dennett's careenium thought experiment, which she summarizes roughly as follows. Imagine a two-dimensional world containing many tiny balls that roll, collide, and stick according to simple local rules. Configurations of balls can be interpreted as symbols, and over time these configurations get caught up in self-perpetuating cycles. Dennett's question, as she puts it, is whether the symbols push the balls around; her answer is that they do not — the balls push the balls around — but the symbol patterns are nonetheless the level at which the interesting causal regularities live. She uses this to argue that worries about mental states being epiphenomenal rest on a confusion, and that folk psychology is safe.
