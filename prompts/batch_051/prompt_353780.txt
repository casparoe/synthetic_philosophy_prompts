Six months ago my mother died, and I have spent much of the time since trying to decide whether I can accept the view Schrödinger takes from Vedanta in his writings on the self: that the apparently separate minds I meet are not a plurality of consciousnesses at all, but one consciousness appearing in many points of view; that the self which speaks in the first person behind my face and behind hers was never two things; and that death closes a point of view without extinguishing what looked through it. I keep almost believing it and keep losing it; grief insists on the irreplaceable separateness of the face I remember. I want a poem that works out that tension rather than argues it — something I could bear to read at her memorial.

Please write a pantoum of five quatrains. In each quatrain, the second and fourth lines should repeat as the first and third lines of the next, so that the poem advances by carrying its own past forward in new combinations. In the final quatrain the forward motion should break and fold back, so that the poem's last line is also its first line. Please let the form do the philosophical work: the repeated lines should be written so that their sense shifts slightly in each new context, as if the difference between one self and many selves is only a difference of arrangement. The closing fold should read as a death that is not an ending but a return to what was already there at the beginning. The poem should not name Schrödinger, Vedanta, atman, or maya, and should not quote any source; a reader who knows the view should recognize it from the repetition and the return, and a reader who does not should still find it a good, self-contained poem.
