I've been rereading a bunch of epistemology lately, and the one dispute I can't let go of is the long standoff between epistemic contextualists, pragmatic encroachers, and plain invariantists over cases where practical stakes seem to change whether somebody "knows" something. The shared datum is the familiar bank-style scenario: same person, same evidence, same ordinary inclination to say he knows the bank is open on Saturday; then his wife points out that if they fail to deposit by Saturday the check bounces and their credit gets wrecked, and he suddenly hedges and says he doesn't really know after all, he'd better double check. The contextualist says the word "know" just has a stricter meaning in the high-stakes context, so the guy knew in the low-stakes context and the word shifted under his feet. The pragmatic encroacher says the stakes didn't shift the word, they removed the knowledge: whether you know depends partly on how much turns on being right, not only on your evidence. The invariantist says both camps have been talked into a mistake, and the high-stakes "I don't know" is just loose talk, an exaggeration, or a non-literal retraction. As far as I can tell this has been running since the nineties with smart people on every side, all of them agreeing about the surface facts of the vignette, and none of them moving.

My working theory, which is probably just my rationalist-blog training showing, is that the whole thing is a disagreement about what the word "know" is for, and that the sides are answering different questions under one label. The contextualist is doing semantics: describing how competent speakers actually use "know" across contexts. The pragmatic encroacher is doing something closer to normative epistemology or decision theory: saying what an agent may treat as settled given what's at stake. The invariantist is defending a clean, stakes-free notion of evidential support and treating the rest as noise. If I'm right, theres no single proposition they all disagree about, and the debate feels stalled because it's really three projects sharing a word. But maybe I've flattenned it. Diagnose the structure of this debate for me. Is it a substantive disagreement in epistemology, a merely verbal one about the word "know", or a case where different questions got the same name? If it's substantive, what hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, and what would actually move it—experimental pragmatics, corpus data, a formal semantics with a stakes parameter, a decision-theoretic result, or something else? If it isn't substantive, say what each side is really after and what they should concede to stop talking past each other.
