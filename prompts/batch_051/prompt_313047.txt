Consider the following argument, which purports to show that a person who knows an ordinary empirical proposition may rationally refuse to examine any evidence against it, because she can know in advance that all such evidence must be misleading. I am convinced the conclusion cannot be right, but the steps look individually plausible, and I need a step-by-step diagnosis rather than a general hunch.

Read 'evidence against p' throughout as evidence whose content supports the negation of p. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Amina knows that p, where p is an ordinary empirical proposition (say, that a particular colleague is in the library), and her justification for p is adequate by ordinary standards.

(2) If p is true, then any evidence against p is objectively misleading: it points an inquirer towards a proposition that is false.

(3) Amina can know (2) a priori, since it is a conceptual truth about evidence and misleadingness.

(4) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if Amina knows that p, and she knows that p entails that any evidence against p is objectively misleading, then she knows that any evidence against p is objectively misleading.

(5) Therefore, without inspecting any future piece of evidence, Amina now knows, of any piece of evidence against p, that it is objectively misleading.

(6) If Amina knows of a piece of evidence that it is objectively misleading, then she is rationally permitted to set that evidence aside: she need not lower her confidence in p in response to it, and she need not examine its contents before retaining p.

(7) When a piece of evidence E against p is later brought to Amina's attention, she can apply her standing knowledge from (5) to E, conclude that E is objectively misleading, and then, by (6), rationally retain p without examining E's contents.

(8) Since the reasoning generalises to any knower and any known proposition, anyone who knows something is rationally permitted to ignore all counter-evidence. But a rational inquirer is answerable to counter-evidence, so such dogmatism cannot be rationally permitted. At least one step must therefore be at fault.

Your task is to say exactly where the argument gives way. In particular:

(i) Is the argument valid as written? If it is, say so and explain why the conclusion, though unwelcome, follows.

(ii) If it is not valid, identify which numbered step or steps fail, and for each failing step state whether it is false, ambiguous, an invalid inference, or a suppressed premise that does not survive scrutiny.

(iii) Consider whether 'objectively misleading' means the same thing in step (2) as it does in step (6), and what the strongest reading of each step is. Consider also whether the knowledge attributed to Amina at step (5) is still available at step (7) once evidence has actually been presented, or whether the presentation itself could undermine that knowledge. If you think a premise such as the closure principle in (4) is true only under a restricted or schematic reading, state that reading and assess the remainder of the argument under it, rather than simply rejecting the premise as controversial.
