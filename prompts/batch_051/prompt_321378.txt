Over the holidays I got into an argument with my sister, who's a psychiatrist, about something I can't quite resolve. Everyone I know thinks it's admirable when somebody remakes himself the hard way: the pathologically shy guy who spends a decade forcing himself into public speaking until he's genuinely outgoing, the person who overcomes a vicious temper through years of therapy and meditation, the addict who white-knuckles through withdrawal and rebuilds a whole personality around sobriety. We treat those people as having accomplished something, maybe even as having become more fully themselves. But now put a device or a drug in the story. Suppose a different man with the same crippling shyness gets a neural implant that reshapes the same trait over the same ten years, same endpoint, same person-shaped result, except the implant is doing part of the work his willpower did in the first case. Or suppose a pill reliably makes someone less avoidant and skips five years of practice. Most people I've talked to, my sister included, find the second path less admirable and less authentic, maybe even a kind of loss of self. But if the endpoint is stipulated to be the same from the inside and the outside, what exactly has been lost? The effort? The process? The role of choosing? Something about continuity or ownership? I cant tell whether we're tracking a real moral or metaphysical difference or just a bias in favor of whatever looks like hard work.

I want the strongest principled answer I can get to this: is there anything that justifies treating effort-based self-transformation and technology-based self-transformation asymmetrically even when the final trait, behavior, and phenomenology are stipulated to be identical? Test whatever principle you find against at least these cases: someone who takes the device under duress; someone who was born outgoing and never had to work at it; someone who does the hard work but only because a parent pushed them into it; a drug that merely removes the background anxiety so the person can then do the slow practice themselves; and a device that makes the change instantly but no more completely than the slow route. Then give an honest verdict: does the asymmetry survive, should we keep it, reverse it, or give it up? And on the back of that, say what you'd tell a transhumanist about whether radical self-transformation is desirable and what, if anything, is genuinely lost on the technological route.
