In my arguments about foreign policy I follow a rule: when someone reaches for a historical analogy — a "Munich" appeasement parallel, a "Vietnam" quagmire comparison, a "Thucydides trap" warning — I treat the analogy as carrying no evidential weight unless the person can state the specific causal mechanism by which the past case is supposed to transfer to the present. Surface resemblance, however vivid, does not count for me, and since most analogies in public debate fail this test I usually end up dismissing them. Is this rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it a bias against pattern recognition dressed up as a demand for rigor? Under what conditions should a historical analogy legitimately move my beliefs, and is there a version of the rule that keeps its benefits without its costs?
