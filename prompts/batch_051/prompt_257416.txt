As a PhD student in philosophy of biology, I have spent two years reading the gene's-eye-view debate, and I am struck by how little the two sides have moved though they share the same population-genetic facts. Dawkins and Williams hold that natural selection is fundamentally a matter of alleles persisting at different rates, with organisms and groups serving as vehicles; Gould, Sober, and Wilson reply that selection can act simultaneously at several levels and that the gene's-eye description is one bookkeeping scheme among others, sometimes misleading because it hides trait-group and species-level dynamics. After half a century, neither side has converted the other, even though both sides now draw on the same formal framework.

The deeper puzzle is that neither side seems able to state an empirical prediction the other cannot absorb. The Price equation lets one write the change in a trait as a covariance between the trait and fitness plus a transmission term, and the units over which the covariance is computed — genes, organisms, or groups — can be chosen. A gene's-eye advocate can redescribe a group-selection result in terms of gene frequencies; a multilevel-selection advocate can redescribe a gene's-eye result as a partition over groups. When I press colleagues on which data would settle the dispute, I get incompatible answers: some say a uniquely correct causal decomposition exists and an experiment would find it, others say the decomposition is interest-relative and no experiment could, and still others say the question was never empirical at all.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is really two disagreements sharing one name: a conceptual one about what 'unit of selection' should mean — replicator, interactor, or locus of causal responsibility — and a methodological one about which decomposition is most likely to generate good research, with each side silently using its own answer to the first to settle the second. I would like a structural diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive but about explanatory values rather than facts, a hidden empirical question behind mismatched terminology, or an ill-posed question? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result showing when one decomposition is uniquely appropriate, a terminological convention, a change of question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected?
