Okay I need help finding the exact error in an argument of mine, because the conclusion can't be right as stated. I've been trying to get straight on the wrong kind of reasons problem -- the one where a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him, and everyone agrees you have some reason to admire him but it doesn't make him admirable. I think the whole distinction might be overated. Here's the argument I've been defending:

(1) A reason to have an attitude is just a consideration that counts in favor of having that attitude.
(2) The fact that having an attitude would benefit the person who has it counts in favor of having it.
(3) So the fact that an attitude would benefit you is a reason to have it.

(4) The right kind / wrong kind distinction says that some reasons to have an attitude don't make the attitude fitting or correct, because they don't bear on the object of the attitude; they bear on the having of it.
(5) So that distinction has to treat being-a-reason-for-A and bearing-on-whether-A-is-fitting as two things that can come apart.

(6) But if they can come apart, fitting can't mean just "what there is most reason to have" -- otherwise wrong-kind reasons would automatically make the attitude fitting. So fitting has to mean something like matching its object: admiration is fitting only toward the admirable, fear only toward the dangerous, etc.

(7) And here's the rub. "The admirable" just is "that which it is fitting to admire," and likewise for the dangerous, the contemptible, the enviable, and so on. There's no independent handle on the object-side property.

(8) So the object-side fact and the fittingness fact are the same fact under two labels. The only substantive notion left is the generic one from step 1: counts in favor of having the attitude.

(9) Therefore the right/wrong kind distinction doesn't do real normative work. It just notes, trivially, that some considerations concern the object and some concern the having of the attitude. But that can't be right, since people treat the wrong kind of reasons problem as genuinely hard and not just a labeling confusion.

So which step fails, and why exactly? Or is the argument sound and the literature is making a fuss about a triviality? Be specific.
