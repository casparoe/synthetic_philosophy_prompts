I have been organizing a metaethics reading group this term, and I have repeatedly been struck by how much weight the sentence "You can't get an ought from an is" is made to carry in our discussions, especially since the people wielding it seldom seem to mean the same thing by it from one week to the next. At a session last month it was used to shut down a colleague's argument that certain parental obligations are natural for human beings, because other primates with similar patterns of parental investment exhibit similar patterns of care. The objector plainly took the slogan to refute the entire line of reasoning by itself, but I could not tell whether he meant a narrowly logical thesis (no set of purely descriptive premises deductively entails a normative conclusion), a semantic thesis of the sort associated with Moore's open-question argument (normative terms are not synonymous with naturalistic ones), an epistemological thesis (purely descriptive evidence cannot by itself justify an ought-judgment), or a far stronger metaphysical thesis (there is an unbridgeable gulf between the descriptive and the normative, so that descriptive facts are simply irrelevant to what ought to be the case).

I would like you to separate out the distinct precise claims this slogan could be used to assert, arranged from the nearly trivial to the radical. For each reading, say briefly what it actually commits one to, whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or very likely false, and what sort of counterexample or objection would test it. Then explain which of these readings, if any, would actually license dismissing an inference from facts about primate parental care to a conclusion about human parental obligation, and which readings would leave that inference entirely untouched or merely misdescribed. I am less interested in being told which single reading is "the right one" than in having the full menu laid out with verdicts attached.

The answer should be written in American English and pitched for a graduate student who has read a little metaethics but has not worked through these distinctions systematically.
