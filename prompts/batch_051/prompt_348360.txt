I'm on the actuarial/research side of a small reinsurer, mostly pricing risks where the data is thin to nonexistent — a novel failure mode, a cyber policy on a portfolio nobody's written before, tail dependence between two perils that have never both been stressed at once. A few times a month I have to hand someone a distribution over a continuous unknown quantity, and the standard move is some version of "we have no information, so be uniform": uniform over the parameter, maximum entropy, the principle of indifference. I'm the one on the team who reads rationalist blogs, so I'm also the one muttering that uniform over x isn't uniform over x^2, and if your answer flips under a reparameterisation then "uninformative" is a bit of a polite fiction.

What I want is a reusable procedure — a checklist or flowchart-in-words — for choosing a prior over a continuous parameter when the data won't help us yet. It needs to be runnable in about half a day by someone numerate who hasn't read any philosophy and won't, and it needs to end in something I can defend to the chief underwriter in a paragraph. These priors go into pricing and capital decisions, so a systematically wrong one means we misprice the whole line.

The procedure should:

- say how to pick which quantity to be indifferent over, and mark clearly where that choice is arbitrary;
- distinguish the cases where an invariance/Jeffreys-type move actually settles the parameterisation from the cases where it just re-notates the problem;
- say what to do when the natural indifference prior is improper, or only defensable after truncating at bounds we invented;
- say where the procedure should stop and say "go elicit an actual expert prior" rather than any indifference argument;
- include a sensitivity step: how much the final price or decision moves when I switch between defensible parameterisations, and when I should report a range instead of a number.

Run it on three worked cases: (1) a prior for mean time between failures of a new pump design with zero field data, (2) a prior for the fraction of accounts in a portfolio that default in a 1-in-250-year stress year, and (3) a case where I'd have guessed the right move was just to be uniform, and the procedure hands back an answer my boss won't like because it prices the risk much higher than gut feel.

Be explicit about what's settled — my understanding is that the basic Bertrand's-paradox point, that indifference underdetermines continuous priors and the answer changes under monotone transforms, is not in dispute — versus what people who actually work on this still fight about (whether invariance/Jeffreys priors genuinely solve it versus relabel it, reference priors versus elicited priors, whether maximum entropy fixes the parameterisation, wether the whole problem even matters once there's any data). Don't present a contested view as the consensus.

Write the answer in British English.
