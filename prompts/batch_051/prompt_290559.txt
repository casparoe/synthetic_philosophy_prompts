I'm a philosophy professor, and I keep coming back to the Locke chapter because the undergrads get worked up about it. A grad student of mine has been defending a fix to Locke's memory criterion, and to be honest it feels like it takes the wind out of your brave officer. Let me put it to you. Grant that your example shows Locke's flat criterion — sameness of consciousness extending back to the very action — is nontransitive: the general is the officer (he remembers taking the standard), and the officer is the boy (he remembers being flogged for robbing the orchard), but the general is not the boy, because he remembers nothing of it. Fine. But the Locke defender can amend the view without abandoning its spirit. Let stages of a person be tied by a chain: two stages belong to the same person when there is a sequence of intermediate stages such that each one remembers the stage before it. Then the general is the boy, because the chain runs through the officer. Each link in the chain is exactly the sort of memory connection Locke said constitutes identity, and your example already yields those local links on Locke's criterion. So why doesn't a chain of identity-preserving links preserve identity, just as a chain of welded links joins its first link to its last? Your transitivity complaint seems met. What is left of your objection?
