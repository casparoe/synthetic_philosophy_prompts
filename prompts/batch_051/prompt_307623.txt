I'm grading a take-home midterm for my upper-level course on the ethics of markets, and I want a second set of eyes on one answer.

Here's the exam question:

"For our markets and morals unit: explain Michael Sandel's 'corruption' objection to letting markets operate in certain domains, and carefully distinguish it from unfairness-based and coercion-based objections. Then apply it to one of the following cases: paid kidney donation, post-disaster price gouging on bottled water, or vote selling. Finish by stating the strongest limit of the objection, in your view."

Here's the student's answer:

"Sandel's corruption objection says markets are never value-neutral: an exchange embodies and sends a message about how the good is to be valued. When that message is the wrong way to value the good, the exchange itself is corrupting, and it also gradually reshapes how participants value the good in practice. This is different from the unfairness objection, which says markets distribute goods by ability to pay and therefore leave the poor without access, and from the coercion objection, which says sellers may be forced by desperation. Both of those are about background conditions or outcomes, whereas the corruption objection is about the meaning of the exchange.

Blood is the standard illustration. When blood is donated, the practice expresses solidarity and the idea that one's body contributes to a common good. When blood is paid for, the payment announces that blood is just another unit of supply, and that announcement causes donors and recipients to think of blood that way; as the commercial framing spreads, the gift practice erods. The corruption is exactly this replacement of civic valuation by market valuation.

For post-disaster price gouging: charging $20 for bottled water after a hurricane isn't wrong only because desperate people are coerced or because poor people are shut out. The price signal itself expresses the valuation that survival necessities are commodities like any other, to be allocated by willingness to pay. And once that message circulates, people internalize it: they come to see emergency relief as a commercial service, and the background norm that neighbors owe each other aid in a disaster corrodes. So the good and the relation around it are corrupted.

The strongest limit of the corruption objection is that it cannot, by itself, show why the market's valuation of a good is the wrong one. Sandel notes that markets change how we value things and then treats every such change as corruption. But markets often alter valuations without corrupting anything (used-book stores, farmers' markets). So unless he supplies an independent account of a good's proper valuation, 'corruption' just names the fact that the market's valuation is new or unfamiliar. Also, the causal claim that prices erode non-market attitudes is empirical; people sell some things all the time while keeping them deeply meaningful."
