I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company and recently got pulled onto an ML team that was fine-tuning a customer-support model with RLHF. The pipeline we used was pretty standard: we collected a few hundred thousand pairwise comparisons from crowdworkers ("which of these two replies is better?"), trained a reward model on those choices, and then optimized the model against the reward model with PPO. What struck me the whole time was a split in how we treated the word "better." For helpfulness and tone, the crowdworkers' preferences were basically treated as ground truth; we averaged or majority-voted them, and if raters consistently preferred a certain style, that became the target. But for a list of sensitive topics (harassment, self-harm, medical or legal advice, a few company policy items), the raters were overruled by a hand-maintained rule list and scripted refusals. Those refusals were written by a seperate team (lawyers plus a safety group) and, as far as I could tell, they weren't derived from any preference data at all—nobody asked the crowdworkers whether they'd rather have the model refuse or answer those queries. We still called the whole thing "aligning the model with what users want."

I can't shake the feeling that this setup quietly commits us to a pretty specific view of what makes a response good, or of values in general, but I don't have the vocabulary to pin it down. Does the pairwise-preference part presuppose that "good" just means "whatever the sampled raters tend to pick," and that the right target is reward-maximizing behavior, full stop? If so, what does the hard-coded refusal part presuppose instead—that some things are out of bounds regardless of what anyone prefers, which sounds like a different theory of value? Are the two parts consistent, or are we running two incompatible philosophies in one system without noticing? And if we replaced the underlying view with something else—say, the idea that there's an objective fact about which replies are good and the raters can be systematically wrong about it—what would we actually have to change in the engineering? I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory (or theories) this practice presupposes, a sense of which parts of the practice strictly force that theory and which parts are merely compatible with lots of theories, and where the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined. Please write it as continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
