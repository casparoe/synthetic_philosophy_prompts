Last week a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether computational complexity theory has anything to teach epistemology, and by the end I had argued myself into a position I now want to stress-test. The position is roughly this. Complexity theory shows that several of the inferences an ideally rational agent is supposed to perform — exact probabilistic reasoning over a large set of hypotheses, updating on one's total evidence, recognising the logical consequences of one's beliefs — are intractable in general, and for some tasks even approximations are provably hard. I took this to be a normative point rather than a merely practical one. If an ideal of rationality is impossible for any physically realisable agent even in principle, then it cannot set the standard against which our actual reasoning is measured, since the principle that ought implies can rules it out. Ideals such as logical omniscience or full Bayesian conditionalisation therefore turn out, on this view, to be less like genuine regulative ideals and more like category mistakes dressed up as norms for finite minds. My friend pushed back by saying that an ideal can be unattainable and still do useful work, much as a frictionless plane does in physics, but I was not moved.

I would like an honest audit of how exposed this position actually is. What would have to be true for the claim that intractability has this sort of normative bite to be false? Which considerations — formal results, arguments, intuitions, or findings from other areas — would count most decisively against it, and which would merely make it awkward? I am especially interested in whether my friend's regulative-ideal reply can be sharpened into a genuine defeater, whether there are complexity-theoretic considerations that cut the other way (for example, tractability in sufficiently structured environments, or resource-bounded notions of approximation that preserve the ideal's content), and whether the position has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability — and, if so, whether that is a vice for a view of this kind. I am more likely to be moved by formal results and worked-through counterexamples than by appeals to intuition. Please order the defeaters from most decisive to least damaging, and write your answer in British English.
