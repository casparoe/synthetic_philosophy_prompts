I've been trying to work out the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument: the claim that the linguistic evidence available to a child is too thin to determine the grammar she ends up with, so something substantive must be built in. Large language models now seem to acquire much of their grammar from text alone, and I can't tell whether that weakens the argument, just relocates the built-in contribution into the model's architecture and training setup, or is irrelevant because the two learning situations are too different. If I could spend a year of independent study getting to the bottom of one conceptual issue here, which should it be? The candidates I keep coming back to are: whether current models genuinely begin with less language-specific structure than a child; whether the argument's real target is just the general point that finite data underdetermine a rule; whether the built-in/acquired distinction holds up once architecture and training objective are counted as part of what the system starts with; and whether the live question has shifted from grammar to how meanings and concepts are acquired. Which of these deserves the year, and why that one rather than the others? Give me an ordering with the reasoning made explicit: for each candidate, which of the other questions it depends on, whether settling it would actually push toward a nativist or an empiricist position, and what new argument or evidence would most move it up or down.
