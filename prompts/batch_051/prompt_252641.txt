I'm one of the people who runs the philosophy club at my high school, and we keep coming back to this same fight about how much you owe people in terms of explaining yourself. Like, one person will say you're being fake if you don't tell your friends exactly what's going on with you, someone else will say you only owe people honesty, not a full explaination, and if they misunderstand you that's kinda on them, and a third person will say that a lot of the pressure to be an open book is just adults (and sometimes friends) wanting to control you. I'm tired of people picking a side by vibes, so I want to make a "what do you actually think about transparency?" quiz for the club, the kind where your answers to concrete situations sort you into a camp instead of just asking "do you believe in a duty to be transparent?"

The quiz should have maybe eight to ten questions. Each one should be a concrete case with two or three answer choices, and there should be a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto something like these positions: a strong transparency duty (you owe people a clear, articulable account of yourself whenever your actions touch them), a sincerity-only view (don't lie, but you don't have to make yourself understood), a relational view (the duty depends on how close the relationship is), and an anti-legibility view (demanding transparency can be a way of controlling people, and being hard to read is sometimes fine or even good). I'd also love it if the key could include whatever other positions fall out of the answer combinations, like the view that most of the time you can't even fully explain your own reasons, so the whole duty asks for something impossible. Please build it from the logical space of answers first and then say which position sits at each combination, and flag any combinations where nobody seems to live, or where the questions can't really separate two positions, rather than forcing everyone into a tidy camp. The cases should include at least: a friend who says "I'm fine" when they're clearly not, a partner asking why you like them and you can't put it into words, a school making students do emotional check-ins, a parent demanding to know why you changed your mind about something big, and a neurodivergent kid being told they have to make their communication style comfortable for teachers. You can add other cases if the tree needs them, but keep the audience high schoolers, so the situations should feel like stuff that actually happens to us, and don't use a bunch of technical labels in the questions themselves.
