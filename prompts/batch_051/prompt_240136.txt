I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I have been working through Kant's political essays for the first time. In his 1795 essay Perpetual Peace, in the section on the law of nations, Kant draws a sharp distinction between a single world-state and what he calls a federation of free states. On his account, international right points toward the latter: a standing congress of independent republics that preserve their sovereignty while agreeing to settle disputes by legal process rather than war. The federation should not, he argues, possess coercive sovereign power over its members, because a genuine world republic, once endowed with the authority to enforce law globally, would be vulnerable to the same absolutism that a republican constitution is meant to prevent. The federation therefore functions as a kind of negative substitute for the positive ideal of a world republic: an arrangement that can be dissolved or renewed, that lacks the power to compel its members, and that nevertheless makes perpetual peace thinkable.

My question is this: suppose Kant could observe the European Union as it operates today, with its common market, its directly elected parliament, its Court of Justice, its monetary union, its passport-free travel zone, and its recurring crises over fiscal transfers and the rule of law. Would he regard the EU as moving toward the world-state he warns against, or as remaining within, or capable of being brought back within, the federation of free states he defends? More specifically, I would like you to apply his distinction between a sovereign state and a league without coercive sovereign power to what is usually called the EU's democratic deficit. Would Kant take the deficit to be evidence that the EU should develop stronger democratic institutions even at the price of becoming more state-like, or would he take the deficit to be the unavoidable cost of avoiding a despotic supranational authority? Do not answer from biographical guesses about Kant or from what he might have read; anchor the answer in the structure of the argument I have just described, and be explicit about the places where his categories strain when applied to a body that possesses some but not all of the powers of a state.
