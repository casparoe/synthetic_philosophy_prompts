A colleague of mine keeps insisting that pain asymbolia basically settles an old argument about pain. The argument is whether the badness of pain is built into the raw sensation itself, or whether the sensation and the "this is awful, make it stop" part are two seperate components that are only glued together in normal people. In asymbolia, the story goes, people report that the pain is still there and just as intense—they can tell you exactly where it is and how strong it is—but they don't dislike it, don't grimace, don't pull away, and don't dread it. My colleague treats this as nearly decisive evidence for the two-component view.

I'm not so sure. It seems to me you'd first have to show that what's preserved in these patients really is the same pain with one piece missing, and not a different state altogether that merely shares a name with pain. So here's what I want from you: how would you actually test the claim that asymbolia preserves the sensory core of pain while deleting the badness? Give me the strongest test, or combination of tests, you can think of. For each test, say what pattern of results would favor the two-component story and what would favor the "it's a different state" story. Then be honest about what even a clean positive result would leave open—does any test actually settle wether the feeling itself is the same once the caring-about-it part is gone, or is that residual question just a matter of how we choose to use the word "pain"?

Keep the structure easy to follow: describe each test concretely, then say what it would and wouldn't show. End with a final line that begins "Bottom line:" and gives a one-sentence summary of your overall answer.
