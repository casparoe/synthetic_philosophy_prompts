I help run a monthly community discussion group at the public library. We're usually about twenty adults—renters, homeowners, a couple of small landlords—and each session gets two hours. Next month I want to do something on a real proposal in our city to end single-family-only zoning within a half mile of transit, so fourplexes and small apartment buildings would be allowed where only houses are allowed now. I don't want a lecture and I don't want a pro/con debate. Design a runnable activity for the session: a role-play, a simulated planning commission hearing, a game with scorecards and payoffs, whatever does the job. The goal is that the participants run into the problem themselves: each of them does the locally-reasonable thing, and together they produce an outcome that shuts out people who aren't in the room—and only afterwards do they see that they were, in fact, the NIMBYs. The word 'NIMBY' shouldn't be said until the debrief.

Give me:

- specific roles with private interests, including a few that aren't obviously good or bad—e.g. a long-time homeowner caring for an elderly parent in a garage ADU, a renter scared of being displaced by a luxury building, a small landlord, someone who commutes an hour into town for work; plus at least two "missing voices" who never get to be in the room (a night-shift nurse driving 90 minutes, a family in a shelter, a cook who works downtown but lives three towns over) and only appear as cards the participants can choose to read or ignore
- exact rules and payoffs, including what each role gains or loses under build / don't build / delay, so that obstruction and foot-dragging feel rational role-by-role but disastrous in aggregate
- a minute-by-minute run of show for two hours, with a built-in moment where the missing-voice cards arrive and the group has no procedure that requires anyone to read them
- who gets to speak, who can propose amendments, who votes, and who doesn't

Then a debrief that maps moments in the game onto the ideas: concentrated costs and diffuse benefits, local control as exclusion, exit vs. voice, whose preferences get to count as "neighborhood character," and whether a right to the city should depend on already living in it. Say what will predictably go wrong when this is actually run (people breaking role to be agreeable, a fake consensus, someone getting genuinely angry, a participant checking out because the vote doesn't effect them) and how to recover each time. And be honest about what the simulation can't reproduce: no one in the room actually loses a home or pays a higher property tax when the vote goes against them, so the stakes are borrowed and the facilitator has to supply their weight.
