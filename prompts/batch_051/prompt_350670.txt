I spent thirty years doing atmospheric chemistry, so I'm used to thinking in probabilities and expected outcomes, but I've only been reading philosophy since I retired. Lately I keep coming back to the Pascal's mugging argument, and it bugs me more than it probably should. I tried to write it out cleanly, and I want to know whether it's valid, and if it isn't, exactly which step fails. Here's my reconstruction: (1) A stranger asks for $10 now and promises to deliver an astronomically large benefit later — say 10^20 units of whatever I value — if I pay, and nothing if I don't. (2) Since the promise is logically possible, I can't rationally assign it probability exactly zero, so my probability p that he'll deliver is positive, however tiny. (3) The expected value of paying is p × 10^20 utils minus the small utility cost of giving up $10, call it 10 utils. (4) As long as p is bigger than one in 10^18 or so, that expected value is comfortably positive, and nothing I know rules out a probability that large. (5) Expected utility maximization therefore says I should pay. (6) But the stranger can always up the promised reward until the calculation favors paying for any positive p, which makes every street con come out as a good deal. (7) So something in here has to give.

I keep wanting to reject step 2, but I can't tell weather that's a real objection or just me being squeamish about tiny probabilities. Please walk through the argument and tell me: is it valid as stated, and if not, which numbered step fails, on what reading of it, and what suppressed premise would be needed to make it valid? Also, since I'm trying to learn how philosophers think about this, tell me what argument or consideration would change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I'd prefer the answer in American English.
