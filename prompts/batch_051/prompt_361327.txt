In many affluent liberal democracies, there is a public arts council or endowment that subsidizes literary fiction, poetry, literary translation, and serious theater, alongside public libraries and public broadcasters that commission original drama. The state does not, by and large, subsidize romance novels, horror, fantasy series, video games, or reality television, even though these works often reach vastly larger audiences and shape the popular moral and political imagination more immediately. Where public money does enter commercial culture, it is typically justified on economic grounds, as industrial policy or cultural export, rather than as support for works of aesthetic or moral value. A work of literary fiction that sells a few hundred copies can be judged worthy of public subsidy, while a fantasy series read by millions is expected to make its own way in the market. What theory of the public value of literature and the arts does this pattern of funding presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories would license it; and say what would have to change in public funding if the governing theory were replaced by a view on which the value of fiction consists simply in the enjoyment, insight, or enrichment of its readers, however those are measured.
