In section 2 of the preface to Friedrich Nietzsche's The Genealogy of Morals (translated by Horace B. Samuel), I came across this passage:

We have no right to be "disconnected"; we must neither err "disconnectedly" nor strike the truth "disconnectedly." Rather with the necessity with which a tree bears its fruit, so do our thoughts, our values, our Yes's and No's and If's and Whether's, grow connected and interrelated, mutual witnesses of one will, one health, one kingdom, one sun—as to whether they are to your taste, these fruits of ours?—But what matters that to the trees? What matters that to us, us the philosophers?

I understand the tree metaphor, but I don't understand what kind of connection Nietzsche is demanding. Is he saying that philosophical errors come from treating truths as isolated, or that a philosopher's whole set of values must have a single underlying character? How would that standard apply to his own genealogy of morality, where he treats good and evil as having arisen through historical shifts and mixtures across different groups? I'd like help working out whether this passage is a claim about logical consistency, about psychological unity, or about something else entirely.
