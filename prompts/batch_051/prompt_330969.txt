I'm writing a scene in which a character plays a piano piece, and I keep wanting to describe the music itself as sad, not just the character's mood. But I've been reading around in the philosophy of music and I've hit something that seems to contradict itself. Philosophers often say it's perfectly fine to call a piece of music sad, even though music can't literally feel sad, because sounds don't have mental states. Yet many of the same philosophers insist that saying music is sad is not just a shorthand for saying it makes the listener feel sad.

Here's what's bugging me. If the sadness is not a literal property of the sounds, and it also isnt just the feeling the music causes in the listener, then where is it? It seems to me there are only two possible places for an emotion to live: in the thing itself, or in the person experiencing it. Since the first one is ruled out, don't we have to conclude that sad music is nothing more than music that happens to make people sad — and if not, aren't we just treating a metaphor as if it were literal? Is there any good reason to think the sadness is really in the music, and does it actually matter for how I should write the scene?
