I am writing to you because a question has been nagging at me since my brother began mirror therapy for phantom limb pain, and I would like a careful reconstruction of what that practice presupposes rather than a defense of any one position. My brother lost his left arm below the elbow in a motorcycle accident a little over a year ago. His phantom pain takes the familiar forms: the missing hand feels clenched so tightly that the imagined fingernails dig into the palm, and no amount of willing it open helps. In his therapy sessions he sits with a mirror placed at his midline, angled so that the reflection of his intact right arm appears where the missing left arm should be. He performs slow movements with the right hand and arm while watching the reflected limb, and the visual illusion of the missing limb obeying his intention often releases the cramp, at least for a while. What puzzles me is that the treatment seems at once to treat the felt body as something one can fool with a mirror and as a seat of real motor intentions that can be re-educated through movement, and I cannot tell whether those two aspects presuppose the same theory or quietly conflict.

I want to know what theory of the body, and of pain, this practice only makes sense under. The natural frame is Merleau-Ponty's account of the lived body, the body schema, and motor intentionality, especially his treatment of the phantom limb as neither a nerve-end memory nor a mere refusal to accept the loss, but a phenomenon of a habitual body that still reaches into the world with the limb that is gone. But I do not want you to assume that framework is correct; I want you to use it to make the practice's commitments visible, and then to ask how much of it the practice actually needs. Does mirror therapy strictly presuppose that the felt body is a system of practical capacities and possibilities, organized around what the person can do, rather than a fixed anatomical representation or a machine supervised by a central controller? Or is the practice equally intelligible on a representational view, on which the brain maintains an internal model of the body and the mirror merely supplies corrected visual input to update that model? What does the therapy's reliance on visual illusion plus voluntary movement imply about whether the body schema is multimodal and revisable through action? Is the clinical idiom of retraining the brain consistent with what the therapy itself seems to assume, or does the treatment quietly presuppose a lived body while the official description presupposes something more Cartesian? And if one took the lived-body presupposition seriously, what would have to change in the design of prosthetics, in the choice of rehabilitation exercises, or in the way clinicians explain phantom pain to patients, as against a neural-reprogramming picture?

Please distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories could license it. An honest account of where the practice does not decide between Merleau-Ponty and a more computational theory would be more useful to me than a forced reading. One constraint on the form of your answer: write entirely in continuous prose, the way one would write a considered letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
