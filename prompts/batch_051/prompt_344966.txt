I sit on the grants committee of a small private foundation whose charter says only that we are to support excellence in any human endeavor. We meet quarterly, consider a dozen proposals, and award between $5,000 and $50,000 each. None of us has philosophical training; every award has to be defended in two or three sentences of plain language, and the other trustees are quick to pounce on hand-waving. I have been turning over a question on my own: is it good to be excellent at something even if no one benefits from it? My hunch is that unobserved excellence can be worth something, but I cannot say why or where that thought runs out, and I do not want my hunch to rig the procedure. Most proposals claim some benefit, but a few do not: an artist who will destroy the work at the end of the exhibition, a mathematician who wants to prove a theorem with no application that almost no one will read, and a mountaineer planning a solo winter route with no photographs and no record. Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Keep the distinctions that matter for the question of excellence without benefit, and drop the ones that do not affect our decisions. Then run the procedure on those three proposals, making sure at least one run ends in a decision the committee will not like. Also say, without running it fully, how the procedure would treat a proposal that is plainly excellent in a craft we find repellent, such as a master forger. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.
