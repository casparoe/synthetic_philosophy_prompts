Last evening a friend and I spent several hours arguing about whether mathematics can get by without completed infinities. He took the finitist side: Hilbert's hotel is a trick of language, Cantor's transfinite hierarchy is an artifact of asking questions the formalism was never meant to answer, and the mathematics we actually compute with is either finite or about rules and approximations. I pushed back with the familiar arsenal — the diagonal argument, the completeness of the reals, the nonconstructive existence proofs running through analysis — and by the end we were stuck, because neither of us could say what the discipline would become if his position were actually put into practice. His challenge was: stop appealing to familiarity and tell me concretely what would happen if we really did it.

Here is the counterfactual. Suppose that in 1935, after Gödel's incompleteness theorems had been published but before the postwar expansion of academic mathematics, the major mathematical societies, journals, and university departments jointly adopted a professional publication standard, enforced through peer review, hiring, and prizes rather than by law. A proof is publishable in mainstream venues only if every assertion about an infinite totality that it uses or establishes satisfies constructive constraints: an existence claim about an infinite domain must come with an explicit witness or an effective procedure for producing one; a universal claim may not be established merely by refuting its classical negation, so there is no unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle over infinite domains; and a function from an infinite set must be given by a rule, not posited as a completed correspondence. Classical theorems that cannot meet the standard are not declared false, but they are demoted to a second tier — "conditional," "ideal," or "nonstandard" results kept out of the ordinary journals and out of what counts for hiring and prizes.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of the next fifty years, to 1985. I want a chain of reasoning, not a story. Which parts of the inherited canon survive translation into constructive terms? Be concrete about the ordinary canon: what becomes of the completeness of the reals, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, the intermediate value theorem, or Zorn's lemma, and which of their proofs get reframed as statements about approximations, algorithms, or relative consistency? Which areas of mathematics stall, which flourish, and where do mathematicians retool or move — to logic, computation, physics, engineering? How do the incentives shift for young researchers, textbook writers, and departments, and how is the rule gamed or reinterpreted? And does the discipline end up smaller but more secure, or genuinely crippled? Throughout, separate the links in your prediction you are confident about from those that are guesses, and state which sociological or mathematical assumptions would have to fail for the prediction to reverse. I do not want a literature survey or citations; reason from general knowledge of how mathematics and professions actually work.
