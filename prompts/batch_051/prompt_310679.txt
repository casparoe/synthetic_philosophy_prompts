I have been turning over a parallel between two problems that are rarely discussed together, and I would like your judgment on whether it is a genuine structural analogy or a superficial resemblance.

On one side is the question of whether physics could ever be known to have reached its final theory. The principled difficulty, as I see it, is that any theory that has survived some finite run of tests has indefinitely many rival theories that agree with it on all those tests and diverge somewhere in the untested future. No finite body of favorable observations can, by itself, certify that the next result will not overturn the theory; the data contain no observable mark that the sequence of successful predictions will now continue forever.

On the other side is a standard result in formal learning theory, in the tradition of identification in the limit. A learner is shown, one at a time, the positive examples of an unknown language drawn from some class; after each example, it must conjecture a grammar. It identifies the language in the limit just in case, for every language in the class and every presentation of that language, its conjectures eventually stabilize on a correct grammar. The result I have in mind is that the class consisting of all finite languages over an alphabet, together with the single infinite language containing every string over that alphabet, is not identifiable from positive examples alone. For any finite sequence of examples is compatible both with the target's being the infinite language and with its being the finite language consisting exactly of the examples seen so far; a learner that ever switches to the infinite language can be fooled by a finite language that presents just the examples that prompted the switch and then goes silent.

The parallel I see is this. In both cases, an inquirer is trying to converge on a hypothesis with rivals that match every observation made so far, and in both cases the environment supplies no observable certificate of convergence—no datum marking the moment at which it is safe to stop revising. The physicist who has just gathered a long but finite run of successful predictions is, on this reading, in the same position as a formal learner who has just received a long but finite positive sample.

Is this a real identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching? If it is real, does the formal negative result transfer to physics as a principled limit on the recognition of a final theory? What would have to be true of theory-choice in physics for the analogy to break down—in particular, does the physicist's ability to design targeted experiments rather than passively receive positive data, or the role of simplicity and explanatory unification in selecting theories, mark a structural difference? And does the parallel predict something about the conduct of physics that you think is false, and if so, what does that reveal about where the analogy stops?
