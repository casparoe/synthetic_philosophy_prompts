Marco said the whole thing only does its work if Nietzsche meant it literally: the universe has finite stuff and infinite time, so every configuration of it—including your life, exactly as lived—has to repeat forever. Lena said that's reading a physics argument into a book that's doing something else: the demon thought experiment isn't a claim about cosmology, it's a test of whether you can affirm your life as it was, and the test works whether or not the universe in fact recurs. Marco answered: if it's only hypothetical, a person can just say "neat, but it won't happen," so the test loses all its weight; the recoil is only rational if you believe it could happen. Lena came back: no, the weight comes from having to affirm your life with nothing edited and nothing escaped, repeated endlessly—not from believing the physics; vividly imagining it can reveal what you actually want. Marco said that turns Nietzsche into a self-help exercise, which is exactly the flattening he was fighting, and that the eternal return was a metaphysical point about being and time.
