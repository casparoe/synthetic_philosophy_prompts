Occupational therapists, when they're helping someone relearn to reach for a cup after a stroke, often don't break the movement into joint angles or command steps. They sit the person in front of a actual cup they want (their own coffee, a glass of water when thirsty), position the table and chair so the cup is just inside what's reachable, and tell them to look at the cup and go get it. They also tend to say practice 'in context' does something that the same movements out of context don't, and they'd rather change the environment than keep correcting someone's kinematics. What theory of perception, intention, and skilled movement does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, separate what the practice genuinely commits a therapist to from what it merely fits well with, and say whether a computational motor-control picture could swallow these choices too. Then tell me what rehab practice would have to drop or change if the implied view turned out to be false.
