A city government is considering replacing part of its safety regulation with a tradable personal risk budget, denominated in micromorts, where one micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death from a specified activity. Every adult resident would receive an annual allowance of, say, 1,000 micromorts. An independent actuarial board would post and revise micromort prices for all lawful but risky activities: driving a car at, say, 0.01 micromorts per mile, riding a motorcycle at 0.3 per mile, a skydive at 8, a general anesthetic at 5, a scuba dive at 10, and so on. Any business selling a scheduled activity would have to debit the customer's ledger at the posted rate and would receive the micromorts as a tradeable asset. Unused allowances could be sold on an exchange; shortfalls would be covered by buying credits or borrowing against future years at interest. Speed limits, helmet laws, roadworthiness inspections, and the like would remain exactly as they are—the ledger prices only the residual fatality risk that remains after those rules.

Analyze how this scheme would actually play out. What does it incentivize for prudent low-mileage drivers, for habitual motorcyclists, for parents deciding whether to let a teenager drive, for the businesses that collect micromorts, and for the actuarial board that sets the rates? How would it be gamed—through reclassification of activities, unbudgeted informal risks, firms laundering risk or arbitraging personal allowances against corporate balance sheets, or fraud in the credit market? Where would the market price of a micromort settle, and would that price carry the information a person needs when deciding whether a given risk is worth taking? Finally, state whether the system would deliver anything like genuinely informed risk-taking for the ordinary case of driving, or whether it would simply convert the question of whether driving is worth it into a price that most people ignore until their ledger runs dry—and say which failure modes, if any, could be fixed without turning the ledger back into the old regulatory regime.
