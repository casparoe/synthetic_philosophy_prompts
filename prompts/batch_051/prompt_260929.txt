Let's say a state decides to make joining the social contract into an explicit, actual act of consent instead of something you're just born into. It sets aside a border zone — call it the Antechamber District — that it administers as little as possible: no police, no courts, no tax office, no property registry, no criminal code, basically a state-of-nature enclave inside the state's own borders. Any adult who wants to become a full member of the main territory has to spend two years living there first. People born into the main territory are exempt and can come and go. At the end of the two years you either sign the civic covenant (taxes, laws, jury duty, all of it) or your barred from entering the main territory for life. Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Who actually moves into the antichamber and who decides full membership isn't worth it, how does life inside it evolve, what games get played around the deadline, and does the final signing produce the kind of genuine consent the designers wanted — or just sort people by wealth, patience, and appetite for violence? Then tell me where the mechanism breaks first and whether any fix (shorter stays, early buy-outs, a minimal court) can be added without turning it back into the thing it was meant to replace.
