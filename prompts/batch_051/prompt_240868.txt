My medieval ethics professor has been working through cases about where exactly the sin is located, and her rulings so far are:

- A monk gets hit by a vivid, unwanted image of fornication during prayer; he gives it no approval and pushes it away at once. Her ruling: not a sin.
- A man forms the settled intention to poison his neighbor, but before he can do anything someone else accidentally throws out the poison. Her ruling: a sin, no question.
- A woman is forced into adultery against her will and never consents in her mind at any point. Her ruling: not a sin.
- A rich man gives a large sum to the poor solely so people will think he's pious, not because he actually cares. Her ruling: a sin.

Next week's case is a man who forms the settled intention to steal from his employer, then actually steals the money and is caught only after it's spent. Will she say this is exactly the same sin as the unexecuted intention, a greater or additional sin, or something else? I'm less interested in the bare call than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the theft case, and wich single further case would most cleanly settle which rule she actually holds. Oh, and answer in American English.
