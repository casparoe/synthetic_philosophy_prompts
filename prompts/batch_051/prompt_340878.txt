Now that I have retired from a forty-year career in physical oceanography and have begun reading the philosophy of science seriously, structural realism is the position that has unsettled me most. As I understand it, the view is that what tends to survive scientific change is mathematical structure—equations, relations, symmetries—whereas the entities a theory posits are apt to be discarded; the realist impulse should therefore fasten onto the structure rather than the furniture. That description fits my professional life uncomfortably well. The numerical models I worked with for decades contained parameterisations of sub-grid-scale turbulence that nobody believed were literally correct, water masses that are convenient notional volumes rather than observed objects, and estimates of a deep overturning circulation assembled from sparse measurements and model assumptions. The equations, and the relations between quantities, did genuine work; the entities often felt like scaffolding.

Please describe the most important implications of structural realism for someone in my position. Three things puzzle me in particular.

First, how should I now think about the reality of the things I spent a career treating as though they existed? Does the structural realist give me licence to keep speaking of, say, Antarctic Bottom Water or the meridional overturning circulation as real, or should I regard them as derivative shorthand for structures that are the proper objects of belief?

Second, what is the practical difference between believing in the structure and believing in the entities? If I write about the climate system for a general audience, should I change how I introduce deep currents, eddies, or so-called tipping points? Or does the distinction matter only inside a metaphysics seminar?

Third, and this is what troubles me most: my models also contained components we knew to be false but retained because they made the whole work. Structural realism seems to tell me to trust what persists, yet in a climate model much that persists—a damping term, a closure scheme—is not truth-bearing at all. How does structural realism propose to distinguish the structure I should take seriously from the structure that is merely a useful contrivance? If it cannot draw that line, is that a serious weakness of the view, or have I simply framed the question badly?

I would be grateful if, in your reply, you would say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the point you are least sure about. Please also write your answer in British English.
