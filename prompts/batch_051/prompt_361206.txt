I'm putting together a syllabus section on corruption and I keep running into the same mess. Half the papers treat corruption as a list of obviously bad acts (bribes, kickbacks, embezzlement), and the other half say that's provincial Western moralizing because in lots of places gift-giving, patronage, and personal loyalty are just how institutions actually work. I don't want to adjudicate any single case yet; I want the spec first. What would an adequate theory of corruption have to get right? Write the desiderata list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (gift vs bribe, illegal vs corrupt, corrupt motive vs bad outcome), and the empirical findings about clientelist systems it can't just ignore. Seed it with at least these cases: a customs official who will only process your paperwork if you pay a "facilitation fee" everyone pays; a politician who gives government jobs to her relatives; a diaspora community where the local MP is expected to pay school fees for constituents and is seen as corrupt if she refuses; a corporation that lobbies for a tax break that probably wouldn't survive a neutral analysis; and a judge who rules for the side her brother-in-law represents. For each desideratum, say what case or consideration forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (legalist, institutionalist, anthropological, whatever). Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the part I most want to see — and tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding popular theories. Skip a full scorecard of named theories, I'll do that myself.

And don't pretend to be above the fight. Give me your actual view on which desiderata survive the objection that "corruption" is just a Western export, and be explicit about where your list is most likely to fail: what argument or cross-cultural case would make you drop or rewrite one of the non-negotiables.
