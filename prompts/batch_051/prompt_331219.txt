I recently joined a research group that builds optimization methods for machine learning, and one live decision on our roadmap is whether to invest in an evolutionary outer loop—evolution strategies or something similar—around ordinary gradient-based training. The decision keeps dragging me into a dispute that seems to have been going on forever, both in the papers I have been reading and in our internal design reviews, and it appears completely stuck. On one side are people who maintain that evolution is a stupid optimizer: it is gradient-free and therefore brutally sample-inefficient, it tends to get marooned on whatever local peak of a shifting fitness landscape happens to be nearby, and, because living things only need to be good enough to survive and reproduce, it is really a satisficer rather than an optimizer in any sense an engineer should care about. On the other side are people who reply that this satisficing process produced eyes, powered flight, and complex multicellular life, and that one should not expect it to look like an optimizer at all. They argue that natural selection has no fixed scalar objective analogous to a loss function and that evolutionary search works precisely in regimes where gradients are unavailable, discontinuous, or misleading; on this view, calling evolution a stupid optimizer is a category mistake, and the real question is what gradient descent can and cannot learn from it.

What frustrates me is that neither side moves. The same phenomena—evolvability, apparent maladaptation, functional trade-offs, the ruggedness of fitness landscapes—get cited as decisive evidence for opposite conclusions, and after a year of reading groups and design meetings I still cannot say what the disagreement is even about. I am not asking for a verdict about who is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is and why it has stayed stuck. My working hypothesis is that it is not really a factual disagreement about what natural selection does, since both sides mostly accept the same natural history; rather, it is a dispute about what the word optimization is supposed to require, and about which side's standards are the right ones to import into machine learning. But I can imagine a different story: there might be a hidden empirical question about the structure of fitness landscapes, or about the sample complexity and scaling behavior of evolutionary search, that turns out to be doing the real work.

Could you lay out the plausible diagnoses, say which one best fits the way each side actually argues, and tell me what would count as progress on each diagnosis—a formal result, a set of benchmark comparisons, a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question, or something else? If the honest answer is that the debate is unlikely to make progress because it is really several different questions sharing one label, I would like to know that too, and what the separate questions would be.
