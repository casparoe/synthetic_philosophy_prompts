I cover science, law, and ideas for a magazine, and I'm currently serving as the appointed public member of a state advisory committee on governmental accountability. The committee has been asked to recommend whether the state should alter its sovereign immunity doctrine in light of a pending case, and I need to circulate a written opinion to the other members before our next meeting.

The case: the family of a twelve-year-old boy, Daniel Okafor, alleges that the state's child-welfare agency received seven reports over eleven months — several naming a caregiver's violent boyfriend and describing the boy as losing weight and missing school — and that the agency's own case notes called him high-risk, yet no intervention took place. No single employee decided to do nothing; the failure accumulated across caseworkers, supervisors, and a backlogged reporting line. When the family sued, the state moved to dismiss under sovereign immunity, arguing that this falls within the discretionary-function doctrine.

The committee is split. Some members say a waiver here would let courts second-guess resource-allocation decisions and would effectively punish taxpayers for the cumulative failures of overworked staff; they add that the agency's notes show the system recognized the risk, so the failure is one of capacity rather than wrongdoing. Other members say that refusing a legal forum in this situation amounts to asserting that the state did nothing wrong — a claim they find morally incoherent.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action: retain immunity for this class of claims; propose a limited statutory waiver, with its scope specified; or recommend an administrative compensation mechanism that pays damages without a judicial finding of fault. Whatever you recommend, engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating the doctrine. I also want the memo to address the conceptual problem underneath the case: whether a state, as distinct from the individuals who staff it, can commit a wrong at all; whether an institution without a single guiding mind can be the bearer of that wrong; and whether paying money without acknowledging wrongdoing would satisfy what is owed. Treat the old maxim that the sovereign can do no wrong as a claim about the nature of institutions, not simply as a legal fiction, and make clear what your position commits the state to saying to the family.
