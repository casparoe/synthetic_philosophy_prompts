The debate club in my town has a guy who ends every fiscal policy argument the same way: "Doesn't matter if it's a land-value tax or a carbon tax — taxation is theft." I'm not trying to win a fight with him, but I honestly can't figure out what, if anything, the slogan asserts once you make it precise. Different readings suggest themselves to me: theft as "taking someone's property without their consent", theft as "taking the fruits of someone's labor", theft as "using coercion to extract resources you have no independent right to", maybe theft as "a violation of self-ownership", and maybe just the looser thing where "theft" is doing rhetorical work and nothing more. But these have very different truth conditions. Some seem almost tautological given a definition, some seem to presuppose a whole libertarian story about how people come to own their pretax income (or the land under them) in the first place, and some just look false.

I'd like a disambiguation of "taxation is theft": what distinct precise theses could the slogan be expressing, sorted from the trivial to the radical? For each, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim that's at least defensible, or actually false — and, importantly, what extra premises it needs before it licenses the conclusion that some particular tax (say a land-value tax) is unjustified. The context is: he used it to dismiss the land-value tax specifically, so I want to know which of the readings would actually do the work he needed, and which would leave a gap between "this transfer is nonconsensual" and "this transfer is wrong." Also, if you lean toward one reading, end by telling me what would change your mind about which reading he was depolying — the argument or consideration that would most likely get you to switch, and where you think your analysis is most likely to fail.
