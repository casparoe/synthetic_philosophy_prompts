Here are the criteria I use for calling something a natural human language rather than a communication system: its messages are built from recombinable units, not holistic signals; it can produce and understand indefinitely many novel utterances; it has shared, stable form–meaning pairings; users acquire it from older users rather than reinventing it; and it can refer to what is absent, hypothetical, or temporally distant. Now imagine a deaf adult who grew up without access to an established sign language and who uses a homesign system with roughly four hundred distinct, consistent signs. The system has a few recurrent ordering principles, such as pointing to the person or object before producing an action sign, and the adult can use it to report that a neighbor was here yesterday. But no one else has ever become fluent in it; it marks negation only by an inconsistent head-shake and a facial expression; and it has no way of building one proposition inside another. Go through the criteria honestly. Is this system a language? Where the criteria leave the answer undecided, say whether we should amend the criteria, deny the case the label, or rethink whether the label should be doing so much work for sign-like systems.
