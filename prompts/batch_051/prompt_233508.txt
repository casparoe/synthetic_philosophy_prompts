Here's a thing I've been chewing on. Popper's old worry about Darwinism was that natural selection is unfalsifiable because fitness just means whatever actually leaves more offspring, so survival of the fittest is empty. The usual reply is that fitness is not actual success but a probabilistic disposition to succeed in a given enviroment, measured independently by things like reproductive physiology. Now vary the classic tautology thought experiment. Imagine we can run 1000 replicate populations from identical genetic starting points in a controlled lab ecosystem. Before the experiment, we compute each variant's expected per-generation growth rate from physiological measurements, and these predictions are common knowledge among the researchers. In 40 runs the variant with the highest predicted growth rate goes extinct by generation 20, and in 4 runs the variant with the lowest predicted rate fixes. Nobody disputes that drift caused these outcomes. On Popper's criterion, does this setup make natural selection genuinely testable? If the high-fitness variant fails in some prespecified number of runs, would Popper have to count that as falsifying natural selection, or only as falsifying the auxiliary model that connects physiology to expected growth? And if auxiliary hypotheses always absorb the failure, does the variation just push Popper's tautology worry down a level rather than answering it? I'd like you to work through the dialectic, not just give the standard line.
