I come to this as a retired field ecologist who has only lately begun to read moral philosophy seriously, and a question that now preoccupies me is one I used to leave to others. The familiar arguments against eating animal products seem to me to assume that the person doing the choosing lives in a rich country with a dependable supply of plant foods. I have been trying to state the conditions under which the use of animal-sourced food might be defensible when plant-based alternatives are scarce, and then to test those conditions against a real case at the edge.

Here is the list of criteria I have arrived at. For a person's use of animal-sourced food to be permissible, I would require all four:

(1) Necessity: there is no adequate plant-based food available to that person, at that time and place, at comparable cost and effort and of comparable nutritional adequacy.

(2) Welfare: the practice does not impose more pain, fear, or confinement on the animals than would plausibly occur under the realistically available alternatives.

(3) Ecological embedding: the practice belongs to a local production system that can be sustained without degrading the land or water on which it depends, rather than to an industrial system whose harms are exported or hidden.

(4) Character: the practice does not systematically train the person, or the surrounding community, to treat sentient life as a mere resource in contexts where alternatives are available.

Now consider a case I know well from my fieldwork. A pastoralist household in the Sahel grazes goats on rangeland that receives perhaps 400 millimeters of rain in a good year, too little and too variable for reliable legume cropping. Milk, and occasionally meat, supply most of the household's protein and fat during the dry season, when the cereal stores are thin. The animals live outdoors, browse shrubs and crop residue, are slaughtered quickly by a practiced hand, and their manure sustains the soil; but in drought years the browsing pressure is heavy, and tree regeneration suffers. Imported plant protein appears only intermittently: a development agency drops a fortified legume-based ration at the market, but it is expensive, widely disliked, and absent for months at a time. The household could, in principle, sell the goats and try to live from purchased food, but transport costs would absorb much of the herd's value. And once or twice a year, at a wedding or a religious feast, a goat is slaughtered even when the stored grain would, strictly, have sufficed.

Does this household's use of animal-sourced food satisfy my four criteria? Go through each one honestly; where the verdict depends on factual details I have not specified, say what further facts would settle it. Then tell me whether the shortcomings, if any, lie in the household's practice or in my criteria — in particular, whether I have smuggled rich-world assumptions into the criteria, and how the criteria would need to change to govern cases of scarcity without becoming toothless.
