In the course of a long retirement from the geosciences, I have lately been reading Aristotle's Physics, and a passage in Book II, chapter 3, has lodged in my mind. Aristotle is discussing causes and illustrates his scheme by saying that the absence of a pilot can be counted among the causes of a shipwreck, alongside such things as the storm and the vessel's construction. I understand that later philosophy would call this causation by omission; what interests me is a different comparison. In much of my own work on slope stability and dam safety, we routinely explained failures by citing things that did not happen: the relief well that was never drilled, the piezometer that failed to register, the toe drain that was left out of the design. Engineering reports treat these absent items as straightforward parts of the causal story, not as second-rate or merely rhetorical causes. This looks to me very close to Aristotle's move: allowing a non-event to enter the causal explanation because the corresponding positive event was expected, or because its presence would have made the outcome less likely.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. On the face of it, both Aristotle and the engineer are doing the same thing: holding an absence responsible against a background of expectations, functions, or norms. But I can also see ways they might come apart. Modern physical theory is comfortable saying that the only real causes are actual processes and fields; the engineer's missing relief well seems to be explanatory only relative to a design plan or a regulatory standard, whereas Aristotle may have intended something stronger about privation in nature itself. Is there a structural identity here, or merely a shared metaphor of things that are not there? What would need to be true of Aristotle's account of privative causes for engineering-style reasoning about omitted components to count as an instance of it? And is there a case where the parallel predicts one would be acceptable but the other not—for example, an indefinitely long list of absent things that we rightly ignore—that would tell us whether the two are really the same kind of explanation?
