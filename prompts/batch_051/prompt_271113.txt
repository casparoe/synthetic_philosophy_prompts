I've been thinking about an odd asymmetry in the way we blame people, and I want you to help me test it.

If my neighbor says "I'm firmly resolved to fix your fence on Saturday" and doesn't show, I'll think less of him: he's flaky, maybe unreliable. If he says "I promise I'll fix your fence on Saturday" and doesn't show, I'll think he didn't just fail me but wronged me. Practically, the only difference is the word "promise" and whatever act of mind went with it. That seems enormous.

Hume digs at exactly this in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5 ("Of the Obligation of Promises"). He writes:

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

So his conclusion is that the obligation doesn't come from the inner act at all; it comes from the convention. Fine. But now I have this asymmetry: if promise-keeping is just a convention like rules of a game or good manners, why do we treat it as special? We don't think you've committed a distinct kind of wrong by eating salad with a fork in a culture that has discarded the fork rule, or by refusing to observe a chess rule after both players agreed to quit. Those conventions release us when the practice stops serving its point or the participants step out. But people talk about promises as if they bind even when they're useless, unwanted, or made to someone who has since died. What justifies that? Is there something in the structure of promising that makes it unlike other conventions, or is this asymmetry just a bit of sentimental inflation? Should we keep it, weaken it, or give it up?
