Small confession: I've been keeping a running document of my cousin Mara's verdicts about when it's rational to follow the crowd, and I still can't reverse-engineer the rule she's actually using. She thinks about this obsessively—restaurants, apps, hiring committees, you name it—and she's clearly running something more precise than "majorities are usually right" or "never conform." I'd like you to be the rational choice / social epistemology expert here: think carefully, start by brainstorming the candidate rules she might be running, and don't lock in too early.

Here are the cases I've recorded so far. Two identical restaurants, A and B: the first three people in our group each checked a different review site and all picked A, and I'm fourth with a private text from a local friend saying B is better. Mara says pick A anyway; by then the pile of public choices outweights one unvetted private text. Same restaurants, but the first three are coworkers at a team dinner and I suspect the organizer picked A and nobody wanted to be difficult. Mara says don't follow—those probably weren't real signals. A new messaging app everyone's moving to; it looks worse for privacy to me, but all my friends are on it. Mara says switch: that's not a quality cascade, it's coordination; the value is being where everyone else is. In the urn game, where each player privately draws a ball and the true urn is either majority-red or majority-blue with each draw two-thirds accurate: if the first two say blue and the third drew red, Mara says say blue, because the public history already dominates a single private draw. But if the first two had no draws and just flipped coins, she says the third should trust her red draw; coin flips carry zero information. Finally, a promotion committee votes publicly in sequence and the first four say yes; Mara is less crisp here, saying the cascade logic suggests following but public committees stack reputation effects on top of information, so the threshold gets blurrier and she'd want to know if the votes are secret.

The new case: I'm the ninth person on a hiring panel, voting publicly and sequentially, and the first eight have all said yes. It turns out all eight had read only the same one-page internal memo before voting—no one had independent outside information—and I've got an independent source of my own saying no. What does Mara say? I'm more interested in the rule than the call: reconstruct the theory she's implicitly running, say which other rules fit her track record just as well, where they come apart on this case, and which further scenario would best tell them apart. If her record underdetermines it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each extrapolation, and give me your best guess about her verdict with your reasons for it.
