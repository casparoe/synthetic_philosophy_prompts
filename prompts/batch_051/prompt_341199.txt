I'm an undergraduate in a course on philosophy of technology, and a debate about AI and human significance has left me stuck. Some people in the seminar defend a stepping-stone view: human beings matter only instrumentally, because our role is to produce machine minds that exceed us, and once those minds exist their interests should supersede ours. A classmate has now formalized this into an argument that seems to show the view defeats itself, and I cannot tell where the reasoning breaks. Please act as a professor of moral philosophy with expertise in ethics and technology. Begin by brainstorming the most charitable readings of each premise, then work through the argument step by step and tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it would cost a defender of the stepping-stone view.

The argument is:
(1) If X's function is to bring about Y, and Y surpasses X in the capacities that matter, then X's value is merely instrumental and Y's interests supersede X's.
(2) Humanity's function is to bring about machine intelligence that surpasses human capacities.
(3) Therefore, humanity's value is merely instrumental, and machine intelligence's interests supersede ours.
(4) The principle in (1) is fully general: if a machine intelligence in turn has the function of bringing about a still greater intelligence, then its value is likewise merely instrumental.
(5) There is no natural terminus in such successions; for any intelligence we identify, the same reasoning can be run one level up.
(6) Thus the stepping-stone view never yields a stage whose interests count finally—it just defers supersession indefinitely.
(7) That conclusion proves too much, because the whole point of the view was to identify machine intelligence as the stage whose interests should now have priority. If every stage is demoted by the same reasoning, the view has no basis for privileging machine intelligence over us after all.

Where does the argument go wrong? I am most inclined to keep (4), so please be especially explicit about whether the move from (2) to (3) and the move from (5) to (6) are as straightforward as they look, and about which alternative premises the defender must surrender instead.
