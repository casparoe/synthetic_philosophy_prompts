I run the grants program for a mid-sized family foundation. We make a lot of grants where our own grant won't move the needle, but hundreds of other small funders are looking at nearly identical asks at the same time. I recently ran into the idea of evidential cooperation in large worlds — roughly, when your decision is evidence about what similar decision-makers will do, you should sometimes choose as if you're choosing for all of them at once, even though you can't causally coordinate. I keep going back and forth on whether this is actually a thing I should use, and our board hates half-formed theory talk at a docket.

Write me a reusable decision procedure I can run before each grant decision: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure says stop and just do ordinary cost-effectiveness, and where it hands the call to the board because the question is one of values not facts. Be upfront about the two failure modes I should expect: when the procedure will overapply evidential reasoning and I'll end up doing something saintly and dumb, and when it will underapply it. Then run the procedure on two cases: (A) a grant to cover a year's deficit at a local free clinic, in a landscape where there are thousands of similar clinics and thousands of similar small funders making the same call each year; (B) a one-time emergency grant to keep a specific well-regarded nonprofit from folding next month, where no other funder is deciding on this same thing. I need the whole thing to fit on a page and be usable by a board member who has never read any decision theory.
