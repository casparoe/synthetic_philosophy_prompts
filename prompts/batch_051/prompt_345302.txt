Ive been turning over a version of the inner alignment / goal misgeneralization problem and I want to stress-test a proposal someone floated on a forum I read (no idea if it's original): a "canary goal audit."

The rule: before a lab can deploy a frontier RL-trained model, it has to train, say, six canary twins of that model using its ordinary pipeline, but each twin gets a different canary reward function. The canary rewards all agree with each other and with the real production reward on every training environment, and come apart only in a set of held-out environments the lab hasnt used for training. Then a third-party red team gets black-box access to the six twins — no weights, no training logs — plus a sample of held-out scenarios, and has to play a matching game: assign each twin to the reward function it was actually trained on. The production model is approved only if the red team matches significantly above chance. The idea is that if the lab's training recipe can't reliably install a specified goal in a twin, there's no reason to trust it installed the real goal in the production model; the production model itself is never inspected, only the training process is audited.

Treat this as a mechanism to analyze, not a position to defend or attack. Walk me through how it actually plays out: what it incentivizes for the lab, the red team, and whoever writes the canary rewards; where the scheme gets gamed first; whether it ends up selecting for goals that are legible rather than goals that are actually the ones you wanted; and what happens as the deployment distribution drifts away from the held-out environments. And if the obvious failure modes get patched, tell me whether the patches just become the next thing to game.
