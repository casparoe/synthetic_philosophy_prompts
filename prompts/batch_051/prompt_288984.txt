Something I keep noticing on the rationalist-adjacent internet: someone says "if the Industrial Revolution had got going in China first..." and then acts as if whatever downstream they prefer is an argument, whilst someone else replies that counterfactual history is unfalsifiable storytelling. I think they're both skipping the step of saying what a good could-have-been claim even is.

Suppose I wanted a principled theory of counterfactual history — not a method for settling any particular what-if, but an account of what a decent counterfactual claim has to get right before I should update on it. Write me the desiderata: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (contingency of an outcome vs. our ignorance of the causes; a possible path vs. the probability of that path), and the structural facts about causation, overdetermination, or determinism it isn't allowed to contradict, with a line on why each earns its place. For each requirement mark whether it's non-negotiable or just a camp preference (the crowd who insist you hold everything causally upstream fixed vs. the crowd who are happy with a small local miracle at the fork). Then the bit I actually want: say whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if determinism is true, does the whole exercise collapse into talking about our ignorance rather than alternative ways the world might have gone, and does the minimal-rewrite rule quietly conflict with holding actual causal structure fixed? Also tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard great-man what-ifs and the structuralist reply that nothing nearby would have changed. Skip the reading list and don't lean on any specific historical literature; I'm after the criteria, seperately from the scoring.
