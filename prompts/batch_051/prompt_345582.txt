There's this fight that keeps breaking out on the forum between two camps about whether religion makes people better and whether secular societies can actually sustain trust. I've finally convinced both of them to do a proper adversarial collaboration instead of another hundred-comment thread, and they've asked me to draft it. The positions, as I understand them:

Camp A (the social-technology camp): religious communities are an unusually effective social technology for trust and prosocial behavior because they bundle a bunch of mechanisms that are hard to fake at once — costly membership, repeated public signals of commitment, supernatural monitoring, shared sacred values. On this view godless societies mostly coast on norms inherited from a religious past, and secular people who point to low religious attendance and low crime are confusing correlation with causation in the opposite direction.

Camp B (the institutions camp): trust and prosociality mostly come from rule of law, material security, and institutions. Religion is one cultural package among many, it rides along with those variables, and its apparent effect is mostly selection: the kind of people who show up to services every week are also the kind of people who volunteer, they're older, more settled, etc. They point to high-trust, very secular places and say theres just not much of a gap there.

Write the document this collaboration should produce. I want:

(1) A numbered list of claims both camps can sign — substantive ones, not just "religion and morality are complicated." Include what they can jointly say about in-group vs out-group effects, about cross-country correlations being messy, and about the causal arrow question.

(2) The precise remaining disagreements, each phrased so that its own camp would say "yes, that's what I mean, in writing." I especially suspect that part of the fight is that the two camps are using "moral" to mean different things — something like "honest and cooperative toward strangers" vs "effortful self-discipline and virtue" — so untangle that if its real rather than just papering over it.

(3) The cruxes: the sub-questions where, if you could settle one, at least one side would update.

(4) For each crux, a concrete natural experiment, intervention, or comparison (real or realistic, but don't reach for a citation — describe what you'd compare) that both camps agree bears on it. Write down in advance what each camp predicts it shows, and what result would count as a serious problem for each side.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section and then a disagreements table.
