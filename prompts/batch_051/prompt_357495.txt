I run a private grant-making foundation that makes roughly forty grants a year, staffed by five program officers and overseen by a board that signs off at the end of each round. Our review process is deliberately sequential: each officer reads a proposal, records a fund/do-not-fund vote and a few lines of comment in a shared sheet, and does so only after seeing the votes and comments of everyone earlier in the order. I have begun to suspect that our collective judgment is no better than — and sometimes worse than — the sum of our individual judgments. By the time the fourth and fifth officers vote, their own reading of the proposal often seems to disappear into a paraphrase of whoever went first. I gather there is a body of theory about information cascades and rational herding holding that, when people act in sequence with private information and a public history of earlier actions, it can be individually rational for late actors to ignore their private signal and follow the crowd, so that a group locks in on an outcome that almost nobody's private information actually supports. I want to know whether that theory can be turned into something my staff can actually use without having to read the economics.

Please design a reusable decision procedure for our grant rounds that does two things: detects when a vote is being driven by a cascade rather than by independent judgment, and tells us what to do when it is. The deliverable should be a checklist or flowchart in words that each program officer can run on a proposal and that I can run across a whole batch. It should lay out the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficiently good answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it should hand the decision to a person because the next question is one of values rather than facts. Work within our real constraints: roughly two hours of total staff time per proposal, three rounds a year, no outside experts for most decisions, and a board that will ask what we changed and why. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do — especially what it cannot detect from our existing records and what we would need to start recording in order for it to work.

Then run the procedure on two proposals from this round. Proposal A comes from a long-standing grantee our board holds in high regard; all five officers voted to fund, but the later comments largely paraphrase the first two, and two of the later officers had written private scoring notes raising a serious concern about the project's design before they saw the early votes. Proposal B comes from a first-time applicant using a method much of our field considers disreputable; the first officer voted no with a dismissive one-liner, the next two followed quickly, and the fourth and fifth officers recorded genuine doubts about the "no" in their private notes but voted no anyway. I would rather you commit than survey options: pick one procedure, apply it firmly to both cases, and say plainly which part of our current practice it would force us to change — including wherever it yields a decision we will not like.
