I'm an undergrad in a Chinese philosophy course, and we just read Mencius and Xunzi back to back. I'm stuck on something that seems more interesting than the usual good vs bad human nature slogan. The Mencius passages we read (the child about to fall into a well, the ox) seem to show that people have spontaneous moral responses: not that they are fully virtuous, but that the beginnings are already there. Then in the chapter of the Xunzi that argues human nature is bad, Xunzi opens by saying, roughly, that people's nature is bad and their goodness is a matter of deliberate effort. Soon after he asks something like: if human nature were not bad, where would ritual and rightness come from? That move is confusing me. It sounds like he thinks the existence of moral norms itself is evidence that human nature is bad, because if we were already good, we would not need those norms. But isn't that exactly the point Mencius could make: our spontaneous responses are the raw material from which ritual is shaped, and the need for shaping does not show the raw material is bad?

So I want to compare the two views on the relation between natural moral responses and artificial moral forms (ritual, rules, rightness). Take a definite position: is Xunzi's argument from artifice a genuine problem for Mencius, or does it beg the question? Who actually gives the better explanation of why norms are both continuous with human nature and not natural? I'm leaning toward Xunzi being more coherent but I want a committed answer, not a survey of options.
