I'm drafting a novel about a woman who, at nineteen, did something terrible to her sister and has spent twenty-five years trying to atone for it. The whole book rests on her being really responsible for what she did — not just a cause of it, but morally on the hook in a way that makes the guilt and the atonement real. My brother-in-law, who did a philosophy PhD before fleeing to tech, says my premise is impossible and sketched this argument on a napkin:

(1) What a person does flows from the way she is: her values, beliefs, desires, habits, character.

(2) So if she is to be ultimately responsible for what she did, she must be ultimately responsible for the way she was that produced the action.

(3) But she can be ultimately responsible for the way she is only if she chose to be that way.

(4) Any choice to be a certain way would itself be an action, and by the same reasoning would have to flow from a way she already was, so she'd need an earlier choice, and an earlier one before that, with no starting point.

(5) In fact there is no first self-chosen point: go back far enough and you hit heredity, environment, chance, and unchosen events that shaped the way she was at nineteen.

(6) So she is not ultimately responsible for the way she was.

(7) Therefore she is not ultimately responsible for what she did.

(8) And nothing here is specific to her; the same chain applies to everyone, in every case. So nobody is ever ultimately responsible for anything.

I cant accept that conclusion — it would wreck not just my book but most of how people actually hold each other accountable. So where does it go wrong? I'd most like to keep the claim that shes responsible for the act even though she didn't create herself from scratch. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why that step looked so good, and what it costs to reject it.
