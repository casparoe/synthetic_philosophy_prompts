Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether we owe gratitude to people in the past—a legislator who secured the school system we later passed through, an ancestor whose savings made our education possible. My friend defended the following argument for the conclusion that such gratitude cannot get off the ground, not because we are excused from it but because the required intentional relation never existed. I find the conclusion too quick, but I cannot say where the argument goes wrong. Please evaluate it step by step: say whether it is valid, and if not, identify the first step that fails; if a step is ambiguous, give the strongest reading on which it is true and then check whether the argument still goes through.

(1) Gratitude is owed only to a person who intentionally benefited one.
(2) To intentionally benefit someone, the benefactor must act with that beneficiary as the object of her intention: she must be able to think of the beneficiary as someone who will be benefited by her act.
(3) A benefactor in the distant past could not have thought of me as a particular individual, since I did not yet exist and she had no singular concept of me.
(4) If she could not think of me as a particular individual, then she could not have me as the object of her intention.
(5) Therefore, however much I gained through her act, she did not intentionally benefit me.
(6) Therefore I owe her no gratitude; the gratitude relation was never so much as a candidate.
