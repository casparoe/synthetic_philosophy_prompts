I am a PhD student in comparative federalism, and I teach a graduate seminar on subsidiarity and the vertical division of powers. Twenty students will attend the next session, which runs for 90 minutes. The course has already covered the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union, so the students recognize the institutions; what they do not yet have is a feel for why the four systems allocate legislative, administrative, and fiscal power so differently. Please design a structured in-class negotiation that gives them that feel, with the idea emerging from the decisions and frustrations the activity produces rather than from a preliminary lecture. By the end, participants should come away seeing that subsidiarity is not a single presumption in favor of the local level, but the shifting result of three distinct allocations: who sets the rules, who administers them, and who pays.

The activity should use one shared, non-technical problem, identical across four parallel tables: nitrate pollution in a river basin. Three upstream regions with intensive agriculture discharge runoff into the river; one downstream region bears the concentrated drinking-water costs. All four regions contribute to a central budget. Reducing nitrate loads requires farmers to adopt costly practices such as buffer strips, cover crops, and reduced fertilizer, and compliance is plausibly verifiable only by local inspectors who know the farms.

Each table should negotiate under a different constitutional rulebook. I have summarized the baselines so that the rulebooks can be run without consulting outside sources; refine them as needed, but preserve their logic.

- US rulebook (dual federalism): the central level may legislate only on the basis of an enumerated cross-border commerce power; it may not commandeer the regions, meaning it cannot require them to pass laws or administer central programs; it may spend and attach conditions to grants, but regions may decline the money. General regulatory power remains with the regions.
- German rulebook (executive federalism): the central level may enact framework standards binding on all regions, but the regions implement and enforce them through their own administrations; a fiscal equalization mechanism narrows but does not eliminate regional fiscal differences.
- Swiss rulebook (subsidiarity with fiscal equivalence): competence stays with the regions unless the constitution expressly assigns it to the centre; the centre may set standards but the regions implement them; under fiscal equivalence, the level that decides a policy and benefits from it should bear its costs.
- EU rulebook (conferral, subsidiarity, proportionality): the centre has only the powers conferred on it; in shared competences it may act only where the regions cannot sufficiently achieve the objective and the centre can achieve it better, and only to the extent necessary; directives may bind the regions as to the objective while leaving legal form and administrative method to them.

Each table, staffed by five students (three upstream regions, one downstream region, one central authority), must reach decisions on four questions:

1. Which level may set binding numeric water-quality standards?
2. Which level issues permits and inspects farms?
3. Which level pays the upgrade costs, and through what instrument (general taxes, user fees, conditional grants)?
4. How is regional noncompliance corrected?

To keep prior knowledge from doing the design work, label the four rulebooks A through D during the negotiation and reveal which is which only at the start of the debrief. The 90 minutes should roughly consist of: 5 minutes of framing that deliberately does not name the doctrinal principles; 45 minutes of table negotiation; 15 minutes of report-back in which each table states its answers and the single moment its rulebook produced friction; 20 minutes of debrief; and a 5-minute buffer.

Please write the response in exactly the following titled sections, in this order and with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Design logic (200 words): what the session is meant to make participants feel and why the parallel-rulebook simulation produces that better than a lecture would.
2. Setup, roles, and materials (250 words): room arrangement, the four tables of five, role cards, a simple map of the basin, and a low-complexity scoring system using chips or points rather than money. Each role should have something to protect, such as fiscal room, local control, compliance, or downstream water quality.
3. Rules and sequence (350 words): facilitator instructions precise enough to run without improvisation, including each negotiation milestone, how a table resolves disagreement, and the default if a table deadlocks.
4. The four rulebooks (400 words): how each baseline becomes binding constraints on legislation, administration, and financing, with the fiscal parameters each table needs; a teaching assistant should be able to hand a rulebook to a table and run it.
5. Debrief script (300 words): a sequence that first draws out each table's decisions and moments of friction, then reveals the real systems and maps those decisions explicitly to subsidiarity, proportionality, fiscal equivalence, anti-commandeering, and executive federalism.
6. Likely failure points and fixes (200 words): predictable first-run failure modes, such as tables treating their rulebook as optional, central-authority roles turning into lectures, or discussion drifting into agricultural policy, with concrete fixes for each.
7. What the activity cannot reproduce (150 words): the respects in which the simulation inevitably departs from real federal and supranational systems, and what the facilitator must therefore supply afterward.
