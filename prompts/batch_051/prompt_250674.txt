I'm a novelist and I've been reading Leibniz's New Essays Concerning Human Understanding, the preface, where he says the mind is never entirely without perceptions—that at every moment there's an infinity of them in us, mostly too faint to notice, like the roar of the sea being made up of waves we don't hear one by one. It's kind of wrecked me. I want a deadpan satirical craft essay, maybe 700 words, in the voice of a novelist who has taken that passage literally and now thinks the whole striving to exhaustively render experience is metaphysically doomed: a finite mind can't ever get to the bottom of even the smallest scene, because the texture just keeps branching into smaller unnoticed things. The essay should be called something like "Against the Grain of the Real" and the joke has to be that she tries to prove this by enumerating everything in her immediate surroundings—the dust on the windowsill, the way the radiator ticks, the slight pressure of her chair, the half-thoughts that flit by—and each sentence makes the case that she both can't succeed and can't stop. The target is the "total capture" aesthetic in literary fiction, not any real writer, and the essay has to stay deadpan and in-voice, no winking. By the end a reader should feel the real question is whether finitude makes description tragic or just ordinary. And don't let her cop out by saying "therefore write loosely"—if she gives up exhaustiveness, she has to sit with the fact that she's still writing.
