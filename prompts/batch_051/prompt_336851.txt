After an illness this spring left me in severe pain for several days, I found myself unable to stop thinking about a strange thought: what if the feeling itself had nothing to do with my saying that I felt it? I started reading about epiphenomenalism, and I have written out an argument that now troubles me. I would be grateful if you would examine it step by step and tell me whether it is valid, and, if it is not, exactly which step fails.

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, then the felt character of a pain is causally idle: it never causes any physical event, including my judgement that I am in pain.

(2) But I am justified in judging that I am in pain only if I make that judgement because the felt character is present.

(3) Therefore, if epiphenomenalism is true, my judgement that I am in pain is unjustified.

(4) Yet my judgement that I am in pain is justified—indeed, it is as certain as anything I know.

(5) Therefore, epiphenomenalism is false.

The conclusion is appealing, but I suspect step (2) is doing too much work, or that the word 'because' is sliding between two meanings. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, is it sound? If it fails, identify the exact step and state how it should be revised.
