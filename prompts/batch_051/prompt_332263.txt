Consider the following dispute. When we predict a complicated system by adopting the intentional stance — treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — are we registering real patterns in the system, or are we imposing a useful fiction on machinery that contains nothing belief-like? Daniel Dennett's notion of a real pattern gives the dispute a testable-sounding shape: a pattern is real to the extent that it compresses and predicts the data, whether or not the pattern corresponds to anything physically salient. But it is one thing to state that criterion and another to apply it to a disputed case. Your task is to design the strongest test you can of the claim that a particular intentional-state ascription identifies a real pattern. Choose a concrete system for which the question is genuinely open — a chess engine, a trading firm, a bird flock, a language model, or something similar — and justify the choice. Specify the two models you would compare, one approximating the design stance and one approximating the intentional stance, and say why the physical stance is either intractable or not the right competitor in this case. Then specify the data you would collect and the measure that would tell you which model better compresses and predicts the system's behavior. Be explicit about what result would favor the view that the intentional pattern is real, what result would favor the projection view, and what even the best-designed test could never settle about whether the system really has beliefs as opposed to merely behaving in ways that make belief-ascription useful.
