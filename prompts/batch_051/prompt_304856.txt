I'm a retired physicist and I've recently got into philosophy, so maybe I'm primed to like this view. The idea is that the self is a centre of narrative gravity: like the centre of mass of a body, not a real extra component, but an abstraction you use to track and predict the organism's story. The centre of mass of a chair isn't there in the same way as the wood, but you can still calculate it and use it. Fine. My self would be the hero of the story the brain tells, not a thing in the brain.

The bit I can't get past is this. In physics the useful fiction never does anything, isn't wronged, and doesn't have to be real, because nothing depends on it being real. We were happy with all sorts of real-as-pattern structures in physics too: a whirlpool is not a separate thing, but it's also not nothing, and no one minded. But a whirlpool doesn't find that conclusion unacceptable; I do. My self seems to be the thing that is currently weighing the theory, noticing its own resistance to it, and is also the thing that stubbed its toe this morning. If the self is a fiction, who just got persuaded, and who would prefer the theory to be false? The centre of mass of a chair has no views about centres of mass.

So I have a theory that tidies up a lot of the mess around personal identity, and a firm intuition that there is someone here who could be wrong wether he exists. Give me the strongest version of both sides: the case that the intuition is itself generated by the narrative machinery and should be discounted, and the case that the theory leans on a bad analogy and quietly assumes the very self it says isn't there. Then tell me which one should be revised, and what a principled compromise looks like, rather than just biting one of the bullets.

And please write the answer in British English.
