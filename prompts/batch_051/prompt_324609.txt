I am a novelist, at work on a novel whose central character is a girl born deaf into a family that, for reasons internal to the story, never exposes her to a signed language. In trying to make her inner life persuasive rather than merely imagined from the outside, I have been reading linguistics, and I have extracted a working definition of language that I now find myself testing against the novel as I write it. The definition runs like this. A language has (1) a stable lexicon, in which particular forms are paired with particular meanings; (2) a grammar, the means to combine a finite stock of units into indefinitely many novel utterances; (3) displacement, the capacity to speak about what is absent — the past, the future, the hypothetical; (4) conventionality, the tie between form and meaning resting on shared custom rather than on transparent resemblance to the thing meant; and (5) cultural transmission, each new user acquiring the system from other users rather than constructing it alone.

In the novel, the girl — call her Maren — has, by age eight, built a gesture system of her own. The signs are stable from month to month; she combines them into sequenced utterances that make clear who did what to whom; she can recount an event from the day before and ask about a trip that has not yet happened. Some signs began as pantomime, such as smoothing a skirt for her grandmother, but many have since worn down into forms no longer transparently imitative. Her parents understand much of what she produces and can manage rough approximations of her signs, but they never converse with each other in them; they borrow her vocabulary back to her, and she remains the only fully fluent, productive user. No community has formed around the system.

Here is the question the book keeps asking me, and I want it reasoned through rather than answered by feel: by the five criteria above, is Maren's system a language? Take each criterion in turn and say whether the case passes, fails, passes only partly, or leaves the matter undecided — and whether the criterion itself is the right one to insist on. Then turn the result back on the criteria. If the system is not a language, what exactly is missing, and would giving it that missing thing make it one? If it is a language, what should I conclude about requiring cultural transmission and conventionality at all? I ask because the novel is partly about how signed languages were for a long time denied the status of language, and I want to know whether my own definition would make a subtler version of the same mistake.
