For the past few weeks I have been on a school-board committee deciding whether to end a well-loved after-school enrichment program that children in the poorer neighborhoods cannot attend. Closing it would save the district money, but none of that saved money would go to the children who are already excluded, and I keep thinking of Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas.' There, a city's prosperity depends on one child's being kept in misery; most citizens accept the bargain, but a few walk away even though, so far as the story tells us, their leaving makes no one better off.

Read the story through the lens of prioritarianism. The leveling-down objection holds that equality cannot be valuable for its own sake, because then taking benefits away from the better off could be an improvement even when no one is better off and some are worse off; prioritarianism claims to avoid this by caring only about welfare gains and losses, with gains to the worse off weighted more heavily. Are the walkers in Omelas making precisely the move that objection condemns—leaving a genuinely good life in order to stand apart from an arrangement while the child remains as miserable as before? Or could a prioritarian still endorse walking away, for example because complicity itself is a welfare loss, or because the act of leaving is the only protest with any chance of changing the child's fate? Does the story expose the leveling-down objection as resting on too thin an account of what belongs in a person's welfare, or does it show that the walkers' refusal is more expressive than effective?
