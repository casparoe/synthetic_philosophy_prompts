I've been rereading Plato's Phaedrus lately, after a disagreement with a friend about whether a text can mean something its author never intended. In the Jowett translation, Phaedrus describes the speech Lysias has just delivered:

"Lysias has been writing about a fair youth who was being tempted, but not by a lover; and this was the point: he ingeniously proved that the non-lover should be accepted rather than the lover."

I keep returning to that sentence. On its face it's just a summary, but it has become, for me, a test case for the question of where a text's meaning lives. I've collected these readings. (1) The intentionalist reading: the speech means what Lysias meant to prove, and Phaedrus's summary faithfully reports that intention. (2) The formalist reading: the speech's meaning is carried by its own argumentative structure—its premises and conclusions—independently of Lysias's mental states. (3) The dramatic reading: Lysias's speech has its meaning only in virtue of being placed in Plato's dialogue as a foil to Socrates's later speeches; Lysias's intentions drop out. (4) The reader-response reading: the speech means what Phaedrus makes of it—his admiration, his eagerness to reproduce it, his sense that it is a feast of discourse. I have the feeling I'm missing a well-known kind of reading. Give me one that is not on this list and not a restatement or combination of any of them. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good, and whether it should move my disagreement with my friend.
