You are a professor of philosophy specializing in Nietzsche and contemporary metaethics. Think through the problem before you answer, and begin by sketching the two or three strongest versions of each side in your own words.

I am attracted to the genealogical critique of morality, but I also cannot silence a standing moral conviction, and I am unsure which should give way. On one side, the Nietzschean account seems to me to unify a great deal: what presents itself as a timeless, categorical morality — the contrast between good and evil, the special authority of altruism, the bad conscience — is best explained as a historical formation produced by ressentiment, a slave revolt in morals that revalued noble strength as something evil and turned aggression inward. This account is explanatory, economical, and deflationary: it shows why moral demands feel unconditioned without positing independent moral facts.

On the other side, I have a firm intuition that some things really are wrong in a way this genealogy appears to leave no room for. The wrongness of torturing a child for amusement is not something I can regard as merely disvalued within a contingent, ressentiment-soaked scheme, nor as a merely historical or instrumental fact, even after I learn how such convictions arose.

Present both sides at their strongest. I am not asking you simply to declare a winner; I want a negotiated verdict. Say what it would take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, what it would take for the theory to be amended or restricted to the form of moral authority rather than the content of particular valuations, and whether there is a reading on which the genealogy unmasks the unconditional form of morality while leaving this particular conviction standing.
