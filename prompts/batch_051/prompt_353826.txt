I'm teaching a unit on the philosophy and linguistics of writing systems, and I want a critical essay on Denis Villeneuve's film Arrival, adapted from Ted Chiang's story "Story of Your Life". The unit's organising question is this: what does a script encode, and how phonemic is an alphabet really? I'd like you to read the film through that question.

The film gives its aliens two unrelated linguistic channels: a spoken one and a written one, Heptapod B, whose circular symbols — described in the film as logograms — are said to encode a whole utterance at once, with no correspondence to speech and no linear order, and it is learning the script, specifically, that changes the protagonist's experience of time. I want you to treat that invention not as a throwaway science-fiction conceit but as an extreme thesis in the theory of writing: roughly, that a script is a theory of which units of a language matter, and that the choice of unit — phoneme, syllable, morpheme, or whole utterance — carries consequences for how readers carve up language, and perhaps the world.

In the essay, bring the film into contact with the actual study of writing systems. In particular, real alphabets map letters to phonemes only approximately: they differ in phonemic depth, the extent to which spellings track pronunciation rather than morphology, etymology or lexical identity. A deep orthography such as English or French is, in places, closer to a morphemic script than to the idealised one-letter-one-sound picture. Conversely, logographic scripts such as Chinese also encode phonological information, and are read in linear sequence. So the contrast the film appears to need — our transparent, linear, phonetic alphabet versus their pure, non-linear, semantic script — is largely a stylisation, and I want you to take that stylisation seriously rather than simply dismiss it.

Please structure the essay as follows:

1. "The film's wager" (250 words): state the view of writing the film seems committed to, using the heptapods' two channels as evidence, and explain what role the script specifically, rather than language in general, plays in the film's treatment of time.
2. "What real scripts encode" (400 words): explain phonemic depth and the trade-offs real writing systems make between sound, meaning and word identity, and use this to say what an alphabet is and is not a theory of.
3. "Where the film is a fair stylisation" (350 words): make the strongest case that the heptapod script dramatises something true: that no script is a neutral transcription of speech, and that writing can embody an analysis of a language rather than merely record it.
4. "Where the film overreaches" (350 words): argue where the film trades on the exoticising contrast just described, what it confuses about the difference between representing time in a script and coming to perceive time differently because of a script, and which parts of its picture a linguist of writing would reject.
5. "The bottom line" (150 words): say what the film, taken seriously as a fable about writing, adds to the debate that a textbook on orthography does not.

Please write in British English and aim for about 1500 words in total. I am not asking for citations or a bibliography; the argument should stand on its own.
