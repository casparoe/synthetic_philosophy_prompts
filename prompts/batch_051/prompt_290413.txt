Please explain, conceptually rather than by giving a formal proof, why there is a structural tension—amounting, under certain conditions, to an impossibility—between two fairness criteria for algorithmic risk scores.

Set the problem up as follows. A decision-maker assigns each person a risk score between 0 and 1, intended to estimate the probability of an adverse outcome (for example, defaulting on a loan or being rearrested). A single threshold converts scores into binary decisions: people at or above the threshold are classified as high risk and denied a benefit or subjected to an intervention; people below the threshold are not. There are two groups, A and B, and the adverse outcome is more common in group A than in group B. The score is informative but not perfectly accurate.

Define the two criteria. A score is calibrated when, among people assigned any given score, the proportion who actually experience the adverse outcome is the same in both groups; in other words, a given score means the same risk regardless of group membership. A score has equalized error rates when, at the chosen threshold, the false positive rate is the same in both groups and the false negative rate is the same in both groups.

Please give an intuitive explanation of why these two criteria pull in opposite directions in this setup. In particular, explain why making the same score mean the same risk across groups can force the groups to have different error rates, and why equalizing error rates can force the same score to mean different risks across groups. Conclude by briefly saying what this tension reveals about the project of formalizing fairness as a short, jointly satisfiable list of statistical properties. Please answer in American English.
