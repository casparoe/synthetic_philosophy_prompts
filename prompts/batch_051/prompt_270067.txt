I teach introductory physics at a college whose students mostly are not going to become physicists, and I'm in the middle of a syllabus fight with a colleague. She argues that the generous thing is to give students crisp definitions, the relevant equations, and worked procedures on day one, so that they always have something reliable to hold onto. I've been reading Whitehead on education, and I find myself defending the opposite order: a student who is made to absorb exact rules before she has had any direct, somewhat ungoverned encounter with the phenomena ends up with what Whitehead called inert ideas — knowledge she can recite on a test but never thinks to use, recombine, or stretch when the world does not announce itself as a textbook problem. On Whitehead's picture there is a rhythm: a romantic stage in which the learner is seized by the subject matter in a rough, emotional, unregulated way; a precision stage of definitions, drill, and exact procedure; and a later stage in which the now-precise knowledge is put back into free play. My colleague and I disagree about what happens when the order is inverted, and we've both admitted we don't actually know how we would tell who is right.

So I want to figure out whether my view is the kind of view that can be tested, and if so how to test it properly. Take the precise claim as this: when the precision stage is imposed before the romantic stage, the resulting knowledge is different in kind from knowledge built in the recommended order — it is more inert, in the sense that students can reproduce it on demand but do not carry it into new settings. I'd like three things. First, a concrete design, ideally one I could run across two or three sections of my own course: what to vary, what to hold fixed, what to measure (and when, and with what instruments), and what comparison would be decisive. Second, an account of which patterns of results would count for the claim and which against it, including the possibility that the two orders produce identical exam performance but different downstream behavior. Third, and most important to me: what would even a fully positive result fail to settle? Is there any measurement that could distinguish truly inert ideas from ideas that are merely weakly learned, or from the effects of students simply being less motivated? And would a positive result license the further claim that my colleague's order is a pedagogical mistake, or would that still be a philosophical step beyond the data?
