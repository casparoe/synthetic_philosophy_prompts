I direct the grants program at a mid-sized private foundation. Several times a year we must choose among finalists that are good in fundamentally different ways: a nutrition intervention that prevents a small number of child deaths, an arts program that makes a neighborhood a cultural destination, a legal-aid project that changes who can hold landlords accountable. Our current procedure scores every proposal on a single numerical rubric, and I have watched it produce a false precision: staff game the weights, and the scores imply comparisons the underlying judgments cannot support.

I have come across a philosophical distinction that seems relevant, but I do not know how to use it. The idea is that two options can fail to be strictly rankable in more than one way. They can be exactly equal in value. They can be incomparable, in the strong sense that no positive comparison between them is available. Or they can be on a par: neither is better, the two are not equal, and a small improvement to one would not automatically tip the balance. The literature often separates both from incommensurability in the strict sense, which means only that there is no common unit of measure and does not by itself rule out one option being better than the other. It is also said that comparing options across very different goods usually requires a covering value, some broader value that encompasses both, and that the absence of an obvious covering value is not by itself evidence that the options are equal or that no comparison is possible. On this view, when options are on a par, forcing a precise ranking is a mistake rather than a solution.

Please turn these distinctions into a decision procedure my grants team can actually run on a pair of finalists. I want a reusable procedure, not advice about one grant cycle. It should include: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on; where the procedure stops with a result of "these cannot be ranked on this scale" or hands the matter to the board; and which steps are values judgments rather than technical tests, marked as such. It must be usable by grants officers with no philosophical training in under an hour per pair, and it must be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a proven child-nutrition program against a hard-to-quantify neighborhood arts program; (2) a legal-aid project that will shift power against a capital grant that will make a community institution permanent; and (3) a case you choose in which the procedure tells my team not to force two proposals onto a single ranking and instead to surface a board-level choice, even though avoiding that outcome is part of what the board expects from the rubric.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: where it cannot replace judgment, and what goes wrong if someone uses it as an algorithm for making the choice rather than as a discipline on how the choice is made.

Write the response in American English.
