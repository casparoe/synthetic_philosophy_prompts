In a working group on behavioral genetics we arrived at an argument I find disturbing, and I would like a diagnosis of which step fails rather than a survey of the field.

Let T be a quantitative trait, and let G1 and G2 be two populations whose mean values of T differ, with G1 scoring higher. Consider this chain of reasoning.

(1) Within each population, T is highly heritable: roughly 80 percent of the variance among individuals within G1, and likewise within G2, is statistically attributable to genetic differences among those individuals, with the remainder attributable to environmental differences.

(2) When genetic differences account for most of the variation among individuals within each group, the most natural explanation of a mean difference between the groups is that that difference, too, is substantially genetic.

(3) If a between-group mean difference is substantially genetic in origin, then changing the environment of the lower-scoring group can close at most a small part of the gap, since the principal cause of the gap is not environmental.

(4) Therefore the G1–G2 difference in T is substantially genetic, and environmental interventions aimed at closing it should have only modest effects.

(5) But the same reasoning can be run where its conclusion cannot be true. Split a single genetically diverse lot of seed corn between two plots, fertilize one and not the other. Within each plot the environment is nearly uniform, so nearly all the variation in plant height within each plot is genetic: within-plot heritability is close to 1. Yet the mean height difference between the plots is entirely due to fertilizer, and adding fertilizer to the poor plot closes the gap completely.

(6) Hence, by (2) and (3), the corn-plot height gap is substantially genetic and fertilizer can close at most a small part of it, which is exactly what we know to be false. Something in the argument proves too much.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep (1) and (5) if I can: (1) is a within-group variance decomposition, and (5) is an experimental fact. Tell me precisely what high within-group heritability does and does not license us to infer about between-group mean differences, and about how a highly heritable trait responds to environmental change.
