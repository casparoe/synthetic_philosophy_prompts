I'm a PhD student in history (modern Europe, mostly) and I'm in a methods seminar that keeps drifting into philosophy of social science, and I keep coming back to something that seems inconsistent in how my own field actually works. On one hand, historians are very comfortable saying that big events were overdetermined: there were several causes any one of which might have been enough, there's no single "the" cause, and rerunning history is a mug's game. But on the other hand, the same people write monographs that rank causes for hundreds of pages — the July Crisis was the trigger, the alliance system was the deep structural cause, railway mobilization timetables were a necessary precondition, nationalism was a permissive condition, and so on. They'll say the assasination in Sarajevo was the proximate cause but it's wrong to call it "the" cause of the war, and then spend a chapter explaining why the real cause was something else. There's even a running joke that asking a historian what caused X gets you N different answers, each delivered as if it were the answer.

So what theory of causation and explanation does this practice actually presuppose? Does it need a notion of degrees of causal importance measured on a single scale, or is causal selection really interest-relative or contrastive, so that different what-if questions license different selections? Does talking about overdetermination while still ranking causes presuppose something like causes as weighted factors, or a sharp distinction between making an event inevitable and explaining why it happened at this time and in this place? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory historians seem to commit themselves to, separate the parts the practice strictly presupposes from the parts that mere conventions are compatible with several philosophical views, and say what would have to change in how historians write if they took a contrastive or counterfactual theory of causal explanation seriously.

You're a philosopher of social science who also works on analytic metaphysics of causation. Take your time and think carefully — start by brainstorming the different things one might mean by overdetermination and the candidate theories of causal selection, then give me your reconstruction.
