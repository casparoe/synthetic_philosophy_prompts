"Explain the difference between Hume's is–ought gap and G. E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy. For each one, say what it rules out if it's correct, and then say how the two are related."

One student's answer (slightly cleaned up from handwriting) was:

"Hume's is–ought gap says you can't derive an 'ought' from an 'is.' If an argument's premises just describe how things are and its conclusion says how things should be, the argument is invalid unless a normative premise is being smuggled in somewhere. Moore's naturalistic fallacy is the mistake of identifying goodness with a natural property like pleasure, or of assuming that whatever is natural is good; his open question argument shows that any definition of good in natural terms fails, because you can always ask whether the thing with that property is really good, and the question stays open. These are basically the same point looked at from two sides: the facts of the world can't settle what we ought to do. So when someone moves from 'people naturally enjoy cooperation' to 'people ought to cooperate,' they're committing the naturalistic fallacy, and the is–ought gap is why. It follows that no moral conclusion can ever be validly derived from purely descriptive premises."
