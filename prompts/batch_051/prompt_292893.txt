I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and my dissertation work has pushed me toward a fairly specific view about moral worth. I would like the inverse of the usual survey question: not "what are the standard objections to this view" but "what would actually count against it, and how exposed is it?"

The view I have come to accept is this. An action has moral worth to the extent that the reasons for which the agent acts are identical to — or a non-accidental approximation of — the features that make the action right. Worth is a coincidence relation between the action's right-making features and the agent's motivating reasons. Three commitments follow. First, an agent can act with full worth without being able to articulate the right-making features, so long as those features are what non-deviantly explain her action; unreflective decency can have worth, and this is not a puzzle the view needs to explain away. Second, the motive of duty confers no special worth; acting from duty has worth only in those cases where the fact that the action is one's duty coincides with the right-making features. Third, the agent's own normative beliefs are worth-irrelevant except as a vehicle: they matter only when, and because, they are how the right-making features get their grip on her.

I am not asking for the two stock objections — overdetermination cases, or the charge that this collapses into a fetishism for moral properties. I want a map of the view's exposure. Which cases, results, or nearby arguments (about skill, spontaneity, moral testimony, akrasia, blame, or the difference between doing the right thing and acting rightly) should a committed defender concede as counting against the view? Which should they dismiss as merely awkward or as changing the subject? And has the view been insulated by its qualifiers — "degree," "non-accidental approximation," "the features that make the action right" — to the point that it no longer risks anything? I would personally find a clear counterexample most persuasive: a case where the motivating reasons and the right-makers coincide but worth seems absent, or where they diverge but worth seems present.

Please structure the answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. "The view, restated" (about 150 words). State the strongest version of the position, resolving ambiguities in its favor.
2. "The case for" (about 250 words). Say what the view explains that its main rivals do not, so the cost of abandoning it is explicit.
3. "The defeaters, ranked" (about 500 words). Present what would count against the view, from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether a committed defender would concede it or dismiss it.
4. "Where I should move" (about 200 words). Identify the one or two defeaters that should lead me to revise the view, and sketch the revision.
5. "Verdict" (about 150 words). Assess whether the view, as standardly qualified, is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind.
