I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of physics, and I have been working on the question of what it would take for physics to be finished, and whether we could know that it was. For the purposes of this prompt, I will state the test I have been using, and then describe a case that is giving me trouble.

The test has four criteria. A proposal deserves to be called a final theory only if:

1. Scope closure: the proposal, together with its auxiliary assumptions, assigns a determinate probability or determinate value to every outcome that can be physically measured by us, and none of those assignments has failed.
2. Anomaly resistance: there is no known phenomenon the proposal treats as outside its intended domain, and its core commitments have survived every test that has been carried out against them.
3. Non-derivability: the proposal is not currently known to be a special case or effective limit of some deeper theory.
4. Uniqueness: the proposal is unique up to mathematical equivalence, meaning that the remaining formulations are genuinely the same theory, not merely different theories that happen to agree on predictions.

Now consider the following situation. After a long period in which fundamental physics stabilizes, the community arrives not at a single theory but at an exhaustive family of theories. All members of the family agree exactly on every quantity observable below a fixed energy threshold and within the part of spacetime that is in principle accessible to us. They differ only in their claims about what happens above that threshold, beyond the accessible region, or in regimes no feasible experiment could probe. The family is known to be complete in the sense that every theory reproducing the accessible predictions is one of its members. The members are not mere notational variants of one another; they are mathematically inequivalent.

Does this family satisfy my four-part test, and should this situation count as the end of physics? Go through the criteria one by one, identify exactly where the case becomes borderline, and then commit to a verdict. Either argue that the uniqueness criterion should be weakened so that the family counts as a final theory, and explain what that does to what physics would have achieved; or argue that the uniqueness criterion should be retained, and explain what further ingredient we would need and whether we could ever be in a position to know we had it. I am asking for a committed answer rather than a survey of options, so defend the position you take.
