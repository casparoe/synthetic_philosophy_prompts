Here is a parallel I have noticed and cannot decide about. First-past-the-post and proportional representation appear to instantiate a more general divide between committing to the mode of a distribution and preserving the whole distribution. In a first-past-the-post district, the seat goes to the plurality winner, and the support for every other candidate, along with any surplus support for the winner, plays no further role. Under proportional representation, seats track the overall distribution of votes, so a party's representation approximates its share of support. This looks structurally identical to the difference between using a maximum a posteriori estimate, which selects the most probable hypothesis and discards the rest of the posterior, and carrying the full posterior forward, as in Bayesian model averaging, where each hypothesis retains influence in proportion to its posterior mass. On this analogy, first-past-the-post is to proportional representation as a point estimate is to a distribution. Is this a real identity of structure, or only a loose analogy resting on the phrase winner-take-all? If it is real, do the familiar pathologies of first-past-the-post—strategic voting, wasted votes, the squeeze on third parties—have counterparts among the pathologies of point estimation, and is gerrymandering helpfully seen as redrawing district boundaries so that a favored outcome becomes modal for reasons of boundaries rather than support? If it is not real, where exactly does the structure come apart? One candidate is that an electoral system allocates fixed offices to agents who will then act, while an inference procedure is answerable to data, but I do not know whether that difference defeats the analogy or merely limits what can transfer.
