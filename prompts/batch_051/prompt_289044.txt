I'm a philosophy professor — my own work is in philosophy of biology, so formal epistemology isn't really my patch — and every spring I teach our survey course in philosophy of science. There's a knot I keep tripping over, and I'd like to know whether it has a name or whether I'm smearing together two or three different things. Here's the example I use. Suppose a theory T is proposed in 1980, and it was built, more or less explicitly, to fit a body of evidence E that has been sitting in the literature since 1920. Everyone agrees E supports T. But when I try to say why in Bayesian terms, the math tells me it doesn't support it at all: by the time T arrives, E is already part of background knowledge, so P(E) is 1 and updating on E can't move P(T). If I try to fix this by "forgetting" E and reconstructing what my prior would have been before 1920, the prior is obviously invented after the fact, so any confirmation I get out of it look rigged. So there's a puzzle about how already-known facts can confirm a new theory.

What I want to know is whether that exact configuration has an established label, and whether it's the same problem as the one about prediction versus accommodation — i.e., whether a theory earns more credit from risky novel predictions than from accommodating data it was designed to fit. My gut says these are different issues, because the first is a technical snag in the updating story even when the theory wasn't gerrymandered, while the second is about what kind of evidence has more epistemic weight. But maybe the literature treats them as one thing, or maybe there's a name for only one of them. I'm not after just a label; I want to know how the phenomenon should be carved, what the nearest named concepts are, and exactly where the boundary between it and the accommodation question lies.
