I keep going back and forth over an argument someone put to me about the child's right to an open future, and I cannot find the false step. It seems to prove far too much, so I want to know where it goes wrong and what it would cost to say so. The argument runs like this. (1) If children have a genuine right to an open future, the right protects not merely the child's survival but a range of worthwhile adult options that must remain open until the child can choose among them. (2) If a right protects a range of options in this way, then knowingly and avoidably foreclosing one of the options in that range, without authorization, sets back the interest the right protects and so violates it. (3) Ordinary child-rearing knowingly and avoidably forecloses options of exactly this kind: raising a child in one language forecloses fluency in another; raising a child inside or outside a religion forecloses the corresponding forms of life; choosing a school, a town, or a way of life models some futures and forecloses others. (4) The child cannot authorize these foreclosures, because the child is not yet the adult whose options are at stake and lacks the capacity to consent. (5) Therefore ordinary child-rearing violates the child's right to an open future. (6) If ordinary child-rearing violates that right, then a state that takes the right seriously cannot leave these decisions unregulated; it must supervise them in advance, since after the fact the options are already gone. (7) Hence a state committed to the child's right to an open future is committed to licensing parents, or to something close to it, and to overseeing the language, religion, schooling, and residence choices through which parents shape a child's future. That conclusion strikes me as unacceptable, and not just because it is intrusive: a state that regulates choices this intimate has abandoned the liberal distinction between the state's role and the family's. I want very much to keep the idea that the right to an open future does genuine justificatory work, and I also want to keep the judgment that ordinary parents who make controversial choices for their children are not thereby violating their children's rights. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it force me to give up?
