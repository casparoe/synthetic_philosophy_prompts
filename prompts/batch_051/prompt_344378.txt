So I write historical novels for a living, and I keep getting into this argument with my editor about how much I'm allowed to change about the real people I put in my books. She sent me a philosophy podcast where a guy laid out what he called the standard view about our duties to the real dead, and I scribbled down a paraphrase while I was driving. I probably mangled something, because the last part seems to undercut the first, but I definately can't put my finger on where the problem is.

The summary I ended up with goes roughly like this. Once a novelist uses a real person's name and circumstances in a book, she takes on the same obligation a biographer has: don't make things up about them that readers will take as fact, because readers do take fiction about real people as fact, and putting the word novel on the cover doesn't shield you. That first part sounds right to me. Then he said the duty is total: fidelity to every checkable detail, because changing what a real person actually said or believed is also a small lie about them, and a small lie differs from a big lie only in size, not in kind. Then, near the end, he said that since the dead can't suffer or ever learn about the book, the real injury when a novelist misuses a dead person falls on living descendants and on the public's memory of the past, which means the novelist's obligations are ultimately just obligations to the living. That last move feels like it gives away everything the first part promised, but maybe I'm just being dense. Or maybe he meant it as the standard objection that view has to answer, and I missed a distinction he was drawing.

Can you audit my paraphrase for me? I want to know which parts a careful defender of that underlying view would sign off on, which parts are the podcaster, or my memory of him, overreaching or misstating, and whether the conclusion that our duties are ultimately only to the living actually follows from the accurate parts.
