I'm writing a little dialogue piece for a science-and-religion reading group and I keep getting stuck on Newton. The group read the General Scholium (the short essay at the end of the Principia) plus a few passages from his theological manuscripts, and we got into a argument about what he'd make of modern fine-tuning. The obvious answer is that he'd be delighted: he already argued that the orderly motions of the planets point to intelligent design, so wouldn't he love the claim that the cosmological constant is fine-tuned to an absurd degree for life? But I think that's too fast. His design argument was grounded in features of the world we actually observe—e.g. the planets all revolving in the same direction and nearly the same plane, which he thought could not have arisen by chance alone—whereas fine-tuning arguments run through a space of other possible universes with different constants, none of which anyone has observed. He also had a strong voluntarist streak: God isn't constrained by an independent rational order, so as I understand him he might find the claim that God had to choose a universe like this one almost impious. And I'm not sure how his famous refusal to feign hypotheses would bear on the multiverse reply, which seems to be hypotheses feigned all the way down.

So, concretely: before I write the scene, if I put Newton in a coffee shop with a modern cosmologist who presents fine-tuning and the multiverse as the two live options, how should Newton respond in a way that's recognizably him and not just generic theist pushback? I mean specifically: which parts of the modern argument would he accept as a legitimate extension of his own design reasoning, which would he reject or find unintelligible, and where would he get hung up on the move from actual observed regularities to unobserved possible worlds? I don't care about the niche details of his private theology; I care about the shape of the position, and I want the dialogue to earn its ending.
