For a class I'm teaching on Rousseau and the arts, I want to use his argument against the theater in the Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre (the one he wrote in response to d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article proposing a theater for Geneva). The part I'm interested in goes something like this, though I'm paraphrasing not quoting: Rousseau says the pleasure of theater is essentially passive and self-regarding: you sit in a dark room, watch imitations of virtue and suffering, are moved to tears or admiration, and then leave feeling as if you've done something morally fine, when in fact you've only consumed a performance. The theater lets citizens substitue an aesthetic experience of virtue for the real practice of it, and the actor is a person whose trade is to counterfiet feelings for applause.

Now here's the thing I want you to work through. Suppose we keep everything else about Rousseau's Geneva the same, but the only kind of theater licensed is fully participatory: the audience is in the action, the plot is improvised around collective decisions, spectators are assigned roles as fellow citizens resolving a common dilemma, and there's no fixed script or fourth wall. My question is: does Rousseau's argument still go through, or does it lose its main premise? Or does it just relocate the problem? I'm not asking whether you think Rousseau is ultimately right; I'm asking what happens to the argument in this changed setting.

Please write your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion. The five items should: name the premise that the participatory setup directly threatens; say whether that premise is load-bearing or dispensable for the rest of his argument; identify any part of Rousseau's argument that survives unchanged; say whether the participatory version might actually make things worse on Rousseau's own terms; and end with a one-sentence verdict on whether the argument is dead, in need of repair, or mostly intact.
