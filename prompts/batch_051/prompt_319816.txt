You are a philosopher of art who regularly advises arts organizations on the relationship between a work’s moral character and its aesthetic value, and who is comfortable translating that debate into an operational policy. I chair the programming committee of a publicly funded repertory cinema. About every other week we must decide whether to screen a film made by someone credibly accused of serious wrongdoing, or a film whose content embodies a morally repellent vision — and every meeting re-runs the same argument. One side says the work and the artist are wholly separate: judge the film as a film. The other says some moral defects seep into the work itself and make it worse as art. The board wants a written, reusable policy rather than case-by-case improvisation, and the board must sign off on the policy and on the schedule, so we need something we can defend in public. Nobody on the committee has philosophical training, and a decision has to run in under an hour.

Before you build the procedure, briefly map the live philosophical positions this dispute draws on — autonomism, ethical criticism, moderate moralism, immoralism, and any other distinction that earns its keep — in your own words, and say which of those distinctions survive contact with a practical programming decision. Then give me the procedure: an ordered checklist or set of tests, written in plain language, whose verdict is one of “program without comment,” “program with contextualization or framing,” “do not program,” or “flag for full committee judgment.” The procedure should operationalize distinctions such as whether the moral wrong is internal to the work’s design or merely biographical, and whether the work invites the audience to endorse a morally repellent vision or merely depicts one. Mark explicitly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls in disguise, and say what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can by itself determine when a work should be shown with a note rather than not shown at all.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases. (a) A film whose form is widely admired but whose content is propaganda for an abhorrent cause. (b) A formally ordinary film by a director widely regarded as a genius who has been credibly accused of serious sexual assault, where the abuse is not depicted or referenced in the work. (c) A case you choose that pushes the procedure to its limit. Make sure at least one example returns a verdict the committee will find uncomfortable, and say plainly what following the procedure in that case would cost.
