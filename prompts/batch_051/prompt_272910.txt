I've been meditating and journaling on and of for the last year, and I've noticed something odd: paying attention to my own mind can be the most clarifying thing I do, or it can actively make things worse, and I can't tell in advance which it'll be. I started writing down my gut verdicts on little cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them, or whether I'm just attached to some incompatible intuitions.

My cases: A club tennis player starts consciously monitoring her footwork during a tight match, and her level falls apart. Verdict: the self-monitoring hurt. A chess player loses, then spends an evening replaying the game and finds the exact moment he rejected a sacrifice without calculating it; he plays differently and better the next week. Verdict: the reflection helped. A novice meditator is told to notice whenever the mind wanders, but finds that checking whether he's still attending is itself what keeps pulling him off the breath. Verdict: not helpful, at least as instructed. An exhausted junior doctor catches herself thinking "I'm too tired, I should recheck this dose," then catches the follow-up thought "no, you're fine, don't be weak," and treats the second thought as a warning sign that the first was right. Verdict: the move of doubting her own confident thought helped. A person choosing between two apartments spends a week running pros/cons lists in her head, picks one, and then feels deflated and second-guesses herself for months; her friend bought the first flat that felt right and is still happy. Verdict: the self-conscious deliberation made the choosing worse, not better. And then there's the one that bothers me most: I've had nights where three pages of "what am I actually feeling and why" untangle everything, and other nights where the same three pages just wind me tighter. Verdict: I honestly don't know.

My provisional principle is that thinking about your thinking helps when the underlying process is slow and revisable, but hurts when it gets inserted into a fast, practiced skill. But that only fits the chess case because the reflection happened after the game, and it can't handle the apartment case at all. Can you repair it or replace it? What would the repaired principle say about cases I haven't listed — say, an author who edits each sentence as she drafts versus one who edits only after a full draft, or someone who tries to figure out mid-holiday whether they're actually happy? And which of my verdicts would a defensible principle force me to drop or fudge?
