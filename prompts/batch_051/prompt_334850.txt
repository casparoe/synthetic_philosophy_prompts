Two friends of mine, both midlist novelists, got into it at a con over a word in a fantasy novel they'd both just read. The book calls the chalky powder left after a spell decays "spell-ash." Mira said the author should have coined from Greek or Latin instead, something like cantispodium, because a dead-language stem gives you a term with no misleading associations, a root you can inflect into adjectives and verbs as the series goes on, and a technical register that marks the magic system as a discipline rather than a kitchen. Dev said that's the swindle: the Latinate word isn't precise, it's just obscure — it's only "precision" is that no reader has any prior grip on it, so the author gets to freeze a meaning by fiat and the reader pays the tax of learning private code. A word in a novel, he said, has to work on a first read for the actual audience, and "spell-ash" does its job on the page. Mira shot back that fantasy readers learn coinages from context all the time — it's part of the genre's pleasure — and once learned, cantispodium is exact and won't collide with anything, while compounds like "spell-ash" sound like something from a ren-faire and fall apart the moment you need the abstract noun or the verb. She added that Latin and Greek roots travel: a Spanish or French reader can feel the shape of the word even if it's invented. Dev said it travels as decoration, not as meaning, and that judging a fictional word by how it works in languages the book wasn't written in is just a fancy way of never judging it at all. They told me to adjudicate, and I honestly can't.
