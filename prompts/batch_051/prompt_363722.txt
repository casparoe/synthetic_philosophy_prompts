I am a retired research chemist who has recently begun working through the philosophy of mathematics, and I have been asked to set one essay question for this term's final examination in an upper-level undergraduate course on the philosophy of mathematics. Please act as an experienced examiner in philosophy of mathematics. Before you draft anything, take some time to plan the question: work out in your own notes which conceptual distinctions a first-class answer would need to draw, and then design the question so that it can be answered well only by a candidate who has actually drawn them, rather than one who has merely memorised the standard labels.

Produce one essay question on Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme. The examination is to be answered in 90 minutes. The question must not be answerable by reciting a summary of the book. It should force candidates to do all of the following: distinguish a counterexample to Euler's conjecture, V − E + F = 2, from a counterexample to a proof of that conjecture, using Lakatos's picture-frame and twin-tetrahedron cases to explain the difference; explain what a hidden lemma is, and how proof-analysis can reveal that an apparently global counterexample is also a local one; and assess exactly what the label quasi-empirical commits Lakatos to about mathematical knowledge, including whether proof can still yield certainty if proofs themselves are fallible.

To prevent candidates from succeeding on rote learning, the question should include a short, self-contained, newly invented vignette — a few sentences in which an imagined mathematician responds to a counterexample in a particular way — and should ask candidates to identify which of Lakatos's strategies, if any, the response instantiates, and whether that response is defensible.

Please write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme in British English. Do not ask candidates for a bibliography, citations, or historical scholarship; everything the question needs should be contained within it. For the marking scheme, use five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty marks in total. After each point, add a one-line note on the specific way weaker candidates typically mishandle it; the errors you penalise should be genuine misunderstandings, such as conflating global with local counterexamples, reducing monster-barring to mere name-calling, or treating quasi-empirical as equivalent to a posteriori.
