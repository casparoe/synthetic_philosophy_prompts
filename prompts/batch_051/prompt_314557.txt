I am writing a near-future novel in which photorealistic video can be generated cheaply on a phone, and one of my characters is a documentary photographer trying to decide what still counts as evidence now that recordings no longer serve as the backstop against lying that they once did. I need a working definition of 'evidence'—specifically, of when a photograph, audio clip, or video counts as evidence of what it appears to show—that gets the following eight cases right:

1. a body-camera clip of an officer striking a handcuffed man, with an intact chain of custody and forensic metadata matching the scene;
2. a fabricated screenshot of a social-media post that was never posted, circulating without any provenance;
3. an unedited phone video of activists wearing realistic police uniforms while beating a protester—the beating really happened, but as a performance—later used to support the claim that police beat a protester;
4. a deepfake video of a candidate making an abusive remark, where the candidate really did say those exact words, but only in a private audio recording that has been spliced into a synthetic public setting;
5. a genuine wide-angle photograph of a packed rally, cropped so that only a few dozen people remain visible, used to support the claim that the rally was poorly attended;
6. a hash-notarized video whose end-to-end provenance is certain, but whose operator deleted three minutes of footage before uploading it, with the notarization covering only the shortened file;
7. a synthetic audio clip of a dead person's voice reading a letter he wrote, used to support the claim that he said those words;
8. the sworn, motive-free testimony of a bystander who witnessed the events in (1), consistent with the body-camera clip.

I am confident that (1) is evidence and that (2) is not; my instincts on the remaining six pull me in different directions. I suspect the word is doing two jobs at once—one about whether a recording is what it purports to be, and another about whether it actually supports the claim being made—and I want to know whether a single definition can do both. Propose the definition, run it through each of the eight cases, mark where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts.
