I'm a software engineer by trade, not a lawyer or political theorist, but I've spent the last year reading American constitutional history on the side, and I've been invited to give a talk next month to a faculty seminar in a political science department. My talk defends what I'm calling a "manifest tenor" reading of Hamilton's case for judicial review. The thesis is that Federalist No. 78, usually cited as the canonical defense of judicial review, actually supports a much narrower power than is typically claimed: courts are warranted in refusing to enforce a statute only when the statute conflicts with the manifest tenor of the Constitution — explicit textual prohibitions, structural allocations of power, and clear grants of authority — not when it offends broader, contested principles such as substantive due process, unenumerated rights, or evolving standards of decency. A central piece of my argument is this passage from Alexander Hamilton, Federalist No. 78:

"The complete independence of the courts of justice is peculiarly essential in a limited Constitution. By a limited Constitution, I understand one which contains certain specified exceptions to the legislative authority; such, for instance, as that it shall pass no bills of attainder, no ex post facto laws, and the like. Limitations of this kind can be preserved in practice no other way than through the medium of the courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void. Without this, all the reservations of particular rights or privileges would amount to nothing."

My claim is that the phrase "manifest tenor" is doing real work: Hamilton's justification ties the legitimacy of striking down a statute to the clarity of the constitutional command, because only then can a court plausibly claim to be enforcing the people's earlier deliberate choice rather than substituting its own judgment. If judges are going to invalidate laws on the basis of broad moral or political principles, they need a separate justification; they cannot, on my reading, borrow Hamilton's.

The audience is a political science faculty seminar, mostly people who work on democratic theory, American politics, and constitutional development, but no one who identifies as an originalist or as a formal legal philosopher. Several of them have said in past discussions that judicial review is presumptively undemocratic because unelected judges override the elected legislature; others, especially the American politics scholars, accept judicial review as a settled feature of the system but suspect that "textualist" and "originalist" theories are just covers for conservative outcomes.
