As part of my work on the Darwinian theory of culture, I want to work through the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge of summer 2014 as a case study. I know the empirical history in broad outline: a person filmed themselves dumping ice water over their head, named several others, and gave them roughly twenty-four hours either to perform the same act or donate to the ALS Association; variants, parodies, and celebrity versions spread rapidly across platforms, and the campaign ended up raising on the order of a hundred million dollars. What I want from you is not the history but a careful memetic analysis.

First, set out the ontology. On a particulate-replicator view of culture, what exactly is the meme here—the observable performance, the challenge rule, the nomination mechanism, the link to ALS, or the whole package as a memeplex? Use the distinction between copying the product and copying the instructions, and say which one does the real replicating. If you think the natural unit is the challenge format rather than any individual video, justify that by saying what stays stable across variants and what varies.

Second, run the selection story with some discipline. Consider fidelity, fecundity, and longevity separately, and identify exactly where the Darwinian analogy earns its keep and where it starts to strain. Pay particular attention to the twenty-four-hour rule: is it best understood as a trait that raised descendant-production, as a mutation that arose and swept, or as part of the environment in which variants competed? Then say whether the spread is better explained by selection on replicators or by biased cultural transmission in the Boyd–Richerson sense—content bias, where the public and costly act drew attention; model bias, where celebrities and peers made copying more likely; and frequency-dependent effects—and whether the donation option was a good meme or a good cause attached to one.

Third, give your overall assessment of what this case shows about the theory. Does it confirm that cultural change can be modeled as differential replication, does it mainly show that memetic language redescribes ordinary social and moral psychology, or does it expose a particular weakness—for instance, that the fitness of the variants was continuously managed by human intentions, platform algorithms, and a charitable goal that sits awkwardly inside a selfish-replicator model? I want you to say plainly where the meme’s-eye view is illuminating, where it is forced, and where it is simply silent.
