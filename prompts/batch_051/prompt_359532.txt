Please argue against the view that Richard Jeffrey's probability kinematics (Jeffrey conditionalization) is a fully adequate generalization of Bayesian conditionalization to cases of uncertain evidence.

Set up the issue as follows. An agent's credences are a probability function P over an algebra of propositions. When evidence makes a proposition E certain, the agent updates by standard conditionalization: the new credence in any H is P(H | E). Ordered sequences of such certain evidence commute, because after learning E and then F the final credence in H is P(H | E ∧ F), the same as after learning F and then E.

Uncertain evidence does not single out any proposition as having become certain; it may directly shift the probabilities of the members of an exhaustive partition. Let {E_1, ..., E_n} be such a partition, and suppose the evidence moves the probabilities of its cells to new values q_i = P'(E_i). Jeffrey's rule assumes rigidity — the conditional probabilities inside each cell do not change, so P'( · | E_i) = P( · | E_i) — and then updates every other proposition H by

P'(H) = Σ_i q_i P(H | E_i).

Argue that this rule fails exactly where standard conditionalization succeeds. Successive Jeffrey updates on different partitions are not in general path-independent: an agent who receives the same two pieces of uncertain evidence in the opposite order, from the same prior, can end up assigning different credences to the same proposition. Give a compact numerical example over a four-atom state space, using two binary partitions, to exhibit this failure of commutativity. Explain the role the rigidity condition plays in the result and show that the effect is not an artifact of a contrived prior. Use the example to argue against the adequacy claim: as standardly stated, Jeffrey conditionalization does not specify a canonical partition or a privileged order of application, and it therefore lacks the normative status that standard conditionalization has. Do not cite or list sources; the argument should rest entirely on the definitions and the example.

End your answer with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states your overall conclusion in one sentence.
