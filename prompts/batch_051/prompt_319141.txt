I'm a science journalist writing a long essay about the Fermi paradox, and my editor wants the closing pages to do more than rehearse the positions a careful reader will already have encountered. The draft as it stands covers: the rare-conditions view, on which abiogenesis or complex life is so improbable that solitude is the default; the great-filter view, which locates the improbability in a later step that technological species almost never pass; the brief-window view, on which civilizations are common but seldom detectable at the same time; the high-cost-or-low-payoff view of interstellar expansion, on which settlement never repays its investment; the zoo hypothesis, in which others watch and deliberately refrain from contact; and berserker-style scenarios, in which the galaxy is quiet because something eliminates anyone who becomes conspicuous. I take that to be most of the standard catalogue, so this is not a request to recall the obvious one I left out.

What strikes me while organizing these is that they nearly all work by taking one ingredient of the Drake-style picture and pushing its value to an extreme: a step too rare, a window too short, an expansion too costly, another civilization too silent or too hostile. I want an explanation that does not work by adjusting one of those dials toward zero or infinity, but instead changes what the paradox is about — what question is being asked or which observations a solution must explain. And it must not be a rewording, tidied-up special case, or hybrid of anything in my list. Give me one candidate, set out its mechanism in enough detail to show it is genuinely distinct, and then tell me honestly whether it is any good and where it is most vulnerable.
