I keep being struck by what looks like the same structure in two places that have no business being connected: path dependence in economic history and the way neural networks get trained with stochastic gradient descent. Economic historians say history is "locked in": early, partly accidental choices — QWERTY, VHS over Betamax, rail gauges — create increasing returns or switching costs, so you end up in one basin of attraction and later cannot cheaply move to a better one even if it exists. In machine learning, the final weights of a network also depend on the random initialization and the order of the training examples: small early fluctuations push the run into one of many roughly equivalent minima, and after enough training the solution is entrenched, even though rerunning with a different seed might have found a different minimum with slightly better generalization. That is almost exactly the historian's question of whether a rerun of history with different accidents would have produced the same outcome. In both cases the process looks non-ergodic: the long-run outcome is sensitive to early noise, and there are increasing costs to switching basins.

I cannot tell if this is a genuine structural identity or just the vacuous thought that "early differences compound." If it is genuine, what actually transfers? For instance, ML people have found that many SGD minima are connected by low-loss paths — would that predict that historical lock-in is less binding than it looks, so the QWERTY-style "inferior outcome" story is overblown? Or does the analogy break because human agents can foresee lock-in and design institutions (standards bodies, compatibility layers, property rights) that change the landscape itself, whereas gradient steps just slide downhill and never model the future? I'd like a careful verdict on where the mapping lines up and where it comes apart.
