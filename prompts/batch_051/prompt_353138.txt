A friend who works on AI alignment keeps telling me we won't need to understand a powerful AI's reasoning to keep it honest: just have two copies of it debate the question in front of a human judge, and let the judge poke at them with follow-ups. His whole argument is that telling the truth is easier then defending a false claim, so the honest debater wins. I keep pressing him on one specific failure mode and he waves it off. What stops the dishonest debater from simply making its case too big to audit? Suppose the real answer is that some proposed policy will raise unemployment. The honest AI gives a tight three-page argument built around one regression. The dishonest AI submits a nine-hundred-page brief: hundreds of sub-arguments, mostly irrelevant, and one subtle but fatal flaw buried somewhere in the middle, and the judge—human or even some smarter but still time-bounded judge—has to find it. Spotting the flaw is cheap only after you've already read the whole thing, and reading the whole thing is exactly what the judge's budget rules out. I don't see why the truth-is-easier line survives that, but I also don't know if my worry is the kind of thing that can be tested rather than just argued about, so that is my question.

Set out the strongest test you can of the claim that debate between AI systems remains truth-tracking when a dishonest debater is allowed to obfuscate by adding length and complexity. I want a test that separates "debate found the truth" from "the judge got snowed or gave up," and I want you to be explicit about what a clean positive or negative result would and would not settle. Don't give me a reading list; I want the actual test design.

Please organize the response into these sections, in this order:

- Operationalization (about 120 words): what counts as truth-tracking here, what kind of questions and judges you would use, and how you would measure decided-correctly versus undecided.
- The experiment (about 180 words): the concrete conditions, including how you would vary the dishonest debater's length budget, what the honest debater is allowed to do in response, and how you would keep the test from just measuring judge fatigue.
- What a positive result shows (about 100 words): how to interpret it if judge accuracy holds up as obfuscation increases.
- What a negative result shows (about 100 words): which confusions would still be in play if accuracy falls, for example whether the judge was too weak, the questions too hard, or whether a stronger judge might reverse it.
- What no experiment settles (about 120 words): the residue that cannot be tested even in principle, especially whether the result transfers to systems much smarter than the judge.
- My bottom line (about 80 words): your honest read on which side is favored, and why.
