I'm a novelist working on a book set in a small island commonwealth whose founders took Aristotle's contrast between acquiring for a household's use and accumulating money for its own sake as a political program. I want you to work through how the central monetary institution would actually function, rather than judge whether it is just.

The institution is this. The commonwealth mints a single coin, and all taxes, wages, and local debts must be settled in it. But no one may hold more than a modest statutory sum of idle coin for longer than thirty days: anything beyond that must be spent, committed to an ongoing enterprise that employs people, or placed on deposit at the civic granary, where it earns no interest and can be withdrawn only for an approved productive use. Money loans at interest are unenforceable; the courts will not collect them, though they will enforce partnership contracts in which the lender shares in the losses as well as the profits. Any coin above the idle threshold remaining in an estate at death reverts to the treasury.

What economic behavior does this actually produce? Where will saving go once coin cannot be an idle store of value—into land, boats, livestock, art, foreign currency? What disguised-interest contracts and other evasions would appear first, and in what form? Does the partnership carve-out skew investment toward risky local ventures, ship voyages, or loans routed through foreigners? Does the ceiling on idle balances fall hardest on the poor, who cannot easily convert spare coin into productive property, or on the rentier class the founders meant to prevent? And does the boundary the founders wanted to draw, between money as an instrument of a well-run household and money as an end in itself, survive contact with a complex commercial society, or does competition erode it? Trace the second-order effects I have not named, and say whether any patch could preserve the scheme's aim without gutting what makes it distinctive.
