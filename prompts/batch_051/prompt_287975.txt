I'm writing a paper on the dispute over whether image-generation systems are genuinely creative, and the argument keeps stalling because "creative" is made to do too much work. I'd like you to give me a working definition of creativity — more precisely, a definition of what it is for a process or a product to be creative — precise enough to grade the following cases. The cases are a cloud formation that happens to resemble a whale; a landscape painter working from a photograph who makes small compositional changes; a mathematician who proves a new theorem by applying a standard technique that no one had thought to apply in that domain; a chess engine that plays a move strong enough to surprise its own developers; a text-to-image diffusion model that produces an image unlike any prior human work from a text prompt; and a child improvising a novel melody with no model in mind. I take the first to be clearly not creative and the last to be clearly creative; the middle four are where I am stuck.

My suspicion is that "creative" is doing at least two jobs at once — one about the novelty or value of the product considered on its own, and one about the kind of process or agent that produced it — and that the diffusion-model case is exactly where those two jobs come apart. If you agree that there are two jobs, give me both definitions, run all six cases through each one, say where each definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and mark which cases change their verdict when you move from one definition to the other. If you think a single definition can handle all six cases, run the cases through it and defend whatever verdicts an ordinary speaker would find surprising. Lead with your proposed definition or definitions in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
