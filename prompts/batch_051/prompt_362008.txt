Here are my gut reactions about vague predicates, and I want to know what they add up to. One grain of sand clearly isn't a heap, and a hundred thousand grains clearly is a heap. But for the middle-range piles I don't just think the cutoff is unknown to me: I think there's no fact of the matter about wether they are heaps. Yet if you show me one of those in-between piles and ask whether it is either a heap or not a heap, I want to say yes, obviously, and I can't see how to hold that together with the no-fact-of-the-matter thought. For a color chip halfway between red and orange, I'm confident that the claim that it's both red and orange is false, and that if it's red then it's not orange, even though I can't say it's red and can't say it's orange. I also think the tolerance principle — if n grains make a heap then n minus one grains make a heap — is false, but I can't point to a counterexample, and that bothers me. And borderline itself feels vague: I don't think there's a sharp line between the clear heaps and the borderline ones. Is there a theory of vagueness that captures all of this — epistemicism (a sharp but unknowable boundary), supervaluationism (borderline claims are neither true nor false, but excluded middle still holds), or some repair of one of them? Which of my reactions does it force me to drop, and what does it say about a case I haven't listed: a continuous condition like alcohol dependence that a clinic has to code as yes-or-no for insurance?
