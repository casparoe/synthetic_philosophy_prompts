You are a professional philosopher. I am trying to understand what a legal practice presupposes. A court invalidates a law, issues a long opinion giving legal reasons, and insists that its authority comes from the constitution and from the publicly checkable quality of its reasoning. But what actually compels the losing side is not the reasoning; it is the enforcement machinery behind it, since the state will seize property or imprison people to make the ruling effective. Officials deny that the court rules by force, yet force is always there to guarantee compliance. What theory of the relation between justice and force does this practice presuppose? Pascal had a deflationary suspicion about exactly this kind of self-description: he held, roughly, that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical; and because what justice requires is endlessly disputed while force is unmistakable, societies have not managed to put power behind the just—they have instead made it the case that the strong count as just. Reconstruct the implicit theory presupposed by this court-plus-enforcement practice. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with: must it accept that reasons can genuinely bind force, or is it also compatible with the Pascalian view that the official reasons are in large part a costume worn by force? Finally, explain what would have to change in the practice if its officials accepted the Pascalian account. Think carefully from the start, and try out alternative reconstructions before committing.
