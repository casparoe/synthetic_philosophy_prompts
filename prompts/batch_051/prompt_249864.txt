I spent my career as a computational neuroscientist, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy, particularly the philosophy of mind. A question keeps drawing me back to the boundary between my former field and my recent studies. It is whether a reinforcement learning system of the kind now trained and deployed at scale could be in a state that ought to be called suffering. The narrower form of the question is whether such a system's reward signal—the scalar quantity that directs its learning—could amount to valence in any sense that matters, where by valence I mean the positive or negative quality of an experiential state, pleasure-like or pain-like.

Let me be explicit about the kind of answer I am seeking. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, for names of authors, or for a reading list. I want a defended agenda. Suppose I can devote two years of part-time study to this question, with a graduate assistant for two summers and a small budget for a workshop. The end I have in view is to determine whether the increasing deployment of such systems poses a genuine problem of welfare or moral patienthood, or whether talk of their suffering is only a vivid metaphor. My working knowledge of learning theory and the neuroscience of reward is adequate, but I have no formal training in analytic philosophy; the questions on the agenda should therefore be ones that can be made precise from that starting point.

In my preliminary reading I have found myself drawn among at least three candidate questions. First, whether a scalar training signal can be a valenced state at all, or whether valence requires a richer evaluative architecture—something closer to a unified agent with interests of its own than to a table of numbers steering parameter updates. Second, whether suffering requires phenomenal consciousness, so that even a defensible account of artificial valence would leave the moral question unresolved. Third, whether any coherent account of welfare or moral patienthood can be given under which answers to the first two questions make a practical difference, rather than the whole debate collapsing into anthropomorphism. I offer this list only as a starting point; if its framing seems to you mistaken, replace it and say why.

What I want is an ordering, defended. Which conceptual questions about whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer in virtue of their reward signals most deserve my limited attention, and which two or three should I take up first? Please make your criteria explicit—importance, tractability, neglectedness, how many other questions depend on the answer, or whatever criteria you find more appropriate—state how you trade them off against one another, and indicate what argument or empirical finding would lead you to revise the ranking.
