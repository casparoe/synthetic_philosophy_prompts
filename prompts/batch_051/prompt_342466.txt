People in animal ethics keep saying insecticide use is the next big moral catastrophe: a single spray season can kill trillions of small invertebrates, and many of them act like they're in pain when poisoned—curling, grooming, escaping, learning to avoid the stuff—so the line is that spraying a crop is morally closer to a slow-motion massacre than to weeding. The other side says this is exactly the mistake: the behavior is just damage-triggered machinery with no subject of experience behind it, so pest control is protecting food from biological robots. Both sides talk as if the dispute is empirical, but I genuinely don't know what empirical result would move it.

Treat the claim that insecticides cause suffering on a massive scale as a testable hypothesis. Design a concrete study, or a small battery of them, that would distinguish real suffering from sophisticated unconscious nociception, without building in a definition of consciousness that your side already wins on. Make the measures speak to what spraying actually does—field-rate exposures, sublethal doses, time to death on treated foliage, or whatever you think matters—not just a generic insect pain test. Be specific: the organism, the setup, the dependent measure, the predicted pattern if insects suffer, and the predicted pattern if they don't. Then say what a clean positive result would and would not settle—does it settle that routine spraying is wrong, or does it just move the fight to aggregation, trade-offs, and how much insect welfare counts against cheap food? And flag the part of the claim that no experiment on the insects themsleves can reach.
