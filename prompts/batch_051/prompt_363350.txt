I'm a retired physiologist -- spent most of my career on insect olfaction and a bit of electrophysiology -- and I've been reading philosophy of mind for the last few years, so the insect sentience debate sits right at that intersection for me. The thing I keep turning over is the scale argument. As I understand it: even if the probability that any particular insect is sentient is quite low, the sheer number of insects (something like 10^18 at any given time) makes the expected number of sentient lives at stake enormous, so a low probability shouldn't be waved off. But that argument only bites if you accept the expected-value framing, and anyway the probability input is doing all the real work.

Here's the proposition I want your actual credence for: at least one order of insects outside Hymenoptera (that is, outside bees, ants, and wasps) contains species whose adult members are sentient, in the sense that there is something it is like to be them and at least some of their states feel better or worse to them. I don't want "it depends what you mean by sentience" or "we can't know" -- I want a number, and I want the reasoning decomposed. In particular, how much of your credence rides on (a) what sentience requires neuroanatomically, e.g. whether you need a centralized integrative structure or whether distributed processing can support it; (b) how strong the behavioral evidence is -- avoidance learning, motivational tradeoffs, analgesic effects -- as evidence of experience rather than unhurt reflexes; (c) broader phylogenetic or continuity considerations across invertebrates; and (d) the scale argument itself or other pragmatic pulls, which maybe shouldn't move the credence but often do. Give rough probabilities for the sub-questions where that makes sense, say how they combine into the final number, and tell me which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what one experimental result or observation would move your number the most.

Also, please seperate what's actually settled among people who work on the relevant science from what's genuinely contested philosophically -- don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus. And if you catch yourself being moved by the scale argument when the probability itself was never established, say so; I want the self-critical version.
