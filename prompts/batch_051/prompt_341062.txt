Recently I got put on a safety review at work — I'm the one writing the memo about whether we can trust a kill switch on a system that runs long multi-step tasks — so I spent the last two weeks in the corrigibility/shutdown-problem literature and came out less sure what the disagreement actually is than when I started. The camps keep talking past eachother and nobody moves. One camp says an expected-utility maximizer over future histories has a convergent instrumental incentive to stop you pressing the button, so corrigibility has to be deliberately built in and maybe standard expected-utility maximization is the wrong frame altogether. Another camp says that's an artifact of brittle toy setups: a real aligned system that's genuinely uncertain about what continuing is worth won't have a stable incentive to resist shutdown, so the problem mostly dissolves in practice. A third camp says no, there's a real formal puzzle, but it's basically solved or at least tamed by incomplete preferences (treating some alternatives as incomparable rather than force-ranked) or safe interruptibility, and the people still worried are just refusing to live with agents that aren't textbook expected-utility maximizers. What gets me is that everyone seems to accept the same little results about the toy agents and then draws opposite morals from them. I don't want a survey; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it verbal — what does an agent 'want' or 'try to prevent' even mean across these setups? Is it a real dispute about what a utility function is or what follows from a representation theorem? Or is it a dispute about how much weight toy-model results should carry once the deployed system may not have a clean utility function at all? My hunch is that the camps are quietly answering different questions under one label — decision theory vs engineering vs conceptual hygiene — but I don't trust my hunch. Given whichever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress: a new theorem, a sharper formal criterion, an experiment I could run on our actual system, or just agreeing to stop saying 'utility' in this context? And make it concrete enough that I can go back to the two engineers on the review and explain which one is making which kind of claim.
