A friend and I spent a long evening arguing over whether a given act counted as civil disobedience, and I came away realising that we had been using the term in importantly different ways without noticing. My friend wanted to restrict the label to open, non-violent, conscientious breaches of law that are intended to communicate opposition to a particular law or policy and whose agents accept the legal penalty. That struck me as too neat, because it seemed to exclude a number of cases I would unhesitatingly call civil disobedience while letting in almost nothing that is messy or covert. We never managed to settle the underlying question: is civil disobedience best understood partly by the agent’s communicative intention and willingness to suffer punishment, partly by the function such acts serve in a democracy, or partly by some other feature?

I would like a working definition of civil disobedience that sorts the following cases, which I have gathered since the argument: (a) a suffragette chains herself to railings, is arrested, and then goes on hunger strike in prison; (b) a shopkeeper quietly serves customers of all races in defiance of a segregation ordinance and tries to avoid detection; (c) a civil servant leaks classified documents that reveal an unlawful surveillance programme and then flees the country; (d) a hacker defaces a government website to protest against a data-retention law; (e) a motorist breaks the speed limit to get a badly injured passenger to hospital; (f) a crowd smashes shop windows and loots during a demonstration against police brutality; (g) a person breaks a law whose existence she did not know; (h) a prisoner refuses food to protest against conditions in the prison; (i) a large march deliberately blocks a road without a permit, the participants making no attempt to conceal themselves and expecting to be arrested; and (j) an official quietly refuses to carry out an order she believes unjust but does not publicise her refusal.

I am confident that (e), (f), and (g) are not civil disobedience, and confident that (a) is. I am genuinely undecided about (b), (c), (d), (h), (i), and (j), and my friend and I divide sharply over several of them. Please propose a definition, run it through all ten cases, and tell me where it delivers a surprising or counterintuitive verdict. If it misclassifies cases I have treated as clear, either revise the definition or make the case that my initial confidence was misplaced. I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in seeing a definition built under pressure from these cases.
