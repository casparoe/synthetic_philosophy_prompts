I spent my career as a physicist before I got seriously into philosophy a few years back, and I've been reading Otto Neurath lately. Like everyone, I first met him through the boat metaphor (we are always rebuilding the ship at sea, no dry dock), but the thing that has grabbed me is his ISOTYPE project: he wanted a picture language with standardized symbols arranged by explicit rules, so that a chart about housing or public health could be read by anyone regardless of native language. The whole point was to keep the slipperiness of words out, each symbol fixed and unambiguous and reusable.

Now I am typing this and the emoji keyboard is sitting at the bottom of my screen, and it looks like the world has grown itself a de facto picture language that Neurath never got to see. And its the opposite of what he designed. Emoji are standardized in one sense, Unicode keeps the code points stable, but wildly unstandardized in another: the same symbol renders differently on different platforms, and the meanings drift faster than any committee can track. The sob face means laughter, not grief; the skull means the speaker finds something so funny they died laughing; the eggplant means something else entirely. People drop these pictures into otherwise grammatical sentences and readers parse them fine, and nobody planned any of this.

So here is the question I've been turning over: what would Neurath actually have made of emoji? Would he treat them as a vindication of his bet that people want to communicate with pictures across language barriers, or as a nightmare case of exactly the ambiguity he spent decades trying to design out of picture language? Take his actual commitments seriously, his socialism, his faith in planned standardization, his worry about words carrying unwanted connotations, and reconstruct his likely verdict, including where he might be torn. And then say whether his ISOTYPE rules, if someone imposed them on emoji today, would fix the system or destroy the thing that makes it work.
