A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about frugality, and I came away unsure what I actually think. My friend kept insisting that the point of frugality is to promote an outcome—fewer resources wasted in the aggregate—and that my own thrift matters only as one instrument toward that outcome. I kept replying that frugality is a standard for how I use what is mine, not a license to manage other people's consumption. But my own responses to concrete cases pull in both directions, and I want to know whether there is one defensible principle behind them.

Take the role of a professional moral philosopher who has worked on the difference between deontological constraints on conduct and consequentialist promotion of states of affairs. Please begin with a short brainstorm of candidate principles, then work toward the best one.

Here are the verdicts I am most confident in.

1. If I replace a working phone just to have the newest model, I have been wasteful—not merely imprudent. It would bother me morally even if I could easily afford it.
2. If I buy a five-year-old used sedan rather than a new car I could comfortably afford, that is fine, even admirable.
3. A friend said she was about to replace her two-year-old laptop. I said she might not need to, and she kept it another year. I think I did something mildly good; I would not have pressed it if she had told me to leave her alone.
4. The small firm I partly own keeps its employees at the statutory minimum wage even though the business is profitable and could pay more without strain; the money simply sits in a reserve. I think this is wrong in a way that case 2 is not, even though both are instances of spending less than one could.
5. I taught my children to patch clothing, use their pencils down to the stub, and wait before replacing toys. I think that was good, perhaps required, even though I was deliberately shaping their consumption.
6. A municipal measure would have steeply taxed single-use packaging. I agreed it would reduce waste far more than all my personal thrift will. I still declined to support it, sensing—without being able to defend the sense in the moment—that it was the wrong kind of intervention.

My tentative principle is this: frugality is a virtue of self-governance in consumption, something one may practice and teach but not impose. Yet stated so simply, that principle seems to threaten verdicts 3 and 5, and it may just be restating my resistance in case 6.

What principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures these six verdicts? State it precisely, and say which clauses are motivated by which cases, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up or reinterpret. Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: the small nonprofit whose board I sit on can either run workshops teaching low-income families to repair, budget, and use things up, or give the same money as unrestricted cash and trust families to spend as they wish. I feel the workshops better express the value of frugality, but I also suspect that feeling is paternalistic. What does your principle predict about that choice? Finally, tell me whether the principle is more naturally classified as a deontological constraint on how I conduct myself or as a consequentialist strategy for increasing frugality in the world, and why that classification matters.
