I recently joined the board of a small land trust, and we have been stuck for months over whether to restore a drained wetland. The conservation-minded members argue that restoration would heal a degraded system, reconnect habitat, and support a diverse, self-sustaining community of organisms. The members who think in terms of wild animal welfare reply that a wetland that is thriving by conservation standards would mostly generate enormous numbers of r-selected invertebrates and amphibians whose typical lives are brief and end in starvation, desiccation, or predation; if individual welfare is what matters, it is far from obvious that adding millions of such lives is a moral gain, and leaving the site degraded might be better on net. The impasse confuses me because both sides accept the same demographic facts — high fecundity, negligible parental care, heavy early mortality — so gathering more biological data seems unlikely to move anyone. My working hypothesis is that the real fault line concerns whether biodiversity and ecosystem integrity are valuable in themselves or merely as proxies for aggregate welfare, but I suspect that is too simple. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress?
