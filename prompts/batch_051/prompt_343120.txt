I am preparing for a seminar on whether non-human processes—pathogens, climate shifts, volcanic eruptions—should count as historical actors, and before I read further I should like the specification problem settled properly. Please write the desiderata that an adequate theory of non-human historical agency must meet, where the purpose of the theory is to let us assign explanatory weight to such factors across decades and centuries without sliding into environmental determinism. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. The account must at least handle the Black Death, the Tambora eruption of 1815 and the cold summer of 1816, the Little Ice Age, and the introduction of Old World pathogens into the sixteenth-century Americas; but I am more interested in the constraints than in any particular narrative of these cases.

Three distinctions seem to me candidates for non-negotiable status, and I want them stress-tested rather than assumed. (1) Being a cause of a change, being an agent in it, and being the protagonist of a historical narrative are three different relations; a theory should say which, if any, it needs and whether an entity can occupy one role without the others. (2) A necessary background condition, a precipitating event, and an ongoing constraint do different explanatory work, and it is not acceptable for a theory to slide between them for convenience. (3) A factor can be a difference-maker in some counterfactual without being what the story is fundamentally about; the theory should explain why that distinction matters. I also suspect that 'climate', 'a pathogen', and 'a volcanic eruption' are not units of the same kind—a volcanic eruption is a dated event, a pathogen reproduces, a climate is a statistical aggregate—so the desiderata must either respect those differences or justify treating them under one framework. A further requirement the account must meet, rather than wave away, is the differential vulnerability problem: why the same climatic stress produced famine in one society and adaptation in another. If social organisation is doing the explanatory work there, the theory must say so plainly and limit the non-human factor's role accordingly.

Finally, for each desideratum say whether it is a hard constraint that any defensible view must meet or a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pairs of desiderata that cannot, so far as you can see, be jointly satisfied. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude popular accounts.
