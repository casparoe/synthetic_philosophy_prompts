I’m in a philosophy of population ethics course, and we’ve been reading Sidgwick’s Methods of Ethics. He argues that, strictly speaking, utilitarianism should maximize total happiness rather than average happiness, so increasing the number of people can be right even if average well-being drops a little, as long as the total goes up enough. That sort of move felt plausible in lecture, but I was arguing with my roommate about it later and noticed I don’t actually think this way in alot of ordinary cases.

Here’s the asymmetry that’s bugging me. If a couple is deciding whether to have another kid and they say something like: we know our existing two kids and we’ll be a bit worse off, but the new child’s life will be worth living, so total family happiness increases—almost nobody treats that as the end of the story. People think there is something off about summing happiness across family members and letting the new child’s positive welfare outweigh the others’ losses. But if you ask the same people whether a city of two million reasonably happy people is better than a city of one million where each person’s happiness is slightly higher, many will say yes, the two-million city is better overall because more people get to live decent lives. Similarly, conservationists often talk about a species being better off when its population is larger even if each animal’s conditions are a bit worse. So ordinary thought seems total-sum at the species or city level and average-sum at the family level.

Is there a principle that can justify this asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency we should give up? I want the strongest candidate principle you can find, tested against cases where the family grows large enough to look like a city and a city shrinks enough to look like a family. Should someone who accepts Sidgwick’s point keep, reverse, or revise one of these judgments? Also, please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead to a different verdict, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
