Someone in my city is trying to get a "compromise exchange" off the ground, and I can't decide if it's clever or a disaster. Here's the mechanism as he describes it. Residents file two things on a platform: an offer and an ask. An offer is a moral practice you currently follow—something you treat as required or forbidden—that you'd agree to change for a year. An ask is a practice you want some number of other residents to adopt or give up. You can set a threshold on your ask, and your offer only goes live once enough other offers satisfy it. So I could offer to go car-free for a year, conditional on at least thirty people giving up factory-farmed chicken for a year. The platform bundles offers and asks into cycles so that a whole set of commitments triggers at once, and every person in the cycle gets their condition met. Once you're in, your name is public, you post a deposit, and if you drop out without a hardship exemption you forfeit the deposit to a local charity and get flagged on your profile.

The pitch is that this creates gains from trade across moral systems: people who mostly care about climate can "purchase" a behavior change they want from neighbors who mostly care about animals, and pay for it with a concession of their own, without anyone having to agree about the underlying reasons or which side is right.

What would this actually do to the city's moral life over, say, five years? Who joins and who stays away? What do the offers and asks tend to look like, and what happens to the norms around them? What are the gaming and failure modes—people offering concessions they'd have made anyway, groups coordinating fake accounts to hit thresholds, asks used as outrage signaling, people treating the deposit as the price of bowing out, whatever else you see—and which of those failures, if any, can be fixed without turning the thing into something entirely different?
