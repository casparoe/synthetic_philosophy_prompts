I've been developing a distinction I call custodial inspiration, and I want an honest assessment. By oracular inspiration I mean what is claimed of the initial bearers of revelation—prophets or apostles—where God supplies the content in a direct way. By custodial inspiration I mean something else: that the careful translation, revision, and transmission of a revelation already given is itself guided by the Spirit, not by producing new words of God but by faithfully carrying existing words across languages, cultures, and textual corruption. On my view, the Septuagint did not need the legend that the seventy translators independently produced identical wording for it to function as scripture; the authority was in the custody of the text, not in a fresh oracle. Similarly, KJV-onlyism errs by upgrading custodial inspiration into oracular inspiration and fixing it to one textual product.

The 1611 translators themselves seem to me to occupy exactly the custodial position. In the preface The Translators to the Reader, they write: "Zeal to promote the common good, whether it be by devising any-thing ourselves, or revising that which hath been labour'd by others, deserveth certainly much respect and esteem, but yet findeth but cold entertainment in the world." That is a defense of revising and carrying forward, not a claim to have received a new revelation.

I want to know whether this distinction is coherent or whether I have just renamed authority and called it a mode of inspiration. Does the tradition already have this under a better name? Give me the three strongest objections to custodial inspiration as I've stated it, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
