I'm studying for a game theory midterm and spent the weekend going through a study guide someone posted on the course forum. It summarizes dominance reasoning like this: 'A rational player never uses a strategy if there's another strategy, pure or mixed, that does strictly better against every possible profile of strategies by the other players. So you start by crossing out all such dominated strategies; after some have been removed, strategies that weren't crossed out can become dominated relative to the smaller game, so you cross those out too, and you repeat until nothing more can be deleted. The strategies left standing are your prediction. This iterated elimination of dominated strategies gives you exactly the rationalizable strategies, and every surviving strategy can be part of some Nash equilibrium. Also, mixing doesn't really expand the method: if a mixed strategy strictly beats another strategy, then at least one pure strategy in the mix must already strictly beat it, so you can carry out the whole deletion using only pure strategies as dominators.'

My professor then went through an example where a row is not strictly beaten by either of the other two rows on its own, but is strictly beaten by a fifty-fifty mix of them, and said this is precisely why you have to be careful about the difference between pure and mixed dominators. I don't know enough too see how much of the study guide actually survives that example. Which parts of this summary would a careful game theorist accept, which would they wince at, and which are just wrong — in particular, does the professor's example refute the guide's claim that deletion can be done with pure strategies alone, and are the guide's claims about rationalizability and Nash equilibrium right?
