I've ended up convinced that weakness of will — akrasia, if you like — is nothing over and above hyperbolic discounting, and that the classic picture of knowing the better while doing the worse is an illusion you get from splicing time-slices together. On my view, the person at a distance prefers the larger later good; as the smaller sooner good gets close, its discounted present value jumps above it and they switch; then afterwards the horizon recedes and the old preference comes back, which shows up as regret. So there is no moment where a single agent judges A better than B and does B anyway — just a series of sincere preference reversals. Weakness of will is the shape of a discount curve, not a seperate phenomenon.

What would count against this? I want a genuine map of where the view is exposed, not a flattering one. Give me the observations, experimental results, and arguments that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Specifically: what would a convincing case of clear-eyed akrasia have to look like to be a real counterexample rather than something the view can redescribe? Which of the standard objections — akrasia without immediacy, the phenomenology of being overcome rather than switching preferences, people defeating their own precommitments — would a defender have to concede count against the view, and which would they dismiss as compatible? Does the position stick its neck out anywhere: what does it predict that rival accounts don't, or has it been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability? If it has, is that actually a problem for a view of this kind?
