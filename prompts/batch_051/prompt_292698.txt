In the United States and many similar credit systems, credit-reporting bureaus build files on virtually every adult without asking consent: they record loans, missed payments, addresses, bankruptcies, and increasingly rental and utility histories. They sell access to these files to lenders, landlords, insurers, and employers, and they keep the resulting credit scores as proprietary products whose formulas need not be disclosed. The individual described in the file is neither paid nor asked permission, but does have a legally enforceable right to see much of the file and to demand that inaccurate entries be corrected; the permitted uses of the accurate information are fixed by law, not chosen by the individual. What theory of information and ownership does this institution presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view of whether information about a person belongs to that person, to the entity that compiled it, or to no one at all, and of what a person is entitled to control about their data. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, give a committed answer: which underlying view best explains the system as a whole, and what in practice would have to change if the law instead treated facts and inferences about a person as that person's property? I want a definite position with reasons, not a menu of options.
