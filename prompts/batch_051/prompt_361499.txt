Consider the following argument about the crime of blackmail.

(1) If you may legally do X, then you may legally tell someone that you will do X.
(2) You may legally reveal a true, non-defamatory, embarrassing fact about another adult, provided no independent duty—contractual, fiduciary, or privacy-based—forbids the disclosure.
(3) So you may legally tell someone that you will reveal such a fact.
(4) You may legally ask another adult for money.
(5) If you may legally declare that you will do X, and you may legally request money, then you may legally declare, "Pay me, or else I will do X."
(6) Blackmail is just the declaration, "Pay me, or else I will reveal such a fact."
(7) Therefore, blackmail should not be a crime.

I suspect this argument is valid and that the real lesson is that blackmail statutes are inconsistent with the ordinary principle that we may bargain over lawful acts. But I am not sure the inference is as clean as it looks. Evaluate the argument step by step. Is it valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what interpretation of that step? If every step goes through, does that really force the conclusion that blackmail should be legal, or does it instead show that one of the paraphrase steps misdescribes what blackmail is?
