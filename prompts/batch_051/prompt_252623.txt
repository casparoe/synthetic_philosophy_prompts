A regulatory working group applying the precautionary principle proposes the following mechanism for technologies whose most severe plausible harms would be long-lasting and effectively irreversible—engineered organisms intended for open release, long-persistence nanomaterials, and comparable cases. Before a developer may commercially deploy or widely field-test such a technology, it must post a bond. The bond is not calibrated to expected damages, since those are exactly what is unknown; instead, an independent panel sets it according to the estimated option value of waiting—roughly, the value of preserving the choice to postpone deployment until better evidence about the harm profile arrives. The bond is held in escrow for a fixed review period. If the main uncertainty is resolved in the developer's favor during that period, the bond is returned with interest. If substantial uncertainty remains at the end of the period, the bond is forfeited to a fund for long-term monitoring and compensation. If harms attributable to the technology emerge during the review period, the forfeited amount goes first to victims.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave, rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. What would it reward and punish, and which kinds of projects or developers would be filtered out? How could it be gamed—through the estimate of the option value, the length of the review period, the definition of "effectively irreversible," or the standard for what counts as resolving uncertainty? What failure modes arise from concentrating the estimate in an independent panel, from the mismatch between a developer who captures the upside and dispersed potential victims who bear the tail risk, and from the moral hazard of a compensation fund that makes harms more tolerable? What second-order effects would plausibly appear in investment behavior, insurance markets, jurisdiction shopping, or the politics surrounding the panel? Which failure modes could be repaired by amendments such as tranching the bond, making it tradable, or exchanging a shorter review period for stronger post-market monitoring—and which of those repairs would create new failure modes of their own? Throughout, please separate what is fairly settled about precaution, irreversibility, and option value from what is genuinely contested among informed people, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
