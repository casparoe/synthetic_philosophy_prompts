So I'm a software engineer, and for a few years I worked on ranking algorithmns for a social feed, and this has been chewing at me. I recently read a summary of Kierkegaard's 1846 piece The Present Age, where he argues that the daily press produces something he calls the public — a kind of abstract, anonymous crowd that nobody in particular is accountable for. His argument, as I understood it, is that the medium itself does moral damage: it levels individual difference and turns people into spectators who have opinions about everything but commit to nothing. The point isn't just the content of the papers; it's the structure of a one-to-many press talking to a faceless mass.

But he was writing about newspapers: a few editors and writers on one side, a large readership on the other. Today we have social media, where the old gatekeeping is gone, everyone is simultaneously a publisher and part of the crowd, and the feed is tuned to maximize engagement by whatever keeps people scrolling. My question is whether his argument survives that shift. Which premise exactly does social media put pressure on — the anonymity of the audience, the one-to-many structure, the press as a recognizable profession, or something else — or does the change actually make his argument stronger? And if the original depends on assumptions that aren't true anymore, is there a repaired version that still says something real about why online discourse feels the way it does, or does the argument basically die once the press stops being a stable institution?
