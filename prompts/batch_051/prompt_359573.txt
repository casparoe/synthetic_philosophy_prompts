I retired a few years ago from a career in physical chemistry and have since been reading my way through moral philosophy; recently a friend pointed me to a sociological taxonomy that distinguishes honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures, and it has been bothering me ever since. As I understand it, an honor culture ties a person's standing to public reputation, so that slights demand face-to-face retaliation and appearing weak is itself a loss of standing. A dignity culture treats worth as intrinsic and inalienable, and expects wrongs to be handled by impartial institutions rather than private vengeance. A victimhood culture keeps dignity culture's reliance on third parties but borrows honor culture's acute sensitivity to slight in an inverted form: standing is claimed by being publicly recognized as harmed or oppressed, and the remedy is social or institutional pressure. I realize the taxonomy is usually applied to contemporary disputes, but I would like to test it against an old text: the Iliad.

Please read the quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon in Book 1, with its aftermath kept in view — Achilles' withdrawal, the embassy's offer of compensation in Book 9, the death of Patroclus, and Hector's decision to face Achilles outside the walls — through this lens. I want to know where the honor-culture frame genuinely illuminates the poem, and where the poem resists or complicates it. For example, Achilles' refusal of Agamemnon's gifts and his appeal to Zeus for the Greeks' defeat seem to be about something other than straightforward reputation-management; and the poem's treatment of Hector seems to give an internal view of honor's costs and obligations that the taxonomy may not capture. As someone trained to prefer the simplest model that fits the data, I am especially interested in whether the taxonomy is doing real explanatory work or just relabeling what the poem already says. And I would like an answer to the comparative question that first drew me in: does a careful reading of the Iliad support any claim that one of these three cultural moralities is superior, or better fitted to certain conditions, or does the poem show the comparative project to be misconceived? Please write this as a sustained piece of criticism, faithful both to the text and to the analytical categories, and be explicit about the places where the lens distorts the poem rather than illuminating it.
