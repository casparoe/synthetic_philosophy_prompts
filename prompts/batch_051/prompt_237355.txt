A colleague of mine who works on the history of political thought recently offered a general-audience summary of Kierkegaard's critique of the press, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. The summary runs as follows. Kierkegaard regarded the nineteenth-century daily press as the great enemy of genuine ethical individuality. On his view, the press manufactures what he called the public, an anonymous abstraction in which everyone has an opinion but no one acts and no one bears responsibility. By substituting endless reflection and second-hand chatter for the decisive commitment of the single individual, the press levels qualitative distinctions and turns people into spectators of their own age. My colleague then drew what seemed to me a suspiciously constructive lesson: Kierkegaard is best read as an early media critic whose remedy for these pathologies was a reformed press, one committed to plain facts, transparency, and accountability, so that citizens could receive the information they would need to take a personal stand. Please assess whether this is a fair summary. I would like you to separate what is faithful to Kierkegaard's actual diagnosis from what is simplified, modernized, or read into it, and to say whether the colleague's concluding prescription follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate. In your reply, please use continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and write in American English, as you would in an email or letter.
