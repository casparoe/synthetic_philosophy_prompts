I have come to hold the following view about rational belief and decision under uncertainty, and I want to know how exposed it really is.

The view. An ideally rational agent's evidence typically underdetermines her probabilities: for most propositions there is no uniquely correct real-valued credence. Rather than pretend otherwise, she should represent her opinions by a set of admissible probability functions — all those compatible with her evidence — and should choose between acts by the following permissibility rule: act A is permissible just in case there is no alternative B whose expected utility is at least as great as A's under every probability function in the set and strictly greater under at least one. I am drawn to this view because it refuses to manufacture precision the evidence does not supply, and because it lets me treat the familiar Ellsberg-type preference for a bet on an urn of known composition over an otherwise identical urn of unknown composition as rationally permissible without positing any separate taste for ambiguity. I also find it attractive as an account of severe uncertainty in matters such as a new technology's failure rate, where a single prior feels like overconfidence dressed up as knowledge. So the position is: belief states are credal sets, and practical rationality in the face of such sets is permissiveness governed by the dominance-style rule just stated.

What I want. Tell me what would count against this view, taking the case against it as seriously as you can. I want a map of its exposure, not a recitation of stock objections. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as a real cost, dismiss it as a misdescription, or absorb it by refining the view. Distinguish two things throughout: evidence that would, if it stood, show the view false or untenable, and evidence that merely makes it less attractive or harder to apply. I would also like an honest judgement at the end about whether the view, as it tends to be defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a normative view of this kind or an appropriate feature of it.

Start from the following considerations, all of which I have heard pressed, but go beyond them rather than only assessing my list. First, the dilation worry. I know a coin to be fair, so every admissible probability assigns one half to the proposition that it will land heads. I will be shown a light that is meant to report the outcome, but I know nothing about whether the light is reliable, anti-reliable, or unrelated to the coin. After conditioning each admissible probability on the light's announcing heads, the resulting set of posterior probabilities for the coin's having in fact landed heads runs, so the charge goes, across essentially the whole interval: an item of evidence has made my belief about a known-fair event dramatically less determinate, and I may now refuse bets I would have accepted before seeing the light. Is that a reductio of a specific commitment — the set model, the conditionalisation rule, or the claim that all evidence-compatible probability functions are admissible — or is it the correct response to a signal of wholly unknown reliability? Second, the guidance charge. The permissibility rule rules out only strict dominance, so in many pairwise choices it will leave both acts permissible; in exactly the choices where precision matters most, the view may be largely silent, and in sequential choices that silence may yield an incoherent-seeming sequence of choices each of which was permissible at the time. Third, the foundation charge. Precise expected utility can be derived from a clean set of axioms about preferences. The charge is that the credal-set view lacks anything comparably compelling, and that the particular permissibility rule is under-motivated relative to nearby rules — for instance, the rule that one should maximise worst-case expected utility, or the rule that an act is permissible whenever it is expected-utility-maximising under at least one admissible probability. Fourth, the arbitrariness charge. If the set is simply all probability functions compatible with my evidence, I get dilation and near-vacuous posteriors of the kind just described; if I narrow the set to escape that, I need a non-arbitrary principle for narrowing, and the view was supposed to be the doctrine that refuses arbitrary precision. Fifth, the re-description charge: that much of what the view says about choice can be mimicked by a precise expected-utility maximiser equipped with a suitably transformed utility function, so that the view has not eliminated ambiguity attitudes but only relocated them from the utility function into the credal set.

For each of these, and for anything more fundamental I have missed, tell me whether it threatens the whole position or only a dispensable detail, and what a sophisticated defender's best reply is. Pay attention to what the view would have to look like for these considerations to land: which conditional premises my position depends on, and what a decisive counterexample or theorem would have to establish. For example, would a result showing that the view is dynamically inconsistent in sequential choices under its own permissibility rule settle the matter, or can the defender simply say that permissibility is a claim about terminal choices and not about plans? Does the view make different predictions from precise expected utility plus ambiguity-averse tastes in any behaviourally observable way, and does it matter if it does not? Which cases or bodies of evidence does the view handle better than its precise rivals, since a view should earn its keep by predicting something its rivals cannot comfortably predict? And if the view can be rendered immune to every known objection by a suitably careful formulation, say clearly what the immunised formulation has to give up, and whether what remains is still the interesting position I described.

Finish with a single sentence beginning 'Verdict:' that states in one sentence how well the view survives the best case against it.
