I've been going in circles with my roommate about a weird pattern in how people read fiction (I later learned it has a name, imaginative resistance, but the label doesn't settle anything), and he layed out an argument that I can't quite dismantle, so I'm hoping you can stress-test it with me. Start with the observation that readers happily accept all sorts of impossible things in stories — a machine that reverses time, animals that talk, a country where the laws of physics only hold on Thursdays. Once the story stipulates it, nobody complains. But if a story stipulates certain moral things, people do complain. For instance, many readers refuse to accept a story's claim that a woman did the right thing by killing her baby, even when the narrator says so and offers an absurdly irrelevant reason for it. They insist it isn't really true in the story, or that the author can't just declare that.

My roommate's argument is this. (1) Readers routinely imagine things they believe are physically impossible when fiction asks them to. (2) So there is no general limit on what we can imagine; no proposition is off-limits just because it conflicts with what we presently believe about the world. (3) But readers systematically resist imagining certain moral claims that conflict with their own values — the baby case, or a village where torturing a cat for fun is morally praiseworthy. (4) Since (2) rules out a general limit on imagination, the only explanation left for (3) is a specifically moral limit: we cannot imagine certain moral falsehoods as true. (5) Therefore, some moral falsehoods are impossible in a strong sense — they cannot be made true even in a fiction, so moral truth has a special modal status that ordinary physical truth lacks.

I suspect (4) is doing something sneaky, maybe sliding from we won't to we can't, or ignoring boring explanations like genre conventions, but I can't make the objection stick. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise should I reject? If it isn't, which step fails exactly, and is the conclusion still reachable by a better argument? And what would the strongest alternative explanation of the resistance be that doesn't require giving moral truths a special modal status?
