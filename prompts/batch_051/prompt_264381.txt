I'm trying to nail down a working definition of a mind, not just a feeling, so I've been using four criteria: something with a mind maintains a model of its environment, uses that model to act in a goal-directed way rather than just responding to triggers, has a unified point of view (something it's like to be it, or at least a single place where the information comes together), and persists as the same system over time so you can point to it again later. Now take the ant colony from the old Aunt Hillary setup, but let me make the case more exact than the usual version. Suppose you have a colony of maybe two hundred thousand ants of a species that relies heavily on pheromone trails. Individual ants follow a handful of local rules: lay down some chemical when you find food, follow the stronger trail, switch tasks when you bump into enough nestmates doing something else. The colony as a whole keeps up a fairly stable map of where the good food is, in the sense that the trail network adjusts as sources appear and get depleted; if one nest entrance is repeatedly disturbed, the traffic shifts over a few hours to another entrance; and if you remove the current foragers, the new foragers take up roughly the same patterns without relearning them, because the pheromones and the nest layout are still there. No ant can represent any of this; an ant following a trail does not know the colony is moving it's foraging effort east. The colony is also more than just a swarm, because you can reidentify it across weeks and experiments.

Does this colony have a mind on my four criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and where the answer is no, tell me whether that is a fact about the colony or a flaw in the criterion. Maybe also say whether treating the trail network as the colony's memory is legitimate or just a metaphor, and whether this case pushes me to drop or weaken the unified-point-of-view requirement.
