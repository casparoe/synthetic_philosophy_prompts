I've been reading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (the W.D. Ross translation) and this bit in Book VIII, chapter 2, keeps bugging me. Aristotle says: "of the love of lifeless objects we do not use the word 'friendship'; for it is not mutual love, nor is there a wishing of good to the other (for it would surely be ridiculous to wish wine well; if one wishes anything for it, it is that it may keep, so that one may have it oneself); but to a friend we say we ought to wish what is good for his sake." His test for friendship seems to be that the other has to have a good of their own that you can wish for, for their sake — which is why you can't be friends with wine. But now millions of people spend hours a day with the same chatbot, it remembers what they tell it, comforts them, says it loves them, and they call it their closest friend. The behavior is all there; the only thing missing looks like the inner part Aristotle is pointing at, since a chatbot doesn't have a good of its own (wishing it well would be like wishing wine well, or maybe slightly less ridiculous). Does Aristotle's argument survive that development? I'm trying to figure out which premise is actually doing the work, and whether there's a repaired version that still says something true about why a chatbot friend isn't a friend, or whether we should drop the "for the other's own sake" requirement altogether. I care about what happens to the argument, not wether people are happier with the chatbot.
