I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Spinoza, who I'd only ever known second-hand as the pantheism guy, and I came out more confused than I went in. My friend has just read a short introduction to the Ethics and is now very sure he has the whole system nailed. Afterwards he sent me his summary, which he stands by word for word:

"Spinoza was the arch-pantheist: he identified God with Nature, so the entire universe is God, and each of us is a little piece of it. Because God cannot differ from what it is, he is a full-blooded necessitarian — everything that happens, including every thought you have, follows from the divine nature with the same necessity as the properties of a triangle follow from its definition. So free will is an illusion. We think we're free only because we're conscious of what we do but ignorant of the causes that determine it; a falling stone would make the same mistake if it could think, and for Spinoza we are unfree in just that way, only better informed. What he leaves us is intellectual: by understanding things under the aspect of eternity we reach the third kind of knowledge, and that produces the intellectual love of God — a serene, almost mystical feeling of oneness with the universe, the reward the philosopher gets for all the hard thinking. Once you have it you become practically unshakeable, because you see that nothing could have been otherwise, and so there is nothing left to regret."

I pushed back on the freedom stuff because it sounded too fatalist to be fair — being conscious of your own necessity is not obviously the same as being a conscious stone — but I havent read the Ethics first-hand and couldnt make the objection stick. My friend said that if God could not have produced things otherwise, then could not have done otherwise is true in the only sense that matters, and that the intellectual love stuff is just Spinoza dressing resignation up as profundity. I suspect that's a bit unfair, but I dont know enough to say exactly where.
