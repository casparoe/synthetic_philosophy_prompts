I am preparing a short, philosophically serious dialogue for a seminar on life-and-death decision-making, and I would like you to write it. The topic is whether the doing/allowing distinction—killing versus letting die—marks a moral difference in its own right, over and above whatever further differences it usually tracks, such as certainty of outcome, the expectations an agent has created, the psychological cost of intervening, and the wider social consequences of a policy.

The scene is a hospital clinical ethics committee that is reviewing a ventilator-triage rule. Under the proposed rule, during a severe shortage, a patient already receiving ventilation may not be disconnected in order to reallocate the machine to one or two newly arrived patients with much better prospects, but clinicians may permissibly decline to initiate ventilation for a newly arrived patient with poor prospects so that the last machine remains available for a better candidate. One character, an asymmetry defender, holds that this distinction can be morally sound: the doing/allowing difference is intrinsically weighty, and once the team has begun ventilation it has made itself the agent of the patient's continued survival in a way that changes what it would be doing if it stopped; withholding initial support, by contrast, leaves the disease as the cause of death. The other character, an assimilationist, holds that the distinction is inert in itself: once cases are matched for certainty, cost, created expectations, the patient's dependence on the agent, and the effects of a public rule, the historical difference between stopping and not starting is not itself a moral reason. Write the dialogue so that both characters are equally competent and resourceful, neither is a strawman, and they respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another. At some point they should try to construct a pair of cases that are identical in every factor except the doing/allowing difference, and argue about whether any residual moral difference remains. Let them press the hardest available cases on each other, and end without a forced consensus: each should state the one consideration from the other's side that most unsettles their own view.
