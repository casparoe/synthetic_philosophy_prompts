Construct a thought experiment in which the Ship of Theseus problem is transposed onto a public clock tower’s bell, and the two most influential answers — one that ties an artifact’s identity to the continuity of its original material, and one that ties it to the unbroken spatiotemporal continuity of the functioning object — pull in opposite directions. The twist to engineer is that the candidate made from the original material is inert and no longer performs the bell’s function, while the candidate that performs the function contains none of the original material, and yet both are recognizably presented to the public as “the same bell.” Constraints: the material replacement must occur through ordinary, piecemeal maintenance over a century; no one may intend, at the time any old part is removed, to preserve that part for a future reassembly; and the two candidates may not simply be two bells with the same shape — the functional candidate must have the same public role, sound, and location, while the material candidate must be assembled only from the portions removed during the replacement process, with no new material added. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, do the check: state the two candidate objects and the relation each bears to the original; articulate the material-composition criterion and the spatiotemporal-continuity criterion precisely; give the verdict each yields and explain why; identify the structural feature of the case that is doing the work; explain what would happen if that feature were removed; and finish with the strongest objection a defender of each criterion would raise against the other’s treatment of the case.
