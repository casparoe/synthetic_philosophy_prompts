Lately I've been going back and forth with a friend in an econ program who basically just repeats Friedman at me — the F-twist, that the realism of a model's assumptions doesn't matter, only its predictive accuracy. I want to test that against a bunch of verdicts I actually hold and see whether there's a coherent principle underneath, or whether I have to drop some of them.

Here are my verdicts. The ideal gas law treats molecules as non-interacting point particles; I'm completely fine with that, since it predicts pressure-volume-temperature behavior well for dilute gases. But using the same no-air-resistance model to predict how long a feather takes to fall in a normal room seems wrong — in a vacuum chamber it's fine, but in the room the thing you're ignoring is doing all the work. Friedman's billiard player, where the expert is assumed to shoot as if they solve the relevant physics equations, strikes me as acceptable for predicting the shot, even though the player is doing no such math, because practice and selection make the as-if true behaviorally. Yet common knowledge of rationality in game theory — everyone rational, everyone knows everyone rational, and so on — I don't trust at all for predicting actual play in one-shot games, even though it's also an as-if assumption. I think Ptolemaic epicycles were a bad model in a way the ideal gas law isn't, even though adding enough epicycles made them fit the observed planetary positions quite well. The Modigliani-Miller theorem assumes away taxes, bankruptcy costs, transaction costs, and differences in borrowing rates, and I find it deeply useful as a benchmark for which frictions matter, but I'd never use it to predict an actual firm's debt-equity ratio. And modeling a cow as a sphere for radiative heat loss? I actually don't think that one's a joke; it's a fine order-of-magnitude move.

So my tentative principle is roughly Friedman's: a false assumption is fine as long as the model predicts accurately enough for the purpose at hand. But that seems to license epicycles and common knowledge of rationality if they happened to fit the data, and I want to resist that. Is there a better principle that captures most of my verdicts? If not, which ones should I overturn, and why? And what would your principle say about a case I haven't listed: a market model that assumes all agents have identical preferences and information, used to predict the direction of price changes after a supply shock? Please also tell me where you're least confident — what kind of counterexample or argument would most likely change your mind about the principle you end up defending.
