I was at a dinner thing last week where people got onto whether history has a direction, and one guy kept falling back on 'history repeats itself.' Every time someone brought up long-run trends—literacy rates, declining violence, the spread of democracy, whatever—he'd wave it off with the line, as if saying that cyclical recurrences just settle that there's no real arrow, only ups and downs. I pushed back and he told me I was being pedantic, but I think he was smuggling in a much stronger claim than the proverb supports. I've heard it do other jobs too: once in a politics class someone used it to argue a current crisis would play out just like one from two centuries ago, and another time to justify a grand theory about the inevitable rise and fall of great powers.

Lay out the different precise claims 'history repeats itself' could be making, from the nearly trivially true to the wild. For each, say whether it's something pretty much everyone could accept, something substantive and actually defensible, or something probably false. Then say which reading or readings would actually support the use my dinner-table guy was making of it. It would help if you tied some of the readings to the older cyclical thinkers—Polybius and his cycle of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun on the rise and fall of dynasties, Spengler and Toynbee on civilizational life-cycles—so I can get a sense of where the slogan is gesturing at a real theory and where its just noise.
