Consider the Ellsberg three-color urn: ninety balls, thirty red, and sixty balls each of which is either black or yellow, with the number of black balls, b, known only to lie between 0 and 60. A single ball will be drawn, and a bet pays $90 if the named color, or one of the two named colors, is drawn, and $0 otherwise. Conditional on a given composition b, the expected values in dollars are as follows: red, 30; black, b; red-or-yellow, 90 − b; black-or-yellow, 60.

Now add one change to the standard case: the agent is permitted to randomize. For any choice, she may submit a chancy device that ends up placing one of the available bets according to probabilities she selects. I want you to study this variant with respect to the two classic pairs of bets, first red versus black and then red-or-yellow versus black-or-yellow, under each of three rules: maximin, which chooses the act with the greatest minimum payoff over the possible values of b; minimax regret, which chooses the act that minimizes the maximum shortfall between its payoff and the best payoff achievable at that value of b; and a Harsanyi-style Bayesian rule, which assigns a uniform prior to b on [0, 60] and maximizes expected utility.

For each rule, state exactly which act or mixture it recommends, and say whether mixing improves on what the rule could achieve with pure acts. Then say where the three rules agree and where they come apart, and determine whether any of them reproduces the characteristic Ellsberg pattern of preferring the known-probability bet in each pair. Pay particular attention to whether the divergence between maximin and minimax regret is created, or merely revealed, by the availability of mixtures.

Close with a philosophical assessment. Your calculations should let you say something precise about the following question: does the difference between maximin and minimax regret show that decision under ignorance is not one problem but two, one about guaranteeing a floor and one about limiting hindsight regret, and what does that imply for the familiar contrast between Rawls's maximin reasoning behind the veil of ignorance and the Harsanyi-style view that a rational chooser should assign equal probabilities and maximize expected utility? Be careful not to overstate the analogy; identify both its point and its limits.
