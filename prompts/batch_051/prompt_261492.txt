I sit on the Automated Decision Systems Review Board of a mid-sized city. Our public housing authority has used a vendor's optimization engine for two years to schedule and prioritize maintenance and repair work across roughly 9,000 units. The contract describes the engine's objective as maximizing the expected remaining service life of the housing stock per dollar spent, and the vendor states that the engine also searches over a constrained space of repair bundles, contractor schedules, and deferral options. Over the past year the system has produced a pattern that surprised even its designers: it repeatedly defers a class of apparently cheap, high-impact repairs — plumbing leaks in occupied units — while aggressively scheduling expensive exterior projects in mostly vacant buildings. The vendor maintains that the behavior is fully consistent with the approved objective and constraints, because leaks interact with other hazards in non-obvious ways, vacant buildings lie on a different failure curve, and the search process finds global solutions that look wrong to a human reviewer who can only inspect cases one at a time.

The board is split. Two members want us to reject renewal unless the vendor discloses the exact objective function and the formal search space, on the grounds that the observed behavior radically underdetermines both what is being optimized and over which options: without the specification, the vendor can always retrofit an explanation, and no amount of output auditing will let us distinguish an approved objective with a cramped search space from a different objective altogether. Two other members want us to approve renewal on the grounds that behavior is the only evidence we ever have about any optimizing system — elected officials' decisions included — and that demanding the literal objective would either be theater, since vendors can write down whatever objective they like, or a recipe for gaming, since residents and contractors would learn exactly how decisions are scored. The chair has asked me to draft the board's decision memo. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; give the committee criteria it can apply to the next contract; and treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously enough that its proponents can recognize their own position in the reasons even as they are outvoted.
