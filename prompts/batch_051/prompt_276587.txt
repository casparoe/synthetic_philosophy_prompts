I have been trying to make sense of two verdicts about self-examination that pull in opposite directions, and I would like a careful comparison of them. On one side there is an old and still influential view, traceable to the Socratic praise of the examined life, that turning inward and interrogating one's own motives makes a life better: self-knowledge is supposed to make choice more deliberate, to expose self-serving rationalisations, and to loosen the grip of unacknowledged habit. On the other side, a substantial strand of clinical psychology treats self-focused thought as a frequent engine of suffering. People who brood on their mood, replay their failures, and repeatedly ask why they are the way they are tend to become more depressed, to remember and interpret events more negatively, and to solve practical problems less well. Two distinctions cut across this debate and make it harder to settle. The first is between rumination, understood as passive and repetitive dwelling on negative content, and reflective self-examination, understood as exploratory, specific, and directed at comprehension or change. The second is between an abstract, evaluative style of self-questioning — why am I this way, why did this happen to me — and a concrete, process-focused style — how did I get from that remark to this mood, and what could I do differently next time.

Compare these two broad pictures of self-examination: the philosophical or therapeutic defence of it and the clinical suspicion of it. Is the apparent conflict merely verbal, so that self-examination means something different in each picture? Is it a genuine disagreement about which forms of self-focused thought help and which harm? Or is the most defensible position a conditional one, according to which self-examination is beneficial only when it takes certain forms, under certain conditions, or for certain people? Be as precise as you can about what has to be true for self-examination to become harmful rather than illuminating, and about which features of the act, the topic, or the person make the difference.
