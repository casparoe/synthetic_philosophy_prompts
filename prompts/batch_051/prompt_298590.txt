I'm trying to get clear on what a theory of preference-inference even has to do before I read more papers that all seem to talk past eachother. By preference-inference I mean the project of getting from observed behavior to what somebody "really" wants or values: the thing economists gesture at with revealed preference, cognitive scientists gesture at with rational analysis, and the inverse-reinforcement-learning crowd is trying to automate. My actual use-case is knowing which of these could ground a system that learns human prefrences by watching us rather than by being told.

Write the desiderata. What cases does any adequate theory have to handle — the smoker who says he wants to quit and keeps smoking, someone who'd undo a choice the second she realizes what it caused, somebody acting on a false belief, somebody being polite or strategic rather than directly going for what they want? What distinctions can't it collapse — want vs. constraint, what you'd choose vs. what's good for you, stable vs. transient preferences, what the behavior reveals about ends vs. about beliefs? And what formal awkwardness does it have to avoid or explain away — like the fact that zero reward for everything makes every policy optimal, so observed behavior alone is radically ambiguous? Give the case or reason behind each requirement, mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just a camp's taste, and tell me which two or three requirements are the real killers that most candidates die on. Then the part I actually want pinned down: is any pair of them jointly unsatisfiable in full generality, especially unique recovery plus robustness to ordinary human irrationality?
