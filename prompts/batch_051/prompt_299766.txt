You are a professional philosopher of historiography, with a specialist's grasp of debates about presentism and anachronism. Work through the following problem carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate distinctions before settling on the strongest one.

The discipline of history contains a striking asymmetry in its attitude toward anachronism. If a historian writes that a twelfth-century legal code was unjust, or that an eighteenth-century medical practice was barbaric, colleagues will usually object that this is presentism: the historian is judging the past by the standards of the present. But many of the same colleagues will unhesitatingly write that the medieval economy suffered inflation, that gender structured inheritance in early modern families, or that a community experienced collective trauma after a massacre—even when the historical actors themselves had no such concepts, could not have described their situation in those terms, and might well have rejected the descriptions. The discipline seems to treat evaluative anachronism as a methodological vice while treating conceptual or explanatory anachronism as unavoidable, legitimate, or even necessary for historical understanding.

Your task is to determine whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is an inconsistency historians have learned to live with. Proceed as follows:

1. Brainstorm the candidate principles that might distinguish the two kinds of anachronism—for example, differences between judging and explaining, between actors' categories and analysts' categories, or between thick evaluative concepts and causally informative ones.
2. Identify the strongest of these principles and formulate it as precisely as you can.
3. Test it against nearby cases, including at least: a historian who explains a peasant revolt as class conflict even though the participants framed it as a religious uprising; a historian who calls a past institution unjust by the standards of its own victims rather than the standards of our own time; and a historian who applies a contemporary category such as mental illness or sexual orientation to a person who would have understood themselves through categories such as sin, possession, or demonic temptation.
4. Give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept as it stands, revised, or given up? If it cannot be drawn, what follows for the possibility of non-presentist history—must the historian adopt only the categories of the people studied, and is that even a coherent methodological demand?

Do not produce a reading list or references; support your answer entirely by argument.
