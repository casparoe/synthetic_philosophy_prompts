I have been trying to work out what I actually think about duties of rescue by recording my verdicts on concrete cases before I let any theory tell me what I should think. My present hunch is roughly this: I am required to bear modest costs to avert serious harm, and perhaps required to bear quite large costs when the harm is immediate, face to face and not of my making, but I am not required to take on burdens that would restructure my life, such as giving up an organ or most of my savings. I expect the cases below will put pressure on that hunch, and I would rather find the hunch repaired than defended.

Here are my verdicts, reported as honestly as I can. (1) There is a toddler drowning in a shallow pond on my way to work; I could save him by wading in, ruining a good pair of shoes and missing a meeting that matters to my career. Walking past would, I think, be seriously wrong, not merely ungenerous. (2) A charity shows me a photograph and the name of a particular child whose village lacks access to clean water, and tells me that for the price of the same pair of shoes I could protect that child and several others from a waterborne disease this year. I do not donate. I feel guilty about it, but I do not think I have done something as wrong as walking past the pond; in fact I am unsure I have done anything wrong at all, and I cannot defend the asymmetry. (3) If a friend of a friend needs a bed for one night because of a flood warning, and I have a spare room, I think refusing would be wrong, even though it costs me only mild awkwardness. But if an old schoolmate asks me to guarantee a bank loan of £40,000 to stop his eviction, I think refusing is clearly permissible, even though the harm is grave and he is standing in front of me asking. (4) I am told with certainty that a child will drown next week unless a broken fence along a canal is repaired now; the repair costs no more than the shoes. I think failing to prevent that death would be far less wrong than walking past the pond, and I suspect the fact that the danger is not yet present should not matter, but I want to know whether that verdict of mine is defensible or a quirk to be abandoned. (5) If several of us are standing at the pond and no one moves, I think each of us acts wrongly, and 'someone else might do it' is no excuse. Yet when I walk past someone sleeping rough on a cold night among many other passers-by, I do not think each passer-by who does nothing has acted wrongly, though the harm is ongoing and the cost of helping in the moment is low. (6) I would not be required to sell my flat, move to a cheaper one, and give the difference to a charity that prevents childhood deaths, even though this would very likely save lives; and I would not be required to donate a kidney to a stranger. But I think I would be required to phone the fire brigade when my neighbour's house is burning, even if doing so risked nothing more than being delayed. There seems to be a line between emergency rescue and general beneficence, and another line between my money and my body, and I need someone to draw those lines or tell me that no stable line exists.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State it precisely, and say which case or cases motivate each clause of it. Then tell me what that principle says about a case I have not been able to settle: whether I would be required to take a considerably more dangerous but much better-paid job in order to donate the extra earnings to a charity that prevents childhood deaths, as against staying in a safe job and giving only what I already give. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and why; if one or two of my verdicts have to go, say which ones and what it costs to give them up. If my initial hunch is roughly serviceable, repair it; if it is not, replace it. Please answer in British English.
