Consider two entrenched legal texts:

1. The First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution: “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.”

2. Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights: “Everyone has the right to freedom of expression. This right shall include freedom to hold opinions and to receive and impart information and ideas without interference by public authority and regardless of frontiers.” Article 10(2) then adds that the exercise of these freedoms “may be subject to such formalities, conditions, restrictions or penalties as are prescribed by law and are necessary in a democratic society… for the protection of the reputation or rights of others…”

Suppose a European state criminally punishes a speaker under a hate-speech statute for publishing a video that attacks a religious group in crude and dehumanizing terms, and the European Court of Human Rights upholds the conviction as necessary to protect the targeted believers. On the same facts, a U.S. court applying the First Amendment would very likely treat the punishment as unconstitutional. Do these two texts embody a deep conflict of principle about the point of free expression, or can they be reconciled without turning either text into a rhetorical front? If they can be reconciled, what exact distinction does the reconciliation depend on, and is that a distinction the drafters could honestly have intended? If they cannot be reconciled, which approach should a third country adopt and why?

Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Take a definite position and defend it rather than merely surveying possible readings.
