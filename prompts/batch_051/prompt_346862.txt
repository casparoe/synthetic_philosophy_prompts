Ok so this might sound like a weird thing to still be chewing on, and a bit embarrasing to still not have settled, but a couple years ago I went on a trip with friends from different countries and we got into a long argument I keep coming back to. I'm not an academic, just someone who thinks about this stuff to much. The argument started over tipping. I'm British, and when I was in the US my American friend insisted that once you're in the States you basically owe the 20 percent tip in a sit-down restaurant, because the server's actual wage is built around it and if you don't tip you're not making a principled point, you're just taking money out of a worker's pocket. My German friend said that can't be right, or at least not for a tourist: expecting a visitor to prop up a broken wage system with his own money is asking too much, and anyway it's a local custom, not a moral rule. Then we moved on to other stuff. In Germany my friends do wait at completely empty crossings, partly because kids watch adults to learn how to cross, and my German friend got annoyed when I crossed anyway. In Britain, where I'm from, queueing is a whole thing: pushing in front of someone in a queue feels genuinely offensive to people, but is it immoral or just rude? And a friend once got visibly hurt because I kept my shoes on in his apartment, which he thought was disrespectful, though I genuinely didn't know. So now I'm stuck on the general question: how much are everyday conventions like these actually moral obligations, especially for a short-term visitor who didn't grow up with them?

I'd like you to act as an expert on everyday ethics and social norms. Please rank the following conventions by how morally obligated a short-term visitor is to follow them, where the visitor is someone staying maybe two weeks, not moving there. Give each one a score from 0 to 10 (0 means no moral obligation at all, just a preference locals have; 10 means a visitor does something genuinely wrong by not following it) and a sentence or two explaining the score. Then say whether any of them change if we're talking about a local instead of a visitor. Here's the list: tipping 20 percent in American sit-down restaurants; waiting at pedestrian lights in Germany; queueing properly in Britain, meaning not cutting and not leaving gaps that confuse people; removing your shoes in a home where the host's family always does; standing on the right and walking on the left on escalators and station platforms; not eating on public transit in cities where that's the norm; haggling in a market where haggling is expected, versus staying quiet where haggling would be seen as rude.

Before you give the scores, do a quick brainstorm of the criteria that should push a convention toward being a real moral obligation or away from it. Things like whether breaking it actually harms or misleads someone, whether someone's income depends on it, whether locals reasonably rely on it, whether it exists to protect a third party like kids, whether the mere fact that this is how locals do it carries any moral weight on its own, and whether the cost to the visitor matters. Then apply the criteria consistently so the ranking isn't just vibes. If you think an item splits into important sub-cases, say that and score the sub-cases instead of giving one fake number. I'm not looking for a survey or a bibliography, just a careful defended ranking. Take your time and think it through step by step.
