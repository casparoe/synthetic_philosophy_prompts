I work as a software engineer and run a small reading group whose members have no formal philosophical training. We have been trying to work out whether the human mind is modular, and we keep talking past one another because "module" seems to mean several different things: sometimes an informationally encapsulated subsystem with a narrow interface, sometimes merely a specialised component, sometimes something fast, automatic and unavailable to conscious inspection. In my own work, a module is an encapsulated unit that communicates only through a defined interface, and I do not want to carry that software meaning over into cognitive science unthinkingly; but I also do not want to treat every use of "module" as if it were the same claim. I would like a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on the modularity of mind on the basis of what they believe about concrete cases, not on the basis of which label they like.

Please write this decision tree as a flowchart-style classification instrument for a technically literate audience with no philosophy background. Aim for roughly ten to fourteen branch nodes and at least five leaves: Fodor-style modest modularity (only peripheral input and output systems are modular, while central belief fixation and reasoning are not); massive modularity (central cognition is itself decomposable into many specialised modules); a general-purpose or no-modularity position; a dual-process hybrid (fast, automatic processes are modular but higher reasoning is a distinct non-modular system); and a developmental position (modular structure is built during learning and is not pre-specified innately). If your tree cannot find a leaf for any of these, say which one and why.

Build the tree from the logical space first, not from the list of names. Work out the core commitments and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the actual positions in that space. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, mark it and say in one sentence whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Keep the architectural sense of "module" (informationally encapsulated, mandatory, implemented in fixed neural hardware) separate from the functional sense (specialised for a task or a domain), so that a reader can agree that some process is specialised while disagreeing that it is encapsulated without the tree silently conflating the two.

Phrase every branch as a concrete question or forced choice that an intelligent non-specialist can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Questions of this sort would be appropriate: whether a well-informed adult can choose to see a visual illusion as it really is; whether an expert solving a problem can draw on anything they know from any domain of life; whether a fast, automatic response that uses arbitrary background information should still count as modular; whether natural selection could have produced a single general-purpose reasoning mechanism or would have had to produce many special-purpose ones; and whether an acquired skill built up through practice can count as a module or only an innate system can. You may replace these examples, but every node should be answerable from experience or ordinary reasoning rather than from knowledge of the literature.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment, the consequence its defenders usually find hardest to accept. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say where the residual difference lies. Keep the whole instrument self-contained: no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
