I recently joined the risk–benefit committee of a public health agency, and our first contested file has turned the old dust-specks-versus-torture problem — whether sufficiently many barely noticeable harms distributed across many people can outweigh one severe harm to a single person — into a matter of institutional bookkeeping. We evaluate interventions, such as screening programs, environmental controls, and mass notifications, that distribute very small costs across very large populations, and the committee must decide whether to adopt a formal ledger rule for counting those costs.

The proposal on the table has three clauses. First, any individual harm that falls below an explicit perceptual threshold — operationalized as a change smaller than one just-noticeable difference on a standardized annoyance or discomfort scale, or a loss of time below what a subject can reliably report — is entered in the social-cost ledger as zero, and remains zero no matter how many people receive it. Second, the zeroing clause lapses for any individual who accrues more than k sub-threshold harms within a rolling 24-hour period; when the kth harm arrives, all of that individual's sub-threshold harms during the period are retroactively entered into the ledger, with an additional multiplier to offset their having been invisible to the initial accounting. Third, harms above the threshold are counted normally, and a sub-threshold harm that later produces a detectable consequence is reclassified as a harm to the person who suffers that consequence.

I want you to treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Walk me through how it would actually behave if implemented. What would it lead the agency's own analysts to do when they model interventions, and how would it change which interventions reach the committee? How would proponents of an intervention package their evidence and framing once they understand the rule? Where is the first gaming likely to appear — for instance, in slicing one detectable harm into many sub-threshold harms, in the choice of scale used to set the threshold, or in timing exposures to stay under the rolling cap? Is the retrospective clause administratively workable, given that someone must establish which sub-threshold events occurred, how many there were, and who caused them? Finally, tell me whether the ledger rule would in practice preserve the intuition that tiny harms do not add up across persons, or whether it would quietly produce the opposite of that intuition — and which clause would be the first to be abandoned under political or legal pressure.
