I've been toying with a version of the raven paradox that seems to land somewhere worse than the usual one, and I'd like you to tell me exactly wich step, if any, fails. The argument as I've been running it goes like this. (1) A generalization of the form "All F are G" is confirmed by observing a positive instance, meaning an observed thing that is both F and G. (2) If an observation confirms a hypothesis, it also confirms any hypothesis logically equivalent to it. (3) "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." (4) A green apple is non-black and non-raven, so by (1) observing one confirms "All non-black things are non-ravens." (5) By (2), then, observing that green apple confirms "All ravens are black." (6) Now repeat the same moves with blue: "All ravens are blue" is equivalent to "All non-blue things are non-ravens," and the green apple is non-blue and non-raven, so by (1) it confirms the latter, and by (2) it confirms "All ravens are blue." (7) So the same observation confirms both "All ravens are black" and "All ravens are blue," which can't both be true if there are any ravens. I keep going back and forth between thinking step (2) is the culprit and thinking the whole thing is sound and I should just get used to "confirms" being a much thinner relation then I wanted it to be. So: which numbered step fails, and what's the most plausible repair? Or is the argument actually fine, and if so, what does that tell me about confirmation?
