In my upper-level aesthetics seminar this term we keep circling a question about aesthetic testimony — whether you can legitimately form or justify an aesthetic judgment on the basis of what someone else tells you, like trusting a friend's rave about a film enough to say you know it's good, or deferring to a critic's verdict on an album. Last week I thought we were making real progress, and then one of the sharper students shut the whole thing down with 'well, there's no accounting for taste,' which got a roomful of nods and moved us on. I'm enough of a philosopher to know that one-liner is doing real work there — it's being used to close off the idea that aesthetic testimony could ever justify a judgment — but I'm not sure which of several quite different claims it's smuggling in, and I'd rather not just wince at it next time. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that 'there's no accounting for taste' might be used to express: a bare pyschological claim that people in fact diverge in their tastes, a no-wrong-answers claim, a claim that aesthetic judgment requires firsthand acquaintance, an autonomy or authenticity claim, a relativist or non-cognitivist thesis, plus whichever others you can pull out of it. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive-but-defensible claim, or just false. Then say which reading or readings would actually support the anti-testimony conclusion the student wanted, which ones are being made to look like they support it while not quite getting him there, and which reading he probably had in mind when he said it. And if you have a view on which reading I should push back on first, I'd like that too.
