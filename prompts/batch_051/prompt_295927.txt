I keep hearing people describe competitive debate and the newer "steelmanning" meetups as a kind of sport, and I want someone to actually adjudicate a specific case for me instead of giving me a lecture on what a sport "really" is. But I also don't trust my own criteria, so check those too. Here are four features I've been treating as jointly making something a sport:

(1) There's a rule-governed competition with a winner determined by the application of scoring rules to performances, not just by who persuades the most people in the room on the night.
(2) The scoring purports to track a skill that can be improved by training, so a higher score is meant to indicate better performance of the activity's internal goal—whether or not the scoring actually succeeds at tracking that.
(3) The activity is adverserial in the strong sense that competitors can make each other's performances worse by performing well themselves, rather than just performing alongside one another like parallel gymnasts.
(4) The community can intelligibly say someone won while performing badly, which requires the scoring and the craft to be distinct enough that a bad call or an upset is possible.

Now the case. A league assigns each two-person team one side of a live policy question a week in advance, and the assignment doesn't care what they actually believe. Teams do a written exchange, then a live session in front of a three-judge panel and a small audience. The rubric is the odd part: points are given for raising the strongest objection to your own view and answering it, for accurately restating the other side's argument before attacking it, for retracting a subclaim once it's been shown shaky, and for specifying in advance what evidence would have changed your mind. Points are taken off for topic-switching, for turning an opponent's concession into a victory lap, and for "winning on delivery." The audience is visible and can react, but judges are instructed to ignore applause. The judges don't need training in logic, and in practice the panel is mostly not philosophically trained; they sometimes reward confidence and fluency over care.

Walk me through the four criteria and tell me, one by one, whether the league qualifies, fails, or whether the criterion just underdetermines an answer. Then tell me whether the criteria themselves need changing and which one is doing the least work. And then answer the thing I actually care about: if we did call this a sport in something other than a limp sense, would that make the arguementation better or worse, and for whom? I worry, for instance, that the retract-a-subpoint rule would teach people to perform a kind of fake, strategic uncertainty. As part of your answer, say how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the one point you'd be least willing to defend if someone pressed you on it.
