A city council is considering a pair of linked rules designed to keep housing costs and low wages from drifting apart. Under the proposal, the allowed annual rent increase for any sitting residential tenant is capped at the percentage growth in the city's median gross hourly wage over the previous twelve months; rents can never be required to fall, so if the median wage is flat or falling the allowed increase is zero. At the same time, the city's minimum wage is automatically set each January to exactly forty percent of that same median gross hourly wage. Both figures are produced by the city's statistical office, updated on a fixed annual schedule, and not subject to council discretion. Proponents describe the linkage as a fairness device: the floor under wages and the ceiling over rent increases are anchored to the same index, so neither renters nor low-wage workers can quietly fall behind.

For the purposes of the scheme, the median wage is computed across all city residents working at least one hour a week, including part-time workers, and includes tips and bonuses. I want the proposal analyzed as a mechanism, not a moral position: how it would actually behave once landlords, employers, and other parties notice the incentives it creates.

Answer in exactly six numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding material. Cover, in order: (1) the effect on landlords' maintenance, turnover, and exit decisions; (2) the effect on employers' hiring, hours, and payment arrangements in low-wage sectors; (3) the feedback loop between the two rules that operates through the median wage itself; (4) the main short-run winners and losers; (5) the first places the scheme would be gamed or arbitraged; and (6) the most consequential long-run failure and whether it can be fixed without abandoning the linkage.
