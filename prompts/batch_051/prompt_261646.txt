I'm a PhD student in technology law, and I keep turning over a parallel that I'm not sure is actually there. I've been reading the AI-safety literature on "tool AI" versus "agent AI" at the same time as some older common-law material on agency, and the two conversations sound oddly alike. On the AI-safety side, a tool AI just computes answers or does a narrowly specified job without objectives of its own, whereas an agent AI has goals and pursues them by planning and acting on the world. On the legal side, a principal is bound by the authorised acts of an agent who acts on the principal's behalf with some discretion; an employer is generally not vicariously liable for an independent contractor; and a mere instrument or tool is not an actor at all, so the human using it is the one responsible. Both sides seem to be asking the same question: when does a delegated system stop being something you use and start being a doer in its own right?

But I suspect this is where it come apart. Legal agency looks like it turns on authority, control, and whose intentions count, whereas AI-safety agency turns on internal goal-directedness and capability, regardless of what anyone authorised. So I want to know whether there's anything deep here or whether "agent" is just doing two different jobs. If the parallel were genuine, I'd expect the hard cases to line up in a useful way: apparent authority and non-delegable duties on the legal side, and scaffolded LLMs that write and execute their own plans or tool AIs with hidden objective functions on the AI side. What would have to be true of both sides for something like vicarious liability to transfer cleanly to agent AIs, and where does the mapping first break? I'd love to know whether the legal categories are a real resource for thinking about tool vs agent AI, or just a source of false comfort. Oh, and please answer in British English.
