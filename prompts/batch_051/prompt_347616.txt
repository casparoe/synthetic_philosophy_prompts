Take Putnam's Twin Earth argument. Two people could be internally identical, down to the same brain states, and still mean different things by the word "water," because one was raised around H2O and the other around a superficially identical liquid we can call XYZ. The moral of the story is that what a speaker means by a word is not fixed by what is in their head; something about their environment does part of the fixing. Now here's the new wrinkle. Suppose we run the same large language model, with the same weights, in two worlds. In both worlds the system has never seen, touched, or tasted water; it learned the word entirely from text. The only difference is that in one world the human texts it trained on were about H2O, and in the other they were about XYZ. Internally the two copies are identical, and they will produce the same answers to the same questions. Do they mean different things by "water"? If they do, is that still Putnam's argument, or a different argument wearing its clothes — one where "the environment" has quietly become "the corpus," and the causal contact with H2O and XYZ is something only the authors of the training texts ever had? Does Putnam's own idea of the division of linguistic labor already cover this, or does treating a language model as part of that community stretch the notion of a linguistic community past its breaking point? And if they don't mean different things, does that show the original argument secretly depended on each speaker's own causal contact with the referent? What I want to know is which premise actually takes the hit, and whether there's a repaired version that keeps the externalist conclusion without pretending that nothing changed.
