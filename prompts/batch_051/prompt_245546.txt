I hold a position about the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I want you to argue against it as effectively as possible while mapping exactly where the view is exposed. The position is the conjunction of three claims. First, the boundary claim: there is no non-arbitrary line between restoring a person to species-typical functioning and improving a person beyond it. The notion of species-typical functioning is itself partly statistical and partly evaluative; it marks a central tendency or an ideal rather than a natural kind with discoverable edges. Second, the normativity claim: even where the distinction can be drawn clearly, as with a malfunctioning organ on one side and an optional upgrade on the other, it does not by itself track what is permissible, what is obligatory, what insurance should cover, or what medicine should offer. In particular, classifying something as treatment rather than enhancement does not settle whether it answers to a need or merely to a preference. Third, the institutional claim: most of the work the distinction appears to do is delegated to it by institutions of rationing, professional authority, and a contested picture of what a human being is for. Its apparent moral significance is largely an artifact of those arrangements, not a foundation for them.

Now tell me what would count against this view. I want a ranked map of its exposure: which findings, cases, or arguments should most unsettle me, from those that would force wholesale abandonment through those that would require only local qualification, down to those that merely complicate the rhetoric. For each defeater, say which of the three claims it targets and whether it counts against the conjunction or only against one component in isolation. Do not limit yourself to the obvious attacks; include the strongest versions of what a defender of the distinction would say, even if you ultimately reject them.

In particular, I want an honest treatment of at least the following candidate defeaters: an account of biological function or disease that gives the distinction a non-arbitrary basis; evidence that judgments about treatment versus enhancement are stable across patients, clinicians, and cultures in ways that suggest the line is doing real descriptive work; a demonstration that abandoning the normativity claim would leave us unable to condemn coercive or eugenic deployments of enhancement, or that it collapses into the much stronger and less plausible claim that no distinction can ever do normative work; and a story about how an admittedly fuzzy but useful distinction could earn its keep in concrete allocation decisions, which would undercut the institutional claim. Where possible, test the view against cases rather than staying at the level of definition: cochlear implantation, stimulant use without a diagnosis, hormone treatment for age-related decline, or selecting embryos for traits unrelated to disease.

For each candidate, I want to know whether I, as a committed holder of this position, should concede it as a genuine cost, and how large the cost is. Finally, assess the charge that the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — that its three claims jointly rule out any possible counterexample by reinterpretation — and tell me whether, for a view of this kind, that would be a defect or a feature. I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that shows the distinction bearing real weight in a concrete decision both sides could inspect, rather than a purely theoretical redescription that leaves practice untouched.
