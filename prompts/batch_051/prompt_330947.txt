I have been turning over Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor chapter, in particular the Inquisitor's claim that most people experience freedom as a burden and would trade much of it away for guaranteed bread, entertainment, and relief from the weight of deciding their own lives. I want to turn this from a story into a concrete mechanism and ask you to work out how the mechanism would actually behave.

Suppose a private association—call it the Haven—offers a lifetime membership contract on the following terms. Any adult judged competent may join entirely of their own volition. On joining, a member surrenders four decision rights for as long as they remain: (1) choice of occupation and residence; (2) choice of spouse, and of whether and how many children to have; (3) the right to speak at general assemblies or to criticize the association's leadership in public; and (4) the right to hold or dispose of more than a small personal allowance. In exchange, the association guarantees every member lifetime food, housing, medical care, modest entertainment, and an assigned job it deems useful; it also provides regular access to a trained class of comforters whose task is to relieve guilt, doubt, and the anxiety of having chosen wrongly. Children born to members become members at adulthood unless, during a one-year window at age eighteen, they explicitly opt out and leave. Members may leave only at the end of a seven-year term; a member who leaves departs with personal effects alone and receives no pension, savings, or property. The association is governed by a separate class of Stewards, who are not themselves under the membership contract. New Stewards are chosen every two years by competitive examination, from both members and outsiders, and are re-confirmed by other Stewards rather than by the membership.

Treat the Haven as a mechanism rather than a moral proposal. If it began operating tomorrow on the outskirts of a developed country, who would join, and who would select out? What would the examination and self-perpetuating re-confirmation turn the Stewards into over time? Once members cannot complain or leave except on a seven-year clock, what happens to the quality of what the association supplies? What happens to the second generation, raised inside the contract? Where is the system gamed first, by members, by Stewards, and by firms or governments outside? Which of these tendencies could be fixed by adjusting the membership terms, and which are inseparable from the very idea of trading freedom for security? And after fifty years, does the experiment refute the Inquisitor's claim, confirm it, or change the subject?
