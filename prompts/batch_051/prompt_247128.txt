I have no training in archaeology or philosophy, but I keep coming back to a version of the claim that the shift from hunting and gathering to farming was a wrong turn rather than an obvious improvement. As I understand it, the argument runs something like this. Foragers in many places lived relatively well: they worked fewer hours, ate a wider range of foods, and moved around enough to avoid the crowding that breeds disease. Farming, by contrast, meant long and repetitive labor, a diet built around a few staple crops, more frequent infections, shorter and less healthy lives, and, because grain could be stored and controlled, the rise of durable inequality between the few who held the surplus and the many who produced it. Once populations grew and people depended on the fields, the argument continues, there was no going back, so the whole arrangement was a trap that most individuals had no reason to enter willingly. I find this story gripping, but I also notice that its claims about the past are usually inferred from general assumptions about how food, labor, and population fit together.

Recently I have been reading about how archaeology has changed. It used to be hard to say much about how a particular skeleton actually lived; now researchers can estimate workload from wear on bones and joints, reconstruct diet and nutrition from chemical isotopes in teeth and bone, read signs of malnutrition and disease from disruptions in growth, and infer inequality from variation in house sizes and burial treatment. This seems like a genuinely new ability to test the argument's premises directly, rather than accepting them as plausible inferences.

So I want to know whether the argument that agriculture was a mistake survives this development. Does it still go through in its original form, does it need repair, for example by separating its empirical claims from its evaluative ones, or does it collapse once its factual premises can be checked? I am less interested in a survey of what archaeologists currently believe than in what happens to the argument once it becomes answerable to direct evidence.
