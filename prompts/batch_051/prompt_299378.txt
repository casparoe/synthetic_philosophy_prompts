I have recently taken up a post in the secretariat of a select committee reviewing the framework for central bank independence, and at every session someone reaches for the phrase Ulysses contract to characterise the 1997 settlement, when the incoming government made the Bank of England operationally independent. I know the broad facts: the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee was given day-to-day control of interest rates in order to meet an inflation target set by the Chancellor; the arrangement rests on ordinary statute, so a future Parliament could reverse it; and the standard justification is that elected governments face a time-inconsistency problem, perpetually tempted to loosen before an election or to spring an unexpected inflation, and so must tie their own hands in advance. But the more I hear the analogy invoked, the less clear I am about how far it genuinely runs.

I would like you to analyse the 1997 settlement through the lens of precommitment and time-inconsistency, in both directions, rather than give me the history. Map the analogy carefully: who or what is the Ulysses figure, what exactly are the ropes, who or what are the Sirens, and how should the payoffs before and after independence be described? Then press on the points where the mapping strains. Is this really a case of an agent binding itself, or is it closer to strategic delegation to officials the government still appoints and whose remit the Chancellor reissues each year? Does it matter that the commitment remains legally reversible by a later Parliament, and if so, what is doing the actual binding? Can a precommitment analysis even register the democratic objection to placing a distributive instrument in the hands of unelected officials, and if it cannot, what are the accountability mechanisms doing instead? Say plainly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Finally, tell me what the episode does to the theory: whether 1997 should count as a vindication of precommitment as an account of institutional design, an anomaly to be explained away, or a case that shows the Ulysses analogy was doing more rhetorical than explanatory work, and whether some other model captures the remainder better.
