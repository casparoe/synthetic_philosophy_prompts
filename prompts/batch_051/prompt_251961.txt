I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading about decision theory on the side because I'm implementing a preference-elicitation module for a planning tool. I've started treating money-pump arguments as a kind of regression test suite for the preference model: if the structure the system learns can be pumped, I want to know exactly which failure mode that corresponds to. So far I've collected three families of pumps, and I'm trying to determine whether there is a fourth that is structurally different rather than a variation on one I already have.

The three I have are these. (1) The strict-cycle pump: an agent with strict preferences A > B > C > A, who currently holds C, will pay a small fee to swap C for B, then B for A, then A for C, ending up back at C with less money. (2) The intransitive-indifference pump: an agent who is indifferent between A and B and between B and C, but strictly prefers A to C, can be led from A to B to C for small compensations and then charged a real fee to get back to A. (3) The hyperbolic-discounting pump: from a distance, a hyperbolic discounter prefers a sufficiently larger later reward to a smaller sooner one, but once the sooner date arrives the preference reverses; a trader can therefore charge a fee to swap the smaller-sooner reward for the larger-later one while both are still future, and then charge a second fee to swap back once the sooner reward becomes immediate, leaving the agent with the original dated reward minus both fees.

Is there a fourth money pump that is genuinely different in mechanism — not a rewording, special case, or combination of these three? If there is, give the setup, the sequence of choices, the axiom of rational choice it exploits, and how it differs from the three above. If you think these three already exhaust the structurally distinct cases, say that instead and explain what a genuinely new pump would have to exploit that none of them does. Throughout, please keep settled results separate from live disputes among people who work on this: say which parts of the argument are uncontroversial and which depend on contested assumptions, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
