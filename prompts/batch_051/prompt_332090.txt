I am a high school student who has been reading about free will on my own, and I keep getting stuck on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows: for you to be ultimately responsible for an action, you would have to be ultimately responsible for the mental states from which the action flowed; but to be responsible for those states you would have to be responsible for the earlier states that produced them, and so on backward without end. Since no one chooses their own starting point, no one could be ultimately responsible for anything, and blame or praise in the basic desert-entailing sense would be impossible.

Yet my own verdicts on cases do not seem to obey that conclusion.

(a) A sober, unhurried driver chooses to read a text while driving and injures a pedestrian. I judge that he deserves real blame for that choice.

(b) A person raised in a violent household who was never taught any impulse control performs the same act. I judge the act equally bad, but I cannot say he deserves the same blame.

(c) A person who had a decent childhood repeatedly indulges his temper over many years, knowing what he is doing, and eventually hurts someone in anger. I blame him more than the driver in (a): his cruelty feels self-built.

(d) A kind, patient person develops a brain lesion that disables impulse control and then lashes out. I do not blame him at all.

The difficulty is that the ground I draw between (b) and (c) is not itself a ground that (c) ultimately chose: he did not choose his raw temperament, his early influences, or the causes of his earliest acts of self-indulgence. The Basic Argument seems to say that the difference between (b) and (c) is just another causal fact about their backgrounds, not a difference in what either ultimately deserves.

Is there a coherent principle about when blame is deserved that captures my verdicts in (a) through (d)? If not, which of my four verdicts must I give up, and what simpler principle replaces them? Please state the principle precisely, identify which case fixes each clause of it, apply it to further cases (for instance, someone who tries to reform and fails, or someone whose inborn temperament makes self-control unusually easy), and say honestly which of my four verdicts it cannot accommodate.
