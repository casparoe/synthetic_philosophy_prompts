i'm a phd student in philosophy of private law, poking at boilerplate and consent for a dissertation chapter on what "agreement" is supposed to mean. i've got a set of gut verdicts about when fine print actually binds someone, and i want you to either find the principle behind them or tell me which ones i have to give up. here are the cases, as concretely as i can make them:

1. streaming service signup: i tick "I have read and agree to the terms" without opening them, and the terms are the usual stuff — arbitration clause, no resale, no scraping. verdict: fine.

2. parking garage hands me a ticket stub with "terms on reverse" in tiny print, including a disclaimer of liability for items stolen from the car. verdict: eh, fine — a garage disclaiming theft liability is kind of what you expect.

3. mid-90s software, license printed inside the shrinkwrap, says breaking the seal means you accept its no-reverse-engineering and no-resale terms. verdict: fine, everyone understood that was the deal.

4. photo hosting site, same kind of checkbox as case 1, but clause 47 grants them a perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free license to use my photos in advertisements. i clicked through without reading. verdict: not fine. this is the one that keeps me up: it's identical to case 1 in every way except the content of the unread term.

5. hospital admission form with an arbitration clause and a "you agree to pay any uncovered charges" term, which you sign on the way into the ER. you're not in pain yet, but you're definitely not reading boilerplate. verdict: not fine.

6. credit card issuer mails an amendment adding arbitration, with a real 30-day opt-out by phone. i threw it in the recycling because it looked like junk mail. verdict: fine — the opt-out was actual, i had my chance.

7. baseball ticket, back says you assume the risk of injury from foul balls. verdict: fine.

8. app says "we've updated our terms" behind a banner with a single "continue" button, and continued use counts as acceptance. the change moves disputes from court to arbitration with a class-action waiver, and you have to open the terms yourself to see that. verdict: not fine.

my tentative principle is: a term becomes part of the deal only if it isn't so surprising or buried that a reasonable person in my situation wouldn't expect it, and i had a fair opportunity to learn about it before being bound. but i'm not sure that gets case 4 without pretending i actually read clause 47, and i'm not sure it doesn't also nuke case 1.
