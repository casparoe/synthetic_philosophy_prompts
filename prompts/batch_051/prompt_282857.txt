I'm doing a module on the ethics of autonomous weapons, and the tutor gave us a working definition of meaningful human control that we're supposed to stress-test. On this definition, a use of force has meaningful human control only if three things all hold: (a) a human who can be held responsible understands the specific situation well enough at the moment the system acts; (b) a human has enough time and information to veto or redirect what the system is about to do; and (c) the system's behaviour is predictable enough to that human that, if the system does something wrong, it isn't surprising. We have to apply the definition to cases, and I keep going back and forth on one.

The case is a naval close-in weapon system, the sort of automated radar-guided gun that ships use as a last-ditch defence against anti-ship missiles. The captain switches it to automatic mode as the ship enters a high-risk area, and from then on it will engage anything matching a missile-like speed and radar profile on its own, because the flight times are too short for a person to approve each shot. The officers set the engagement zone and rules of engagement in advance, and they can switch the whole system off, but there is no meaningful per-shot approval. It also has a known failure mode: under certain clutter conditions it can mistake small fast civilian boats or even friendly aircraft for missiles, and the crew know this but accept the risk because the alternative is being hit. Does this count as meaningful human control under (a)-(c)? Go condition by condition, and be honest about where the conditions don't settle the answer. Then say whether the case shows that (a), (b), or (c) needs revising, and what the revised definition would say about a fully autonomous loitering munition whose human operator selects only the area and the time window, never the individual target.

You are a philosopher of technology who works on military AI and the responsibility gap. Think carefully, and don't just pattern-match to the usual verdict that this lacks meaningful human control. Present your answer as a numbered list with exactly five items: (1) condition (a), (2) condition (b), (3) condition (c), (4) the overall verdict on the case and where the conditions underdetermine it, (5) which condition should be revised and what the revision predicts for the loitering munition case. Each item should be a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
