Most legal systems make it dramatically harder to become an adoptive parent than a biological one. To adopt, a person must typically pass home studies, income and criminal-background checks, interviews, and sometimes mandatory preparation courses, and the placement can be refused on the strength of an official's all-things-considered judgment about the applicant's future fitness. To have a child biologically, the same person must meet no ex ante standard at all; the state becomes involved only after the child is born, and then usually only once abuse or neglect has been demonstrated. The same society, then, treats a child's prospects as weighty enough to justify intrusive prospective screening of one class of parents but not weighty enough to justify even minimal prospective screening of another class: child protection is organized ex ante in the adoptive case and ex post in the biological case.

I want to know what theory of the parent–child relationship this pattern of practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that biological parents have a pre-institutional entitlement to raise the children they produce, while adoptive parenthood is a state-created status that the state may condition as it likes? Or is the same pattern equally compatible with a welfare-based view that would in principle screen all prospective parents and screens only adopters because the state already controls adoptive placement, so that the asymmetry tracks feasibility and causal position rather than any view about rights? Where does the child's interest in an open future fit here—the idea that a child's fundamental interest is to reach adulthood with as wide a range of significant life options as possible still intact? If that interest genuinely justifies vetting adopters, what does its silence in the biological case commit the system to? I would like a reconstruction that separates what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identifies any tension between its ex ante and ex post halves, and says what would have to change, both legally and in the underlying theory, if the operative principle were that every child's open future counts equally.
