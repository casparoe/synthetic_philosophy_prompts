I keep coming back to a passage in Schrödinger's What Is Life? (1944) — the part where he characterises the chromosome structures as somehow containing the entire pattern of the organism's future development, written in a sort of code-script, and says they are at once law-code and executive power, or something to that effect. I'm paraphrasing from memory, so don't rely on the exact wording, but that's the gist. He wasn't quoting anyone, as far as I know, but people have run with that idea ever since. It seems to have become the founding text for the whole DNA-is-a-code analogy that everyone now uses without thinking. But I'm starting to suspect the analogy is doing more work then it should.

So my question is: when people say DNA is a code, how far does that analogy actually hold? I want an audit, not just a list of disanalogies. What exactly maps onto what — what is the encoded message, who or what is the sender, who is the receiver, what is the substrate, what is the decoding machinery, and where does error sit in the picture? Then tell me the places where it breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the work the analogy is being asked to do. In particular, Schrödinger's passage seems to smuggle in a kind of preformationism where the whole future organism is already written down in the germ cell. Is that a fair reading of him? And if so, does modern molecular biology support it, or is that exactly the part of the analogy that has to go? I want the bottom line first — how literal should I be about the word code here — and then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
