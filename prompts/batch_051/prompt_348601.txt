I've been writing a seminar paper on the color phi phenomenon — the illusion in which two differently colored dots flashed in quick succession at different positions are reported as a single dot moving continuously and changing color partway along its path — and I keep constructing an argument whose conclusion I find unacceptable. In the literature, the two standard ways of handling the phenomenon are sometimes called Orwellian and Stalinesque. On the Orwellian story, the subject first has an experience that does not include the color change, and this experience is then silently overwritten once the brain has processed the second dot's color; the subject ends up sincerely describing an experience she never had. On the Stalinesque story, the construction of conscious experience is delayed until after the second flash, so the subject really does have the moving-dot experience, but only on a tape delay; the apparent now of experience lags behind the events themselves.

The argument I keep falling into runs as follows. (1) In a standard color phi setup, attentive subjects give stable, sincere reports of a single dot moving continuously and changing color roughly halfway along the trajectory. (2) The best explanation of such a report is that there is a determinately timed conscious experience with just that content, occurring at some definite point relative to the flashes and to the subject's neural processing. (3) The content of that experience includes the color change, which depends on the second dot's color; so the experience could not have been causally complete before the second flash was processed. (4) Therefore the experience must be located after the second flash, either as an Orwellian rewrite of an earlier, phenomenally different experience or as a Stalinesque delayed first presentation. (5) But if one of these two options must be correct here, then there is no introspectable difference between having an experience at one time and having a memory silently revised to seem that way, and no introspectable difference between being conscious of the present and being conscious of a slightly delayed world; the subject's sense of when her own experiences occur is itself one more construction, and provides no evidence about the actual timing of experience. (6) Yet both experimental psychology and ordinary life depend on our reports of temporal order within experience being generally trustworthy; if those reports are systematically ungrounded artifacts, the whole evidence base collapses. (7) Since that collapse is unacceptable, we should reject (1): the color phi report cannot be taken at face value, and the phenomenon is an artifact rather than a window on experience.

I find myself unwilling to reject either (1) or (6), and I suspect the trouble lies somewhere between (2) and (5), but I cannot identify the false step or state the position that rejecting it would leave me in. Where does this go wrong, if it does? Which premise should be given up, and what does giving it up cost?
