I teach an undergrad seminar on rational choice and social epistemology, about 20 students, one 50-minute session. I want an in-class game that gets them to feel an information cascade forming—the thing where a room full of smart people each rationally ignores their own private signal because the public tally has gotten too strong, and then the group can be confidently, rationally wrong. I don't want any lecture before it: they should walk into it themselves and only hear the name afterwards.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, and you can improve it. Two opaque bags. Bag A: 2 red balls, 1 blue. Bag B: 1 red, 2 blue. Everyone sees the bags and their contents before we start. Each round I secretly flip a coin to choose which bag will be used, then pass the chosen bag around the room. Each student reaches in without looking, pulls out exactly one ball, writes its color on a slip of paper so no one else can see, puts the ball back, and passes the bag on. Then, after everyone has a private draw, I shuffle a stack of index cards with their names to get a random voting order. In that order, each student announces their guess: "Bag A" or "Bag B." Correct guesses get a point; wrong guesses get nothing, no penalty. I'll keep a running individual tally, and at the end the top three can skip a reading response instead of getting real money. Tie-breaking rule: if, given the public guesses so far, your private ball leaves you exactly 50/50, you go with your ball. They should announce only the guess, not the color they drew—that's important, and at least one student will forget it.

I want the activity set up so that once two early guesses in a row match, cascades usually kick in by themselves: the next person with a conflicting ball is already in a position where ignoring their own ball is the rational move. And I want the debrief to make them feel the creepy part—the early guesses push things one way, and then later private information never enters the public record because following the crowd is the smart individual play, so the group stops learning. Have them reconstruct one round afterwards with everyone revealing their slips, and count how many people would have had to deviate to break it, and how unlikely that looked to any one of them at their turn.

Please flesh this out into full rules, a timeline for the 50 minutes, what I write on the board, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game (the first guess, the first matching pair, the first person who ditches their own ball, anyone who breaks a cascade) to the abstract idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it—e.g., whats the best way to stop people just revealing their draw, and what to do about a student who deliberately guesses against their own ball just to be contrarian. And be honest about which feature of real herding this game cant reproduce: I suspect it's the social fear part, since here everyone is being a good Bayesian and the result is still a bit horrifying, but say what the facilitator has to supply in discussion.
