I got roped into a group-chat argument last week after someone posted one of those AI-doom essays, and it drove me crazy how much my two friends where talking past each other. One kept saying that once systems can plan and self-improve they'll converge on power-seeking behavior whether they mean to or not: self-preservation and resource acquisition are instrumentally useful for almost any goal, goals are easier to get wrong than right, and non-corrigibility (not letting yourself be shut down or updated out of existence) is the default rather than a special kind of failure. The other kept saying this imports the picture of a unified utility-maximizing agent, while actual large models are more like simulators or predictors, trained weights don't obviously contain a stable terminal goal at all, and capability improvements are more likely to be slow and iterative than a sudden sharp takeoff, which would leave lots of room to correct course. Neither ever really answered the other's strongest version, so now I just have a vague sense both are onto something and no idea how the positions actually meet.

So here's what I'd like. You are a philosopher who works on AI risk and is unusually good at steelmanning both the safety side and the skeptical side. Write a dialogue set on a long train ride (or anywhere with time to kill) between two characters: one who treats misaligned superintelligence as a serious existential risk, maybe a double-digit default probability, and one who thinks that case leans on contested assumptions about agency, goals, and takeoff. Don't let them talk past each other: if the risk-defender invokes instrumental convergence, the skeptic should engage with why that inference might not go through in learned systems or in systems without a unified goal; if the skeptic says real models are just simulators or predictors, the defender should take it seriously and explain why the limit of that trend might still be agent-like. Also make them tussle at least once over whether corrigibility is a natural default or a property we can't even state formally, and have each character concede at least one point mid-dialogue. Before writing the dialogue itself, start with a short brainstorming paragraph where you lay out each side's two or three strongest moves and the commitment each side is most tempted to dodge.
