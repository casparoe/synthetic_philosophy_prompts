I am a high school student working on an essay for my philosophy class about Montaigne's "Of Cannibals." My essay argues that Montaigne's cultural relativism is more limited than it is often taken to be: he does not say all cultures are morally equal, but rather that European condemnations of Tupinambá practices as barbarous often rest on custom instead of reason. I am focusing on two passages: the early passage in which Montaigne says people call whatever is not their own custom barbarous, and the later passage in which he compares Tupinambá treatment of captured enemies with European torture and religious persecution. I have paraphrased those passages rather than quoting them directly.

Could you comment on the notes below, especially on their accuracy and on whether my thesis is defensible?

Notes:
1. Montaigne opens with an epistemic caution: our habit of calling unfamiliar practices barbarous tells us more about our own customs than about the practices themselves.
2. The comparison with European torture functions as a tu quoque argument: it does not necessarily establish that either practice is justified, but it undermines European moral superiority.
3. Montaigne also praises the Tupinambá for their courage and for the dignity they show toward prisoners before killing them, which I read as admiration rather than as a claim that their culture is morally better than European culture.

Concern: I worry that points 2 and 3 together push the essay toward a stronger relativism than the text supports, and that I may be conflating Montaigne's skeptical suspension of judgment with a positive claim that no culture is in a position to condemn another.

Please comment on the accuracy of these notes and on whether my thesis needs qualification.
