Exam question (10 points total). Explain what it is to treat moral uncertainty—uncertainty about which moral theory is correct—as a decision problem, and describe the maximizing expected choiceworthiness (MEC) strategy. Give a concrete example with two moral theories and two actions, and show how MEC would handle it. Then raise and evaluate one serious objection to MEC (for example, the problem of intertheoretic comparisons, the regress or circularity problem, or the charge that MEC is just another moral theory in disguise), and say whether you think the objection is fatal.

Student's answer. Moral uncertainty is what we have when we are not certain which moral theory is right. The standard response is to maximize expected choiceworthiness. You list the available actions, ask how choiceworthy each action is according to each theory in which you have credence, weight those choiceworthiness scores by your credences, and pick the action with the highest sum. For example, suppose I have 60% credence in act utilitarianism and 40% credence in a deontological theory. Donating £100 to a malaria charity is very choiceworthy on utilitarianism, while on the deontological theory donating is merely permissible. Since utilitarianism says donating is clearly better than not donating and deontology says donating is at least permitted, the weighted sum favors donating over not donating, so I should donate. The leading objection is that different theories use different units of choiceworthiness, so adding their scores is arbitrary. But this is no more mysterious than comparing different goods in ordinary practical reasoning; we make such trade-offs all the time, so the objection fails. A deeper objection is that MEC is itself a moral theory, so applying it to reach a conclusion is circular. The standard reply is to assign MEC its own credence and include it in the calculation alongside the first-order theories; running the calculation again dissolves the regress.
