Design a 90-minute classroom game for a 15-person upper-level seminar on rationality and political philosophy. The goal is for participants to feel the force of the dark forest hypothesis before it is named. In its strategic core, the hypothesis runs as follows: if agents cannot verify one another's intentions, if each treats its own survival as non-negotiable and needs scarce resources in order to persist and expand, and if a weak agent can rapidly become dangerous, then the rational response to detecting another agent may be immediate preemption, and the rational default is concealment. The session should not be a lecture for or against this line of reasoning; the rules, incentives, and roles should be arranged so participants arrive at concealment and preemption themselves.

Your design must be runnable by a first-time facilitator, and you must include each of the following:

- A setup simple enough to explain in ten minutes, using ordinary classroom materials (cards, chips, envelopes, or similar) and no real money. Specify how the 15 participants are divided and what information remains private to each player or group.
- A scoring scheme tied to shared finite resources, so that survival matters, growth matters, and hiding forever is not a winning strategy. Include a development mechanic by which a currently weak group can quickly become dangerous, and a rule that makes detection or exposure irreversible.
- Communication rules under which signalling is possible, costly, and non-binding, with no cheap reputation system and no outside enforcer that can guarantee trust.
- A timeline for the 90 minutes that reserves enough structured time for both play and debrief.

Then supply the debrief. Explain how you would map the choices and outcomes to the strands of the argument — survival as a veto value, competition over finite resources, the chain of suspicion, and sudden reversals in the balance of power — without forcing the mapping when participants acted for other reasons. Predict the most likely way the first run will go wrong, and say concretely how you would handle it without rigging the outcome. Finally, be frank about what the game cannot reproduce. The actual dark forest argument depends on astronomical features — the one-sidedness of detection, the potentially lethal cost of communicating, and the absence of a shared framework of signals — that a classroom game can only simulate weakly; state exactly which features the facilitator must therefore supply in words.

Commit to one specific design with definite rules, and do not offer a menu of alternatives. Where a design choice is contestable, make the call, give the reason briefly, and move on. Keep the entire response under 900 words.
