I've been thinking about wealth and abundance as values—whether prosperity has any moral worth beyond what it buys—and I found myself back in Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, Part IV, Chapter 1, in the section on utility and the beauty that comes from the appearance of it. There's this passage that I'd never really sat with before:

“A watch, in the same manner, that falls behind above two minutes in a day, is despised by one curious in watches. He sells it perhaps for a couple of guineas, and purchases another at fifty, which will not lose above a minute in a fortnight. The sole use of watches however, is to tell us what o’clock it is, and to hinder us from breaking any engagement, or suffering any other inconveniency by our ignorance in that particular point. But the person so nice with regard to this machine, will not always be found either more scrupulously punctual than other men, or more anxiously concerned upon any other account, to know precisely what time of day it is. What interests him is not so much the attainment of this piece of knowledge, as the perfection of the machine which serves to attain it.”

I want to use it as a lens on the post-quartz luxury watch revival. The basic facts as I understand them: when cheap quartz watches hit in the 70s, they almost destroyed the mechanical watch industry because they were more accurate and cheaper; then, from the 80s onward, Swiss mechanical watches came back not as timekeepers but as luxury objects—people pay thousands, sometimes more, for watches that are less accurate than a ten-dollar Casio. That sounds like a beautiful confirmation of Smith's point: the buyer cares about the perfection of the machine, not the end. But I'm suspicious of that reading. The modern mechanical watch buyer isn't even pretending that the object is a better instrument for telling time; the value seems to be in complication, finishing, heritage, scarcity, the story. Smith's man with the fifty-guinea watch still framed his interest in terms of the machine's fitness for its purpose; he just overvalued that fitness. In the luxury revival, fitness for telling time often doesn't enter into it, or enters only as a kind of nostalgia. So here's my question: analyze the post-quartz luxury watch revival through Smith's account of the beauty of utility and the overvaluation of the perfection of instruments. Where does his account genuinely illuminate, and where does it misdescribe or just go silent? Does the episode count as one of his "thousand instances," or does it reveal a limit in the theory—that it can't distinguish between loving the perfection of an instrument and loving the instrument as a symbol, a badge, or a work of art? And if the theory can't distinguish those, does it leave us unable to say whether the luxury watch revival is an instance of the thing Smith found slightly irrational, or something else entirely?
