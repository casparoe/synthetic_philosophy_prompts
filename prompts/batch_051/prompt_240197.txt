Below is a passage from Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Epistle Dedicatory (1651), in the original spelling:

“I speak not of the men, but (in the Abstract) of the Seat of Power, (like to those simple and unpartiall creatures in the Roman Capitol, that with their noyse defended those within it, not because they were they, but there) offending none, I think, but those without, or such within (if there be any such) as favour them.”

Consider an argument that tries to turn this remark into a structuralist answer to the question whether there would have been something like Nazi Germany without Hitler:

(1) Hobbes recommends explaining political outcomes by reference to the “Seat of Power” rather than to the particular men who occupy it.
(2) If an outcome is caused by an individual’s unique character, talents, decisions, or pathologies, then an adequate explanation cannot ignore the particular man.
(3) Nazi Germany was, at the level of state power, a case of a man occupying a seat of dictatorial power: the Führer position and the powers concentrated in it.
(4) If the seat explains the outcome, then even with a different occupant—say, a competent Nazi functionary, or a different ambitious right-wing dictator—the broad outcome would have been substantially similar.
(5) Therefore, had Hitler died in infancy, some other occupant of a similar seat of power would have produced a regime broadly like Nazi Germany.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, are the premises true? If it is not valid, or if a premise fails, identify exactly which step or premise fails. Then state what correction would either salvage or break the argument. In particular, say whether Hobbes’s sentence is being used fairly here, and whether the expression “Seat of Power” is doing illicit work at step (4).
