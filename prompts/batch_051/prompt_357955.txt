I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about competition and whether it builds character or corrodes it, and I would like a principle that fits the way I react to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts:

1. My cross-country coach posts everyone's times after each meet. Last week I ran a personal best and still finished ninth on the team; I found the list motivating rather than humiliating.
2. My physics teacher projects test scores from highest to lowest with names. I scored well but near the bottom, and I thought it turned the class into a status contest. I think that is corrosive.
3. Two students I know competed for a single scholarship. The one who lost told me the process made him clarify his plans, and he considered the outcome fair. I share his view that the competition was fine.
4. At family dinners, my father compares me to my cousin on every grade and activity. I think this is corrosive, and it has made me want to hide my school life.
5. In my chess club, the strongest player admitted he had stopped showing the rest of us his opening analysis because he now saw us as rivals. The club became guarded and less fun for everyone. I think that was corrosive.
6. Earlier in the same club, he would show us his lines, and we would attack them until he improved them; we all got stronger. I thought that was clearly good.
7. A student in my grade buys candy in bulk and resells it, deliberately undercutting a classmate's prices until the classmate gives up. I think that is corrosive.
8. I keep a record of my own mile time and try to lower it each week; I don't especially care how my time compares with others. This feels healthy and almost unrelated to the other cases.

I have tried to state a principle, but each version stumbles. My best attempt: competition builds character when entry is voluntary, the ranking measures genuine effort or improvement, and the relationship can continue outside the contest. That fits several cases, but it does not explain why the cross-country time list feels fine to me, since it is a public ranking of outcomes rather than effort, and I cannot easily exit it if I want to run.

I would like you either to repair that principle or replace it. Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, and especially explain the cross-country list and the physics test ranking, which I cannot reconcile: both are public rankings of real performance, and I call one fine and one corrosive. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and what it says about cases I have not listed: a schoolwide Sophomore of the Year award based on nominations; a parent who pays each child for every top grade and posts the siblings' totals on the refrigerator; and an app that shows students their class rank without names.
