Journalist here. I cover privacy and surveillance for an ideas-y section. I'm writing about a small data protection review board in a European city that issues advisory rulings on state demands for citizen data. The board's members are heavily influenced by German data protection law, but they also watch the U.S. openness fights and China's social credit experiments. Their published rulings so far:

- Approved a police request to query the national license-plate register, but only when an officer enters an active case number, and every query is logged and audited. Reasoning: targeted, accountable, doesn't reveal where someone is going unless there's an active investigation.
- Rejected an always-on facial recognition system in the main train station. Reasoning: everyone passing through is scanned, identified, and timestamped without any prior suspicion, and stored faceprints create a permanent misuse risk.
- Approved mandatory SIM registration with verified legal identity, saying the state may require identifiers at the point of service as long as it doesn't capture the content of communications.
- Rejected a social credit pilot that would score residents on paying utilities on time, attending neighborhood meetings, and sorting waste, with high scores moving people up public housing queues and low scores flagging them for home inspections.
- During the pandemic, approved with conditions a contact tracing app that exchanged pseudonymous Bluetooth tokens, kept data on phones, required no location, and auto-deleted after 14 days; rejected a centralized version that recorded location traces.

A machine-learning system will flag usage patterns that look like hydroponic grow houses. When flagged, police can look at a flagged household's usage history without a warrant. The utility says the data will be retained for 5 years, can be used only for drug searches or aggregate grid planning, and will be shared with other agencies only under a memorandum of understanding.
