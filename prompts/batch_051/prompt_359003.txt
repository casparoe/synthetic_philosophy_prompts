I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping someone can tell me whether it is a named thing or just a recurring confusion. In debates about God's existence, there is an argument that runs roughly like this: if a perfectly loving God existed, he would not allow people who sincerely seek him to remain without adequate evidence of his existence; but there are such people; so God does not exist. The reply often combines two claims. First, God has good reason not to make himself too obvious, because overwhelming evidence would coerce belief, and coerced belief cannot ground genuine love or a freely chosen relationship, so God must leave room for doubt. Second, when asked why God does not at least do more than he has, perhaps a private sign to a sincere doubter or a public miracle on demand, the same person will often answer that it would not help, because people can rationalize away any finite evidence: a determined skeptic would explain a sign as coincidence or hallucination, and a miracle as a natural anomaly.

I cannot shake the feeling that these two halves pull against each other. The first treats more evidence as dangerous because it would overwhelm our freedom; the second treats it as pointless because it would do nothing. But maybe I am missing a distinction. I notice the two halves tend to get aimed at different targets: the coercion worry is usually wheeled out against proposals for public, undeniable displays, while the rationalization worry is usually wheeled out against proposals for private, individualized evidence. Is there a name for this tension, or for the paired two-reply move? Is there a recognized position about whether the public/private distinction does real work here, or is it an ad hoc way of keeping the two replies from colliding? I am not just looking for a label; I want to know what the phenomenon actually is, how it differs from ordinary having-it-both-ways, and under what conditions the two replies are genuinely inconsistent rather than merely applying to different cases.
