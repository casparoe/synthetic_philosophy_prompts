I'm trying to make sense of a thing I've noticed in photo and art competition rules, and I'd like a short essay on it. A lot of contests now ban entries made with generative AI, or shove them into a separate category. But the same contests don't seem to bat an eye at panoramas stitched by an algorithm, brackets merged into an HDR image, focus stacks, or composites made from dozens of shots. And modern cameras do a ton of computational photography on their own — noise reduction, white balance, lens correction — and nobody treats that as cheating. I can't see the princpal that's doing the work. It's not "no algorithms." It's not "no pixels that weren't in front of the lens," because composites and sky replacements fake that all the time. It's not "the machine can't make aesthetic choices," because a lot of automatic processing makes choices the photographer never made.

What I want is an essay that reconstructs the implicit theory of creativity or authorship this practice presupposes, if it even has one. Maybe the practice only makes sense on a view where the relevant creative act is something like intending or selecting a scene, or where the causal chain from world to image matters to whether something counts as a photograph. Or maybe the practice doesn't presuppose a single theory at all and is just boundary-policing by vibes. Distinguish what the rules strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with. Then tell me what the rules would have to look like if you instead held that prompting, curating, and iterating on an AI image is a creative act of the same general kind as darkroom work or compositing. And finally, is the current practice coherent, or does it fall apart at the margins — say, when someone uses AI-powered object removal on 90 percent of the frame versus 5 percent, or when the "AI" tool is just doing what the camera already did automatically?
