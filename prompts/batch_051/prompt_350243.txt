I've been trying to think through the Great Man vs. structural debate and ended up with a little theory I want stress-tested. I call it a 'permission cascade.' The idea is that a leader's main causal contribution usually isn't creating preferences or persuading anyone; it's authorizing. Most people in a society already privately hold certain preferences — resentments, revisionist grievances, a willingness to use violence — but they don't act on them because they think the norm against doing so is still in force. What the leader supplies is a public signal that the norm is suspended. A few people act, nothing bad happens to them, everyone updates there estimate of what's actually permitted, and the whole distribution of behavior shifts fast. On this picture, Hitler didn't have to create antisemitism or the drive to overturn Versailles; those were already latent. He supplied the permission. So the 'Hitler or the structures?' question is badly posed: the structures supply the preferences, the leader supplies the authorization.

Is this a real mechanism or just a metaphor? It feels like it must already exist under some other name, and I'd want to know what I'm reinventing. Where does it break — what does it wrongly count as a permission cascade, and when does a leader genuinely initiate something almost nobody privately wanted instead of just authorizing it? Give me the strongest counterexample and tell me whether the theory survives it.
