I keep going back and forth on the idea that compatriots have special claims on each other — that I'm allowed, maybe required, to care more about a fellow citizen than about a foreigner in equal need, purely because we share a citizenship label. But I can't shake the suspiscion that the label isn't doing the real work, and that what people actually lean on is shared language, reciprocity, or the fact that you'll run into the same people again. That's an empirical hunch, so maybe it can be tested.

Suppose you could run any study you want. Start with a minimal setup in which participants split real money between an in-group and an out-group, but the groups are assigned by lottery among strangers and have no history, no shared culture, no face-to-face interaction — just a label. Then add conditions one at a time: a shared accent or language, a history of reciprocal exchange, formal citizenship with voting rights and taxes, common physical surroundings. Design the strongest version of this, and tell me what pattern of results would show that compatriot specialness is a genuine, irreducible feature of people's moral cognition rather than a stand-in for those other things. Then be honest about what even a clean result would leave open: if it turns out people don't weigh the citizenship label at all, would that tell us anything about whether they ought to? Or if it turns out they do, would that justify the special claim?
