So I just got back from a long argument with a friend about whether it matters when a language dies, and I'm still turning it over. He kept coming back to the same point: languages have always died, and if speakers themselves are shifting to bigger languages for work and school, then trying to prop up a dying language is just nostalgia feeding on other people's choices. I was defending the view that each language death is a genuine loss, and I ended up listing every argument I could think of. The annoying thing is I'm pretty sure I'm missing one.

Here's what I've got so far—the arguments for preserving endangered languages as I know them:

(1) the knowledge argument: each language carries knowledge (ecological, medicinal, taxonomic, oral history) that doesn't really survive translation into a big language;

(2) the justice/identity argument: language shift is rarely a free, unforced choice—it happens to communities under pressure, exclusion, or outright discrimination—so maintaining a language is partly about repairing an injustice and respecting people's identity;

(3) the scientific argument: every language is a data point about the possible shapes of human language and mind, and losing it makes it harder to figure out what's universal versus just an accident of the languages that happened to survive long enough to be studied;

(4) the aesthetic/biodiversity argument: linguistic diversity is worth having the way biodiversity or a plurality of artistic traditions is worth having—a world with many languages is just richer.

I think these are basically the standard ones. Give me one more. Not a rewording of any of these, not a special case of one of them, not some mashup of two of them—a genuinely diffrent argument. In particular, I'm curious whether there's one that doesn't bottom out in 'something is lost' (knowledge, identity, data, beauty) but has a different shape, like something about how we should decide under uncertainty or about the process of language shift itself. Tell me what the argument is, explain what makes it different from the four on my list, and say whether it would survive my friend's objection—or whether he'd have to move to a stronger objection to answer it.

And can you write the answer in American English? I'd rather it matched.
