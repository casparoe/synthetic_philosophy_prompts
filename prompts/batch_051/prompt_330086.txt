I've been in a pragmatics seminar where the discussion keeps returning to a dispute about presupposition accommodation, and I would like to stop the endless exchange of intuitions and see whether any observation could settle it. One of us holds that when a hearer is told something that presupposes a proposition they did not already accept — for instance, being told that John's sister is coming to dinner when they had no idea John had a sister — the hearer performs a genuine, largely automatic update: the proposition that John has a sister is entered into the conversational score, and this updated score then feeds forward into what the hearer is disposed to say, accept, reject, and remember. The other of us holds that this is a rational reconstruction applied after the fact. On that view, nothing like a represented scoreboard is actually updated on-line; the hearer simply lets the presupposition pass, or settles afterwards on an interpretation that makes the speaker's contribution acceptable, and talk of common ground is only a concise way of summing up that forbearance rather than a claim about a mental transaction. Since the two stories agree in easy cases, and even about many of the words the hearer eventually produces, we have been unable to prise them apart.

Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a hearer who accommodates a new presupposition is really carrying out an on-line update to a structured common ground, rather than merely exhibiting post-hoc forbearance. Be concrete about the design — for example, what you would measure about later references, rejections, memory, or processing speed — and say which pattern of results would count for the real-update view, which for the rational-reconstruction view, and what even a decisive result would leave unsettled about whether the conversational score is psychologically real. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question. End by saying how confident you are in your bottom line and identifying the single point in your answer you are least sure of.
