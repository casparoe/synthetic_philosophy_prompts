I am a novelist. I write character-driven literary fiction, and I outline two or three novels a year, each organized around a small number of moral choices that are meant to carry the book's weight. The problem I keep encountering is this: a choice that feels, in the outline, like a genuine moral dilemma often turns out, once I interrogate it, to be something weaker—a hidden third option I refused to see, a preference wearing moral language, or a hard choice with a determinately right answer inside the character's own settled commitments. I want a reusable procedure, not a piece of criticism, that I can run on any scene before I draft it.

The philosophical question I read toward but cannot operationalize is whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible at all: whether a person can face two genuine requirements that cannot both be satisfied, so that whatever she does, something with real normative force remains unfulfilled; or whether every apparent conflict is, on proper analysis, a conflict between lesser considerations that a deeper, all-things-considered judgment resolves. I have absorbed only the rough vocabulary—prima facie and all-things-considered obligation, the principle that ought implies can, the question whether guilt or regret after acting is evidence of a real unresolved requirement or mere psychological residue, and the idea that two duties would, taken together, entail a duty to do both. I cannot translate any of that into a test I can run on a scene. I am not asking for a survey of positions, a reading list, or citations; I need the procedure itself, with the distinctions rendered as working tests.

Please write it as an ordered sequence of tests. For each test, state exactly what I am looking for; what counts as a sufficient answer in each direction; when the test stops the whole inquiry and sends me back to the outline with the verdict "not a genuine dilemma"; and when the procedure must stop and hand the decision to me because the remaining question is a values judgment rather than an analytical one. Mark those handoffs explicitly rather than smoothing over them. The procedure should separate at least four outcomes: a genuine dilemma, a hard or tragic choice with a determinate but costly answer, a temptation in moral disguise, and a false binary that dissolves under re-description. At the point where the procedure would conclude that two requirements genuinely conflict and neither defeats the other, it should say so in exactly those terms and flag that the scene is now committed to the thesis that genuine dilemmas are possible; do not smuggle in that conclusion, and do not rule it out by definition.

The procedure must be runnable by me alone, in a single afternoon of outlining, without formal philosophical training, and rerunnable after a draft changes. It should be built to catch my own wishful thinking—the places where I have manufactured a dilemma by hiding the third option from myself—but it should not pretend the underlying dispute is settled.

Then apply the procedure to three scenes I am currently wrestling with, showing how each one moves through the tests and where it exits:

1. A physician at a rural clinic has one functioning ventilator and two patients who will both die very soon without it; there is no higher authority, no transfer protocol, and no colleague to consult.
2. A courier in an underground resistance movement learns that the safe-house has been compromised. She can misdirect the police toward a friend's apartment, which would buy time for the cell of twenty to disperse but would likely get the friend arrested and tortured, or she can warn the friend and lose the cell.
3. The scene I most want to keep as a genuine dilemma: a judge discovers mid-trial that the defendant in a capital case is the son she gave up for adoption decades earlier. Recusal is legally possible but would make the relationship public and, in this setting, would almost certainly lead to his conviction by a harsher bench. I have long believed this is the purest dilemma I have written.

Do not soften any result. At least one of the three applications must return a verdict I will find unwelcome; if that happens to be the judge, show me exactly which test the scene fails and why I am not entitled to keep calling it a dilemma. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: what it cannot replace in the novelist's own judgment, and what assumptions it silently imports about whether value conflicts can ever be irresolvable.
