In programming, version-control systems face a question about identity over time. Git's storage layer treats a file's content as its identity: a blob is named by the hash of its bytes, so a one-character edit produces a different object, and two files with identical bytes are literally the same stored object. Its user-facing layer, however, talks about the same file changing across commits, and tools display diffs as the history of one continuous thing. I suspect this is not merely bookkeeping: it looks like the difference between a bundle theory of identity, on which a thing just is its qualities taken together, and a substance or continuant view, on which the thing persists through changes in its qualities. But I also worry that programming simply has two different identity predicates — reference identity and structural equality — and that I am pattern-matching the word "same" across two fields. Is the parallel deep or superficial? If Git's storage model really is bundle-theoretic, what would it predict about merges, renames, and copied files, and where do those predictions fail when Git has to decide whether two versions are versions of one file? Finally, can the programming distinction help explain why mutation is hard to think about, or does it just restate the problem?
