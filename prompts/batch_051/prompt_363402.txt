My working principle has always been the principle of alternative possibilities: you're morally responsible for what you did only if you could have done otherwise. But my gut reactions to cases are pulling in different directions, and i want to know whether there's a single principle behind them, or whether I need to drop some verdicts.

Cases:

1. A man in a boat watches a swimmer drown. He could easily throw the life ring, but he's annoyed at the swimmer and doesn't. Fully responsible.

2. Same scene, but the ring is locked away and he has no way to reach it. Not responsible.

3. Same scene, but a neuroscientist has secretly implanted a chip that would have made him sit back down if he'd shown any sign of deciding to throw the ring. He decides on his own not to throw, out of pure malice, and the chip never activates. I hold him fully responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise.

4. A man is about to throw the ring, but a stranger on the boat puts a gun to his head and says he'll shoot if the man throws it. The man doesn't throw, and he doesn't throw because of the threat. I don't blame him—or at least blame him far less than the guy in case 1—even though he also couldn't have done otherwise.

5. Someone freely gets drunk, knowing he turns violent when drunk, and then beats a stranger in a state where he literally can't stop himself. I think he's responsible for the beating, which on my stated principle he shouldn't be.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about a sixth case: the boat guy could have saved the swimmer, but only by doing something he didn't know was possible (the latch he thought was locked was actually open)?
