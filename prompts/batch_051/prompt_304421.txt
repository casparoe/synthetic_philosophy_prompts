I recently read the following paragraph in an introductory article on mysticism, and it was written with enough confidence that I want to know how much of it actually survives scrutiny:

Philosophers of religion now tend to treat the ineffability claim as a coherent but limited thesis. The mystic is not saying that nothing can be said at all; the claim is that the central experience cannot be fully or accurately rendered in ordinary descriptive language. Nor is that supposed to make the experience non-cognitive: William James's classic account of mystical consciousness pairs ineffability with a noetic quality, meaning that the experience presents itself as a state of knowledge rather than as a mere emotion. The real difficulty lies elsewhere. To call an experience ineffable is already to say something true about it — namely, that it has a feature that language cannot capture — and it is hard to see how one could know that the experience has this feature without grasping the very content that is supposed to escape language. If the content really does escape language, then the mystic's conviction that the experience is of something is in no better position than any other strong feeling, since whatever can be known can be said. Negative theology is often presented as a way around this difficulty: the mystic gives up positive description and says only what the object is not. But once negative statements are allowed, there is no principled stopping point, so the negative theologian is, in effect, simply saying a great deal about the object — which shows that the apparent boundary between the sayable and the unsayable is an illusion.

Can you give me a point-by-point assessment of this paragraph? For each substantive claim, say whether it is accurate, accurate only with an important qualification, or false. Then address two further questions. First, does the paragraph's final claim, that the boundary between the sayable and the unsayable is an illusion, follow from what precedes it? Second, how would a defender of the view that mystical states can be both ineffable and noetic answer the charge that a state cannot count as knowledge unless its content can be expressed? Keep the assessment conceptual; outside literature is not needed.
