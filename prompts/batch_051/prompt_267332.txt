For a third-year philosophy of economics seminar with about sixteen students and a two-hour block, design an in-class game that makes participants feel the force of revealed preference theory and, above all, the austere sense in which economists say that utility is constructed from choice rather than read off from inner experience. The game should be runnable without real money and should include rules, materials, and a timeline. Arrange it so that the core ideas surface in what the students do, not in what the instructor tells them afterwards: by the end of the session they should have discovered, from their own behaviour, that a utility function can be fitted to a set of choices even though nobody reported any felt quantity of satisfaction; that the same choices can be represented by indefinitely many different utility functions, so the numbers are doing no more than tracking order; and that intransitive choices make a person exploitable, ideally by letting the group actually cycle one student through a money pump. The debrief should map each component of the game onto the formal notions of preference, choice, and utility representation, and should be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, the move from an ordinal representation to the cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility that much moral and political philosophy still treats as meaningful, which the facilitator must supply in discussion. Also describe what will predictably go wrong when the game is first run, such as participants who refuse to trade in the money pump or who notice the intended point too early, and how to handle each failure without spoiling the exercise before the concluding discussion.
