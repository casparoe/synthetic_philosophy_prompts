Last night I got into a three-hour argument with a friend that started over a news story and then spiralled. The story was about a man who tried to shoot someone, missed because his hand was shaking, and ended up with a much lighter sentence than he'd have got if the bullet had landed. My friend, who's more of a consequentialist, kept saying that's basically right: the failed attempt caused less harm, and the lighter sentence still makes sense on deterrence and incapacitation grounds. I pushed back that the man's blameworthiness is exaclty the same — he tried to kill someone — and that letting the sentence swing on something as arbitrary as aim treats him as a tool rather than as someone who did wrong. At one point he accused me of just having a moral-luck view of desert, and I accused him of dressing up a rule-utilitarian insurance policy as a theory of justice. We went round in circles and never resolved it.

I want to turn this into a dialogue to sort my head out. Could you write one, maybe 800–1000 words, between two characters — a retributivist and a consequentialist about punishment — arguing specifically about whether an unsuccessful attempt should be punished like a completed crime. Make both of them genuinely sharp and fair to each other; neither should be a straw man. I want real back-and-forth where they respond to what the other actually said, and I'd like the pressure to land on both sides: the consequentialist has to explain why we punish attempts at all if harm is doing the work, and the retributivist has to explain why something as arbitrary as bad aim should change what a person deserves. It would be especially good if, by the end, they both admit they're more uncomfortable with outcome-dependence than their official positions let on. And can you write it in British English? Interruptions and a bit of heat are fine, just not talking past each other.
