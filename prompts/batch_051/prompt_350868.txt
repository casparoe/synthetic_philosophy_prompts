I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience and my roommate (philosophy) has been running a Libet argument past me that I can't find the exit from. It seems to prove too much — not just that Libet's subjects lacked free will, but that no one's conscious intentions ever cause anything, and then it undermines itself. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it, with the steps numbered so we can talk about where it goes wrong:

(1) In Libet's wrist-flex experiment, the readiness potential — the EEG signature of the brain preparing the movement — starts roughly half a second before the flex.
(2) Subjects report the conscious urge or intention to flex only about 200 ms before the flex.
(3) So the conscious intention shows up after the neural process that brings about the flex is already running.
(4) If the neural process that causes a movement is already running before the conscious intention appears, the intention can't be what causes the movement; that causal work was already underway.
(5) Libet flexings aren't some weird special action. Any deliberate action has the same structure once you look at the physiology closely enough — the conscious thought will always lag the neural preparation.
(6) So for every action, the conscious intention comes too late to be the cause. Conscious will is epiphenomenal: caused by the brain, but causing nothing, like the hum of a motor.
(7) But if conscious reasoning is epiphenomenal, then the conclusion of this very argument isn't accepted by me becuase I followed the premises; it's just another neuronal effect. The argument, if sound, gives me no reason to believe its conclusion.

I don't want to give up (7), becuase then I can't say I believe anything for reasons — not in philosophy, not in the lab. But I also don't see which earlier step to reject without sounding like I'm just refusing to accept data I don't like. Which step should go, and what do I give up by rejecting it? Also, if you had to defend a weaker claim built from whatever survives — like what Libet does and doesn't actually show — what is it?
