I got into an argument with a friend about whether rational egoism and doing the most good are just two rival value systems or whether one is really more rationally compelling than the other, and it sent me back to Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics (or at least decent summaries). The thing people call the dualism of practical reason is bugging me more than I expected. My understanding is this: Sidgwick thought that when you actually systematize common-sense morality, it converges on something like utilitarianism — maximize the general happiness. But he also thought egoistic hedonism, maximizing your own happiness, is just as rationally self-evident, and neither one defeats the other. Practical reason underwrites both but can't adjudicate when they conflict. So instead of one action-guiding system you get two, like an agent with two utility functions and no principled way of combining them.

What I want isn't a book report, but the most important implications of this view. If Sidgwick is right, what does that do to the rationalist/effective-altruism-ish picture where there's usually assumed to be a single impartial ranking to optimize? Does it imply moral uncertainty has to extend all the way down — that a fully informed and fully rational agent could still have an unresolved, principled conflict between self-interest and impartial beneficence rather than some meta-principle that settles it? Does it puncture the idea that an ideal agent's preferences should be complete and transitive, or can you just model the two sources as incommensurable rankings and still call the agent rational? And practically, what are the live options for living with it: accepting two seperate rankings and being honest about unresolved conflicts, hoping reflection eventually collapses them, picking one as primary, something else?
