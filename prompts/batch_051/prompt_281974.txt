Since my father's dementia entered its middle stages and my siblings began disputing whether to honor the living will he signed eight years ago, I have found myself defending a view I did not expect to hold. I want you to stress-test it.

The view is this. Once a capable person has issued an advance directive that is competently made and specific enough to settle the current question, the directive settles the matter. The later self's concurrently expressed preferences—even when they are stable, sincere, and persist across months—should be given no authority, not merely overridden. I mean this to include the hardest cases: the person who wrote years ago that she would refuse life-prolonging treatment for advanced dementia now takes evident pleasure in meals and cooperates with antibiotics. I have come to think of the position in terms of a distinction between the earlier self as the author of the life into which the later period falls, and the later self as its occupant. The occupant's satisfactions and aversions matter greatly as states to be relieved, but they are not instructions to be followed, because the occupant is no longer positioned to author anything: the agential capacities that could make a current preference authoritative are precisely the ones the illness has destroyed. Call this the authorship view.

I would like a map of this view's exposure, not a survey of the standard criticisms. Please reconstruct the strongest case against it and sort the considerations by severity: which would be fatal, which would be seriously damaging, and which merely awkward? Which of these should a committed defender of the authorship view concede count against it, and which can she dismiss as category errors? I especially want to know what would have to be factually true about the later self—not only about what she says, but about how she values over time, how she endorses or disowns her earlier decisions, and how much of herself she still gathers into a single perspective—for the author/occupant distinction to break down. At what point would my withholding authority from everything she now expresses become an insulated, unfalsifiable maneuver rather than a principled line? I would also like to know which other commitments the view silently takes on concerning personal identity, the source of a preference's authority, and how we may treat the later self's protests when we act against them. Finally, be direct about whether the view risks anything at all, and whether, if it does not, that is a defect or simply what it is to hold a view of this kind. It may help to know that I would be moved most by a demonstration that the authorship distinction cannot carry the weight I place on it, and least by bare appeals to the sacredness of current feeling.
