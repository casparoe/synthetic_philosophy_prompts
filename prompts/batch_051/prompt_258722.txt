Here's a counterfactual that's been nagging me. Suppose that in 1900 philosophy as an organised discipline quietly vanishes from the world: every philosophy department is closed, no philosophy journals or courses, and the label "philosopher" basically dissappears. The old books are still in libraries, but nobody is paid to teach them or argue about them as a separate subject, so a physics student in 1925 has never had to distinguish empiricism from rationalism or anything like that. What would twentieth-century physics have looked like? I'm not really after a yes/no; I want the chain of reasoning about mechanisms.

Specifically: would special and general relativity still arrive on roughly the same schedule, or did the young Einstein actually need his reading of Mach and Hume (and would he have stumbled on it anyway)? Would quantum mechanics get its formalism on time, and would the Bohr–Einstein interpretation fight go differently? Would people like the logical empiricists have just become physicists and done the same clarifying work from inside, or would that normative/interpretive work simply not happen? And what does your prediction imply about the Feynman/Weinberg/Hawking line that physics doesn't need philosophy — would the counterfactual support it, undermine it, or just show the line was asking the wrong question?

For each link in your prediction, please say roughly how confident you are, and at the end tell me the single point you're least sure about. British English please.
