I'm a high school student, and I recently got into a debate in my ethics club that I would like help thinking through. We were discussing whether being rich is ever good in itself, as opposed to merely useful. The standard view we started with was thoroughly instrumental: money matters only because of what it purchases — goods, comfort, education, medical care, and the security or freedom those things make possible. One member of the club pushed back, saying this leaves something out. Prosperity, the settled condition of having more than enough, seems to change a person's standing in ways that are not fully captured by any list of purchases: a person with abundant resources stands differently toward the future, toward risk, toward other people, and even toward her own plans, and perhaps that standing has moral worth in its own right even if the money is never converted into anything beyond the condition itself.

What I would like is an answer to this: what are the most important implications of the view that prosperity and abundance have moral worth beyond what they buy, for how we should morally assess two lives? The first is someone who devotes decades to accumulating wealth that she will almost certainly never spend or give away. The second is someone who gives away nearly everything he earns as quickly as he earns it, keeping only a modest cushion. Does the view that abundance has non-instrumental worth change what we should say about these two lives, and if so, how? Please write in American English, and please do something our discussions rarely manage: separate carefully which parts of this are genuinely settled among informed people who work on the ethics of wealth and which parts remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
