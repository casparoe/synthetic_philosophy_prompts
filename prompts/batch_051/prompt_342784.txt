When interpreting a poem or a novel, we commonly treat the author's stated intention as at most one piece of evidence, and many critics hold that it cannot settle what the work means. When a court interprets a will, a contract, or a statute, by contrast, the maker's intention is treated as the central thing to be found, and a reading that set the drafter's intention aside as irrelevant would usually be regarded as a mistake. Both practices are called interpretation, and both concern texts produced by an author. Is there a principled difference between the two cases that justifies this asymmetry, or is one practice working with a confused picture of what interpretation is? Offer the strongest defense you can of whichever practice you think should change, and test it against a case in which the same written language could plausibly be treated either as a legal instrument or as a literary work.
