I am a PhD student in economic sociology working on the methodology of cultural explanation, and I have been trying to settle a boundary question about when a value or norm does genuine causal work in explaining an economic practice, rather than merely accompanying it, rationalizing it after the fact, or marking a group that would behave the same way without it. The working criteria in my department say that for a value to count as a cause of a practice, all of the following should hold.

1. Differential distribution: the value varies across groups or persons, and the practice varies with it, within roughly the same material environment.
2. Priority: the value can be identified before the practice it is supposed to explain, and among people who do not yet have a stake in that practice.
3. Mechanism: there is a specifiable pathway from holding the value to engaging in the practice — for example, the value makes certain options unthinkable, reframes a cost as a duty, or changes what people attend to and compare.
4. Costliness: the practice persists in at least some cases where it is materially costly, not only where it serves the person's material interests.
5. Non-derivation: the value is not simply the most sympathetic description of what the incentive structure would produce on its own.

I have been testing these criteria on a contemporary case that feels liminal. There is an informal lay movement among white-collar workers in a mid-sized American city; call it the Restraint Fellowship. Members treat thrift, debt avoidance, and careful personal accounting as spiritual disciplines, which they call temporance. They speak of money as stored time and of debt as a promise one has no right to make about the future. They keep daily ledgers, decline consumer credit, seek to eliminate recurring expenses as lapses of attention, and hold group reviews of one another's spending. They save or invest a large share of after-tax income, commonly around half, and describe this not as a financial strategy but as a witness to their values. The movement has no formal organization, no significant sanctions beyond mild social approval, and no developed theology outside this ethic. Members teach the practice to newcomers through the shared ledgers, and former members report that after leaving, their saving and spending habits partly drift back toward those of otherwise similar workers who were never in the movement.

The difficulty is that the behavior looks overdetermined. Members are drawn heavily from software contracting and data work, where incomes are volatile and holding large cash reserves while keeping fixed costs low is independently rational. One can read the moral vocabulary as a retrospective gloss on what these workers would sensibly do anyway. Two observations pull in opposite directions. Some members who inherited enough money to stop working continued the same practices and accepted real loss of comfort, which suggests the value is not only a mask for self-interest. But many members appear to have been saving aggressively for years before adopting the movement's vocabulary, and the vocabulary tracks their occupational situation so closely that I cannot tell whether it entered their decision-making or only their self-description.

Go through the five criteria one at a time and say whether the Restraint Fellowship meets each, fails it, or leaves it underdetermined on the information given. Then give your overall verdict: is temporance a cause of the Fellowship's economic behavior in the sense the criteria try to capture? Finally, tell me whether the borderline character of the case should make me revise the criteria — if so, which criterion is doing the wrong work, and what should replace it?
