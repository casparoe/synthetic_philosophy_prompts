Not long ago I discovered that an adviser I had trusted for years had answered every question I put to him with something literally true, while arranging his omissions and emphases so that I held a materially false picture of my own savings. Since then I have been unable to decide whether his misleading truthfulness wronged me in a way a direct lie would not have. Two friends with whom I have argued about this all year have settled into opposing positions—call them the additional-wrong view and the no-intrinsic-difference view. The first holds that lying carries an additional wrong that misleading lacks: in lying, the speaker more directly uses the hearer's reliance on him and, so to speak, proposes to be believed under a norm of truthfulness that he then breaks. The second holds that, once the speaker's intentions, the hearer's resulting beliefs, and the actual consequences are held fixed, there is nothing left of moral significance in the distinction; any residual difference comes from contingent facts about detectability, risk, or downstream effects.

I would like you to draft the document these two friends could have produced through an honest adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, including claims about the cases themselves and about what each side is not claiming; (2) a table of disagreements, with each camp's position stated exactly and in wording its own side would accept as fair rather than as a caricature; (3) a list of cruxes, understood as sub-questions such that resolving one would move at least one of the two sides; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case, question, or comparison that both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. If some portion of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which portion and what disambiguation dissolves it.

Please impose the following constraints. Leave aside arguments that rest merely on lies being on average harder to detect, likelier to be found out, or worse in their downstream consequences, since both sides may accept such differences as contingent. Leave aside appeals to divine command, law, or professional licensing. Restrict the discussion to person-to-person communication in which the hearer reasonably takes the speaker to be accountable for the truth of what he conveys. Have the collaborators make explicit use of, and if necessary refine, at least these four test cases:

- An answer that is literally true but is chosen because the hearer will predictably draw a false conversational inference from it, and the speaker does not correct that inference.
- The very same false belief, with the same speaker's intention, produced instead by direct false assertion, so that the two cases differ only along the lying/misleading dimension.
- A curated disclosure, such as providing a complete list that is technically exhaustive but ordered and emphasized so as to leave the opposite impression.
- A person in a fiduciary or clinical role who, before each answer, asks the hearer to specify exactly what he wants to know, and then answers only that narrow question truthfully while knowing that the hearer is assembling a false overall picture.

For each case, have both sides say what they expect an impartial, thoughtful observer to judge, and whether they expect that judgment to survive reflective scrutiny. Do not attempt to settle the dispute; the purpose of the document is that both parties can sign it, not that one side wins.
