Write a dialogue between two semanticists who can't agree about what makes a generic like 'ducks lay eggs' true, given that most ducks at any given moment—males, ducklings, non-breeding females—never lay an egg. One thinks generics track something like the kind's inherent or characteristic features: what ducks are like as a kind, so head-counts are beside the point. The other thinks generics are covertly context-sensitive: context quietly restricts the domain or supplies a comparison class, and once you spell that out the statement is true only of the relevant ducks (or along the relevant dimension). Have them actually respond to each other, not talk past each other, and make each one concede at least one local point that costs their view. They should work through the hard cases: 'ducks are female' is false even though roughly half of ducks are female; 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true even though almost no mosquitoes do; 'ducks carry avian flu' seems to flip on and off with context. Toward the end they should wonder whether they really disagree about generics at all, or whether they're just using 'true' differently. Both should be equally sharp—neither is the strawman.
