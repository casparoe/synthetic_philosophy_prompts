I am teaching an upper-division undergraduate course called The Value of Culture, and in our unit on endangered languages we have been working through a debate about whether a language's value is exhausted by what it does for the people who use it, or whether a language can matter in a way that is not fully captured by the benefits it delivers to its current speakers. For the next session I am pairing that debate with a screening of Denis Villeneuve's 2016 film Arrival, based on Ted Chiang's Story of Your Life, and I would like a careful philosophical reading of the film through this lens. The film seems useful here because it is pulled in both directions: the heptapod language is initially treated by the military as a technology whose point is practical coordination, even a possible threat, while the film also presents the language as something whose successful transmission matters for reasons that outrun the practical advantage of any single human user.

What I would like is a reading that stays faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the film into an illustration, and it should not distort the philosophical issue to fit the film. Assume I know the plot and do not summarize it. Please work through at least the following scenes without quoting any dialogue: the translation dispute over whether the heptapods are offering something closer to a weapon or to a gift or tool; Louise's choice, once she understands what the language does, to have Hannah and thereby choose a grief she can already see; and the closing indication that the language must be remembered because the heptapods will need human help far later. In particular, test whether the film can make vivid the thought that a language may be worth preserving even when the people affected by its loss would live equally well without it, or whether it quietly reduces the language's value to its cognitive and practical payoffs. Does the film ultimately treat the heptapod language as having merely instrumental value, intrinsic value, or some third position on what makes a language matter beyond its living community of speakers? Where does the film dramatize something the philosophical debate underplays, and where does it resist the reading, so that an honest critic should say so? End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical argument that a standard paper on intrinsic value and language could not. Please write the response in American English.
