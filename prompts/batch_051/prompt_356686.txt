I am teaching a short course in formal epistemology and want to use Kurosawa's Rashomon alongside a unit on Bayesian convergence results: the theorems which say that, under specified conditions, two observers who start with different prior probabilities but update rationally on a shared and growing stream of evidence will eventually come to agree, so that their initial priors wash out. What I cannot decide is whether the film is best understood as a dramatisation of the precise point at which those theorems fail, or as a repudiation of the entire family of models. The witnesses in Rashomon have access to substantially the same observations and the same testimony, yet they end up with incompatible and self-serving accounts of the death, and the film appears to resist supplying any hidden authoritative version against which the others could be measured. Read the film through the lens of the convergence theorems. Where exactly does the Bayesian model break: are the witnesses not updating on a common likelihood, are they disagreeing about the model space, are they reporting strategically rather than sincerely, or is something else going on? Does the film's refusal to restore a single true description show something the formal results cannot represent, or does it simply show a world in which the assumptions of merging are not met? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the film to an illustration of a theorem, and one that does not distort the formal conditions merely to make the analogy work. It should identify the scenes that carry the argument and finish by saying what Rashomon adds to the epistemology of convergence that the theorems alone leave out. Please write the answer in British English.
