I moved into a co-op house last year and this has been bugging me for months. When we first set up the cleaning schedule, we all signed a sheet agreeing to it, and if someone blew off their week, everyone treated it as a real violation — like they’d broken a promise. But by spring we stopped using the signed sheet and just fell into the same pattern anyway. People still did their week, mostly, because they expected everyone else to and nobody wanted to be the one who didn't. Yet now if someone skips, it's just mildly annoying, not a moral breach. Why do I feel such a huge difference between the signed agreement and the unspoken routine, when the actual behavior and mutual expectations are basically the same?

I've been reading David Lewis's Convention, where he defines a convention roughly as a regularity in behavior that everyone conforms to because they expect others to conform and prefer to conform given that expectation, with common knowledge of all this. And Lewis is drawing on Hume's Treatise, where Hume says in A Treatise of Human Nature (Book 3, Part 2, Section 2), "But in order to form society, 'tis requisite not only that it be advantageous, but also that men be sensible of these advantages." Hume seems to be saying society starts from unspoken, unlegislated coordination, not from signing anything.

So here's what I want to know: does anything justify my asymmetry between the explicit agreement and the implicit convention? Is it tracking something like consent, or the ability to hold someone to account, or the fact that a signed sheet creates a promise? Or is it just in my head, and the unspoken routine should be just as binding? And if the asymmetry can't be justified, do I need to get upset at my housemates, or stop being upset about the signed sheet?
