Here's an argument I built for a seminar and now distrust because the conclusion strikes me as too strong. It's about hope and optimism — wether hope is really an epistemic vice.

(1) An epistemically rational person's confidence in a proposition should be proportioned to their evidence for it.
(2) To hope that p is to want p and to lean toward p being true, where that leaning means assigning p at least a bit more probability than the evidence alone licenses. If your confidence just matched the evidence, you'd have sober expectation rather than hope; if you assigned no real probability, you'd merely wish.
(3) From (1) and (2), hope always involves a degree of belief that isn't proportioned to the evidence.
(4) Deliberately maintaining a belief state that isn't proportioned to the evidence is epistemically wrong, and being systematically disposed to do it is an epistemic vice.
(5) So hope is always at least a mild epistemic vice, and optimism, if it's hope-like leaning about the future in general, inherits the same verdict.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: it'd make the hope of parents of missing children, or of patients betting on long-shot recoveries, into an epistemic defect rather than the virtue we usually take it to be. That also makes me wonder whether the moral-virtue story about hope and the epistemic-vice story are genuinely in conflict, or whether the conflict is just an artifact of one bad premise. But I can't find the break. If forced, I'd most want to keep (1) and give up (2), but I worry any account on which hope doesn't inflate probablity collapses hope into expectation or wish.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Give me a diagnosis of the exact step, and then tell me what accepting the fix costs.
