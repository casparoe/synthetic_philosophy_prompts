Professional advantage gamblers and some quantitative trading desks follow a betting norm often called Kelly sizing. According to this norm, a favorable opportunity is not sized to maximize expected monetary value. Instead, the agent stakes the fraction of the bankroll that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, or what is equivalent under repeated compounding, the long-run geometric growth rate of the bankroll. The practice has several notable features. A single favorable bet on a coin toss receives a modest fraction of bankroll rather than an all-in stake, because a large fraction lowers the expected logarithm of terminal wealth. A favorable bet with high variance generally receives a much smaller stake than a lower-variance bet of equal expected value. Two favorable but correlated bets are sized jointly, with each stake trimmed to account for the risk the positions share. No bet is allowed to put the entire bankroll at risk when losing is possible. Throughout, the bankroll, not the isolated wager, is the object whose growth is optimized.

What theory of practical rationality, risk, and the good does this practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that utility is logarithmic in wealth, or only that value under multiplicative dynamics attaches to the long-run growth rate rather than to instantaneous expected utility? Does it commit the practitioner to a particular time horizon, or to treating the avoidance of ruin as lexically more important than any finite gain? Are those commitments competitors, or different descriptions of a single underlying view? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the mathematics leaves the underlying philosophy underdetermined. Finally, say what would have to change if the practice were replaced by straightforward expected-value maximization, and whether the practice can be recovered within expected utility theory at all and, if so, under what restrictions on the utility function.
