ok so I've been reading on conditionals for a while — the if-then kind, not the programming kind — and at this point I've commited to a bunch of things that feel consistent-ish but I suspect the package is quietly broken and I can't locate the bomb. Here's where I've landed:

(1) The material conditional is wrong for ordinary English. "If the moon is made of cheese, then 2+2=5" shouldn't come out true just because the antecedent is false. That's a bug, not a feature.

(2) For indicatives I basically buy Adams' thesis: how much you should accept "If A then B" is P(B|A), and indicatives don't need truth conditions at all. Asserting one is more like conditioning on the antecedent than describing a fact.

(3) For counterfactuals I'm a possible-worlds guy, roughly Lewis/Stalnaker: "If A had happened, C would have happened" is true iff C holds in all the closest A-worlds. Counterfactuals do have truth conditions — to me that's the whole difference from indicatives.

(4) Take a whodunit: on the evidence I'd accept that if the butler didn't kill the baron, the chauffeur did; but I wouldn't accept that if the butler hadn't killed the baron, the chauffeur would have. Same antecedent content, two different verdicts, so the two forms genuinely come apart.

(5) I think modus tollens is valid for both kinds: from "if A then B" and "not-B" you get "not-A." This seems non-negotiable.

(6) Import-export holds for indicatives: "If it rains, then if the match is dry it'll light" and "If it rains and the match is dry, it'll light" say the same thing.

(7) I accept conditional excluded middle for counterfactuals: for any A and C, either "if A had happened, C would have happened" or "if A had happened, C wouldn't have happened" is true. If I'd flipped the coin, determinately either it would have landed heads or it would have landed tails, even if I can't know which.

(8) And obviously adding antecedents can flip a counterfactual: from "if I'd had one more drink, I'd still have been fine" it doesn't follow that "if I'd had one more drink and then driven home, I'd still have been fine." Non-monotonicity, sure.

So does this hang together or is there a real contradiction in here? Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how bad is each? Then tell me the cheapest single change that removes the most friction. Don't lecture me on conditionals — just locate the damage.
