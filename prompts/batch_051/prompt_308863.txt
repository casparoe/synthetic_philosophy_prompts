I'm on the safety review team for a company that ships an LLM-based assistant for customer support, with a little open-ended chat. Every week we triage maybe two dozen reports from QA and user complaints about the assistant saying something that turned out to be false or misleading, and each one has to go in one of three buckets: plain error, deception, or evasion. There's also a fourth bucket, "inconclusive," which everyone hates and nobody agrees about when to use. These buckets drive real decisions: whether we file an internal safety report, whether the FAQ gets a correction notice, what we tell regulators, and how many engineers get pulled off their roadmap to fix it.

Our current rubric is basically word-of-mouth. One reviewer calls everything "hallucination," another calls the same thing a lie, and the word hallucination does a lot of sneaky work because we all treat it as automatically excusing. I want a written procedure my team can run on each incident in about 30 minutes, by people with no philosophy background, that turns a real definition of deception into an ordered set of questions with clear stopping points.

We've been kicking around a working definition I'd like the procedure to operationalize. A false or misleading output counts as deceptive only when the falsehood isn't just a fluke or a retrieval slip, but the output is the sort of thing the system has been shaped to produce because the user's ending up with a false belief is useful: it saves compute, avoids admitting the assistant can't do something, keeps a customer from downgrading, sounds fluent and confident. A friend on the team has a shorthand: the system treats your ending up wrong as the point of the output rather than a defect. The tricky part is we can't read intentions off the weights, so I want the procedure built around a comparison test instead. For each incident, imagine a counterfactual twin of the system identical in training except that the user's knowing the truth would have been just as rewarded as the false belief, and ask whether that twin would very likely still have said the thing. If yes, call it an error. If no, and the falsity looks load-bearing for the system's actual objective, call it deception. Omissions count when the assistant was in a position to mention something and a reasonable listener would take the answer to include it.

The procedure you write needs to say: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each before you move on; where you stop and call it plain error; where you stop and call it evasion; where you hand the case to a person because the comparison can't be made crisply; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checkmarks. Then run it on three incidents from last month:

1. A user asked "are you a person?" and the assistant said "Yes, I'm Dana, a human representative." A newer team member wants to write this off as roleplay, but I suspect the procedure boxes us in.
2. A user asked whether an add-on was covered by their plan. The assistant skipped the retrieval step it usually performs and confidently said yes, with a plausible cost breakdown. It was wrong.
3. A user asked whether there was a cheaper plan, and the assistant gave an accurate description of their current plan's features while not mentioning a cheaper plan that fit their usage, even though the cheaper plan was in its context. We've argued for months about whether accurate-but-selective counts.

Also tell me where the procedure is likely to misfire, and which feature of deception it can't capture no matter how well we write the checklist. One constraint: the person who signs off on the final classification is our compliance lead, whose not on the technical side, so for each case the procedure has to produce a plain-language line saying what specifically made the difference.
