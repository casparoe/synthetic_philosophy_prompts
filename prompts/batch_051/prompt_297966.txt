You're a philosopher of cognitive science who works on animal minds, and I want you to think carefully here, maybe even brainstorm a few candidate principles before committing to one.

Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing and can't resolve. A friend of mine is utterly convinced her dog feels guilt after shredding a couch cushion—ears back, slinking, avoiding eye contact—and she says he knows he did something wrong. The same friend watched a video of a pig in a sanctuary who had learned to carry a bucket over to a caretaker to get a treat, and dismissed it instantly: "that's just conditioning, pigs don't think." She isn't unusual. People happily attribute jealousy, grief, guilt, and excitement to the animals they live with, but the default flips for animals they eat, animals in labs, or wild animals. Then the same behavior gets described in the flattest possible terms: instinct, association, stress response.

And a version of this shows up in science. Morgan's canon, very roughly, says a researcher shouldn't explain an animal's behavior in terms of a fancy cognitive faculty if a boring one would do. It's supposed to be a neutral rule of parsimony. But in practice it seems to get applied with very different intensity by species: the family dog's "guilty look" gets taken seriously as evidence of emotion, while equally complex behavior from a pig or a carp gets read as deflationary as possible.

So my question is this. Is there a principle that actually justifies the asymmetry, or is it just bias plus convenience—pet-love on one side and a motive to keep eating and using other animals on the other? Give me the strongest candidate justification you can find: familiarity? quality or quantity of evidence? stakes? evolutionary closeness? something internal to Morgan's canon about prediction and control? Then test it honestly against nearby cases: a dog raised for meat where that's common, a pig kept as a named pet with a bed, an octopus doing something genuinely novel, a toddler who can't report introspectively either. If the principle doesn't survive, say so plainly: should we get stricter with pets, looser with pigs, or admit the epistemic standards were never doing the real work?
