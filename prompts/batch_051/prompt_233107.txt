I have been puzzling over a feature of archival practice that strikes me as philosophically peculiar, and I would like help in making its assumptions explicit. Consider the holdings of a typical national archive or university special-collections library. The papers of government agencies, corporations, influential families, and celebrated authors are preserved in abundance and catalogued in detail; by contrast, tenant farmers, domestic workers, prisoners, enslaved people, and ordinary city residents usually enter the record only obliquely — through a census return, a property deed, a court file, or a police report — or they leave no trace at all. The standard account treats this asymmetry as an accident of survival or as a neutral reflection of who happened to produce and keep documents. Yet the same institutions continually make active choices about what to acquire, how to describe and arrange it, what to preserve, and what to decline, and those choices are made by particular people with particular ideas about what is historically significant.

What view of memory, evidence, and historical agency does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of how the past becomes a record and of who counts as a proper subject of that record. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would issue in the same curatorial decisions. Then I would like you to consider the alternative: what would an archive have to do differently if it operated on the view that silences in the sources are themselves evidence — evidence of an absence of standing, of a decision not to preserve, of an unequal distribution of recording power — and therefore something an archive ought to document and describe, rather than merely leave for later historians to notice?
