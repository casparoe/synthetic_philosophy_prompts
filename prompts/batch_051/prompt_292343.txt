I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, and I have been rereading the 1958 exchange in the Harvard Law Review between H. L. A. Hart and Lon L. Fuller. I have come to hold a particular view about what that exchange is really about, and I want to know how exposed the view is.

The Hart–Fuller debate is usually presented as a dispute over whether law and morality are conceptually separable. I now think that framing is epiphenomenal. The live disagreement is about whether one can correctly identify the law of a community without taking up the internal point of view of a participant who is asking how to act. Hart's positivism, on my reconstruction, is committed not merely to the social-fact thesis but to the thesis that a detached observer can state the rule of recognition and the law it yields without endorsing or practically reasoning with it. Fuller's natural-law response, at its strongest, is not that unjust enactments are not laws; it is that the enterprise of interpreting rules so that they guide conduct already requires a purposive stance toward the values the rules exist to serve. On this reading, the dispute is downstream of a disagreement about whether legal interpretation is a theoretical-cognitive or a practical-deliberative activity.

What would count against this reconstruction? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything or has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Please distinguish evidence from the texts themselves, evidence from later jurisprudence, and evidence from the philosophy of interpretation. Which defeaters would a committed proponent of my reconstruction have to concede, and which would they properly dismiss? Where should I look for the real stress points? I would personally be moved most by a demonstration that Fuller himself allows that a detached observer can describe law without moral evaluation, or that Hart himself cannot account for interpreting the rule of recognition without something like Fullerian fidelity, but I do not want my priors to set the agenda.
