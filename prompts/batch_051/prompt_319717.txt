Here's an argument I've been chewing on and I can't work out where it goes wrong, if it does. The conclusion is that satisficing is never its own thing: it's either maximizing in disguise or just irrational. Go through it and tell me whether it's valid. If it isn't, say exactly which step fails, give the strongest reading of that step, and check whether the argument goes through on that reading.

(1) A satisficer picks an aspiration level for a decision, then takes the first option she encounters that meets or beats that level.
(2) The aspiration level is either chosen by weighing the expected costs and benefits of setting it higher or lower, or it is not chosen that way.
(3) If it is not chosen that way, nothing makes one cutoff more justified than another, so the aspiration level is arbitrary.
(4) An arbitrary aspiration level can lead the agent to settle for a worse option even though a better option is sitting right there in the choice set.
(5) A procedure that can settle for the worse of two options that are equally available to the agent, for no reason, is irrational.
(6) If, instead, the aspiration level is chosen by weighing expected costs and benefits, then the agent is really just maximizing expected utility subject to search costs, and the aspiration level is just a stopping rule inside that maximization.
(7) So satisficing is either arbitrary (and irrational) or its just maximizing under another name, and there is no independent rational principle of satisficing.

The step I keep squinting at is (4). I think it might be slipping 'in the choice set' in for 'available to the agent', but I can't decide if that matters, or if the real problem is earlier, in (2) or (3), or if the thing is actually sound. Which is it?
