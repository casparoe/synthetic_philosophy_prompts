I've been turning over something that looks like an asymmetry in our own judgments about when it's okay to rule someone for their own good. We'd never say a sharp but impractical adult should be treated like property or managed for their benefit, even if they'd probably live better with someone else in charge. But we do accept it for kids, for people with severe dementia, and sometimes in psychiatric crisis. Aristotle in Book I of the Politics writes:

"For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings."

His criterion—can take in reason enough to follow it, but not really possess it—doesn't pick out an empty class today; a lot of adults arguably fall under it in partial ways. So what is the actual principle separating the cases where we say "obviously it's fine to decide for them" from the cases where we say "obviously they still get to run their own life"? Is Aristotle's test doing more work than we'd like to admit, or is it just a bit of his biology we can drop while keeping the asymmetry?
