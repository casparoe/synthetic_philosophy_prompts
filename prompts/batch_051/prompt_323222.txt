You are a professional epistemologist who works on higher-order evidence and the rationality of belief. I am a university student taking an epistemology course, and I would like you to think carefully about a policy I follow when forming beliefs.

In the course we have been discussing higher-order evidence: evidence about how reliable my own first-order judgment is on a particular question, such as evidence that I am sleep-deprived, that I am systematically overconfident in a certain domain, or that most people with my background get this kind of problem wrong. The material has made me suspicious of a standing policy of mine. Whenever my higher-order evidence suggests that my first-order assessment is likely unreliable, but the first-order argument still looks valid to me, I go with the first-order argument. Last week I trusted a derivation that still seemed airtight even though I knew I had been awake for over thirty hours; I could not find an error in the steps, so I did not lower my confidence. My tutor says this makes me epistemically akratic—roughly, believing the conclusion while taking my total evidence not to support it—and that it is irrational. But the alternative looks worse to me: it would let a statistical generalization about my faculties override the only actual argument in front of me. Is my policy defensible? Under what conditions should higher-order evidence about my own unreliability outweigh a first-order argument that still looks sound, and is there a revised version of the policy that keeps its benefits without endorsing akrasia? Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the different positions one could take about how first-order and higher-order evidence should combine, and make explicit which assumptions my policy is relying on.
