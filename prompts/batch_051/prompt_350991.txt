I work as a policy analyst for a state senator who sits on the judiciary committee, and I need a reusable decision procedure for a problem that comes up every session. We routinely see three to five bills that create or repeal criminal penalties for conduct whose direct harm to other people is at best uncertain: adultery, public drunkenness, Sunday trading, sexual conduct between consenting adults, and similar matters. The arguments on both sides usually come down to community revulsion, tradition, or the worry that certain conduct erodes some shared moral fabric, and my senator has to vote within a few days, often without anything resembling solid evidence. I have no formal legal or philosophical training, the rest of the staff has even less time than I do, and whatever procedure we use has to be something a junior aide can run in about half an hour with only the bill text, the committee record, and a few calls to the sponsor's office. The senator will make the final call, but she wants a clear recommendation with the reasoning laid out.

I have been reading on my own about the mid-century British argument over exactly this problem, the one that grew out of the government report on homosexual offences and prostitution. As I understand it, Patrick Devlin argued that a society may use the criminal law to enforce its shared morality because that morality is what holds society together, while H. L. A. Hart replied that the criminal law should prevent harm to others rather than enforce morality for its own sake, leaving only narrow room for paternalism and public-order concerns. I may be mangling the details, but that is the distinction I want to capture.

Please write me a step-by-step decision procedure my office can run on any bill that criminalizes conduct, or refuses to decriminalize conduct, primarily on moral grounds. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and state where it returns a definite recommendation either to support the penalty or to support repeal, as opposed to where it must hand the question to the senator because the remaining dispute is one of values. It should separate the kinds of justification that keep coming up in that debate (harm to others, paternalism, public order and offense, shared morality) and explain what a staffer should do when a bill mixes more than one of them. It should also require a stated causal story rather than sheer speculation whenever harm to others is asserted, and should distinguish conduct done in private and out of sight from conduct imposed on non-consenting observers. It should force us to say whether a moral sentiment is genuinely widespread and deep or being organized for the occasion. And it should be honest about its own limits: the cases it will misclassify, the gaps where it gives no answer, and the steps that are really value judgments dressed up as factual tests. Then run it on three proposals currently before us: a bill to repeal the state's criminal adultery statute, which I would personally rather keep; a bill to make it a misdemeanor to be intoxicated in public within three hundred feet of a school, backed by a file of specific complaints about noise, litter, and intimidation; and a bill to fine businesses that open on Sunday mornings, defended mainly by preserving a common day of rest and community character. At least one outcome should be one I will not like, and I want you to say which one it is and why the procedure requires it.
