As part of a project on what it means to value something in the wrong way, I have been comparing two bodies of thought that are rarely brought together, and I want your judgment on whether the resemblance between them is structural or merely verbal.

One side is the critique of idolatry that runs from the Hebrew prophets through later theological traditions, on what I take to be an influential reading. The critique is not just that the wrong object has been chosen from a list of candidates. It is that a finite thing whose value is derivative or mediating — an image, wealth, a political institution, a human protector — is promoted to the place of the ultimate. Once that promotion occurs, devotion is not misdirected in degree but corrupted in kind: the more thoroughly one serves the intermediary as though it were the source, the more completely one is cut off from the reality it was supposed to mediate. The intermediary's value was borrowed from that reality, and treating the borrowed light as the source extinguishes the very relation that gave the intermediary its point.

The other side is the problem of distorting incentives in measurement, for which the usual labels are Goodhart's law, Campbell's law, and the literature on perverse incentives. The pattern is familiar. An indicator — test scores, citation counts, clearance rates, revenue targets — is introduced as a proxy for an underlying good that resists direct measurement. The proxy initially tracks the good. Actors are then rewarded for the proxy rather than the good itself, and the correlation decays: the proxy gets gamed, inflated, or manipulated until it measures activity directed at the proxy rather than the good it was meant to track. The standard formulation is that once an indicator is used as a target, its usefulness as an indicator is destroyed.

The parallel I keep noticing looks like this. Both structures have three elements: a source (God, on one side; the underlying good, on the other), an intermediary whose value is only derivative or instrumental (the image or finite good; the numerical indicator), and an agent whose orientation toward the source is supposed to pass through the intermediary. Both diagnose the same failure mode: the intermediary is promoted from instrumental to terminal status, and, precisely because its value was derivative, that promotion corrupts rather than completes the relation. Both also predict a self-defeating form of intensification: more fervent service to the idol, or more rational optimization against the distorted metric, deepens the corruption instead of repairing it.

I cannot decide whether this is a genuine identity of structure or only a loose analogy licensed by the word 'idol.' If it is genuine, what exactly has to be true of agency, value, and measurement for results to transfer between the two domains? In particular, does the theological claim that idolatry is not cured by redoubled devotion to the idol correspond to the claim that strengthening the incentive worsens the distortion — or does the theological claim rest on premises about grace or divine judgment that have no analogue in the metric case? And if the parallel is deep, something like iconoclasm should map onto the standard remedies for distorted measurement, such as separating indicators from high-stakes decisions or deliberately rotating and de-emphasizing them. That mapping strikes me as a place where the analogy may break down: smashing the image is not the same act as removing the incentive. Please state explicitly where the mapping holds, where it comes apart, and at least one thing the parallel would lead one to predict that turns out false.

Finally, in the spirit of the larger project, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested reading of idolatry or of the incentive literature as the consensus view.
