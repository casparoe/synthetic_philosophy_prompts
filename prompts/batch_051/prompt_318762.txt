Last night a friend and I argued at length about Abelard's claim that guilt resides wholly in the will's consent rather than in the outward act. As I would now put the upshot, a person sins just in case three conditions hold: (i) he takes a determinate course of action to be forbidden by God; (ii) he consents to that course of action, where consent is more than entertaining the thought or merely feeling the pull of it; and (iii) the outward performance, its consequences, and the strength of the prior desire add nothing further to the sin. Consider a case we never reached. A man thinks: "If my business partner ever asks me to falsify the accounts, I will do it — though only on that condition, and I have no plan to bring that condition about." The partner never asks. Has that man sinned by these criteria? Work criterion by criterion, say plainly where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and then say whether the case reveals that criterion (ii) needs refinement.
