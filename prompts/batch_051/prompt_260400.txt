I've been teaching public reason for a long time, and there's an asymmetry in how we treat religious and secular appeals in politics that I've never been able to shake. Suppose a legislator opposes an assisted-dying bill and says, on the record, "I'm voting no because God alone has dominion over the end of life." Pretty much everyone in my field, and most pundits, will say she has stepped out of bounds: she has relied on a premise her fellow citizens don't share, and she owes them a reason that could in principle move anyone, not a dictate from a doctrine they reject. The standard fix is that she can vote on her faith but has to give a public reason alongside it—something independent about dignity or the integrity of medicine.

But now suppose she says instead, "I'm voting no because human dignity is inviolable, and helping someone die is incompatible with respecting persons." Same outcome, same foundational claim, same absence of argument. Yet this version gets filed under "moral argument," not "religion in politics," and nobody reaches for the public-reason objection. The secular vocabulary seems to buy her a pass that the religious vocabulary does not.

I've never found a principle that cleanly seperates these two. The usual candidate is that the secular claim presents itself as accessible to all rational persons, while the religious claim announces its source in revelation, which is private. But push the dignity person on why dignity is inviolable and she may just say it's self-evident, or that anyone who doesn't see it is missing something basic. That doesn't look more public than an appeal to revelation; it just uses different furniture.

What I want: give me the strongest possible defense of the asymmetry—the best principle that makes the first move a public-reason violation and the second acceptable, even when the second is equally unargued. Then stress-test it. Run it against at least these cases: (1) the religious legislator who offers both the religious reason and a competent secular argument; (2) the secular legislator whose dignity argument bottoms out in a bare intuition she cannot articulate; (3) a legislator who argues from a hardline Marxist view of property, which is non-theistic but equally sectarian. Then give an honest verdict: does the asymmetry survive? If it does, tell me exactly where the line falls and why those cases don't collapse it. If it doesn't, say which way we should move—do we get as suspicious of unargued secular moralizing as we are of religious appeals, or do we relax the demand on religious reasons?
