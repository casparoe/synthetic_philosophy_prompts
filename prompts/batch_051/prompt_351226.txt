I am a novelist revising a manuscript in which the point-of-view characters' stated convictions and their conduct repeatedly come apart. I want to use Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action (zhixing heyi) as a disciplined revision tool, and I need it translated into a procedure I can actually run on the draft.

Here is the interpretation I want you to work from: genuine knowledge of a practical or moral matter is not separable from the action that realizes it; a character who declares knowledge but does not act does not, by this criterion, know in the full sense; and a character whose conduct reliably embodies the matter can be said to know it even if the character would not articulate it.

Please give me:

1. An ordered sequence of tests for deciding whether a passage of narration or dialogue is entitled to attribute knowledge, conviction, or moral understanding to a character under that criterion.

2. For each test, what in the draft counts as sufficient evidence and what counts as failing it.

3. Explicit stop rules: where the procedure returns a verdict such as not licensed to revise, and where it suspends judgment and hands the decision to me as the author. Mark those hand-off points clearly and say what kind of judgment they require.

4. The hard cases the procedure must be able to handle, including lip service, self-deception, akrasia, knowledge of matters with no practical import, and thwarted or interrupted action.

The context: I will run this procedure at least once on every scene in which a point-of-view character claims to know something, or in which the narration asserts that a character knows something, and on every scene in which conduct appears to contradict stated knowledge. The stakes are the book's integrity: a wrong verdict either turns self-deception into knowledge or strips credit from unarticulated virtue. I have no scholarly background in Chinese philosophy, so the procedure must run in plain language and take about fifteen minutes per scene. Only the direction of any revision is yours; the final prose is mine.

Now apply the procedure to these three passages:

(a) Elena knew her brother was destroying himself; she also knew where he hid the pills, and she said nothing.

(b) Miguel would have laughed at the idea that he knew anything about loyalty, and the word never entered his mind; every Sunday for eleven years he had visited his former teacher, paid the outstanding bills, and left without being noticed.

(c) That night, after years of cruelty, Marcus finally knew he was wrong — and he went on, the next morning and every morning, exactly as before.

I am attached to (c); apply the procedure anyway, and tell me plainly if it requires me to revise a line I want to keep.

Conclude with an account of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, what happens if I deliberately want to depict a character who knows the better and does the worse, and whether the procedure can settle the truth of the underlying moral judgment rather than only the consistency of the fiction with Yangming's criterion.
