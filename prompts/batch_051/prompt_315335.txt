In my philosophy of math class we've been going over Benacerraf's dilemma, and I think I've found a way out of the knowledge side of it, but my TA keeps saying I'm cheating somewhere and won't tell me where. Here's the argument as I'd write it up:

(1) Any mathematical claim we're justified in believing is one we can derive by a formal proof from the axioms we accept.
(2) Checking whether something is a proof is a purely syntactic matter — just checking that each line is an axiom or follows by a rule — so we can do it without any causal contact with the objects the proof is about.
(3) If someone's belief in a mathematical claim is based on a formal proof from axioms that are actually true, then that person knows the claim, because the proof guarantees the claim couldn't be false while those axioms where true.
(4) Our axioms are known to be true without any causal contact with mathematical objects: they're self-evident, or true by definition.
(5) So, from (1)–(4), we have a case of mathematical knowledge that involves no causal contact with mathematical objects.
(6) But Benacerraf's dilemma needs the premise that knowledge requires causal contact with what it's about. So (5) refutes that premise, and the dilemma collapses from that side.

I suspect the trouble is in (1) or (4), but I can't say exactly what's wrong with them — (1) looks true except maybe for the axioms themselves, and (4) is what pretty much everyone says about basic arithmetic. Which step, if any, actually fails, and what's the correct version of the faulty step?
