I teach a third-year undergraduate ethics course called Ethics of Everyday Collective Harm, and I am setting the final take-home essay, which students write in 1,000 words over 72 hours. The course covers causal difference-making, expected utility, and collective action, and students have read the standard discussions of the inefficacy objection to individual action. In past years the question on that topic has produced the same two failures: students either treated voting, meat-eating, and climate emissions as a single stock case, or they deflected the causal question by asking what would happen if everyone acted that way. I want this year's question to block both moves.

Please draft an essay question that presents three cases — voting in a large election, buying one factory-farmed chicken, and taking one long-haul flight — and asks students to state the strongest version of the inefficacy objection for each, then to rank the cases by how much force the objection has and to defend that ranking. The question should force students to distinguish three causal structures: a step-function or threshold case in which an individual's chance of being decisive is minuscule but the stakes and expected value can be large; a probabilistic supply-chain case in which an individual purchase has a small but positive expected effect on production; and a continuous marginal-harm case in which an individual's causal contribution is tiny but non-zero. Then provide a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The marking scheme should name five moves a first-class answer must make, each worth 4 marks, with a one-line note on the specific confusion a student reveals by omitting each move. The question should not invite named authors, citations, or reading lists; it should be answerable by reasoning about the cases themselves.
