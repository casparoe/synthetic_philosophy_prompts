I recently came across a discussion of weakness of will in a behavioral economics book, and I want to know how fair it is to the text it claims to summarize. The author attributes to Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics, roughly the following view: the weak-willed person knows that a larger, later good is better for him, but he discounts future goods so steeply — because of the weight he gives to what is near in time — that the smaller, sooner good wins. On this account, akrasia is not a defect in reasoning; it just is the shape of a person's time preferences, and the incontinent person's action reveals what he really values once his impatience is priced in. The author even calls Aristotle an early anticipator of hyperbolic discounting.

While reading the article I had the W. D. Ross translation open, and a passage from Book VII seemed to me to suggest something more complicated:

"And (2) the incontinent man, knowing that what he does is bad, does it as a result of passion, while the continent man, knowing that his appetites are bad, refuses on account of his rational principle to follow them" (Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII).

I would like an accuracy audit of that summary. Which parts of it would a careful reader of Book VII accept, and which parts treat Aristotle as a differently motivated modern view rather than as his own? In particular, given the passage, is it fair to say that for Aristotle the incontinent person's action "reveals what he really values," or does the text point toward a different diagnosis — such as a failure to exercise, or hold onto, knowledge under the influence of passion — and what does the difference amount to?
