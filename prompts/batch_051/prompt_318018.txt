I keep circling back to one idea when I think about why some areas of science and engineering have moved fast and others crawl: fields advance when they have feedback that is fast enough, cheap enough, and clear enough that a failure actually tells you something instead of just starting another argument. But I dont fully trust my own version of the idea yet, so I want to stress-test it against a real case.

The case I keep coming back to is the Wright brothers because it feels like it should be a clean illustration but maybe isnt. Youve got two bicycle mechanics from Dayton with no college training and barely any money, and they beat Samuel Langley, who had Smithsonian backing, government money, and a lot of prestige. As I understand it, the Wrights went to Kitty Hawk for the steady winds, started with kites and gliders, crashed a bunch of them in ways they could directly observe, became suspicious of the accepted lift tables, built their own wind tunnel, and worked through the numbers themselves. Langley's method seems more like: design the whole machine, launch it off the houseboat, watch it drop into the Potomac. But I don't want the standard heroic-inventor story, and I don't want you to force the case to fit the theory.

What I actually want is a careful analysis of the Wright project through this feedback lens. Specifically: what was the relevant "field" here, one field or two rival approaches? What counts as a test, and what made the Wrights' failures informative in a way Langley's public crashes maybe were not? Where does the "clear feedback makes fields advance" idea genuinely explain why they pulled ahead, and where does it start to strain, for instance where some of the key moves like wing-warping, or treating the propeller as a rotating wing, look more like conceptual leaps or noticing that the feedback itself was being misread than like feedback doing the work? Then run it the other direction: does this episode actually support the theory, or does it show that clear feedback only matters when its paired with something else, like being willing to question your instruments and assumptions? And what should I do with Langley's failure, since in one obvious way he had feedback too, a crashed aerodrome is pretty unambiguous. I want the theory-and-case mapping to go both ways, and I'd rather you tell me where the theory goes silent than pretend the story fits it neatly.
