I am a retired planetary geochemist who spent most of my career in laboratories and at field sites, and since retiring I have drifted into the governance of the Meridian Society, the old scientific society I have belonged to for decades. I now chair its Governance and Rules Committee. The Society's bylaws date from 1953 and have several silences about what happens when the chain of officers breaks; over the years the Committee has issued written rulings on those gaps, and I have been studying them as I prepare for a case that reaches us next month. I have come to think of these as small interregna in the Society's constitution, and I would like you to reason with me about what the Committee should rule if it is to remain consistent with its own precedents.

The bylaws provide for four officers — President, Vice-President, Secretary, and Treasurer — and an Executive Council of nine elected members. The President presides over all meetings and is the only officer empowered to sign the Society's binding instruments. A substitute clause provides that the Vice-President is to exercise the President's functions whenever the President is absent or unable to act. Officers serve for one year, and the bylaws expressly say they continue until their successors are elected. The Council may fill casual vacancies in its own membership until the next annual election. A General Meeting of the whole membership may be called only by the Secretary, including when 25 members request one in writing. The bylaws are silent about the death of a President, about vacancies in the offices of President and Vice-President together, and about where residual authority lies when no named officer can act.

The Committee's rulings that seem relevant are these:

- In 1971, after the President was hospitalized for several months, the Committee ruled that the Vice-President should discharge all of the President's functions, not merely chair meetings, because the substitute clause attaches to the duties of the office rather than to the person.
- In 1986, after the President died in office, the Committee ruled that the Vice-President should become Acting President with full presidential powers until the next annual election, on the ground that the substitute clause exists to prevent a vacancy in the presidency and that death is the limiting case of an inability to act.
- In 1997, after four of the nine Council seats fell vacant at once, the remaining councillors purported to fill all four seats by appeal to the casual vacancies clause. The Committee ruled that appointment invalid: a single casual vacancy could be filled this way, but the loss of nearly half the Council was a general failure of its constitution, and only a General Meeting of the membership could repair such a failure.
- In 2003, when frustrated members convened and voted at a meeting they had called themselves rather than through the Secretary, the Committee ruled the resolutions void. The members' residual authority, whatever it extends to, must be exercised through a lawfully convened meeting; a self-convened meeting is no meeting.
- In 2012, when a fire delayed the annual election for three months, the Committee ruled that the sitting officers remained in office with full authority during the delay, since the bylaws expressly say officers continue until successors are elected, so no gap had actually opened.

The new case is this. Six weeks before the annual election, the President and the Vice-President were killed in the same road accident. The Secretary is now the next remaining officer in the bylaw order. She intends to call a Special General Meeting to fill the two offices, and nobody disputes that this is within her power. The dispute concerns the weeks before that meeting can lawfully assemble. The Society must sign several grant applications and a building-insurance renewal before the earliest lawful meeting can be held; under the bylaws these require the President's signature, and the Treasurer refuses to countersign them without a lawful signatory. The Secretary asks the Committee to recognize her as Acting President until the meeting elects successors. She argues that the logic of the 1986 ruling extends to her, since the Society should not be left without a lawful signatory and she is the nearest available officer. The Treasurer replies that the substitute clause names only the Vice-President, that no chain runs from the Vice-President to the Secretary, and that the 1997 ruling already establishes that a major failure of the offices must be repaired by the General Meeting rather than by officers completing the chain themselves. The Secretary answers that until the General Meeting occurs, someone must keep the Society's affairs from lapsing.

Please give me an analysis that does three things:

1. State what ruling the Committee should issue if it is to be most consistent with its own precedents — whether the Secretary should be recognized as Acting President, and if so with what limits, or whether the presidential functions lapse until the General Meeting acts, or some third option.
2. Identify the candidate principles that fit the earlier rulings and say which of the earlier rulings is the closest analog.
3. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say plainly what principle the Committee would be adopting under each available answer, and specify a further case that would best separate those principles.

I am still learning to think the way philosophers do rather than the way laboratory scientists do, so please also tell me how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
