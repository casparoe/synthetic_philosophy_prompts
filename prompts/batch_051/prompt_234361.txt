Take the simplified quantum suicide setup used in discussions of the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation, and change two parameters at once. In the familiar equal-weight, one-copy-per-branch version, a device causes a branching with two outcomes of equal Born weight (the squared modulus of the corresponding amplitude), one in which you survive and one in which you die; the lesson often drawn is that, because there is always a version of you who survives, finding yourself alive should not be treated as evidence about the underlying weights. Now consider a single run of a branching device that produces two branches: the spared branch, with Born weight 10^-4, contains 10^6 indistinguishable copies of you, all of whom see a green light and continue living; the killed branch, with Born weight 1 − 10^-4, contains exactly one copy of you, who sees a red light and dies one minute later. After the branching, the branches never remerge and the copies never interact.

Before the run, what credence should you assign to the proposition that your next experience will be seeing green? Compute a numerical answer under each of three rules. (a) Branch-counting: each branch counts equally, regardless of Born weight or copy number. (b) The Born rule alone: branches are weighted by Born weight, and copy number is ignored. (c) Born-weighted self-sampling: each branch receives a weight equal to its Born weight multiplied by the number of copies of you it contains immediately after the branching, and all copies within a branch are treated symmetrically. Then assess the standard quantum suicide conclusion: does the original argument depend on the fact that, in the equal-weight, one-copy case, rules (a), (b), and (c) return the same number? Which of the three rules, if any, should a rational agent adopt here, and does the fact that the red-seeing copy dies one minute later make any difference?
