I am a philosophy professor working on the conceptual foundations of behavioral genetics, and I am trying to codify the principle behind my own judgments about heritability claims. I will report my verdicts on a series of cases, mostly as I have defended them in discussion. They feel largely right to me, but I cannot yet state the principle that makes them all fall into place, and I suspect that at least two of my verdicts are in tension. I would like you to extract the best account of heritability implicit in my verdicts, state it precisely, test it against a further case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are the cases.

1. Five fingers. A colleague asks: "Isn't the number of fingers a person has one of the most genetic traits there is? Normal human DNA is responsible for developing five fingers, so finger number must have high heritability." My verdict: this conflates genetic influence with heritable variation. In an ordinary population, variation in finger number is mostly due to environmental causes such as accidents and prenatal insults, so the heritability of finger number is low, even though the normal five-finger developmental program is genetically specified. So heritability cannot be a measure of how much genes do in development. But I want to know exactly what it does measure.

2. Height, two glosses from one estimate. A study in a well-nourished population reports a heritability of height of about 0.8.

(a) A geneticist interprets the estimate this way: "In this population, the main reason one child is taller than another is genetic difference, not environmental difference." I am inclined to accept this as a fair gloss.

(b) An economist, citing the same estimate, writes that even substantial improvements in childhood nutrition would do little to reduce differences in height, because how tall a child grows is largely genetic. My verdict: this does not follow. The estimate tells us about the relative size of genetic and environmental variance in the range of environments actually present; it does not by itself predict what a new or broadened range of environments would do.

I cannot yet see how (a) and (b) are both true, though I have defended both. I want a principle that either reconciles them or forces me to give one up.

3. Between-group differences. A commentator says: "We know the heritability of this test score within the population is substantial; it follows that a substantial share of the average difference between two groups in the same society is genetic." My verdict: the inference is invalid. A within-population heritability estimate does not partition a between-group difference into genetic and environmental sources. I am confident of this verdict and need a definition of heritability that explains why.

4. Selection. A laboratory has selected mice for high voluntary wheel-running over ten generations, and the selected line now runs markedly more than the unselected control line. The behavioral geneticist reports this as a realized heritability. My verdict: this is a legitimate use, though it measures something operational — the response to a controlled selection regime — rather than the same quantity a twin study or genome-wide study would estimate. I want to know whether "heritability" is one concept here or a family of related measures.

5. Missing heritability. A genome-wide study finds that common single-nucleotide polymorphisms jointly account for about 5% of the variation in a behavioral trait for which twin studies estimate a heritability of about 0.5. A reviewer concludes that the twin-study estimates were inflated artifacts. My verdict: the gap alone does not license that conclusion; it could reflect rare variants, gene-gene interactions, gene-environment interplay, or differences in population and design. I am less confident whether the twin estimate should nevertheless be revised downward somewhat. What should I conclude?

6. Divorce. A twin study reports a modest heritability of liability to divorce, and a headline writer reports this as "a gene for divorce." My verdict: the headline is misleading, but the underlying finding is not meaningless. In a society with a legal institution of divorce, variation in personality and behavioral dispositions that make divorce more likely can be partly heritable, and a heritability estimate captures that within that society. In a society without the institution, there would be no such trait to estimate. My verdict seems right to me, but I am not sure how it squares with my acceptance of (2)(a).

7. Reading instruction. I have read that in classrooms where reading instruction is highly standardized, the heritability of differences in reading skill is high, while in a school system with very uneven instruction, it is lower. My unreflective impulse is to say the standardized schools have made reading differences "more genetic," and that the correct moral is not that instruction matters less but that it matters very much. Is there a principle that makes sense of this, or is my impulse confused?

Please reconstruct the best account of heritability that captures these verdicts, and tell me which verdicts — especially the pair in (2) — the account can keep and which it must revise. Then apply the account to case 7 and to one further case of your own devising that tests the principle. When you respond, begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
