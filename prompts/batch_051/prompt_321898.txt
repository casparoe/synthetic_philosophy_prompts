For some years I have followed a standing policy in my reasoning, and a colleague has told me it is the dogmatism paradox turned into a practical rule. The policy is this. When I take myself to know p — having examined the matter carefully and found what I regard as decisive grounds — I do not lower my confidence on first encountering a counterargument or apparent counterexample. I reason that since I know p, p is true, and therefore any consideration that tells against p must be misleading or unsound; my first job is to locate the flaw, not to update. Only if a serious attempt to locate the flaw fails do I reopen the question whether my original grounds for p were adequate. I follow this mostly for conclusions I reached slowly and first-hand, such as settled philosophical views or political judgments, where a new objection is rarely as detailed as my original reasoning. Is this defensible as an epistemic habit? Treat it as a policy: when is it reliable, when does it fail, and is there a version that keeps whatever is right in it without making revision impossible? From the inside, how would I tell whether my repeated failures to locate a flaw are genuine rather than motivated reasoning doing the work of preserving the belief?
