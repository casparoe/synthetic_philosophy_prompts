An asymmetry in our ordinary moral judgments that I keep stumbling over. When someone cannot restrain their appetites for food, drink, or sex, we reach for thickly moral vocabulary — glutton, self-indulgent, undisciplined, weak-willed — and we treat the failure as a defect of character. But when someone cannot restrain an appetite for honour, status, or work, we call them driven, ambitious, or dedicated, and only jokingly "a workaholic." When someone compulsively pursues novelty or information, we call them curious, even if the pursuit is ruining their life. And in the opposite direction, the person who refuses a second helping is admired for "self-control," while the person who subjects the pleasure of music or novels to exactly the same refusal strikes us as rigid, joyless, or repressed. So the very same apparent failure of self-government — and the very same self-imposed restriction — is moralized, admired, medicalized, or pathologized depending on which appetite is at issue. Is there a principle that draws this line, or is it only custom and vocabulary?

Aristotle draws the line explicitly in the Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, chapter 10, while determining which pleasures temperance and self-indulgence concern:

"We may assume the distinction between bodily pleasures and those of the soul, such as love of honour and love of learning; for the lover of each of these delights in that of which he is a lover, the body being in no way affected, but rather the mind; but men who are concerned with such pleasures are called neither temperate nor self-indulgent."

He then extends the point through the other senses, noting that no one is called self-indulgent for delighting extravagantly in music or acting, nor temperate for delighting in them as one ought, before narrowing temperance to the pleasures of touch that we share with the other animals — pleasures he says are justly reproached because they attach us to what is animal in us rather than what is distinctively human. But our own judgments do not seem obviously governed by that rationale, and the rationale may not bear the weight he puts on it.

I would like the strongest possible case that this asymmetry is justified rather than inherited Greek linguistic habit. What principle could explain why a failure of self-restraint about food, drink, or sex is a moral failing, while a comparable failure about status, work, curiosity, or gambling is treated as imprudence at worst, a clinical condition, or even a virtue? Test whatever principle you settle on against nearby cases — recreational drugs that act chemically rather than through touch, collecting, endlessly refreshing a feed, or ascetic practices that deliberately suppress bodily pleasure — and give me a clear verdict: should we keep this asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up?
