Ok so I just got back from arguing with my friend about Bongard problems — those puzzles with six boxes on the left and six on the right where you have to find the rule that seperates them. He kept saying that with twelve examples there's always infinitely many rules, so the answer is never forced by the data; I kept saying some rules are just obviously more natural, and that's the whole reason the puzzles are solvable at all. (We never settled it.) Here's what I want: write a fourteen-line poem that works as a miniature Bongard problem. Two six-line stanzas, the first describing six items on the left, the second six on the right, then a closing couplet. Make the form carry the idea: line 1 should rhyme with line 7, line 2 with line 8, and so on, so each left-right contrast lands line by line. The couplet is the volta — it should state the intended rule, but in a way that also lets the reader feel that other rules would fit the same twelve lines. Pick a rule that isn't visual (this is a poem, not a drawing) — something about the words themselves, the speaker, or the reader — and make it subtle enough that more than one rule fits. After the poem, add one titled section, "Commentary on the choices," about 150 words: state the intended rule, give the strongest rival rule that also fits, and say what the rhyme pairing was supposed to do.
