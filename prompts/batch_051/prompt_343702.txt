I would like your credence in the generalist codifiability thesis: the claim that the moral domain is codifiable in principle — that there exists a finite, learnable set of substantive, finitely statable moral principles whose combined application determines the deontic status of any act, without recourse to a further faculty of moral judgment that outruns the principles themselves. By substantive I mean the principles cannot be trivial or gerrymandered, such as a principle that merely says to do the right thing or to act on whichever considerations are all-things-considered decisive; they must be principles a competent reasoner could in principle learn, state, and apply to novel cases. Moral particularism is, for present purposes, the denial of this thesis.

Do not just give the number. I want the reasoning opened up. In particular, please do the following.

1. Decompose the thesis into the sub-questions on which its truth depends. At minimum address the following, and add others if you judge them necessary:
   a. Are there any features whose moral valence is plausibly invariant across all contexts — for example, does the fact that an act would cause gratuitous pain always count against it, or is every candidate feature capable of switching valence in some context?
   b. Is holism about reasons — the view that a single consideration can change its valence with context — true, and if it is, does it actually entail particularism? Or can a generalist accept holism and retreat to defeasible or default principles whose fixed valence is only a default valence?
   c. Do the standard generalist fallback strategies — highly abstract principles, explicit exception clauses, ceteris paribus clauses, or pro tanto principles with fixed default valence — succeed in preserving something recognizably generalist, or do they in effect concede the particularist's central point?
   d. Is particularism best understood as a metaphysical thesis about the structure of moral reasons, as a normative-epistemological thesis about how moral judgment is best conducted, or as a thesis about codifiability in principle rather than in practice? And how does that interpretive choice affect what the codifiability thesis above must assert?

2. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, making clear which probability belongs to which sub-question.

3. Show how those probabilities combine — including any conditional dependencies among them — to yield your overall credence in the codifiability thesis. If you treat the sub-questions as not fully independent, say exactly how the dependence runs.

4. Identify the sub-question to which your overall credence is most sensitive: the one where a specified change in your probability would produce the largest change in the final number.

5. Finally, describe one conceivable piece of evidence or argument — a kind of case, an empirical finding about moral cognition, or a conceptual argument — that would move your credence the most, and explain how it would enter the decomposition.

For reference, my own credence in the codifiability thesis is about 0.3. A colleague of mine, who holds that a core list of prima facie duties has invariant default valence across contexts and suspects that apparent valence shifts are mostly artifacts of re-describing the act, is at about 0.75. Where in the decomposition is our disagreement most plausibly located?
