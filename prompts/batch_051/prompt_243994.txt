Please explain the relationship between Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result and the no-trade theorem associated with Milgrom and Stokey. Treat the first as the claim that, under common priors, two agents whose posterior probabilities for a fixed event are common knowledge must assign the same posterior to that event; and treat the second as the claim that, given common priors and an efficient initial allocation, there can be no trade whose mutual advantage is common knowledge. In particular, I would like a careful account of whether Aumann's result is best understood as a special case of the no-trade theorem, for example as the case of a simple bet over a single event between two parties, or whether the analogy breaks down once risk attitudes and state-contingent payoffs are introduced. Distinguish the work done by the common-priors assumption from the work done by the common-knowledge assumption in each argument; explain why the no-trade theorem is often summarized by saying that common knowledge of a mutually beneficial trade precludes the trade itself, and whether that summary overstates the result; and finally, indicate what the theorem does and does not imply for the idea that market prices aggregate dispersed private information.
