I am a philosophy professor working mostly in modal epistemology, and I have found myself unable to settle whether a parallel I keep noticing is deep or merely verbal. In first-order logic, Gödel's completeness theorem pairs a syntactic notion with a semantic one: a set of sentences is consistent — no contradiction can be derived from it in a standard proof calculus — exactly when it is satisfiable, that is, exactly when it has a model. The debate over whether conceivability is a guide to possibility seems to me to have precisely this shape. Take a conceivable scenario as a set of sentences; take the act of conceiving as the attempt to derive a contradiction from them; take possible worlds as models. Then the thesis that (ideal) conceivability entails genuine possibility is the claim that modal space is complete with respect to conceivability: consistency of the description guarantees a world that satisfies it. The familiar cases where someone seems to conceive something that is nonetheless metaphysically impossible would be, on this picture, cases of consistency without a model — exactly what completeness rules out for first-order logic. And the standard retreat from prima facie conceivability to ideal conceivability looks like attempting to repair an unsound proof system, but without anybody having proved a completeness theorem for the repaired system.

I would like you to take the role of a professional philosopher who is at ease with both modal epistemology and mathematical logic, and to think carefully rather than giving a quick pattern-match. Begin by reconstructing the strongest version of the analogy on both sides, then stress-test it. I can already see candidate points of failure — possible worlds are not independently specified the way Tarskian models are; conceivability has no fixed proof calculus; completeness is a theorem of first-order logic, while the conceivability-possibility link is a disputed philosophical claim; and the completeness theorem only promises that some model exists, not that it is a model of the intended kind. But I do not know which of these are fatal and which are repairable. What exactly would have to be fixed on each side before an analogue of the completeness theorem could even be formulated for modal space? Does the analogy make any prediction that we already know is false? If the parallel is superficial, tell me what the deep difference consists in.
