I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy preparing a department colloquium paper on punishment, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than just survive it. The paper's thesis is roughly this: no defensible rationale for legal punishment needs, or can be given by, the notion of basic desert — the idea that an offender, simply in virtue of what they freely did, deserves to suffer — and the only honest response is to replace retributive sentencing with a public-health/quarantine model aimed at incapacitation, rehabilitation, deterrence, and norm reinforcement, constrained by three safeguards: least-restrictive-alternative, periodic judicial review, and a bar on preventive detention of people who are merely antisocial rather than demonstrably dangerous. The argument runs: (1) retributivism's justificatory force is exhausted by basic desert; (2) basic desert requires a kind of free will that is unavailable whether determinism or indeterminism is true; (3) every legitimate function of punishment is forward-looking; and (4) the quarantine model secures those functions at lower human cost, while the safeguards answer the obvious worry that a desert-free system becomes unconstrained preventive detention.

The audience is a mixed one and I know it well. There are two compatibilists in roughly Dennett's mold, who think the free will debate is largely verbal and that a deflated, consequentialist-friendly notion of desert survives the determinism challenge; one Kantian retributivist from the law school who thinks forward-looking theories instrumentalize offenders; one hard incompatibilist who agrees with my diagnosis but thinks the quarantine model is just retributivism with a public-health vocabulary, because prediction-based detention is still state violence; an empirically minded criminologist who will press on whether incapacitation actually delivers the crime reduction I need; and a cohort of graduate students who have read the Dennett–Caruso exchange and will want me to say exactly where I part company with Caruso. I can handle the polite version of each objection, but I need the version that lands.

Please give me a prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument. Order the objections by how likely they are to come and how much damage they can do, phrase each one the way that particular audience member would actually say it (including their preferred vocabulary — desert, moral luck, mens rea, base rates, incapacitation effect), and tell me which ones have real force. For each objection: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and a short reply I can deliver in under two minutes, with a note on where that reply is weakest and how they would come back. Include the objection I should be praying nobody asks, and tell me how to answer it if they do. I would rather learn now where my position breaks than discover it from the floor.
