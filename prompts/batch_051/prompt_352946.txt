I'm trying to work out what I actually think about when curiosity is a virtue and when knowledge is worth having for its own sake, going by my gut reactions to cases rather than any settled theory.

1. A mathematician spends a decade proving an existence theorem about some exotic higher-dimensional spaces, with no conceivable application and no pressure from the field. I think this is good: it is almost the paradigm of knowledge pursued for its own sake.

2. A man spends a Sunday counting the blades of grass in his yard and records the number. I don't think this is good in the same way. It feels empty and almost compulsive, even though he ends up with a true and precise belief he did not previously have.

3. A woman reads her partner's old diary to learn how he experienced an event he refuses to discuss. I think this is not fine. His unwillingness to share matters more than her curiosity.

4. A retired engineer reconstructs the full history of her street from deeds, censuses, and old newspapers, purely because she finds it absorbing. I think this is fine and genuinely valuable, even though nothing is ever done with the result.

5. A terminally ill patient asks his doctor for the true prognosis, knowing the answer will cause him months of dread. I think he is right to want it, and the doctor is right to tell him: knowing the truth about his own situation respects him as a person.

6. A healthy person takes an accurate test that reveals the date and manner of her death. I think this is bad, and not merely instrumentally unwise; she should not want to know. But I cannot say why this should be so different from the terminal patient, since both cases seem to involve a bare truth about one's own future.

7. A child pulls the legs off a grasshopper one by one to observe how long it can still move. I think this is bad, but I am unsure whether it is bad as curiosity or only as cruelty.

8. A fan follows a celebrity's break-up obsessively and reads leaked private messages about it. I think this is not fine: it feels both intrusive and empty.

The best principle I can formulate is something like this: curiosity is virtuous only when it aims at understanding, an integrated grasp of some portion of how things hang together, rather than at bare isolated facts; and it is virtuous only when it does not treat another person's unwilling private life as raw material. But I can already see problems with my own principle. It handles the mathematician, the street history, the grass-count, the diary, and the gossip reasonably well, but it does not handle the terminal patient versus the death-date, since both appear to be bare self-regarding facts, and it may also wrongly condemn wanting a single crucial piece of information, such as a child's diagnosis. I also keep going back and forth on whether reading a message a friend accidentally left open on a shared computer, in which he has written something unflattering about me, is something I have a right to do or the same trespass as reading the diary.

Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or most of them? Please state the most defensible principle, or a small set of candidate principles, show exactly how it handles each case, explain what it predicts about the open message case and about one further case you construct, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or requires me to revise.
