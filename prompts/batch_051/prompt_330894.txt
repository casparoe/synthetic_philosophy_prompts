A state government, worried that school accountability measures have become targets and stopped tracking actual learning, proposes a second-order reform. At the start of each year, every school must submit a predicted year-end score for each of its students on a standard low-stakes exam. At the end of the year, the school receives a per-student bonus based on how closely predicted scores match actual scores, with an extra penalty for predictions that are systematically too high or too low. The state's theory is that rewarding forecast accuracy rather than score growth will remove the incentive to teach to the test and inflate scores. Analyze how this mechanism would actually play out: what it incentivizes students, teachers, and administrators to do; what kinds of students and subjects it favors; how it gets gamed; where it breaks down first; and whether its failures can be repaired without reintroducing the original pressure to inflate scores.
