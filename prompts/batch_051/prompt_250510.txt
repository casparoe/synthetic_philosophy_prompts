I am a database engineer, fluent in relational algebra, schemas, primary keys, integrity constraints, and the practice of evaluating a query against different database states, but only a tourist in philosophy. I keep encountering Kripke's position that proper names are rigid designators while definite descriptions are not, and that some identity statements — Hesperus is Phosphorus, for example — are necessary a posteriori. Restate that position in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. As a first attempt from me: treat a possible world as a database state; a name as a primary key or constant whose referent is fixed across every state; a definite description as a query whose result set can vary across states; and a necessary truth as a formula or constraint that holds in every state. But do not quietly build rigidity or the referential semantics of names into your choice of database primitives and then announce that the position survives. Report what did not survive the translation. In particular: does the necessary a posteriori come out as something like a constraint that must hold in every state but whose truth in a given state can only be established by an empirical lookup of two keys rather than by inspecting the schema? Does my framework collapse Kripke's metaphysical necessity to mere truth in every database state, and if so, is that collapse a distortion or a helpful disambiguation? Which elements of his position have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and when something fails to translate, is that a defect of my framework or evidence that the original claim was less precise than it appears? Lead with one sentence saying what survives and what does not, and close by identifying the step in your assessment that is most fragile and the consideration most likely to change your verdict. Hard limit: 600 words.
