Give me a working definition of a grammatical rule: a criterion that separates the facts of a language into those that are rule-governed and those that are merely listed, remembered, statistically common, or accidental. I want a definition explicit enough to force a verdict in each of the following cases, not to fall back on saying that it depends. I am confident only about the endpoints: case 1 is as clearly a rule as anything is, and case 2 is as clearly not one; but I cannot yet state what divides them in a way that survives the rest of the list.

The cases:

1. The English regular plural, including its phonologically conditioned variants (cats, dogs, horses) and its extension to novel nouns, as in the wug test.
2. Suppletion: the past tense of go is went, a form that shares no segments with go and historically belonged to a different verb.
3. The final-fricative voicing in leaf → leaves and knife → knives, which fails in chief → chiefs and belief → beliefs and does not extend to new nouns.
4. The strong-verb pattern sing/sang/sung and ring/rang/rung, and what speakers do with a nonce verb like spling, for which many produce splang or splung while others produce splinged.
5. The defective paradigm of the modals: can has a past tense could but no infinitive or participial forms (*to can, *canning), and must has no such forms either (*to must, *musting, *musted).
6. A phonotactic constraint: English allows onsets like /bl/ and /br/ but not /bn/, and it repairs loans that begin with a disallowed cluster, so that Greek ps- words are pronounced with an initial /s/. Is a negative generalization a rule?
7. The imported Latin plural in cactus → cacti and index → indices, now in competition with cactuses and indexes.
8. A diachronic sound change: Grimm's law, the correspondences by which Proto-Indo-European voiceless stops appear in Germanic as voiceless fricatives (Latin pater beside English father). Is a sound change a rule of a grammar in the same sense as the synchronic patterns above, or a different kind of object?

Run your definition through all eight cases, state the verdict each one receives, and note where the definition gives an answer you did not expect or gives no answer at all. Then identify the verdict you are least secure about, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. I suspect the term does two jobs, one naming a productive procedure and another naming any exceptionless generalization, and that several of my cases split those jobs apart; if you agree, give both definitions and say which cases move between them.
