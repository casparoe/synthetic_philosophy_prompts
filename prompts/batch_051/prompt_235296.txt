I practice commercial litigation and think about agreements almost entirely through common-law doctrine: mutual assent read off objective manifestations, offer and acceptance, misrepresentation, fraudulent inducement, duress, undue influence, unconscionability, capacity, materiality, and reliance. I have never read the philosophical literature on consent as such. I want you to take the main philosophical positions about what makes consent valid—especially the roles of deception, coercion, and competence—and restate them in this doctrinal vocabulary as faithfully as you can.

At a minimum, cover these positions: the view that consent is a mental act of the will of which words and conduct are merely evidence; the view that consent is a communicative act that can succeed or fail in something like the way a promise or a performance does; the view that deception undermines consent only when it concerns facts the person herself would have counted as dispositive; and the view that coercion undermines consent not by bypassing the person's will but by denying her the conditions under which she can exercise the competence she has. For each position, distinguish what translates cleanly, what requires twisting or combining legal doctrines, and what cannot be expressed in the vocabulary at all.

Then give me a residue report: for each element that failed to carry over, tell me whether that is a gap in my legal vocabulary, a sign that the philosophical claim was less precise than it looked, or evidence that legal and moral accounts of consent are simply not answering the same question. Finally, address one doctrinal-philosophical seam directly. In my world, a misrepresentation about a collateral matter can support rescission if it induced assent, while many philosophers say deception only invalidates consent when it concerns what was actually consented to. I want to know whether this is a real substantive disagreement or an artifact of the two traditions using the word consent for different jobs.
