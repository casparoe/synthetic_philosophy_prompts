We're doing a unit on direct democracy in my goverment class, and we read the part of Rousseau's The Social Contract where he says democracy in the strict sense has never existed and never will. The way I understood him, he thinks its unnatural for the mass of people to govern while a small number are governed, and that people could not stay permanently assembled to handle public business. He also seems to tie real democracy to a very small state with rough economic equality and simple habits, where citizens know each other and can gather easily. My teacher told us to compare this to modern stuff like California ballot propositions, Swiss referendums, and Brexit. I keep going back and forth on whether those are cases Rousseau just didn't imagine or cases that actually prove his point. On one hand, millions of voters make actual laws directly every few years, no assembly needed. On the other hand, nobody deliberates together; I mostly read the voter guide and my dad just votes on propostitions based on ads. So I'm supposed to apply the passage, not just say what Rousseau would tweet. If we take his argument seriously, how would it apply to a modern ballot proposition? Would he classify it as real democracy, as a corrupted or thinned-out version, or as something else? And what is the most important thing the California/Swiss/Brexit cases show about his claim that strict democracy is impossible—do they show he was wrong about feasibility but right about deliberation, or something else?
