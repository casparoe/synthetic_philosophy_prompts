I've been chasing an argument about sunk costs and can't decide whether it proves too much or I do.

(1) A rational choice between continuing and abandoning a project should depend only on what each option would bring about from now on.
(2) The resources already spent on the project — money, years, training, hope — are fixed; no present choice alters them.
(3) So the sheer fact that you have already invested in a project is never, by itself, a reason to continue it.
(4) Therefore honoring sunk costs is always irrational, even when the sunk cost figures only slightly.
(5) But the principle the argument must use to justify (3) is that nothing lying wholly in the past can be a reason for present action. And that principle does not stop at sunk costs: it would also make it irrational to keep a promise to someone now dead, to complete a dead friend's unfinished manuscript, or to mourn in a way that changes nothing going forward. I can't accept those verdicts.

So the argument either proves too much, or I should abandon promising, mourning, and fidelity to the dead. I want to keep the thought that sunk costs are different from those things, but I can't state the difference in a way that survives the argument. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
