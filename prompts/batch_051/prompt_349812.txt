I teach a second-year undergraduate module in political philosophy at a British university, and I am putting together an eighty-minute seminar on Rawls's veil of ignorance and the difference principle. The students can state the two principles from the lecture, but they tend to treat the difference principle as a slightly arbitrary egalitarian compromise rather than as the outcome of a procedure of choice under ignorance. In particular, they keep asking why anyone behind the veil would refuse a gamble that offers a decent chance of ending up among the well-off, and they do not see how maximin connects to fairness rather than to mere caution. I want a seminar activity in which the group themselves works through something close to the original position and arrives at a rule resembling the difference principle before I introduce the terminology.

The group is eighteen second-year undergraduates, and I have eighty minutes. Design a runnable game or structured simulation with clear rules, materials I can prepare quickly, a payoff scheme that does not use real money, and enough structure that a first-time facilitator could run it. It should place participants under ignorance about their eventual position and require them to choose among proposed allocation rules, with the incentives arranged so that overconfident gambles have a visible cost and prudent choices have a visible reward. The students should not hear the name of the principle until the debrief. Include a timeline, a debrief that maps key moments in the game onto the original position, the veil of ignorance, maximin reasoning, and the difference principle, and that also marks the distinction between choosing a distribution and choosing the rule that generates it. Be honest about which features of Rawls's view the exercise cannot reproduce, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it. Please write the answer in British English.
