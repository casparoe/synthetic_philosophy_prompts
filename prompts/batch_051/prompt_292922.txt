Here's an epistemic habit I've been following for years and I'd like it checked, because I honestly cant tell anymore whether it's a principle or a tic. I have a colleague, M, who works in a different subfield but whose judgement I trust about as much as my own. Whenever we've argued about a factual matter to a standstill—both of us have laid out all our reasons and neither can name a new consideration—I don't just note the disagreement. I treat the persistence of the gap as evidence against my own view, specifically. My move is to average our credences, and the fact that we didnt converge does the work that no argument managed to do. Last month the question was whether a high-profile replication in my field would hold up; I started at .7, he was at .3, and after two hours with no new arguments I moved to .5.

The intellectual backing I've got in mind is Aumann's agreement theorem: roughly, rational Bayesians with common priors who have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities cant agree to disagree. So our still being apart after knowing each other's numbers and arguments signals that at least one of us is failing some rationality condition, and I figure I should shrink toward him rather than dig in. But I also suspect I'm smuggling in assumptions. Common priors never literally hold between two grown people with different histories, and averaging credences is a hack, not a theorem. And if I'm honest, I only do this with M and one other person I respect—not with students or strangers, even when the same logic would apply.

So: is this policy defensible, or is it ordinary intellectual deference dressed up in Aumann-shaped clothes? What exactly does the theorem license in our case, where the likely culprit is different priors rather than irrationality after the same evidence? And is there a restatement of the policy that keeps whatever humility is good in it without the pseudo-formal averaging?
