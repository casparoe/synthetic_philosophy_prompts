I work as a software engineer, not a philosopher, and a debate keeps resurfacing in my team about whether ordinary acquisitiveness—wanting more than you need, treating salary and equity as a scoreboard, comparing yourself with peers—is mainly a bug or a feature of human motivation. Rather than reach for a moral verdict, I want a reasoned prediction about how the world would actually change if the underlying drive were removed.

Suppose that, starting tomorrow, the change is global, irreversible, and affects every human being: people stop desiring to accumulate more than what they and their families need for a comfortable life, and they stop desiring to be richer than other people. They still want comfort, security, interesting work, social approval, and freedom from boredom. They still care about their families, their friendships, and their reputations for being competent. They still dislike risk and uncertainty. But the pull of "more for its own sake" and the positional value of wealth are gone. Nothing else changes immediately: institutions, laws, markets, and technology remain as they are.

Over the next thirty years, predict what happens to (a) innovation in software and startups, (b) labor supply and the kinds of careers people choose, (c) how firms design compensation and incentives, and (d) how status is signaled once money stops serving that role. I want the causal chain to be visible: name the mechanisms that would do the work, distinguish the effects you think are robust from the ones that are mere guesses, and say which assumptions about human motivation your predictions rest on. Also tell me where your prediction is most likely to break if the assumption that "greed is a distinct drive" turns out to be false—for example, if acquisitiveness is mostly a side effect of insecurity or status-seeking rather than a separate desire.
