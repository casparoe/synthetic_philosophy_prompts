I am taking a seminar on the ethics of allocating scarce health resources — triage, QALYs, and the value of a statistical life — and before I read any more proposals I want the specification of what such a theory has to get right. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of allocating a scarce, indivisible, life-saving resource (a ventilator, an ICU bed, an organ, a drug in shortage) must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp.

At minimum the list should handle: the currency in which benefit is measured (lives, life-years, QALYs); the distinction between a patient's probability of survival with treatment and their expected life-years or QALYs gained; the fair-chances-versus-best-outcomes tension, including when a lottery or weighted lottery is mandatory rather than treating only the best-prognosis patients; the concern that expected-QALY triage disadvantages disabled or chronically ill patients; the identified-versus-statistical patient problem behind the rule of rescue; age-weighting and the fair innings idea; instrumental priority for clinicians or others whose survival has public value; sequential allocation when capacity must be reserved for patients who have not yet arrived; aggregation of small benefits to many against large benefits to a few; decision under uncertainty, including ex ante fairness to individuals and ex post fairness of the distribution; feasibility under acute time pressure with an account of what a clinician can actually compute and explain to a family; and consistency across levels — bedside, hospital, and national — or a principled account of why different levels may differ.

Then I want the part I most need: say which of these cannot be jointly satisfied. In particular, I suspect that equal individual chances and maximizing expected aggregate benefit pull apart, and that non-discrimination on disability and expected-QALY maximization pull apart too; tell me whether those are genuine tensions or whether one of the requirements is misdescribed. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the familiar candidates — straight QALY or life-year maximization, a pure lottery, fair innings, and prioritarian weighting — and briefly say why. Do not spend long scoring the existing views; I will do that in seminar. A table for the requirements is fine.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — either the single most demanding requirement or the strongest jointly-unsatisfiable tension you find — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
