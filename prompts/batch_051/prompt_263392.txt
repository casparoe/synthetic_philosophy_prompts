I keep coming back to a passage in Hobbes’s Leviathan, chapter 18, where he says something like the sovereign’s actions can be iniquitous but not unjust or injurious to the subjects, because the subjects have authorised all the sovereign’s acts and there is no covenant between sovereign and subject for the sovereign to break. I’ve been wondering whether this distinction opens up a broader category I’m calling the iniquity gap: an agent acts badly by some standard, but cannot be said to wrong a particular person because that person’s own prior authorisation cancels their standing to complain. It seems to apply to surgeons whose competent operations still go badly, trustees who make honest but losing choices, and maybe democratic governments using discretion the electorate explicitly gave them. My theory is that resentment here is rationally muted, not just practically unenforceable. Is this actually a different concept from ordinary excused wrongdoing, or just the old sovereign immunity point with softer examples? Has someone already worked it out under another name? And where does it break—what cases either should have a gap and don’t, or shouldn’t have one but do? Please answer in British English.
