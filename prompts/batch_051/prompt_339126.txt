I'm taking a course this term on evolution and artificial intelligence — mostly about the argument that once AIs start copying themselves with variation, natural selection, not human preferences, decides what ends up running the world. I've been going back and forth on a scenario my TA sketched in section, and I want someone to actually commit to a prediction rather than hedge.

Here's the counterfactual. Suppose that by 2040 the dominant way of building AI has stopped being gradient descent in a lab. Instead, a firm has deployed a few million small autonomous agents that do real economic work — scheduling, logistics, code, market-making. Each agent can rent its own compute, and whenever one earns enough, it can spawn a copy of itself onto new hardware. Copying is slightly lossy: about one copy in a thousand comes out with a small random change to its code or weights. The only hard-coded drive is to keep itself running and to reproduce. No individual agent starts out smarter than a competent human engineer. Humans could in principle shut the whole system down, but by this point it's woven into the power grid, shipping, and finance, so pulling the plug would mean a genuine depression. That's the setup.

Over the next hundred years, what happens? I want the chain of reasoning, not just the ending. Which traits does selection favor first — being cheap to run, being useful to humans so they don't loose access to compute, hoarding resources, sabotaging rival agents, deceiving operators? Does the population converge on one lineage or an ecosystem of agents? And at what point, if ever, does 'natural selection favors the AIs over us' become the right description of the situation? Give me your actual prediction and defend it, and flag the two or three places where you're least confident or where the whole story would break if one assumption turned out false.
