I'm a PhD student working on the metaphysics of causation, mostly on omissions, and I've been using a homemade four-part test for calling something a cause. A cause has to be (1) an actual event with a location in space and time, (2) counterfactually necessary for the effect—if it hadn't occurred, the effect wouldn't have occurred, (3) linked to the effect by some physical process or mechanism, and (4) earlier than the effect. I thought these tracked how I actually talk about causes in physical cases. But I keep getting stuck on this scenario: a toddler is drowning in a shallow, calm swimming pool. Three adults stand at the edge and do nothing: the child's father, an off-duty lifeguard, and a random tourist. Any one of them could save the child easily, but each watches the child go under, and the child dies. The father's failure to jump is the thing everyone—including me—wants to call a cause of the death. But under (1) there's no event, under (3) there's no process leading from a non-occurrence to the death, and under (2) the father's individual omission isn't counterfactually necessary: if he had also done nothing but either the lifeguard or the tourist had jumped, the child would have lived. You only get necessity if you hold fixed, contrary to fact, that both of the others would still do nothing. So does the father's omission qualify as a cause on my four criteria? And if it doesn't, does that show the criteria need revising, or that talk of omissions as causes is just something else—moral or legal responsibility dressed up as causation? And here's the part that nags at me: if I have to add a fifth criterion about duties or expectations to make the father count, does that expose something I've been quietly relying on in the "pure" physical cases too, not just in omission cases?
