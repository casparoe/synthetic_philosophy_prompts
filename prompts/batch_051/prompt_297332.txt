Spinoza’s handling of Buridan’s ass in the Scholium to Proposition 49 of Part II of the Ethics has always struck me as a case where a philosopher buys a conclusion too cheaply. As I read the passage, Spinoza grants that a human being placed in a state of perfect equilibrium between two equally attractive objects—if such a state is possible—would not act and would die. But he refuses to treat this as a demonstration of free will. His move is to say that a creature who died in that way would not be functioning as a thinking human being; the example succeeds only because it describes something more like an ass.

A friend in a decision-theory discussion group claims Spinoza is wrong on the facts. She says agents frequently face exact indifference between options with identical available evidence about their own future preferences, and they resolve this by an arbitrary pick. That arbitrary pick, she claims, is not the same as a deterministic resolution by a subliminal difference: no unnoticed preference is doing the work. I suspect the disagreement is partly empirical and partly a disagreement about what we are entitled to build into the word ‘agent’.

Design the best test you can for the empirical component: the claim that a human or nonhuman agent can occupy a state of exact, not merely approximate, indifference, and that choice from such a state is genuinely arbitrary rather than settled by an unnoticed asymmetry. Specify the subject population or setup, the behavioral or neural markers you would measure, which result would favor each side, and what controls would be needed to rule out hidden preferences. Then say clearly what even a clean experimental result would and would not settle about Spinoza’s deeper point—whether acting from indifference can ever be an instance of an agent determining itself rather than being determined.
