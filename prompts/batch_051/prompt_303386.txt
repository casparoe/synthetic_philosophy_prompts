I keep noticing a move in metaethical arguments that I do not know how to classify. I was reading G. E. Moore’s Principia Ethica, and in the part of the book where he examines proposed definitions of goodness, he seems to argue roughly like this: take any proposed naturalistic definition of good—say, good means pleasant. If that definition were correct, then asking whether a pleasant thing is good would be as trivial as asking whether a pleasant thing is pleasant. But the question still seems substantive, so the definition has failed. So far, so familiar. But in recent conversations I have heard people generalize this way: because for every purely descriptive account of a state of affairs, it still makes sense to ask whether that state of affairs is good, moral properties cannot be reduced to descriptive or natural properties at all. They treat the persistence of an open question as evidence not merely against specific definitions, but against any reduction. Is there an established name for this stronger argument pattern, and how exactly does it differ from the argument Moore gives in Principia Ethica? I want to understand whether the step from definitional failure to antireductionism is a simple extension of Moore’s reasoning, a separate fallacy, or something already discussed under a label I have not yet learned. If no established name fits, could you characterize the boundary between legitimate semantic tests for a proposed identification and this stronger conclusion? Please answer in American English.
