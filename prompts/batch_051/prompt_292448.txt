I am a novelist, and I am trying to reason my way through a statutory provision that may or may not reach a practice my publisher now wants to impose. I would like your considered judgement on the interpretation.

The provision is section 7(1) of the Cognitive Liberty Act 2031:

"No person shall, by means of a neurodevice, obtain, record, or process neural data from another person for the purpose of reading, interpreting, or decoding the content of that person's thoughts; and no consent to such processing shall be made a condition of employment or of the supply of goods or services."

I write my novels at a neural interface that turns my subvocal speech (words formed silently rather than spoken aloud) and my overt speech into text. My new publisher has made it a condition of the contract that, during all drafting sessions, I wear a second device supplied by a verification firm. That device records electroencephalographic and optical signals while I compose, but it does not decode any word, image, or proposition in my head. It reduces the signal to a single "Authentic Composition Score": a measure of how strongly the pattern and timing of my cortical activity match the prose that appears in the manuscript in the same interval. The score is used to certify that the text was produced by my own brain rather than pasted from a generative model. The raw waveform is discarded within one second; only the score is retained and transmitted to the publisher.

The drafters of section 7(1) obviously had in mind interrogators extracting hidden confessions, employers reading workers' unspoken grievances, and marketers harvesting concealed preferences. They did not foresee a device whose whole purpose is to establish, not what a person is thinking, but whether a text already on the page was generated by that person's own mind.

Does section 7(1) catch this device and this contractual condition, or does it fall short of them? I care less about the practical outcome than about what each reading has to assume and whether any coherent construction reaches the device. Please argue the question from the words of the provision, its evident purpose, what the drafters would have said had this device been put to them, and what a reasonable reader of the statute would take it to mean. Then say which of these standpoints ought to prevail here and why.

Take the role of a senior jurist and philosopher of cognitive liberty. Work through the rival readings carefully before you commit yourself, and be precise about the exact point in the statutory language on which the case turns. Begin your answer with your bottom line in the very first sentence, and only after that give your reasoning; do not restate my question. Use British English throughout.
