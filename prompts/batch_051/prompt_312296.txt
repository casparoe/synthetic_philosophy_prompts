I spent my career in geochemistry and only started reading philosophy after I retired, so I'll ask this in the plain way it's puzzling me. I've been working through Elizabeth Anscombe's protest at Oxford's plan to give Truman an honorary degree. Her claim, as I understand it, is that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki weren't just regrettable but murder, because you may not deliberately kill the innocent as a means to your own end, even when the end is as good as ending a war.

What I can't square is this. Anscombe was a Catholic, and Catholic moral theology has the doctrine of double effect, which says an act with both good and bad results can be permissible as long as the bad result isn't intended as an end or as a means and there's a proportionally serious reason. Truman's stated aim was to force a quick Japanese surrender and avoid an invasion; the civilian deaths were a forseeable side effect of destroying war-making capacity, not something he wanted for its own sake. So shouldn't the doctrine of double effect, of all things, let Truman off? Why does Anscombe treat the intended/foreseen distinction as no help to his defense? It looks like she's either misapplying her own tradition or secretly commited to pacifism. Where am I going wrong?
