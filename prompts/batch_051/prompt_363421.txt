Please write me a "which resolution of the paradox of phenomenal judgment do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for an advanced undergraduate philosophy of mind course. The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete scenario or a forced choice between specific claims, with two or three answer options per question and a scoring key. The aim is to sort students by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question may mention a position's name or ask students to identify their view directly.

Open with a short preamble that states the paradox in your own words: the apparent incompatibility among the causal closure of the physical, the causal inertness of phenomenal states, and the claim that our judgments about our own phenomenal states are justified or even genuinely refer to them. Then identify the four or five load-bearing commitments that generate the paradox. These should include at least: (a) whether the causal closure of the physical is accepted; (b) whether a judgment's justification or its being about something requires causal contact with that thing; (c) whether a physical/functional duplicate who makes identical reports has the same warrant; (d) whether phenomenal concepts can be fully possessed in virtue of physical/functional facts; and (e) whether the introspective datum of phenomenal consciousness is to be taken at face value or explained away. Construct the quiz from the logical space of answers to those commitments, not from a list of position names: first write the questions and the answer patterns they allow, and only then assign actual positions to the occupied cells.

Each question should use a concrete case. Suggested material, to be supplemented or replaced as you see fit: a blindsight-style subject who can accurately report a stimulus while denying any visual experience; an atom-for-atom physical duplicate who reports experiences; a drug that eliminates all qualitative character while leaving every report, behavior, and task score unchanged; a subject whose verbal report of a pain reliably occurs just before the onset of the pain's qualitative character; and a patient with vivid pain qualia but no correlated behavior or verbal output. The answer options should force a choice among the commitments already identified rather than between vague adjectives.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least six positions: (i) interactionist dualism or any view that rejects causal closure; (ii) type-B physicalism, with or without the phenomenal-concept strategy; (iii) representationalism about phenomenal character, if your questions can separate it from type-B physicalism, otherwise explicitly folded into it; (iv) illusionism or eliminativism about phenomenal properties; (v) quietist epiphenomenalism, which accepts the paradox but denies that knowledge of one's own experience requires causal contact; and (vi) skeptical epiphenomenalism, which accepts the paradox and bites the bullet about self-stultification. If any two of these cannot be separated by the questions you have written, say so rather than papering over the difference, and state what additional question would separate them. For each combination of commitments that no actual position occupies, note whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, give each leaf one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment, phrased for a student who has just learned that the quiz classifies them there.
