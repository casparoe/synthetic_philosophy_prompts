I keep flip-flopping on whether simplicty should count as a virtue in ethical theory, not just a tiebreaker. On the theory side, I'm drawn to views with one fundamental right-making feature — something like maximize well-being, or act only on principles no one could reasonably reject. They have that clean, few-parameters feel: one explanatory idea generates the whole right/wrong distinction, instead of a list that just catalogues our reactions. When someone offers a W. D. Ross-style list of prima facie duties — fidelity, gratitude, beneficence, non-maleficence, and so on — I want to ask why that list and not another, and how you adjudicate conflicts between items without secretly invoking a hidden master principle. Parsimony feels like the right safeguard against gerrymandering.

But then I run the one-principle views through my own judgments and they keep failing in the same way. Take a promise I make to a friend on their deathbed: I'll see to it that their teenage kid gets a box of the friend's letters when the kid turns 18. Suppose that when the time comes I know the kid would be happier never knowing the letters exist, no one else is aware of the promise, and destroying the letters would produce slightly better consequences overall. The simplest consequentialist view says: destroy them; the promise itself adds nothing beyond its effects. I find that not just false but deeply wrong. The promise is a separate source of reasons, and a bump in aggregate happiness doesn't just cancel it. And it isn't only promises. Doing vs. allowing, intending vs. foreseeing, needing a much stronger justification to disregard one's own child than a stranger's — these all feel like real structure in the moral landscape, and each is another primitive the simple theory has to either deny or demote to a "useful heuristic." At some point "heuristic" starts to sound like a way of not listening to the data.

So I'm trying to figure out which side should bend. I could debunk the intuitions: they're shaped by culture, evolution, sloppy affect, and thought experiments with weird stakes, so a parsimonious theory is exactly what we should want when intuitions are noisy. Or I could debunk the demand for parsimony: in physics, simplicity tracks something we can later test, but in ethics the "data" just are our considered judgments; if the moral facts are irreducibly plural, then a clean one-principle theory is a worse theory, not a better one — like fitting a straight line through data that is genuinely curved.

What I'd like is a negotiated verdict rather than a side. Tell me what it would take for the intuitions to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the parsimony-demand to be the thing that goes. Is there a credible move where the one-principle view earns those distinctions — promises, doing/allowing, special ties to kin — from something like the structure of agency or the need for stable expectations, rather than positing extra moral primitives? And if so, how do I tell that apart from just redescribing complexity in a simpler-looking vocabulary? Concretely: is there a way to tell when a moral theory has crossed from clean into overfit?
