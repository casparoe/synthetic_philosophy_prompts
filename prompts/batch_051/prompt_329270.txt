I have been arguing with a friend about a claim that strikes me as both hard to refute and probably too quick. The claim is that a human mind is a finite system — a finite number of neurons, finitely many synaptic configurations, and a finite number of functionally relevant states — so the space of possible human experiences is itself finite. If that is right, then a sufficiently long-lived person, and a fortiori an immortal one, would eventually traverse the whole space; after some point there would be nothing left that could feel novel, and every thought, sensation, or aesthetic response would be a rerun of something already undergone. My friend takes this to show that a vastly extended life would necessarily become a kind of eternal repetition, and therefore could not remain worth living. I suspect there is a slide somewhere in this argument, but I have trouble saying exactly where the slide occurs.

I would like an explanation of how to think about this issue in a conceptually careful way. Specifically, I want to understand what "finite" is doing in the argument: whether a finite set of underlying physical or computational states really entails a finite set of experiences in the relevant sense, and whether "I have had this experience-type before" is the correct criterion for an experience failing to be new. I am less interested in loopholes such as the observation that the brain physically changes across a lifetime, and more interested in the conceptual structure — for instance, the relation between a finite vocabulary of possible states and an indefinitely long sequence of experiences, and the question of whether exhausting a catalogue of experience-types would amount to exhausting experience itself. If the conclusion is mistaken, I want to know which premise, or which step, is doing the misleading work.
