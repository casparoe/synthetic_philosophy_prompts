Section 14(2) of my university's Statutes reads: "A candidate for tenure shall be judged by the contribution her work makes to the discipline in which she was appointed, and the standards of that discipline shall govern the assessment."

A lecturer was appointed in 2016 to the Department of Molecular Biology. Her early work was praised as rigorous mechanistic molecular biology: she dissected a signalling pathway, established causal roles by intervention, and published in the journals the field then regarded as authoritative. From around 2020, her output changed. She now publishes integrative modelling studies that treat pathway behaviour as an emergent property of network architecture; her papers rarely establish a mechanism by direct intervention, and they are read as founding contributions to what is increasingly treated as a distinct field—systems, or network, biology. The university has since split these into separate departments, but her appointment, and therefore her tenure review, sits with Molecular Biology.

She has now come up for tenure review, and the committee must apply Section 14(2). The clause was drafted on the assumption that a discipline would persist through a single career with continuous standards. In this case that assumption has failed. Reviewers drawn from the old discipline say her recent work fails the standards of molecular biology as they practise and teach it, because it does not establish mechanisms by intervention. Reviewers from the new programme say those standards have been superseded and cannot measure what her work contributes, because the explanatory aims themselves have changed. No one disputes that her early and late work are intellectually serious or that they form a continuous trajectory. The dispute is what Section 14(2) requires when "the discipline in which she was appointed" no longer picks out a single community with a single set of assessment standards.

How should the committee read Section 14(2) in this case? I would like an answer that argues from the wording, from the purpose of the clause, from what its drafters would most plausibly have said had they been asked about a discipline caught in a paradigm shift, and from what a reasonable reader of the Statutes would take the clause to mean. Then say which of these approaches should control, and whether a Kuhnian picture of incommensurability—on which the old and new programmes lack a shared standard for judging "contribution"—shows that the clause's presupposition has broken in a way that calls for amendment, or whether the clause, read honestly, already yields a defensible answer.
