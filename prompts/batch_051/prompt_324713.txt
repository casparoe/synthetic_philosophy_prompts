For a feature I am developing on how old debates about moral psychology resurface in contemporary education, I would like a dialogue between a defender of Mencius's view that human nature is good and a defender of Xunzi's view that human nature is bad, or at least unruly, and must be reshaped through deliberate effort. Please write the dialogue as a realistic, substantive exchange rather than two alternating position statements. Both characters should be historically informed and equally resourceful; neither should be a strawman.

Set the exchange in a single concrete situation: the two are visiting a school whose teachers are split over how to design moral education. One faction wants to build lessons around eliciting children's spontaneous sympathy, for example by having them encounter another child in distress and reflect on their own impulse to help. The other faction wants to treat moral conduct as a skill installed from outside through drills, explicit rules, and consequences, on the grounds that children left to their own impulses will grab, hit, and exclude.

The Mencian should develop the idea of the four sprouts, incipient moral predispositions that require careful nurture, and should argue that widespread bad behavior does not show that human nature is bad; it shows that the sprouts have been starved or cut back by circumstances. The Xunzian should develop the idea that our raw nature tends toward self-interest and conflict, that goodness is an artifact of effort, ritual, and education, and should press the Mencian on why, if the sprouts are natural, they so reliably fail without heavy social scaffolding. The two should also wrestle with each other's controlling metaphors rather than merely asserting them: the water that tends downward and the stripped ox-mountain on the Mencian side, and the warped board that must be steamed and pressed straight on the Xunzian side. Let each character force at least one admission or refinement from the other, and let the dialogue end without a tidy consensus but with the precise nature of the disagreement made clearer than it was at the start.
