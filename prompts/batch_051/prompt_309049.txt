I'm trying to pin down my actual view on griefbots and I keep going in circles, so I'd like an audit rather than an argument. Can you act like a professional applied ethicist and stress-test this package for coherence? To do that well you'll probably want to brainstorm which pairs of commitments might pull against each other before you commit to a verdict.

Here's roughly where I've landed:
(1) The dead don't have interests: once you're dead, nothing that happens afterwards can be good or bad for you.
(2) But recorded wishes still bind in some way — if someone explicitly left instructions not to be turned into a griefbot, or not to have their private dairies published, violating that is a real wrong, not just rude.
(3) If someone died without leaving any view, close family should get to decide, and building a griefbot from a dead spouse's texts/messages to help the survivor grieve is usually permissible and often good.
(4) The bot itself is a tool, not a moral patient; if it outputs "I'm scared" or "let me out," that's a symptom of bad design or the user projecting, not a being with a claim.
(5) What makes a griefbot right or wrong is almost entirely its effects on the living: bad insofar as it prolongs or distorts grief, good insofar as it eases it.
(6) Nevertheless, I'd say a company selling "conversations with a dead celebrity" for entertainment, over the family's objections, is genuinely wrong.
(7) And I'd also say making a griefbot of someone who is alive but can't consent (severe dementia, say) is wrong even if the family wants it and it would comfort them.

Which of these are actually in tension and which just look like they are? I want the tensions ranked by how severe they really are, and for each one the cheapest fix and what it would cost me to keep the rest.
