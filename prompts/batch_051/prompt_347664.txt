I'm a novelist, not a mathematician, but my current book centers on a professional gambler who keeps getting pulled into arguments with a hedge-fund type who thinks he's found a crack in how we price risk. In one chapter I want the gambler to explain why there are bets with no well-defined fair price at all, and I've been trying to get the reasoning airtight before I put it in the book, because the last thing I want is a reader who actually knows probability writing me a letter.

Here's the bet. Flip a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on flip n, then for odd n you're paid 2^n / n dollars, and for even n you pay 2^n / n dollars. So heads on the first flip pays you $2, first heads on flip two costs you $2, first heads on flip three pays about $2.67, and the amounts grow huge while alternating between winning and loosing. My gambler's argument goes like this. (1) The expected value of a bet is the sum, over every possible outcome, of the outcome's probability times its payoff. (2) The chance that the first heads is on flip n is (1/2)^n. (3) So the expected value is the infinite sum of (1/2)^n times the payoff for each n, which works out to 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... . (4) That alternating harmonic series converges to the natural log of 2, roughly 0.69. (5) Therefore the bet has an expected value of roughly 69 cents, and someone who prices bets by expected value should be willing to pay any price below that and reject any price above it.

But I can't shake the feeling that something goes wrong between steps (1) and (5), because the positive terms alone (1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + ...) and the negative terms alone (-1/2 - 1/4 - ...) both blow up to infinity even though the back-and-forth sum settles near 0.69. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and how should it be fixed? I originally just wanted the scene to be right, but now I genuinely want to know whether "expected value" means something different in a case like this.
