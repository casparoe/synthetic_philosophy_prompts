I'm a retired natural scientist (chemistry, if it matters), and I spent my career arguing with colleagues about whether some classification was a real kind — an element, a compound — or just a convenient batch of stuff that happened to share a property. Recently I've been reading philosophy of language, and the same question keeps bothering me in grammar, where my old instincts should apply but dont.

Open any dictionary or school grammar and you'll see it: 'noun', 'verb', and 'preposition' are treated as real categories of the language, out there to be discovered, a bit like chemical elements. But 'words that can come right after the' or 'words that end in -ing' are treated as just patterns — useful tests maybe, but not parts of speech. Yet both are perfectly regular distributional classes; you can define either one exactly. Same thing with case. English pronouns reliably mark I/me/my, and we call that a case system, but nobody calls 'verb that takes a direct object' a case, even though it carves out just as regular a slot. So the asymmetry is this: some distributional regularities get promoted to real grammatical kinds and others dont, and most people feel the difference, but I can't find the principle behind it.

Act as a philosopher of language with a solid linguistics background, and think carefully here. Dont answer off the top of your head — start by brainstorming the candidate criteria that could draw the line: semantic content, morphological marking, syntactic function, learnability, psychological availability, or historical accident. Then run each serious candidate through the hard cases. I especially want you to test prepositions. English prepositions are a bit of a zoo: 'of' is almost purely grammatical, 'under' is spatial, 'during' is temporal, 'regarding' is basically a relation borrowed from a verb. If prepositions come out as one natural kind, tell me what makes them one; if they dont, tell me why grammar books keep insisting they are. And do the same for the genitive — is 'my' in 'my car' a case, a determiner, both, or neither, and what does that do to your principle?

Then give me your best account and an honest verdict: is there a principle that justifies treating some word classes and cases as real kinds while treating 'words that follow the' or 'words ending in -ing' as mere regularities? Or is this asymmetry a historical artifact we should stop dressing up as ontology? If you think there's no principle, say so plainly and tell me what we should change in how we talk about grammar.
