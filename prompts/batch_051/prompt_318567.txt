A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about the Ellsberg paradox, and I ended the evening with a sharper version of the very tension the paradox is supposed to expose. I want someone to negotiate between the two things I now find myself holding at once.

The theory I find compelling is subjective expected utility theory. It says that any rational preference among bets can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to a single probability measure over states of the world, and that this follows from axioms such as the sure-thing principle: if two acts agree on some event and differ only elsewhere, your preference between them should be driven only by how they differ. The attraction of the theory is that it gives one unified account of all uncertainty. It denies any fundamental difference between risk, where chances are known, and ambiguity, where they are not: in both cases you have beliefs, those beliefs have degrees, and those degrees determine what you should choose. It also gives a crisp standard of coherence, because preferences that violate it can be turned into a Dutch book: a set of bets, each acceptable to you on its own, that guarantees you a loss.

The intuition I cannot shake is the standard Ellsberg one. One urn contains fifty red and fifty black balls. A second urn contains a hundred balls, each either red or black, but the proportion is completely unknown. If I am choosing a color and will be paid for drawing that color, I prefer to draw from the first urn whether the color named is red or black. That pair of preferences feels not like a mistake but like recognizing that the second urn confronts me with a different and worse kind of uncertainty: I lack even a settled basis for assigning probabilities to the possible compositions. Yet the theory tells me this pair of preferences cannot be represented by any single probability assignment to red in the second urn; it requires simultaneously treating red as less likely than black and black as less likely than red. Worse, if I had a symmetric prior over the possible compositions of the second urn, the theory says the expected chance of red is exactly one half, so I should be indifferent between the urns. My preferences say I am not.

My friend spent the night arguing that the intuition should give way. His position, as I understand it, has three parts. First, all uncertainty is uncertainty about which state obtains, so uncertainty about the composition of the urn is just higher-order uncertainty about which first-order lottery I am facing, and rational belief should integrate it down to a single subjective probability by the law of total probability; once it does, my two choices are jointly incoherent. Second, the Dutch book argument shows that my pattern of preferences has a real behavioral cost, not merely a representational one. Third, treatments of ambiguity aversion that build in a preference for known probabilities—for example, evaluating an act by its worst plausible composition, or by applying a concave function to the set of possible probabilities before taking the expectation—strike him as curve-fitting: they reproduce my choices without explaining why ambiguous information should be penalized.

I am not sure he is right, but I can no longer state cleanly where I think he goes wrong. Is the Ellsberg preference a rational response to missing information that subjective expected utility theory is too coarse to capture, or should the intuition be debunked and dropped once I see the costs of keeping it? What would justify each move, and what evidence or argument would tell me which side to give up? Is there a version of the theory and a reading of the intuition under which both survive—for instance, one that treats my aversion to an unknown composition as a legitimate second-order attitude toward uncertainty rather than a miscomputed probability?
