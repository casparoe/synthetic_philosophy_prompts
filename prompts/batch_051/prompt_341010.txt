In a few weeks' time I shall be presenting a paper at a political philosophy workshop whose regular members are mostly liberal egalitarians working on global justice, together with two or three legal academics who work on citizenship and immigration law. My thesis is this: a state's right to exclude prospective immigrants is best grounded in democratic self-determination — the interest a people has in being the author of its own political institutions and in sustaining institutions it has authored — rather than in property rights over territory, cultural preservation, or the economic interests of current citizens. On that grounding the right is real but only presumptive: it can generally support exclusion, but it is defeated by especially strong claims to admission, above all those of refugees, stateless persons, and people fleeing severe poverty; and it does not license exclusion aimed at preserving an ethnic composition or shielding citizens from labour-market competition.

Before the talk I will circulate a short paper that argues dialectically: it shows that the property-based, cultural, and economic grounds for exclusion each fail to justify a general right to exclude; it then develops the self-determination account and replies to what I take to be the strongest cosmopolitan objections; and it closes by deriving the limits I have just stated. The audience is not uniformly hostile to border control, but they are the sort of people who treat freedom of movement as the moral default and who make every restriction shoulder a heavy burden of justification. What concerns me is that they will test my argument not with the familiar appeal to state sovereignty, which they do not much care about, but by turning its own self-determination principle against it.

Please prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the eight or so objections I should actually expect from this audience — not the ones a restrictionist politician or an enforcement-first commentator would raise — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way one of these liberal egalitarians or citizenship lawyers would really put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, and with an indication of which objections have genuine force and what I can safely concede without surrendering the thesis. Please also include the question I should most hope nobody asks, and say briefly why it would do the most damage.
