Since I inherited a small archive of family letters last year, I have been trying to establish a fact that, I have come to believe, no surviving evidence could ever settle: whether my great-grandfather changed his surname to escape conscription or simply to assimilate. I find myself wanting to say both that there is a determinate truth about his motives and that this truth is now unknowable. That pair of judgments has forced me to confront Fitch's paradox of knowability in earnest.

I hold two commitments. The first is a semantic antirealist commitment of the kind associated with Michael Dummett and the tradition he articulated: a statement is true only if it is in principle knowable, meaning that there is some possible state of information in which an inquirer could recognize its truth. The second is a commitment most of us share: there are truths that happen never to be known by anyone, and my great-grandfather's motive is a candidate for being such a truth. Put in my own words, the first says no truth is unknowable; the second says some truths are unknown and will remain so.

Fitch showed how deep the collision is. Take any truth p such that no one ever knows p, as the second commitment requires. Then the conjunction p and no one ever knows p is itself a truth, so by the first commitment it must be knowable. But it is not. Anyone who knew the conjunction would have to know each conjunct; knowing the second conjunct would, because knowledge is factive, require that no one ever knows p, while knowing the first would require that someone does. The first commitment, applied without restriction, thus rules out exactly the unknown truths the second commitment licenses. If classical reasoning is allowed, the principle that every truth is knowable entails that every truth is known.

My question is whether these two commitments can be reconciled honestly, or whether every reconciliation amounts to special pleading. Lay out the candidate reconciliations and the cost of each: restricting the knowability principle to basic or Cartesian propositions; rejecting the classical inferences used in Fitch's proof for an intuitionistic logic; reading knowable so that the conjunction p and no one ever knows p is not addressed by the principle, perhaps because the second conjunct is treated as indexical or situated rather than as a timeless proposition; distinguishing knowability by some possible inquirer from knowability by an actual inquirer; or simply conceding that one of the two commitments was overstated. For each candidate, say whether it preserves what made the antirealist principle attractive in the first place, or whether it preserves the wording while abandoning the point. Which reconciliation, if any, is the antirealist honestly entitled to, and which should be dismissed as an ad hoc fix designed only to save the principle from Fitch?
