OK here's one I want for my philosophy of mind seminar. I'm teaching the anti-qualia stuff next week — the Dennett line that qualia are an incoherent posit — and I need a thought experiment that does a specific job, not the usual inverted-spectrum thing they've all seen. Construct me a case with these specs.

The case should be about someone whose everyday experience of some familiar thing — coffee, wine, a song, whatever — changes over time, and the person is sincerely torn between two descriptions of what happened: (a) the qualitative character of the experience itself changed, while their evaluative reaction stayed roughly the same, or (b) the qualitative character stayed exactly the same and only their evaluative reaction, preference, or judgment changed. Rig it so that every piece of evidence you build into the case — their behavior, their choices, their emotional responses, their careful introspection over the years, even any rough neural data you want to mention — underdetermines which description is true. And here are the target verdicts: a qualia realist should be forced to say that there is still a determinate fact of the matter about which description is correct, but that they can't in priciple know which, not even through the subject's own introspection. A Dennett-style quiner — the sort who treats talk of qualia as a useful fiction that doesn't pick out real facts over and above dispositions and reports — should conclude that the question "which description is true?" is empty, a difference that makes no difference.

Constraints: keep it realistic — one person living an ordinary life, no brain in a vat, no perfect mind-reading scanner, no sudden lesions or memory loss. The subject is sincere, articulate, and about as good at introspecting as a person can be; no confabulation, no hidden brain damage. Don't cheat by making the subject the only source of evidence and then just announcing the realist is stuck — include the full package of third-person evidence, and let all of it fail to settle the question. Also don't stipulate physicalism or functionalism at the start; the case has to put pressure on the qualia realist from within their own commitments, not just by assuming their view is false.

After you give the scenario, walk me through exactly what the qualia realist has to say, what the Dennett-style position has to say, and why each gets pushed into its corner. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the philosophical work, what would happen if you removed it, and how a qualia realist would most plausibly attack the case without just denying your stipulated facts.
