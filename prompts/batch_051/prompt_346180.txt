Since I retired from a career in the natural sciences and began reading moral and social philosophy, I have repeatedly come across a configuration for which I do not have a name, and I would be grateful for help identifying it — or for being told that it is only an unstable mixture of things philosophy already keeps apart.

In my former laboratory I knew researchers who were, in every ordinary sense, scrupulously truthful. They did not assert anything they believed to be false, and they did not conceal results in the usual sense of suppressing points they knew to be material. Yet colleagues regularly came away from their talks and reports with false impressions. The cause was not carelessness with the facts but a kind of private calibration: unannounced shorthand, classifications the speaker found natural, assumptions the speaker did not realize were local to her, an organization that followed the speaker's own route of discovery rather than the audience's route of comprehension. When the misunderstanding came to light, the speaker could point to the literal record and show that the information had in fact been given, and she would treat the confusion as the audience's failure. She was not being dishonest; she had simply left the work of reconstruction to everyone else.

I have since noticed the same pattern outside the laboratory. A friend will describe a difficult conversation accurately, but only from the inside of her own salience: the facts she mentions are true, the facts she leaves out are not hidden, since she would supply any of them if asked, yet a listener cannot recover what happened without asking a series of questions the friend would have thought unnecessary. Sincerity is present; what is absent is something else — an adjustment of one's expression to what a competent and attentive hearer is likely to understand. The thought I keep having is that the two can come apart, and that one can sincerely speak the truth while failing to make oneself reliably understandable to another person.

Does this gap have an established name in philosophy? I have found discussions of lying, deception, the careful use of true statements to mislead, and candor, but none seems to capture a person who has no intention to mislead and whose truthful speech nonetheless predictably leads hearers into error. Is there a recognized duty not just to speak truthfully but to make oneself, as I would put it, intelligible to one's audience — and if so, how exactly is that duty related to the more familiar ones? For instance, does the failure reside in the speaker's indifference to the audience's likely reading, or does it require that someone actually be misled? How much of the burden belongs to the hearer, who might be expected to ask questions rather than trust a first reading? I am not after a bare label; I want to know what this thing is not, which neighboring concepts it should be distinguished from, and what it should be called if nothing exact already exists.
