I sit on the research ethics committee of a regional health authority. We are being asked to rule on a request from a university epidemiology group that wants to use a de-identified dataset we hold, covering undocumented migrant construction workers, to model tuberculosis transmission and target outreach. The dataset exists because, eight years ago, the authority ran a workplace screening program in which employers pressured workers to participate by threatening dismissal or a call to immigration enforcement; participants were never told their records could be used for research. The program was halted after an investigation, the raw data were sealed, and our authority remains the custodian. The research group now asks us to release the de-identified records for secondary analysis.

The committee is split. One side says approval would make the authority complicit: the researchers did not cause the original wrong, but using the data would convert a rights violation into a standing research asset, launder its provenance, and signal that unethically collected data eventually pays. The other side says the wrong is fixed and non-repeatable; refusing does nothing for the people harmed, while the analysis could improve tuberculosis outreach to precisely the community that was wronged, and withholding the data can look like a second injury dressed up as ethical purity. My own inclination is to approve with conditions, but I have not found a formulation that does not feel like laundering.

I must circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Draft the decision memo: recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; give reasons the committee can actually act on; and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than simply restating our data-protection rules.
