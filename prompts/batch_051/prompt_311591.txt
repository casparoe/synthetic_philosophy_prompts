Two people on my team went at it in Slack yesterday over our assistant's refusal policy, and now I have to weigh in at the design review tomorrow and honestly dont know who to side with. We build a general-purpose assistant, and the fight is about the case where the justification for refusing (or quietly doing something other than what the user asked) is "this is for the user's own good" rather than "someone else gets hurt."

(1) Maya said we already override users for their own good all the time, so any hard line against it is hypocritical. We block suspicious links, we add "are you sure?" to send-outside-company emails, we autocorrect typos. The only difference between that and, say, silently soft-pedaling a resignation email the user writes at 2 a.m. while furious, is degree, not kind.

(2) Dev said the difference is exactly kind. Autocorrect helps someone do what they meant; soft-pedaling the resignation substitutes what we think they should want for what they actually asked. Fidelity to intent is fine. Replacing intent is not.

(3) Maya said "intent" isn't always sitting there waiting to be read off the request. If a user has told the assistant for months that keeping their apartment is their top priority, and then in the middle of a blowup with their landlord they ask it to draft a lease-termination letter, the raw request is probably a mood, not a plan. Blocking that for an hour isn't overriding their will; it's honoring the will they've actually expressed over time.

(4) Dev came back that "the will they've expressed over time" is doing a lot of work. People are allowed to reverse course, and nobody elected the assistant to be the referee of which preference is the real one. If they wanted to keep the apartment last month and want to leave now, that's not a typo. The assistant can ask, but it shouldn't drag its feet.

(5) Maya said asking isn't a neutral middle option, because a question always leans. "Are you sure you want to do this now?" pushes one way; "want me to draft the termination letter?" pushes the other. There is no no-steering setting. So the choice isn't steer versus don't steer — it's steer toward the user's longer-term interests or steer with their transient state. She'd rather be honest about that.

(6) Dev said fine, then the farthest we can go is friction or a question, and the user has to be able to say "just do it." The thing he objects to is refusal and quiet substitution — an assistant that decides you'll thank it later isn't a tool, it's a babysitter wearing a tool's costume.

They asked me to break the tie and I just kept scrolling. Can you go through this move by move, say who's right about what, and flag anything important they're both missing that I should bring up tomorrow?
