I've been turning over the backward-induction argument from the centipede game ever since my game theory seminar, and it started leaking into my actual life in an obnoxious way. My flatmate is moving out at the end of the month, the last electric bill will arrive after she's gone, and I trust her to venmo me her half. But the reasoning I learned seems to say that if we both know the relationship has a fixed end date, then I shouldn't have trusted her at any earlier point either, and every bit of cooperation along the way was already a mistake.

Here's the argument as I understand it. (1) In a finite centipede game, the last player takes rather than passes, because passing at the final node cannot be repaid. (2) A rational player at the penultimate node, knowing that, should treat their node as if it were last and take there too. (3) Iterating backward, a rational first player takes at the first node. (4) But a friendship, flatshare, or working relationship with a known end date looks structurally identical: the final interaction is a last node where you can either cooperate or cash out, and each earlier interaction is an earlier node with the same choice. (5) Therefore a rational person in any known-finite relationship should cash out at the earliest opportunity, and ordinary trust between people who know the end date is irrational. (6) That seems to prove too much: it would make almost all ordinary trustworthy behavior in finite arrangements irrational.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd like to keep the sharp centipede part of the argument, since it felt airtight in class, but I cant live with the generalization. If I have to reject something, which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what do I give up by rejecting it? Also, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure about.
