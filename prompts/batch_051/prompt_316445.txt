I am the lead negotiator for a union local bargaining a three-year master contract, so every negotiation round brings the same dilemma under time pressure with real wages and jobs at stake. Our best terms often depend on the employer believing we will do things we will not want to do once the moment arrives — carry out a strike through a profitable season, or refuse a late better offer because we said we would not reopen talks. My staff frame this as the hitchhiker problem: a rational hitchhiker cannot credibly promise a robber not to report him, while a hitchhiker disposed to keep his promises even when it costs him can; their conclusion is that we should deliberately make ourselves less responsive to late information — less rational in one controlled respect — because that is the only way to make our commitments credible. I need a decision procedure our officers can run before each negotiation, without any theory, to tell when it is genuinely rational for the union to adopt that kind of disposition and when it is self-harm dressed as strategy; whatever the underlying account of rational irrationality actually shows should be baked into the procedure, with the hard judgment calls left visible. Give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the question to the membership because it is a question of values rather than tactics. Then run the procedure on two cases: a public no-concessions posture backed by a strike threat our members do not want to carry out, and an instruction to refuse to reopen talks if a better offer arrives before signing. Include at least one case where the procedure rejects what the officers want, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
