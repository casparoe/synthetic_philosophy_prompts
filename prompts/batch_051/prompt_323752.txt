I teach political philosophy, and I've been sitting in on planning commission meetings for a project about local democracy and who gets a say in the built environment. I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a good name for, and I don't want to just call it NIMBYism, because I think thats a little to broad and it misses something. The city council will pass a housing plan committing the city to three thousand new units, and then some of the same councilmembers vote against just about every specific project that comes up. But the objection is never to the housing goal itself. One project gets opposed because it would shade a playground; the developer lowers the roofline, and then the problem is parking. They add underground parking, and then it's that the units are mostly studios when the neighborhood needs two-bedrooms. They change the mix, and then it's that the design doesn't fit the character of the street. Each individual objection is real, but the criteria are never fixed as a set: satisfying one just makes the next one decisive, and after four rounds of revisions the project dies over something nobody mentioned at the first hearing. I've seen the same structure at the policy level: a city passed an accessory dwelling unit ordinance, then added owner-occupancy, setback, and parking requirements — each totally sensible — until almost no standard lot in the city could legally build one. What I'm trying to isolate is the failure mode where individually reasonable conditions, because they are applied sequentially and the list is never closed, add up to a blanket prohibition. Does that have an established name in urban theory or political philosophy? How does it differ from plain NIMBYism, from moving the goalposts, and from the standard story about concentrated costs and diffuse benefits? If there isn't a name, I'd still want you to coin one and say where the boundaries of the phenomenon are — what counts as an instance and what doesn't. I'm less interested in the label itself than in the exact carving.
