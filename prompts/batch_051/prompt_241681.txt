I'm a software engineer, and I've somehow ended up in a decision-theory rabbit hole — it started with people arguing about reward functions for RL agents and drifted into the philosophy side of preferences. One thing that shows up constantly is that rational preferences are supposed to be transitive: if you'd pick A over B and B over C, you'd better pick A over C, because otherwise you can supposedly be money-pumped, paying to trade around a loop until you're broke. The way a lot of decision theory presents it, transitivity is almost a sanity condition on what counts as a rational agent, not an open empirical question.

Then I came across Warren Quinn's self-torturer, and it won't leave me alone. The setup as I understand it: someone is hooked up to a dial with settings from 0 to 1000. Each one-step increase makes the pain barely worse — so slightly that the person cant distinguish adjacent levels from each other — and each step also pays a meaningful amount of money. At every individual step, moving up looks like a strict improvement: you feel the same as before and you get paid. But at level 1000 you're in agony, and you'd happily hand back all the money to be back at 0. So the pairwise comparisons seem to loop: 1 beats 0, 2 beats 1, and so on up to 1000 beating 999, but 0 beats 1000. And the person making those stepwise choices doesn't seem obviously irrational at any particular point.

So here's what I want to understand: how much actual trouble does this make for the claim that rational preferences must be transitive? Give it to me straight — what would have to be true for Quinn-style cases to be a genuine defeat of that claim, rather than a weird edge case that gets explained away? What assumptions would someone have to reject, and what would an agent or a world have to look like for each rejection to work? I'd also like a sense of which responses to the self-torturer are live positions among people who actually work on this, versus ones that are merely logically possible. Distinguish between considerations that would be decisive against transitivity and ones that would only be awkward, and tell me what an honest defender of transitivity would have to concede counts against their view even if they keep it. Please also seperate what's really settled from what's genuinely contested — I keep seeing this framed as either a devastating counterexample or a trivial non-problem, and I doubt it's that clean. If the transitivity view is well insulated against this, say so, and tell me whether that insulation is itself a problem.
