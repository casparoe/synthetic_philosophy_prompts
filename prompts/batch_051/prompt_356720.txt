I help run a weekly lunchtime philosophy-and-games club at my high school: usually ten to fifteen students, one 45-minute session, no budget for materials. I want to run a session on the strategy-stealing argument and the assumption that makes it work -- that in certain games, having made an extra move can never turn a winning position into a losing one. The rough argument I have in mind is: assume the second player has a winning strategy, let the first player make an arbitrary opening move and then follow that winning strategy as if they were the second player; whenever the strategy would tell them to play a square or token already removed, the first player just makes another arbitrary move, and under the assumption these stolen extra moves do no harm. The conclusion is that the first player can prove, in a minute, that they have a winning strategy, without anyone being able to say what it is. I want the club to feel how strange and powerful that is, and then feel why the argument does not apply everywhere, by playing a near-identical game where having made an extra move can flip a win into a loss.

Could you write the activity for me? The games should use ordinary materials -- paper grids, pens, tokens, or snack food as pieces -- with a payoff scheme in points or snacks rather than money. For the contrast game, it is fine to take the first game and invert the losing condition, so long as there is an actual position where a player would rather pass than move. Give exact rules, a plan for splitting a dozen students into pairs or teams, a timed structure that fits the 45 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments from both games onto the three ideas I care about: the existence of a winning strategy, the nonconstructive nature of the proof, and the assumption that extra moves are harmless.

I also want a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I suspect the two predictable failures are that students dismiss the proof because it never says which move to make, and that they overgeneralize it to games where extra moves can hurt. Please design the debrief so it handles those failures by pointing back to moments in the games they just played, rather than by lecturing.
