I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and for the past month I have been covering a large lake whose water quality is reported weekly: the regional authority publishes a single set of bacterial counts and a threshold below which it classifies the lake as meeting its recreational water-quality standard. On the basis of one such report, in a leisurely summer feature, I wrote without difficulty that the lake meets the state's recreational standard this week, and I would do the same again. Last week, however, I was preparing a short item for the organizers of a week-long children's swimming camp, and I found myself unable to write the equivalent sentence. I wrote instead that the authority reports the standard is met. The evidence was the same report, and the fact asserted was the same; what had changed was the cost of my being wrong, and the speed with which readers would act on the error.

My editor tells me this is not inconsistency. In the feature, she says, I was licensed to state the conclusion outright, whereas in the camp item I should only report what the authority says and let the organizers make the call. I distrust this distinction, but I cannot articulate why.

I want a committed judgment, not a list of options. Am I rationally justified in demanding stronger evidence, or in declining to state the same fact outright, in the high-stakes setting? Or should I fix one evidential threshold and then write or fall silent the same way in both cases? Take a definite position on whether the practical consequences of being wrong may permissibly influence how much evidence I need before I make a factual statement, and tell me where you think the contrary position goes wrong. I am not asking for generic advice about hedging in science journalism; I am asking about the principle.
