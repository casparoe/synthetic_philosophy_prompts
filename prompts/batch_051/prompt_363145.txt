Ive been turning over something about corporate punishment that I cant quite get to the bottom of. When a big company gets caught doing something genuinely bad—hiding emissions test results, laundering money, paying bribes—the same ritual seems to play out every time. The company pays a large fine, publishes a statement about accepting full responsibility and having taken steps to make sure it never happens again, and then nothing much happens to any of the human beings involved. Usually no manager is fired, no executive is charged, and the cost of the fine ultimately falls on shareholders, including people's pension funds. Sometimes the settlement even requires the company to admit responsibility as a legal matter, but no actual person has to admit anything. I get that this is a practical compromise, but I want to know what it philosophically presupposes about blame and who can be an agent.

Could you act as a professional philosopher working on collective responsibility and help me reverse-engineer the implicit theory behind this practice? Maybe start by brainstorming the different pictures of corporate agency that would make this ritual intelligible (the company as a real group agent, as a legal fiction, as a pure incentive system, and so on), then say which of those pictures the practice actually presupposes rather than merely coexists with. I'm especially bothered by the apparent flip-flop: the same institution treats the company as a subject that can accept blame and promise reform, and then treats the punishment as a cost to be passed along to uninvolved people. Is that coherent, or is the practice quietly running two incompatible theories at once? And if we replaced whatever picture is doing the work with a strictly individualist one—only human beings can be blameworthy, so blame and punishment have to land on the specific people who made or enabled the decision—what would actually have to change about how these settlements are done?
