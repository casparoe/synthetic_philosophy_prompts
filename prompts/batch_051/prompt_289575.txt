Last year I got a small inheritance from an aunt—not life-changing, but enough that I started noticing how differently people around me talk about money. One friend keeps insisting the money itself doesnt matter at all, only the freedom it buys; another sort of luxuriates in the fact of having it, like the abundance itself is the point, not just the things he can purchase. At first I thought I was being silly, but now I'm not sure its silly at all. It got under my skin, and now I'm helping run a little reading-and-discussion group on ethics and economics, and we're working through the question of whether prosperity has any moral worth over and above the goods and services it pays for. I want to make a handout that sorts people into the main positions on this, rather than just listing them.

So: could you write me a decision tree (or a taxonomy, if you think that fits better) for this debate, with around seven or eight leaves? The leaves I want included are at least these: (1) a plain instrumental view, where wealth matters only as generalised purchasing power; (2) a capability-style view, where wealth matters only insofar as it expands people's real freedoms and functionings, not merely whatever they happen to prefer; (3) a sufficiency view, where beyond some decent threshold extra prosperity has little or no value; (4) an abundance-as-final-good view, where the sheer existence of more rather than less material wealth is itself valuable, independently of what anyone does with it; (5) a positional or status view, where abundance has a kind of value but only relative to what others have, so it isn't moral value in the usual sense; and (6) a republican/corruption worry, where past a point abundance tends to breed dependence, domination, or civic decay, so its contribution is actively negative. If the logical space has obvious spots nobody occupies, add those as unnamed leaves and say why they're empty; and if two of the views can't really be separated by the tree, flag that instead of faking it.

The branch nodes are the part I care most about: each should be a question somebody could answer in plain terms without already knowing which label it leads to (so nothing like the question 'do you accept instrumentalism about wealth?'). And each leaf should get a one-sentence note on its most awkward commitment—the thing a person holding that view has to swallow. If you can, have the order of questions mirror how people actually hesitate: first whether wealth could matter at all beyond what it buys, then what that extra thing would have to be, then whether the value scales indefinitely or tapers off and maybe goes sour.
