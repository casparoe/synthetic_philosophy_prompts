I've been going back and forth with some colleagues about whether Scanlon's contractualism has the resources to handle choices that differ only in the distribution of risk, and I would appreciate a careful treatment of a case we kept returning to. Scanlon's test, as we're reading it, is that an action is wrong if it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject, and the view is known for rejecting interpersonal aggregation: in the transmitter-room case, a technician's severe pain is not supposed to be outweighed by the mild disappointment of millions of would-be viewers, because no single disappointed viewer has a complaint strong enough to reasonably reject the principle that protects the technician.

Here is the variation we discussed, chosen so that the ex post facts are held fixed. A policy maker must choose between two policies, and either way exactly one person will suffer a severe harm of a fixed magnitude; there is no ex post difference in the number or the severity of the harms. Under Known Victim, A is the person who will suffer with probability 1, while everyone else is guaranteed to be unharmed. Under Random Victim, one person is selected by a genuinely fair lottery from a large pool of N people that includes A, and only the selected person suffers; so ex ante, A faces probability 1 of severe harm under Known Victim but probability 1/N under Random Victim, while each of the other N−1 people faces probability 0 under Known Victim and 1/N under Random Victim. The two policies differ only in how the very same harm is distributed as risk before the victim's identity is fixed.

The question is how Scanlonian contractualism should choose between them. On a literal reading of its anti-aggregation commitment, the N−1 potential victims may not join their minuscule risks into a single complaint that matches A's complaint against being made a certain victim; pairwise, each of them has a far smaller chance of the same harm than A has under Known Victim. Yet under Random Victim someone will, in the end, suffer a harm exactly as serious as the one A would have suffered under Known Victim, and that ex post victim's complaint will be exactly as strong as A's would have been — the view just cannot identify the victim in advance. Does Scanlonian contractualism thereby commit itself to Random Victim, spreading risk thinly across many people in order to spare A a certainty? Or does the move from identified harms to anonymous ex ante risk force some aggregation of expected harms, or a revision of the reasonable-rejection test for uncertainty, after all? Please separate ex ante and ex post objections on both sides, and say whether and why the answer depends on N.
