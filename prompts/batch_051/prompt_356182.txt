Yesterday I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether loss aversion is best understood as a bias, a legitimate preference, or a blanket term that hides several different phenomena. The disagreement made me want to be unusually careful while grading the short-answer question below from a course on judgment and decision-making.

The question I set the class was:

Distinguish loss aversion from the endowment effect. Then explain how loss aversion is supposed to produce the endowment effect, and give one reason for thinking the endowment effect might reflect a legitimate preference rather than a decision-theoretic bias.

The student's answer was:

Loss aversion is the claim that, relative to a reference point, losses have greater subjective weight than equal-sized gains: losing $20 stings more than gaining $20 gratifies. The endowment effect is the observed tendency for owners to demand more to part with a good than they would have been willing to pay to acquire it, usually written as WTA > WTP. The classic mug experiments are the standard demonstration.

The link between them is that ownership moves one's reference point upward. Before the participant owns the mug it is coded as a potential gain; after being given the mug, parting with it is coded as a loss. Because losses weigh more heavily than gains, the amount that compensates the seller for the loss exceeds the amount the buyer would pay for the gain. In addition, because participants were randomly assigned to be buyers and sellers, the two groups start with the same distribution of preferences, so they must value the mug identically; any remaining gap is therefore a violation of rational consistency.

Still, the gap may not show irrational bias. One legitimate-preference explanation is information: owners have had direct contact with the object and so know better how much it is worth to them, while non-owners value it under uncertainty. On this view the higher selling price is a more accurate valuation rather than an error. This suggests that the loss aversion underlying the endowment effect can itself be a legitimate preference rather than a bias.

Give a mark out of 10 (or a letter grade) and, more importantly, provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade: note what the student gets right, flag any distinctions the answer blurs and any steps that do not quite follow, and explain how each issue should affect the score.
