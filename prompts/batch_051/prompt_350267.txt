ok so ive been kicking around a position on peer disagreement that i call "peer decay" and i want to know whether its actually a new view or just a garbled version of something in the conciliationism vs steadfastness debate.

the setup: you find out that someone you took to be your epistemic peer (equally smart, equally informed, equally likely to get it right) disagrees with you on some question. conciliationism says you should move substantially toward them. steadfastness says you can mostly stay put. my thought is both are skipping a second step. the disagreement is evidence, sure, but it's not only evidence about the question — it's also evidence about whether the other person is really your peer on this question. so the rational response should be a loop: first, conditional on them being a peer, you conciliate and move toward their view; second, you downgrade their peerhood a bit, because a true peer probably wouldn't have gotten this wrong; then you recompute, which pushes you back toward your original view. how far back you snap depends on a decay rate that varies by domain. on this picture, conciliationism is just decay rate zero and steadfastness is decay rate infinity, and neither extreme is right in general.

i can already feel two problems. one is that the loop might let you never update at all, because any disagreement can just be met with "well then you're less of a peer than i thought," which sounds question-begging or at least unfalsifiable. the other is the case where you have strong independent evidence you're peers — same training, same track record, same IQ — and you still disagree. maybe peerhood should be sticky there and decay should be slow, but then it looks like i'm just restating "it depends" with extra steps.

so: is this a real position already, or am i reinventing something? where exactly does it break? does the decay-rate framing earn its keep or is it just a way of relabeling the original debate?
