In the philosophy of mathematics I keep encountering the following exchange. Someone raises the familiar causal objection: if numbers and sets exist outside space and time, they cannot affect us, so our beliefs about them cannot be caused by them, and a belief that is not caused by what it is about cannot count as knowledge. A defender of abstract objects replies by comparing mathematical intuition to sense perception. Just as vision puts us in cognitive contact with physical objects, the reply goes, mathematical intuition puts us in cognitive contact with abstract objects; and since we allow that perception yields knowledge of the physical world, consistency should make us allow that intuition yields knowledge of the mathematical world. I would like this analogy audited. Please begin with a one-sentence verdict on whether the analogy can carry the conclusion it is being used to support. Then give an explicit mapping: what in the perception case corresponds to what in the intuition case — the object, the faculty, the causal chain, the medium, the reliability conditions, the possibility of error and illusion, and the convergence across observers. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, address whether the absence of anything like a causal chain running from the mathematical objects to the knower leaves the analogy too weak to do its work, or whether the right response is instead to reject the causal requirement on knowledge in the perceptual case as well. End by saying whether the conclusion — that mathematical intuition grounds mathematical knowledge in something like the way perception grounds empirical knowledge — survives the audit, and if it does not, what better analogy, if any, would serve the mathematical realist. Keep the entire answer under 700 words.
