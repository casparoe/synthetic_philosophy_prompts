Here is an argument I have been trying to make precise, and I want to know whether it goes through.

(1) Deontological constraints, such as the constraint against killing an innocent person, are grounded in the inviolability of persons: each person has a claim against being killed weighty enough that ordinary gains cannot justify killing her.
(2) If a person's claim against being killed is that weighty, then it also gives any third party a weighty reason to prevent that person's being killed.
(3) In a case where one must choose between killing one innocent and allowing five innocents to be killed, the reasons supplied by the five persons' claims outweigh the reasons supplied by the one person's claim, since reasons aggregate across persons.
(4) Therefore, the very considerations that ground the constraint against killing imply that one ought to kill the one to prevent the five.
(5) But a deontological constraint on killing forbids killing the one even when that is the only way to prevent the five.

So the grounding of deontological constraints seems to undermine the constraints themselves. I half-suspect that step (2) or step (3) is doing the damage, but I cannot say exactly how. Is the argument valid? Is each premise true? Where it fails, identify whether the failure is a false premise, an equivocation, or an illicit inference, and give the strongest reading of the contested premise on which it is true. Please also distinguish what is fairly settled in debates over the paradox of deontology from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus.
