I have been working through the case for nominalism about mathematics and find it more compelling than I expected. The view earns its keep on familiar theoretical grounds: it declines to populate reality with abstract objects that exist outside space and time and stand in no causal relations, it dissolves the puzzle of how a spatiotemporal mind could gain knowledge of such objects, and it sits comfortably within a broadly naturalistic picture in which the furniture of the world is whatever our best empirical theories require. The instrumentalist gloss is independently attractive as well. If mathematical apparatus is conservative over our reasoning about the concrete world — if it never by itself licenses a conclusion about nonmathematical reality that could not in principle be reached without it — then treating mathematics as a useful fiction begins to look like a disciplined economy rather than a loss.

And yet I cannot rid myself of the contrary conviction. When I work through a proof, the experience is one of being constrained, not of extending a story in whatever way is convenient. It seems obviously right that there were infinitely many primes before any mind existed, that Fermat's Last Theorem was true during the long period in which nobody could prove it, and that a false proof is not merely an infelicitous move within a fiction but a failure to track how things are. If mathematical claims are true only in the way that claims within a fiction are true, it is hard to see why one development of the fiction is better than another beyond convenience, or why mathematics shows the striking convergence and corrigibility it does across cultures and centuries.

I am not asking which side should win. I want to know what it would take for each side to give ground. What would it take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped rather than accommodated? What would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned rather than defended? And is there a stable reading on which the two are compatible — for instance, one on which the fictionalist's paraphrase and the platonist's truth claims are not genuinely in competition, or one on which the ontology and the phenomenology of proof can both be preserved? Please say what would justify each of those outcomes, and how I would recognize that an outcome had been reached rather than merely relabeled.
