I am caught between a compelling theory and a firm intuition about causation, and I want your considered, committed verdict rather than a survey of the options.

On the theory side, I am drawn to a Russellian eliminativism about causation, or at least a deflationary view. Mature science, and fundamental physics in particular, gets by without causal vocabulary: its laws are typically time-symmetric and specify how states evolve, while the explanatory work that causal talk appears to do can be done by dynamical laws plus initial or boundary conditions. What looks like a special productive tie between cause and effect is, on this view, a projection of our interests as predictors and manipulators, not a joint in nature. The view is economical, it takes physics at its word, and it neatly explains why causal judgments are so sensitive to context and contrast class.

On the intuition side, I cannot shake the conviction that when one gear turns another, or an infection brings on a fever, something more is true than that the first event is a useful predictor of or intervention point for the second. There seems to be an objective difference between the cause and merely prior conditions, and causation seems to be something the cause does to the effect, not merely a dependence pattern selected by our explanatory interests. The absence of causal vocabulary in fundamental physics does not by itself settle this, because I still want to know how to distinguish causal from merely predictive relations, and how to ground the asymmetry between cause and effect, without appealing to something causal in the world.

Should the intuition be debunked and dropped as a piece of folk physics, should the theory be amended to countenance real production even if it is not fundamental, or is there a reading on which the two are compatible?
