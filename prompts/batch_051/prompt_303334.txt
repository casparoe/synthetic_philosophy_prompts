I'm a hearing officer for a state unemployment office and I need a decision procedure I can actually use. We're rolling out a new fraud referral system where cases come to me mostly on a predictive risk score, and I've been told to give the score "significant weight," but nobody has said whether the score alone can carry a finding. Some officers rubber-stamp the score as soon as its above 90; others say a score is never enough because its just a statistic about people who resemble the claimant. A fraud finding makes the person repay every cent plus a 35% penalty and disqualifies them from benefits for a year, so I don't want to wing it. I hear maybe twelve of these a week, each hearing runs about 25 minutes, there are no lawyers in the room, and I have no statistics training. Under our regs, a simple overpayment needs only a preponderance of the evidence, but the fraud label and penalty need clear and convincing evidence.

Here's what the procedure has to resolve. You probably know the two stock cases from the evidence literature: the blue bus, where a plaintiff is hit by a bus she can identify only as blue and 80% of the buses on that route were run by one company, and the question is whether we find that company liable; and the gatecrasher, where 1,000 people are at a rodeo, only 499 paid, and the promoter sues a spectator picked at random from the crowd. The standard line in criminal law is that bare statistics never get you beyond a reasonable doubt, no matter how high. My hearings are administrative, not criminal, so I need the preponderance and clear-and-convincing version of that line. Write me a procedure—questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or policy rather than evidence—that tells me when naked statistical evidence can support an overpayment or fraud finding and when it can't. In particular, I need it to handle the difference between being picked at random from a group and being picked out by an algorithm, and the reference-class problem where a person belongs to a thousand overlapping groups and the statistic looks different depending on which one you use.

Then run the procedure on two cases:

(a) A one-week disaster-relief window took 1,000 unemployment grant applications. The state later established that 810 of the 1,000 applications used one of three nonexistent employers with identical address strings, but a data-corruption issue means it can't say which claimants filed which applications. The claimant is one of 60 people drawn at random from the full list of 1,000 recipients for an audit. He shows up, has no documents, and gives only a generic account of the job. Can I find the overpayment, and can I add the fraud penalty?

(b) The fraud model referred a claimant because she filed at 3:12 a.m. from an IP address that had appeared in a few earlier confirmed fraud cases, and the unit's report says 94% of claims with her flag pattern last quarter turned out to be fraudulent. She was not picked at random from that group—the score singled her out. She brings pay stubs, a dated offer letter, and a supervisor willing to testify. The unit wants the score alone to carry the case. Should it?

Finally, tell me what this procedure can't settle and where it's likely to misfire, so I can write those into the office manual as judgment calls.
