I'm a retired molecular biologist (thirty-odd years in transcriptional regulation, more or less) who's recently fallen into philosophy of biology, and I now sit on the advisory board of our natural history museum. The museum is redoing its genetics hall, and the exhibit team has proposed a big panel sentence: DNA "contains the complete instructions for building an organism, written in the genetic code." The board votes next month. Some members think it's fine public-facing shorthand; a few of us, including me, have come to think it smuggles in a picture of DNA as a literal instruction manual and quietly writes the rest of the cell out of the story. I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before we vote.

Could you draft me a memo for the board recommending what the panel should actually say? I want a concrete, vote-ready recommendation — either an exact sentence or two for the panel, or their sentence kept but with framing that flags it as metaphor. The memo should seriously engage the strongest case for keeping the shorthand (it's accessible, working biologists really do say "the genetic code" all the time, and we don't want to loose the public), not just wave it off.

The philosophical crux I'd like weaved in is whether "code" is literal or metaphorical. In the actual science, "the genetic code" is a mapping between codons and amino acids, instantiated by tRNAs and the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, whereas "coding sequence" or "coding region" means something different — a stretch of DNA read off into RNA that ends up specifying protein. So the word is doing at least two jobs, and neither job obviously requires an intentional sender or a reader who "understands" the message. Weave that in without turning the memo into a lecture.

Please begin with the memo itself, no preamble or cover note, and make the first line of the memo your recommendation. Then, after the memo, add a short private note to me, clearly marked as not for the board, saying how confident you are in the philosophical claims you relied on and which point you're least sure you've got right.
