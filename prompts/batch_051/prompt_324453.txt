I got elected to our county board last year, and two months in I cast a vote that still keeps me up: I supported a warehouse development next to a protected wetland because the county attorney made it clear that if we blocked it the developers would sue, we'd almost certainly lose, and the damages would blow a hole in the general fund that would have meant cutting the mobile-health van and after-school busing. I still think the vote was wrong on its own terms, and I also still think I'd do it again. A friend who teaches political theory just said "oh, dirty hands" and changed the subject, which was not exactly helpful.

Anyway, I now have to lead a ninety-minute session on this for our county's civic leadership program. The group is about twenty people — small-business owners, a couple of pastors, nonprofit staff, two high-school seniors — none of them philosophy people. They'll mostly arrive in one of two camps: either "sometimes you have to get your hands dirty, grow up," or "if a politician says they had no choice, they're just rationalizing." I want them to leave seeing that the interesting thing isn't choosing between those two, but that a person can act rightly and still be stained, and that the stain doesn't disappear just because the decision was justified.

Can you give me a session plan rather than the talk itself? What idea has to be in place before the phrase "dirty hands" even means something to this group, and what should come after that in what order? What's the one carrying example I should return to all the way through — I was going to use my warehouse vote, but I'm worried a too-close-to-home example makes people defend their own instincts instead of think, so would a classic emergency case or something smaller-scale be better? What misconceptions should I expect — I'm sure someone will say "if it was the right thing to do it can't also be wrong" and someone else will say "so politics just means being a hypocrite" — and where exactly in the sequence should I head each one off? How do I check at the end that they've actually understood the idea of a moral remainder, rather than just become more comfortable with cynicism? And what should I deliberately leave out in ninety minutes, and say so? End your answer with a one-sentence summary I could put on the last slide.
