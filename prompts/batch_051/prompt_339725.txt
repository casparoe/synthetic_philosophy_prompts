I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a feature about why students' mistakes in physics are so predictable. I want to open the piece with a concrete classroom example that seperates two rival accounts of what a misconception even is.

First account: a kid who gets physics wrong is running a coherent alternative theory in her head — a whole naive mechanics that's stable across situations, so if she believes motion needs a force, she'll get a family of related problems wrong in the same way every time. Second account: there's no such theory. The kid has a loose toolkit of little gut-level rules of thumb, and she assembles an answer on the spot from whichever pieces get triggered by the particular wording and setup. On that view, errors are local and context-dependent, not the fingerprint of one hidden model.

I need you to build me a case that splits these two apart. Design a short sequence of three or four written physics questions, ordinary middle-school mechanics, no lab equipment, where the coherent-theory account predicts one pattern of wrong answers and the knowledge-in-pieces account predicts a different pattern. The hinge I want you to exploit is this: hold the same underlying bit of physics constant across the questions, but change the surface setup so that different rules of thumb would get triggered. The coherent-theory kid should keep giving the same wrong answer; the knowledge-in-pieces kid should flip-flop as the surfaces change. But it has to come from the questions themselves, not from stipulating whats in the kid's head.

Then do the check. Go through each account and say what it predicts for each question and why. Tell me exactly which structural feature of the sequence does the separating work, and what happens if I delete that feature. Finish with the best attack a defender of the account that comes out looking worse would make on the case — where's it most vulnerable to being called rigged? The point is a feature opener, so it has to be readable by someone who's never heard of either camp, no jargon beyond what I've just used.
