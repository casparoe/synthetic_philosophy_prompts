I am a newly elected mayor of a small city, and although I have no formal background in philosophy, I have spent the last several weeks turning over a decision that I now have to make. I want a careful, principled analysis rather than a pep talk.

I ran for office on two promises above all others: to protect the low-income Riverside neighborhood from redevelopment and displacement, and never to knowingly mislead the public. Last month, the state government offered to fully fund the long-overdue reconstruction of our flood barriers, which engineers say will likely fail in the next severe storm season if they are not rebuilt soon. The offer is tied to a development bill, however, one that would demolish much of Riverside's affordable housing and displace several hundred people. To get the bill through the city council quickly, the state has also made clear that I would need to present the displacement figures in a deliberately misleading way and stay quiet about an internal report that identifies serious risks to the people who would be moved. I have tried hard to find a third option, but none exists. If I refuse, the barriers remain unrepaired and thousands of people face flooding, including many in Riverside. If I accept, I break my most important promises, knowingly mislead the public, and help push out the people I swore to protect.

What I cannot settle is the moral structure of the choice, and that is what I need help with. Is it possible to do something that is genuinely wrong and still be doing what, all things considered, one ought to do? If I take the deal, does the wrongness get canceled out by the thousands of people saved, or does it remain attached to me? Should I feel guilty afterward even if I believe I chose the only responsible option? And would it make sense to say I had become a good politician but a worse person, or is that just a confused way of avoiding the truth?

Please answer as a professional moral and political philosopher. Before settling on your view, work through the strongest rival positions and what each would say about this situation, including the temptation toward each easy answer. Then lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, followed by your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
