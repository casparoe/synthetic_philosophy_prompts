In my animal ethics reading group we've been stuck on wild animal suffering for months. We spent one session on John Stuart Mill's essay Nature (published posthumously in Three Essays on Religion, 1874), specifically the long passage where he catalogues how nature kills—predation, starvation, freezing, poison, being crushed or burned—and says these are the very sorts of acts that would be criminal if a human did them. Everyone in the group accepts the factual picture and agrees suffering is bad, but the interventionists and the non-interventionists haven't moved. The non-interventionists keep retreating to different claims: that naturalness has independent value, that our record of ecological intervention gives a decisive practical reason against, that the sheer scale makes intervention absurd. The interventionists keep returning to Mill and saying the only real difference left is that nature did it, and that can't bear that much moral weight. What kind of disagreement is this actually? A hidden factual disagreement about how interventions would go, a difference about what counts as a moral reason, bedrock intuitions about naturalness, or something about the passage itself being read too strongly? Diagnose it, and say what (if anything) would actually move it.
