I sit on the clinical AI oversight committee at my university's teaching hospital, and I have begun to wonder what our own policy commits us to. We will not approve any predictive model for deployment unless it is certifiably legible: either the model is a compact scoring rule whose coefficients a clinician can read directly, or it is a black-box model accompanied by a separately validated interpretable approximation that a clinician can consult and override. We enforce this even when legibility costs accuracy. Last month we rejected a gradient-boosted model for predicting sepsis that outperformed every approved alternative, because its individual predictions could not be surfaced in a form a clinician could audit; we approved instead a logistic regression that misses more cases. At the same meeting, however, we declined to apply any such standard to a different kind of decision-maker. The hospital continues to allow experienced clinicians to depart from triage protocols on the strength of pattern recognition and clinical intuition they cannot fully articulate, defending this in the language of expertise and judgment.

I would like to know what view of understanding, explanation, and warranted reliance this policy presupposes — if any single view does. Does the legibility requirement presuppose that a decision system may be trusted only to the extent that a human can state the considerations behind its outputs, or only that a high-stakes institution needs an audit trail? Is legibility being valued intrinsically, or instrumentally as a safeguard against silent failure? And is the asymmetry between the opaque model and the opaque clinician one that the policy's implicit theory can justify, or would consistency require us either to extend the legibility requirement to human clinical judgment or to relax it for the machines?
