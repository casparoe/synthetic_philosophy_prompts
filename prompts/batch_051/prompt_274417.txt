I've been chewing on something after watching my department do two things in the same month. For undergrad admissions we get a few thousand applications, and the process is basically: set a bar for GPA and a few other things, read in batches, and once someone clears the bar they're on the admit list; nobody pretends we've found The Best Student, and the whole thing is done in a couple weeks. Then we hired a postdoc, and the same room of people spent three hours arguing about whether the rubric was masking the truly best candidate, insisted on seeing every file before deciding, and talked like leaving the search open one more week might surface a better applicant than all forty we already had. I don't think anyone noticed the inconsistency.

So my quesion is: what view of rational decision-making does each practice presuppose? For the good-enough practice, reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can—is it satisficing with aspiration levels, or is it just expected-utility reasoning that happens to count the cost of reading applications, or are those secretly the same? Then say whether the two practices can live under one theory of rationality or whether the institution is waffling between incompatible ones. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the admissions process if we imported the hiring committee's presuppositions, and what would change in hiring if we imported the admissions one. Keep the answer focused on the implicit commitments; I don't need a history of the debate.
