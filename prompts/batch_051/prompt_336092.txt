Here is a familiar puzzle about petitionary prayer. If God is omniscient, then before I ask, God already knows what I want, what I need, and what I would ask for, so prayer cannot be in the business of informing God. If God is perfectly wise and perfectly good, then God already wills what is best; and if God is immutable, then God's will cannot be changed by anything a creature says. Petitionary prayer therefore looks either redundant — asking God to do what God would do anyway — or futile — trying to move an unmovable will. The task is to say what petitionary prayer is for, or why it is not irrational, while holding fixed both divine omniscience and divine immutability.

I have collected the following responses, which I take to be the standard ones:

1. Obedience. God commands petition, and doing what is commanded can be good because it is commanded, independently of whether the act changes anything. Petition is therefore an act of worship or obedience, and it does not need to move God to be valuable.
2. Formation of the petitioner. Forming one's wants as requests before God restructures desire: it cultivates humility, dependence, gratitude, and an increasing alignment of the petitioner's will with God's. The point is that the asker changes, not that God changes.
3. Means and ends. An eternal and immutable decree can include both an outcome and the petition that precedes it, exactly as a single plan can include intermediate causes. Petition is one of the means God has chosen, and its efficacy does not require any alteration in the divine will.
4. Relationship. Making requests is partly constitutive of personal relationships, including relationships in which the other party is already disposed to act. Petition expresses dependence, trust, and intimacy, and its value lies in what it does to or within the relationship, not in transferring information or altering a decision.
5. Appointed condition. God may have determined from eternity that certain goods will be given only if requested. Asking is not an attempt to change God's mind; it is the satisfaction of a standing condition, much as a parent might resolve to give a child something only if the child asks, so that the asking becomes part of the good.

I suspect there is a further response that is genuinely different from these five — not a special case of the idea that petition improves the petitioner, not a restatement of the means–ends or relationship views, and not a mere combination of items already listed. I am not interested in the skeptical conclusion that petitionary prayer to such a God is simply irrational or pointless; take that off the table. Give me one such response. It may come from any tradition or be your own construction, but it should answer the puzzle as stated rather than change the subject.
