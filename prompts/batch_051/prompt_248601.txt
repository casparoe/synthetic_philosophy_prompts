I have spent the past few months tutoring my nephew in reading and writing, and I keep tripping over the same problem. The textbook says the English past tense is formed by adding -ed, and then immediately lists dozens of irregular verbs that do not follow that rule at all. But the exceptions are not a random heap. Some cluster into small sub-patterns: sing/sang/sung and ring/rang/rung, while fling gives flung and bring gives brought. Others are suppletive, meaning the past tense is supplied by a different word entirely, as in go/went or am/was. Still others look irregular now but are frozen residues of sound changes that stopped operating centuries ago, like the vowel difference in foot/feet. Meanwhile my nephew, who is a native speaker and has heard went his entire life, has started saying goed and wented anyway. Give him a nonsense verb like pling and he will immediately form plinged, though he also accepts plang or plung as a joke. This has made me realize I do not actually know what it means to say that a language, or a speaker, has a grammatical rule.

Before I decide what to teach — a single rule with exceptions, a list of memorized forms, a family of smaller sub-regularities, or something else entirely — I want the specification of what a theory of grammatical rules has to get right. Give me the requirements list: the cases and patterns it has to account for, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for example, a productive pattern in a speaker's mind versus the historical residue of a dead sound change, or a rule versus a stored list), and the empirical findings about child language and speaker judgments it must be consistent with, with a sentence or two on why each is a requirement rather than merely a preference. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable, which ones mainly reflect the biases of a particular school, whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most to exclude the obvious one-sentence views — that a rule is an exceptionless regularity, that it is just a summary of stored forms, or that it is whatever a textbook says. A table for the requirements is fine.

The purpose I have in mind is explanatory and practical at once: the theory should be good enough to explain how speakers actually produce and judge forms, and to underwrite a sane way of describing and teaching the past tense to a child.
