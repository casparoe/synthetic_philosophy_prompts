I've been turning over a problem with the "misconceptions as theories" program in science education, and I'm not sure wether the core argument survives a recent development. The argument, as I understand it, goes: students make errors that are systematic and resistant to instruction (heavier objects fall faster, you need a force to keep a body moving, a thrown ball carries some of the original force with it), and the best explanation of that systematicity is that they aren't merely ignorant — they're running an intuitive theory of physics. Teaching therefore has to get them to replace the theory, not just fill gaps. But now we have LLMs trained only on text, with no bodies, no sensorimotor history, and no need to build a predictive theory of the world in order to act — and yet they'll reportedly reproduce many of the same mistakes when you prompt them casually. That seems to me to attack the inference from "the errors are systematic" to "the learner is applying a theory." If a text-only system with no reason to form a physics theory makes the same errors, which premise of the original argument is actually under pressure? Is the natural repair that both students and LLMs are reflecting statisticial patterns in the language about motion, and the "naive theory" was an overreading all along? Or is there a version of the misconceptions-as-theories view that survives and still tells teachers something useful? I want to know whether the argument is dead, damaged-but-repairable, or barely touched — and if it can be repaired, what exactly the weaker version claims.
