In object-oriented programming, "the same" can mean two quite different things: reference identity, where two names point at one object, and structural equality, where two distinct objects compare equal because all their fields match. The designer of a type decides which fields structural equality checks, and whether it ever counts as identity. This looks to me like the shape of the identity of indiscernibles — the controversial converse of the trivial principle that a thing shares its properties with itself. The principle, associated with Leibniz, says that two things sharing all their properties must be one thing. Max Black's two-sphere universe is the stock attempted counterexample: a world containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres two miles apart, distinct but apparently indiscernible. To a programmer, these are just two heap-allocated objects with equal fields and different addresses. Is this a real structural parallel or a forced metaphor? If real, does the software-design lesson transfer — in particular, that equality must be implemented against a chosen set of fields, so whether Black's spheres are one or two depends on which properties we count as identity-determining and there is no choice-free fact of the matter? Or does the analogy break exactly there, because the programmer chooses an equality relation for a purpose while the metaphysician wants a purpose-independent answer? Where else does the mapping come apart?
