I'm a PhD student in clinical psychology, and I've been going in circles on whether delusions are beliefs. I have a rough principle in the back of my head from the two-factor literature: a delusion is what you get when an anomalous experience (a voice, a weird feeling that your wife looks off) meets a seperately damaged system for evaluating candidate beliefs, so a hypothesis gets accepted when it should have been rejected. But that tells me how delusions form, not whether the thing that forms is a belief. And my gut on cases does not line up under any principle I can state.

Here are my verdicts, as close as I can make them:

1. After a stroke, a man insists his wife has been replaced by an identical-looking stranger. He is cold to her, asks where his real wife is, and sometimes gets agressive when she tries to touch him. Belief, full stop.
2. A woman hears voices saying her neighbours are going to kill her, barricades the door at night, stops talking to the neighbours, and warns her sister not to visit. Belief, no question.
3. A man tells his psychiatrist he is dead and his organs are rotting, and once asked to be buried. But he still blows on a hot cup of tea to cool it and checks for cars before crossing the street. I also think that is a belief, but this feels like it conflicts with what I leaned on in 1 and 2, where the belief showed up in action.
4. A woman who has just had a stroke denies her left arm is paralysed. Asked to lift it, she says she is tired; shown it is not moving, she says it is not her arm anyway. I do not think this is a belief. It feels like an on-the-spot explanation, not something she was committed to before you asked.
5. A man is convinced his wife is cheating on him though there is no evidence. He checks her phone, follows her after work, and brings it up constantly. Belief, and I would not even call the structure pathological, just false and resistant.
6. A patient says the FBI has put a tracking device in his teeth, but he only mentions it when his psychiatrist asks, and he never tries to remove the fillings or hide from the FBI. I keep flipping here: he asserts it sincerely and holds it against challenge, but it is completely inert in the rest of his life.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If not, which verdict would you overturn and why? And what does the principle say about a case I have not listed: someone in an experiment is given false feedback that they have temporary brain damage, and he insists on it for a few minutes until the experimenter contradicts him, then drops it? Does the two-factor idea actually help with any of this, or should I stop using it to decide what counts as a belief?
