I am writing a novel set partly in a university philosophy department, and one of the central characters is an ethicist who defends a distinctive position on how demanding morality is. I have written the character's commitments as she would state them in a seminar, and before I let her defend them in front of colleagues I would like them audited for joint tenability, in the manner of a careful philosophical interlocutor rather than a sympathetic summariser or a hostile critic. I can see that several of the commitments rub against each other, but I cannot tell which frictions are substantive and which are only verbal, so I want the audit to discriminate rather than simply flag everything.

Her commitments are these.

1. The correct criterion of right action is impartial maximising act-consequentialism: an act is right just in case its expected consequences are at least as good, judged from an impartial standpoint, as those of any alternative open to the agent.

2. She nevertheless regards the demandingness objection as the deepest difficulty for consequentialist theories. Her version of the objection is not the tired claim that consequentialism would embarrass us at dinner; it is that a theory which, as a matter of duty, would require a parent to direct resources away from their child whenever the impartial sums come out marginally better elsewhere is in conflict with something essential to the kind of lives that moral theory is supposed to govern.

3. She refuses the most familiar fixes. She rejects agent-centred prerogatives, of the sort that give each agent permission to weight their own projects and close relationships more heavily than impartial value would allow, because she thinks this buys a less demanding morality at the price of abandoning the very impartiality that gives consequentialism its authority. She rejects side-constraints for the same reason.

4. She also rejects rule-consequentialism, on the ground that it is a form of rule worship: it makes an act's conformity to an ideal code, rather than the goodness of its consequences, the fundamental maker of rightness, and so it offers what she considers the wrong explanation of why one should keep a promise in the case where breaking it would produce better consequences.

5. At the same time, she insists that any acceptable normative theory must leave room for the supererogatory: acts that are morally good, and go beyond what duty demands, but are not required.

6. She also says that the distinction between doing and allowing is something a moral theory should preserve rather than merely explain away, while adding that she is not thereby smuggling in a deontological constraint.

7. Her own preferred response to the demandingness objection is a split-level, sophisticated consequentialism. She distinguishes the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure a theory should recommend that agents internalise. She argues that the decision procedure which would best promote impartial value over the long run is one that instils strong habits of partial care for one's children, friendships and local projects, so that the theory does not, in practice, issue the self-fracturing demands the objector describes.

8. She also maintains that the demandingness objection is not an external refutation of consequentialism: it is generated by the very impartiality that makes the theory serious, and the correct response is to diagnose that feature rather than to retreat from it. She adds that this diagnosis does not amount to simply accepting that ordinary agents are required to live in the way the objection describes.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs or clusters are genuinely in tension and which are only apparently so. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what would have to give, and state the cheapest fix. For each apparent tension, say what reading removes it. Then tell me which single revision to the character's position would buy the greatest increase in coherence for the least philosophical cost. I am not asking for a general essay on demandingness; the audit should be tied to the commitments as stated.

One further request about epistemic posture: please separate what is settled among philosophers working on this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. And please write the response in British English.
