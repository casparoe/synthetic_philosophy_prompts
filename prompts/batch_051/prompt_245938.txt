In the Preface to Kant's *Groundwork*, Kant writes:

"Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, 'Thou shalt not lie,' is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason;"

Explain how this passage bears on Kant's famous murderer-at-the-door case: would the passage commit Kant to saying you must tell the truth to the murderer, and why or why not? Then identify one reason why someone might think this passage's standard for moral law is too demanding, and say whether you think it is actually too demanding.

Here's the student answer I need to grade:

"Kant is saying that morality cannot be empirical: the moral law, including the prohibition on lying, has to be valid for every rational being, not just for human beings in particular conditions. This matters for the murderer case because the first formulation of the categorical imperative is exactly what this passage is stating: act only on maxims you can will as universal law. Since a maxim of lying to the murderer cannot be willed as a universal law, Kant has to say you may not lie even to the murderer. In the Groundwork he uses this case explicitly, saying that if you tell the truth and the murderer finds your friend, you are not responsible because you didn't intend the death. So the passage does commit him to telling the truth to the murderer. One reason the standard is too demanding is that it makes a merely formal consistency test override the concrete duty to protect someone from being killed, and I think that's a fair objection, because Kant's rigorism about lying treats an abstract principle as more important than a friend's life."
