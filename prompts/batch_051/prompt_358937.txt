I've been helping redesign the intro physics tutorials in my department, and the existing approach is built entirely around eliciting students' misconceptions before we teach the correct concept. The canonical example: before the unit on forces, we ask students what forces act on a box being pushed across a floor at constant speed. A lot of them say there's a forward force in the direction of motion, and that this force must be present as long as the box keeps moving. The curriculum treats that answer as the visible tip of a coherent false mini-theory of motion—call it 'motion implies force'—and the lessons are designed to make that theory fail publically, on the assumption that students only revise when their old theory stops working.

A new TA keeps pushing back, though. He says the idea that students hold stable alternative theories is a story we impose on errors after the fact. In his view, a student producing that answer might be assembling it on the spot from fragments—a remembered rule, a gut feeling about effort, the wording of the question, a half-remembered diagram from high school—and nothing like a unified theory is actually sitting in thier head. If he's right, he argues, our whole pedagogy is aimed at changing a thing that doesn't exist.

I'm writing the section of our redesign document that lays out what view of knowledge and concepts this misconception-centered approach presupposes. Specifically: does the approach actually require that students have full-blown alternative theories, or is some weaker kind of systematicity enough? And if the fragment view were correct, what would we have to change about how we teach—would we just need different questions, or would the whole 'surface the misconception and confront it' strategy have to go?
