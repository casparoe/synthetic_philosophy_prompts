I am a student currently taking a course on the philosophy of law and evidence, and we have recently been working through the proof paradox: the strain between a purely probabilistic account of legal proof and the feeling that certain bodies of evidence, however likely they make the defendant's responsibility, are nevertheless not enough to support a finding against a particular person. I find myself pulled in both directions. Legal probabilism seems almost too elegant to give up: the fact-finder's task is to update on the evidence and compare the resulting probability with the applicable standard — the balance of probabilities in civil cases, and proof beyond reasonable doubt in criminal cases. On that view, the familiar examples ought to be unproblematic: a bus company that operates 80 per cent of the buses in town should be held civilly liable when the evidence makes it 80 per cent likely that its bus caused the damage, and a spectator at a rodeo where only 499 of 1,000 spectators paid for entry should be liable for non-payment when the evidence makes it just over 50 per cent likely that he got in without paying. But when I imagine actually having to decide those cases, I cannot bring myself to accept a verdict based on nothing but the statistic — and the resistance does not go away if the numbers are raised, which suggests the dispute is not merely about where to set the threshold. I want to know whether this intuition is an illusion that should be debunked — a product of risk-aversion, a failure to internalise conditional probability, a confused demand for certainty, or something of that kind — or whether it tracks a feature the probabilist leaves out, such as a requirement that the evidence individualise the defendant's connection to the particular event, or a distinct norm against treating persons merely as members of a reference class.

Present each position at its strongest, and tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes. If you think there is a reading on which both survive — for example, that Bayesianism is the right account of evidential support but a further non-probabilistic condition governs when a court may legitimately act on that support — articulate it precisely and explain what work each component does. I am more interested in the underlying reasoning than in which author said what.
