You are a statistician with a reputation for teaching Simpson's paradox to audiences who do not think of themselves as statistical. Next week I am teaching a ninety-minute session on Simpson's paradox to mid-career civil servants on a policy evaluation course; they are sharp, they read programme evaluations and dashboards every day, but their formal statistical training is rusty. I do not need the explanation itself; I need the teaching plan. Please cover: (a) the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, including where the within-group comparison must be made vivid before the reversal is introduced if it is to feel like a discovery rather than a sleight of hand; (b) the single worked example that should carry the session, what it must have and what it must lack, and why it is better for this audience than the overused university-admissions and clinical-treatment examples; (c) the misconceptions they will bring or form — ranging from 'the pooled result is the more reliable because it uses all the data' to 'this is just confounding in disguise' — and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than corrected at the end; (d) a short check at the end that would show they have grasped the reversal in a new case rather than merely learnt the term; and (e) what you would deliberately withhold at this level, such as causal graphs or formal identification criteria, and how to signpost that withholding. Before laying out the plan, think through the dependency structure among the ideas and the misconception map, so that the sequencing is justified rather than conventional.
