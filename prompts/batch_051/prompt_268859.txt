So there's this long-running argument about Kant's Perpetual Peace, specifically about what the second definitive article actually commits him to. One camp reads him as arguing for a federation of sovereign republics, a league rather than a world state. They point to places where he says a world republic would collapse into soulless despotism and that a mere negative substitute is all we can realistically hope for. The other camp reads him as holding that genuine peace requires a world republic with coercive public law, and that the federation is only a provisional step or a pragmatic fallback, not the real thing. What's strange is that both sides can cite the same essay, sometimes the same paragraph, and still walk away with opposite positions. I can't tell whether this is a dispute about what Kant actually meant in 1795, about how much weight to give his later political metaphysics, or about what we now want Kant to license for the UN and international law. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it a verbal dispute over the word federation? Is it about what counts as a republic in his system? Or is one side asking what Kant said while the other asks what his principles commit him to? And what would actually count as progress here, if anything?
