I'm a retired geologist who's gotten into philosophy of language over the last couple years, so bear with me if I muddle the terms. A friend at my reading group walked us through an arguement that seems to prove that Italian is Latin — and worse, that no language ever changes into a different one. I can't find the exit. It goes:

(1) Take the gradual path from Latin to Italian, say from a Roman of 500 CE to a Roman of 1500 CE. At each parent-to-child link, the child understood the parent and vice versa; the differences were small — a vowel shifted here, a case ending dropped there, a new way of forming the future. No generation ever found it's own parents unintelligible.
(2) If the speech of two consecutive generations is mutually intelligible and differs only in such small ways, then those two generations spoke the same language.
(3) "Same language" is transitive: if A is the same language as B, and B is the same language as C, then A is the same language as C.
(4) So by a chain of steps from 500 to 1500, the people at the end spoke the same language as the people at the start. The 500 speaker spoke Latin; therefore the 1500 speaker also spoke Latin. But we'd say the 1500 speaker spoke Italian. So Italian is Latin.
(5) Nothing in the argument is special to Latin and Italian. It works for any pair of language-stages joined by a chain of mutually intelligible generations — Latin and French, Old English and Modern English, whatever.
(6) So no language has ever turned into a different language. Language change that produces a new language is impossible.
(7) But that can't be right. Italian is not Latin, French is not Latin, and so on. So one of the steps has to go.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), since they look like plain observations about how slow change works, and I'd like to keep the denial of (6), because giving that up feels like denying that French and Latin are different. My friend warns that whatever we give up has a price — some of the obvious moves make language boundaries come out partly conventional, a bit like the golden spikes I used to hammer into a type section to mark a stratigraphic boundary. The rocks don't draw that line; we do. I'd convinced myself languages were different, though: that Italian simply isn't Latin, not a line we drew. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? Or is the argument valid and I should just bite the bullet on (6)?
