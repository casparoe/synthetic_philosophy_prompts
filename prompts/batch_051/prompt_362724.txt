I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or economics, but I've gone down a rabbit hole on the socialist calculation debate of the 1930s and 1940s, and I keep being struck by how little the two sides seemed to engage each other. On one side, Mises and Hayek argued that an economy without genuine markets in the means of production cannot allocate resources rationally, because there would be no prices for capital goods and no way to gather the dispersed, often tacit knowledge that exists only in millions of individual heads. On the other side, Oskar Lange and the market socialists replied that a Central Planning Board could announce provisional prices for producer goods, instruct managers of state-owned firms to choose inputs that minimize average cost and set output where price equals marginal cost, and then adjust prices up or down in response to observed shortages and surpluses. This trial-and-error process, they argued, would mimic a competitive market and might even improve on it. The version of the story I usually hear is that Lange won on the formal level while the Austrians were later vindicated by the stagnation of real planned economies, but that feels like a dodge: it doesn't tell me what the argument itself actually was about or why it stalled.

My working hypothesis, influenced by thinking about distributed systems at work, is that the two sides were answering different questions. Lange was showing that a planner who already has the relevant data and a definite objective can compute equilibrium prices by iteration, whereas Mises and Hayek were asking how an economy generates and mobilizes that data in the first place, and whether it even exists anywhere in the form a planner would need. But I can also see the disagreement being about incentives (why would managers report true costs or follow the rules?), about what counts as socialism (does Lange's scheme smuggle markets back in through its parametric prices?), or about standards of proof (is an in-principle model enough to answer a practical impossibility claim?). I don't want a verdict on who was right. I want a diagnosis of why the debate stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is: substantive, verbal, or two groups solving different problems under the same heading. And I'd like to know what, if anything, would have counted as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a real-world test, or a conceptual distinction — and whether any part of the dispute was just about words.
