For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early analytic philosophy, I am setting a two-hour exam question on Russell’s theory of descriptions. The question must turn on the following passage from Bertrand Russell, “On Denoting” (1905):

To take a very important instance: there seems no reason to believe that we are ever acquainted with other people’s minds, seeing that these are not directly perceived; hence what we know about them is obtained through denoting.

I do not want a question that merely asks students to reproduce the analysis of “Scott is the author of Waverley.” I want it to force them to work out how Russell thinks the contextual analysis of denoting phrases licenses talk about other minds without acquaintance, and then to assess whether that actually yields knowledge of other minds or only knowledge of truths involving descriptions. The question should also probe whether Russell is here claiming that acquaintance with other minds is impossible, or only that he sees no reason to believe it occurs.

Please draft the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The marking scheme should have five must-hit points, each with the marks awarded and a one-line note on the commonest misunderstanding that prevents candidates from earning them. If you think I am overreading the passage, or that the task as posed rests on a misreading of Russell, say so and give me a revised version rather than forcing the question to fit.
