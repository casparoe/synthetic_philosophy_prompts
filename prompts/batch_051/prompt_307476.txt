Im putting together a worksheet for a metaphysics course I'm TAing, on the simple conditional analysis of dispositions. The target view is: x is disposed to M in circumstances C iff, if x were in C, x would M. I've already got three families of counterexample, and each one breaks the analysis in a different way:

1. Finks: the disposition disappears exactly when C comes around. A live wire hooked to a safety device that makes the wire dead the instant before anything touches it; intuitively the wire is live the whole time, but the counterfactual 'if touched, it would shock' is false.

2. Masks: the disposition is still there, but something else in the situation stops its manifestation. A poison that remains poisonous even though the victim has also taken an antidote that binds the poison before it can do its damage; the counterfactual 'if ingested, it would kill the victim' is false, but we dont want to say the poison lost its power.

3. Mimics: the thing isn't disposed to M at all, but the counterfactual comes out true because some outside mechanism produces M on cue. A non-lethal liquid that would kill you if you drank it—not because of anything in the liquid, but because a device in the cup sprays cyanide into your throat as you swallow. 'If ingested, it kills' is true, but the liquid is not poisonous.

I want a fourth kind, not one of these three. Not a fink, mask, or mimic with the details changed, and not a combination of two of them glued together. It should break the conditional analysis for a structurally different reason—so tell me which direction the failure runs (does the analysis rule a disposition in that isn't there, or rule one out that is there?) and what makes this case unlike the three above. If you think there is no fourth kind, say so and defend it, but try to construct the missing case first. Then be honest: does the fourth kind collapse into one of the first three under pressure, or does it actually earn its place on the handout?
