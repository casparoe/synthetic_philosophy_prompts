I've been going down a rabbit hole about fakes and forgeries after watching a documentary about a forger who turned out near-perfect Vermeer's, and I picked up a popular aesthetics book at a used bookshop to get the philosophical lay of the land. The book gives a confident summary of the debate, and I'm thinking of repeating some of it at a reading group, so I'd like an accuracy check before I do.

Here's roughly what the book says. The real puzzle is the perfect forgery: a canvas that is indistinguishable from the original by every test currently available. One tradition — which the author treats as pretty much the default view among philosophers — holds that if two canvases are indistinguishable to an informed eye, they share all their aesthetic properties, because an aesthetic property just is a property available in perception. So any extra value attached to the original has to come from non-aesthetic sources: prestige, investment, the romance of provenance. A rational buyer who only cares about the art should be indifferent between the two. The opposing tradition says history is internal to the work: a painting is the individual object it is partly because of how it came to be made, so a copy isn't the same artwork just by looking like it. The author says this second view is more respectable than it sounds but ultimately rests on a kind of magic, because it has to claim that finding out a work is a forgery changes how the painting actually looks, which is just moving the goalposts from the canvas to the label. He concludes that forgery is wrong for roughly the same reasons lying is wrong — it's a fraud on the buyer and on the historical record — not because the forged object is aesthetically worse.

Is this a fair summing-up of the actual debate? The line that aesthetic properties just are what you can see is doing a lot of work, and the author says it like it's settled, but I don't know enough to push back. Go claim by claim: what's accurate, what's a particular camp's position being presented as the field's position, and what a defender of the history-internal view would say to the magic objection. And if the book's whole framing is off, or my question rests on a confusion, tell me that plainly instead of just grading the summary.
