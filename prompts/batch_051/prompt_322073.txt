In debates about the nature of sovereignty, a deflationary analogy recurs: the state is, at bottom, just a corporation that succeeded. The usual exhibits are the great chartered trading companies of the early modern period — the East India Company and the Hudson's Bay Company, among others — which had shareholders, raised armies, levied taxes, concluded treaties, and governed territories, while formally remaining private concerns operating under royal charters. Proponents of the analogy infer from these cases that the line between corporation and state is one of degree rather than kind, and that what we call state sovereignty is really a bundle of corporate privileges that outgrew, and eventually displaced, the authority that granted them. Audit this analogy in full. Work out the mapping explicitly: if the modern state is the target and the joint-stock corporation is the source, what corresponds to the shareholders, the charter, the board of directors, the residual claimant, the corporate form itself, and the power to dissolve or re-charter the entity? Then say where the mapping breaks. Attend especially to the features of a corporation that the analogy quietly imports — profit-seeking, transferable shares, limited liability, fiduciary duties to owners, and answerability to a superior legal authority that created it — and specify which of those features a modern state possesses, lacks, or possesses only by stipulation. Separate the disanalogies that are fatal to the intended conclusion from those that merely complicate it. Finally, state what conclusion about the state the analogy can actually support once the disanalogies are spelled out, and whether a better comparison — a legal person, an office, a club, a system of trusts, or none of these — would carry the intended conclusion further. Throughout, distinguish what is historically and conceptually settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Write in American English.
