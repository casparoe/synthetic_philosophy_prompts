I've been going back and forth on modal identity, the business of how I could have been different and still be me. The way I understand the two camps: on one view, the very same object literally exists in lots of possible worlds with different properties (transworld identity); on the other, nothing exists in more than one world, and "I could have been taller" just means I have a counterpart in some other world who's taller and sufficiently similar (counterpart theory). I keep finding my own verdicts split between the two.

Here are the cases and verdicts:

1. I could have been two inches taller. True.
2. I could have been born three weeks premature and spent my first month in an incubator. True.
3. I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. True.
4. If my parents had had another child instead of me, that child would not have been me. True.
5. Still, I don't think there's a sharp line between a possible person who is me and one who isn't. Change one memory: me. Change every memory, every character trait, and every relationship: not me. Somewhere in the middle it's just indeterminate.
6. So I'm comitted to saying "I could have been a different person" isn't determinately false in the vague middle, even though there are clear cases where it's false.
7. I could not, in any context, have been a sea anemone. True.

What principle captures these? The first two plus the origin verdict sound like transworld identity with origin essentialism. The borderline ones sound like counterpart theory, where it's all degrees of similarity. Can one view deliver all seven, or do I have to give one up? And whichever principle you'd defend, what does it say about this case: a world where someone has 99 percent of my genome and a life history almost identical to mine, but the sperm was different — is that a world where I exist, a world with my counterpart, or neither?
