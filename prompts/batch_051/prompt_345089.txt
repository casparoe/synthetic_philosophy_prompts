In my government class we studied the social contract argument in broad strokes: people leave the state of nature by agreeing to surrender some of their natural liberty and submit to a common political authority, and it is that agreement that is supposed to make the resulting authority legitimate. In history, around the same time, we looked at chartered companies, trading corporations that received charters from a monarch and eventually exercised powers over territory that look almost governmental. I want to ask what happens if these two ideas are merged into a single thought experiment. Suppose the parties in a state of nature do not merely consent to be governed, but literally incorporate themselves under a charter as a joint-stock company. Each person contributes land, money, or labor to a common stock and receives shares in exchange; each share carries one vote; a board elected by the shareholders governs; and shares may be bought, sold, or left to heirs. A person can therefore own voting shares while living far outside the territory, and the charter imposes no requirement that the company protect or provide public goods to residents who hold no shares. How does this variation affect the social contract analysis? Work through three specific questions: whether a child born later inside the territory has any political obligation, or any right of exit, despite never having subscribed to or inherited shares; whether an absentee shareholder abroad has a legitimate share of authority over the residents of the territory; and whether the justification for this arrangement still deserves to be called government by consent, or has instead become something closer to government by property and contract. Say what a Hobbesian and a Lockean would each be forced to concede or amend, and indicate exactly where in the standard reasoning the corporate structure forces a different answer.
