Consider the following proposed criteria for a text's having a determinate meaning on an authorial-intention view: (1) the text has an author, understood as an individual mind or a collective body capable of a genuine intention; (2) that author intended the text to carry a determinate content; (3) the words actually used are capable of bearing that content; and (4) if the author had no determinate communicative intention, the text has no determinate meaning, whatever readers may project onto it.

Now take this case. A legislature passes a bill one clause of which was inserted at the final stage as a compromise between two opposed factions. The clause was drafted by a staff lawyer who deliberately chose wording that each faction could honestly read as giving it what it wanted. Faction A votes for the bill believing the clause means X; faction B votes for it believing the clause means Y, a contrary reading; the drafter's own view is that the wording supports both readings, and the bill as a whole is never read in full by any single legislator. No individual's communicative intention matches the enacted text as a whole. A court later must decide what the clause means.

Does this clause satisfy the proposed criteria? Work through (1)–(4) honestly; where a criterion gives no clear verdict, say whether that is the criterion's fault or the case's. Then say whether the case shows that the "author" in authorial-intention theories of meaning must be revised—for example, by admitting group authors, by replacing the author with a suitably informed reader, or by treating the text as a public object that can mean something no one intended. Answer in at most 500 words.
