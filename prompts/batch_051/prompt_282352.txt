I retired from evolutionary biology a few years back and have been reading philosophy of history, and I can't shake the feeling that the old species debates in my field and the periodization debates are the same fight in different clothes. In systematics there is the species problem: are species real things we discover, or just convenient ways of slicing up a continuum? There are obvious discontinuities—populations that won't interbreed—but also ring species, where A interbreeds with B, B with C, and A and C don't, and chronospecies in the fossil record, where a lineage changes so gradually that any boundary between ancestor and descendant is one we impose rather than find. Biologists handle this by being lumpers or splitters and by fighting over which species concept is correct, while paleontologists mostly admit a chronospecies boundary is a convention.

Then I read about the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution, and it's the same moves. The labels look to me like they were mostly stuck on after the fact by people who wanted a dark gap followed by a rebirth, and the Scientific Revolution looks exactly like a chronospecies: take a long, uneven accumulation of changes and place the boundary where it gives the cleanest story. There's the same lumper/splitter question—is it one Scientific Revolution or a dozen micro-revolutions?—and the same worry that "when did the Renaissance start?" is no better formed than "when did Homo erectus become Homo sapiens?"

So here's the parallel I want checked. Both fields seem to be arguing about whether a category carves nature at its joints or is imposed on a continuum, with a spectrum from realism to conventionalism plus a special problem about retrospective designation. But the disanalogy I can't decide about is this: biology has a half-objective criterion in reproductive isolation or gene flow, while history seems to have nothing analogous—institutions and ideas change continuously and there's no interbreeding population of events. Is the parallel deep or superficial? If it's deep, what would have to be true on the history side for results from the species debate to transfer, and what does the parallel predict that turns out false? If it's superficial, which feature of the biological case is doing all the work?
