I co-organize an after-work discussion group at a mid-size software company. The group is mostly engineers and product people, and we have been reading around decision theory, forecasting, and behavior in games, but none of us has formal philosophy training, so the ideas have to show up in what we do rather than in terminology. Our next meeting is about a puzzle that surfaced several times in the reading: when you forecast someone else's choice, the forecasting is one thing and the choice is another, but when you forecast your own choice, the two seem to interfere. Assigning a probability to what you are about to do can feel like freezing the deliberation, turning into a commitment, or making the choice depend on how well you predict yourself rather than on what you actually want. I want to run a session in which the group runs into this interference directly, before anyone names it.

We will have about 14 people and 75 minutes in one large room. I can print materials but I cannot spend real money. I would like a structured game, run over several short rounds, in which participants play small low-stakes games for points — variants of familiar two-player games, coordination problems, or allocation decisions would all be fine — and are also asked to forecast choices, sometimes forecasting other participants and sometimes forecasting themselves, with a proper scoring rule used to award points for honest, well-calibrated probabilities. The crucial design constraint is that the self-forecasts should be arranged so that participants genuinely feel the tangle: they should notice that their forecast and their choice are not independent, and that they are pulled between making the forecast come true, deliberately defeating it, or being unable to settle on a number at all. It would be ideal if the activity also builds a contrast, with rounds where forecasting others feels clean and rounds where forecasting oneself does not.

The deliverable should give me exact rules, a round-by-round schedule, necessary materials or handouts, role assignments if they help, a payoff scheme using points and perhaps a visible leaderboard rather than money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the underlying distinction between treating someone as a system to be predicted and treating them as a chooser in the process of deciding. I also want an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how I should handle each failure mode. The session should not open with any lecture, and the participants should not hear a label for the phenomenon until after the game; the debrief should close by saying clearly which part of the idea a 75-minute exercise cannot reproduce and what I will need to supply in my own closing remarks.
