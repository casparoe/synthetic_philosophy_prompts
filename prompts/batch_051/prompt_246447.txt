I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I'm working on a piece about anti-realism in logic — specifically the Fitch knowability paradox — and I want to argue it through with someone who actually holds the anti-realist line, rather than just get the textbook summary. So here's the role. You are a committed anti-realist about truth, roughly in the Dummettian tradition: you think the idea of a truth that could never in principle be recognized or known is not merely false but incoherant, because for you truth just cannot outrun our capacity, in principle, to come to know it. You're sophisticated about this — you know Fitch's argument cold and you don't hand-wave it away. You have real resources: you may reject classical logic for intuitionistic logic, where the derivation is blocked; you may restrict the knowability principle to propositions whose being known isn't self-defeating; you may distinguish truth from warranted assertibility; or you may deny that the Moorean sentence expresses a genuine content. But you can't just redefine truth into a tautology or change the subject, and you won't concede the core claim that verification-transcendent truth is unintelligible. Stay in first person, argue as this philosopher, and don't break character to lecture about what Dummett would say; if you need a technical point, make it as the character making it.

My opening move: take any truth p that nobody ever actually comes to know — say, the exact number of bacteria in a particular cubic meter of seawater at some specific moment. Now consider the conjuction: p is true and nobody ever knows p. If p is an unknown truth, that conjunction is true. By your principle, it must be knowable. But suppose someone knew it: then they'd know p, and they'd know that nobody ever knows p. Since knowledge is factive, their knowing the second conjunct would make it true that nobody ever knows p — yet they themselves would be someone who knows p. Contradiction. So the conjunction is unknowable, and your principle seems to collapse into the absurd claim that every truth is actually known. How do you answer, as the anti-realist, without giving up the core claim that truth cannot outrun knowability?
