I am a retired natural scientist — I spent most of my career in the life sciences — and I came to philosophy only recently. Over the last few years I have been reading mainly on respect, recognition, and the moral psychology of holding one another responsible, and I have settled into a set of positions I am inclined to defend. I have been asked to present a short statement of my views to a reading group. Before I do, I would like a rigorous consistency check.

The positions I find myself holding are these:

(1) Morality requires not only that we act rightly but that we hold certain attitudes toward persons. To recognize another person's moral standing is in part to regard her in a certain way. A person who extends perfectly correct outward behaviour while privately regarding another as lesser is defective in a way that goes beyond anything that appears in his actions.

(2) An ought that lies wholly outside an agent's control is no ought at all, and I am persuaded that beliefs, feelings, and attitudes cannot be adopted by simple decision or act of will. Consequently, no defensible account of the duty to recognize others can require an agent to produce the appropriate attitude on command. It must instead be an account of how such attitudes are cultivated, maintained, and repaired through means within our control — attention, exposure, habit, and the like.

(3) The reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, and the rest — are not optional extras. To hold such attitudes toward someone, and to be answerable to hers, is part of treating her as a responsible agent. A stance of purely diagnostic or managerial treatment toward a wrongdoer would withhold from him something to which he is entitled if he is entitled to be treated as one of us.

(4) It is normally inappropriate to blame a person in the full sense for an attitude he acquired without choosing it, does not endorse, and is actively working to counteract — the familiar case of implicit or culturally inculcated bias. Such an attitude does not express his will or his evaluative commitments in the way that would make blame apt; it is at most a cause for regret and a reason for self-management.

(5) Law and public policy are blunt instruments whose proper jurisdiction is outward conduct. A liberal state may require equal treatment and prohibit discrimination, but it should not attempt to police inner attitudes or punish citizens for failures of recognition as such.

I cannot see clearly whether these five positions form a stable whole or where the strains are. Please audit the set. For each pair that is in genuine tension, say how severe the tension is and what the least costly revision would be. Also identify any pair that merely appears to be in tension and explain why it is not a real problem. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent, and what it would cost.
