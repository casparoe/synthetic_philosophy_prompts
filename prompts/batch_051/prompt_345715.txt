Okay here's a counterfactual I keep coming back to. Imagine a mid-sized common-law country amends its constitution so that a criminal offence is automatically void for vagueness unless its text is precise enough that a competent programmer could, given the text alone and a full factual description of the incident, write a function that returns guilty or not guilty, without the programmer having to invent any further normative criteria. So 'reasonable', 'undue', 'substantial', 'dishonest', 'in the public interest' all become fatal unless the statute defines them in mechanical terms. The courts take this literally, and it applies to existing offences too, not just new legislation.

Predict, with reasoning, what that legal system looks like after forty years. How does statutory drafting change? How do courts and prosecutors adapt so they can keep convicting the people they beleive should be convicted without getting the law struck down? Which old offences quietly die and which survive in some bloated but decidable form? Does the rule actually deliver what its backers presumably wanted — clearer notice and less arbitrary enforcement — or does it just relocate the discretion somewhere else? Be explicit about which links in your prediction are robust and which are more like guesses, and what would have to be true for the whole thing to go differently.
