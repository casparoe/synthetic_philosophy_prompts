I work at a small forecasting platform whose selling point is that it publishes calibrated probabilities rather than factual assertions. One of our products assigns each restaurant in the city a probability that it will fail its next health inspection, displayed as a number with a confidence interval and trained on public records, past inspection results, and reviews. Last month a data-pipeline error imported a failed inspection that never occurred, and one restaurant's displayed probability jumped from 12% to 81% overnight. The restaurant lost customers and is now suing for defamation.

The relevant part of our state's defamation statute reads: "A person is liable for defamation only if the person publishes to a third party a statement that asserts a false fact about another and thereby tends to harm the other's reputation. A statement of pure opinion, including a rating, a forecast, or an evaluation, is not a statement of fact and is not actionable, unless the statement implies the existence of an undisclosed false fact."

The statute was drafted in the 1970s, long before algorithmic scoring existed. I want to understand how this language reaches our case. Is the model's output a "statement" at all, and if it is, is it a statement of fact, a pure opinion, or something the statute does not classify? I care less about the bottom line than about how the different approaches to interpretation—the text, the purpose of the fact/opinion distinction, and what the drafters would have said if asked—each bear on the case, and which approach should decide when they point in different directions.
