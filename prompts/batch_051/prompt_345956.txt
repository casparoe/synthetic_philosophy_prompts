Before I settle on a position about bivalence—about whether every declarative sentence must be either true or false—I want a specification of what a theory of truth-value gaps actually has to get right. The discussion is usually run case-by-case, and people often choose a logic on the strength of a single example. I want the full list of desiderata first.

Give me the requirements a theory of truth-value gaps must satisfy to count as an adequate semantic and logical account of which meaningful declarative sentences are neither true nor false and how they behave when combined with other sentences. For each requirement, give the motivating case or formal result that makes it a requirement rather than an optional refinement, and state whether you treat it as negotiable or non-negotiable. Cover at least the following and add whatever I have missed:

1. The core cases. A candidate theory should either offer a unified account of, or give a principled reason for treating separately, at least these: borderline instances of vague predicates, future contingents, presupposition failure, and the liar and related semantic paradoxes. A bare list of examples with no account of what unites or divides them is not enough.

2. The contrasts that must not be blurred. At minimum: having a truth-value gap versus having a truth-value glut versus being simply unknown; the predicate not true versus the predicate false, which come apart once gaps exist; meaningful but neither true nor false versus meaningless; semantic indeterminacy versus epistemic indeterminacy; and first-order versus higher-order borderline status. For each contrast, say whether collapsing it is a substantive reductive claim or a failure of the theory.

3. The formal results it must respect, preserve, or reject at an explicit price. Rejecting bivalence does not by itself settle the fate of the law of excluded middle; some gap theories keep excluded middle while allowing both a sentence and its negation to lack truth values, which is exactly why the distinction between bivalence and excluded middle has to be built into the requirements. The theory should also take a position on the T-schema, modus ponens, the status of classical tautologies, and compositionality. For each, say whether it is a fixed constraint or a rejectable commitment, and state the cost of rejecting it.

4. The behavior of gaps under composition. If a sentence is neither true nor false, the theory must specify the status of its negation, of its conjunction with a false sentence, of a conditional in which it is the antecedent, and of the statement that says of it that it is neither true nor false. The interaction of gaps with negation and the truth predicate is where most proposals break, so this should be explicit.

5. The interface with graded attitudes, if gaps are supposed to leak outside pure semantics. If a sentence has no truth value, is a rational agent forbidden from having a degree of belief in it, required to assign it some particular value, or left unconstrained? If the gap is semantically real but rationally invisible, the theory should explain why belief should be proportioned to truth in ordinary cases but not here; if it is rationally visible, the theory should say how. If you think this is a mistake and graded attitudes have nothing to learn from gaps, say that explicitly and defend it as a boundary of the theory.

Then treat the list itself as carrying argumentative weight. Are any subsets of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? Which single requirement eliminates the largest portion of the standard options—say, a classical no-gaps picture, strong Kleene three-valued logic, supervaluationism, and epistemicism? A table scoring the options against the requirements is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.

Finally, tell me which part of your list is most likely to fail. Which requirement is the weakest, and what argument or counterexample would most plausibly lead you to revise or drop it?
