I've been going down a rabbit hole with those old Libet-style experiments where someone watches a fast clock and flexes their wrist whenever they feel the urge, and the EEG shows a readiness potential building up a few hundred milliseconds before they report the conscious urge. I get that part. The thing I keep noticing is a move in popular writeups, and even in some philosophy papers: they say the experiment shows conscious intention doesn't initiate the act, and then almost immediately add that free will is fine because the person can still consciously veto the act at the last moment. But in the original setup, nothing was recording or controlling for the veto, and the readiness-potential result tells us nothing about whether a veto would itself be conscious or not.

Is there an established name for that specific position or move—the "we don't start our actions but we can still stop them" view, or the strategy of relocating free will to an untested last-instant veto? I'm not looking for the big-tent labels like epiphenomenalism or compatibilism; I want to know whether this exact two-stage story has a name of its own, and how it differs from plain compatibilism. And if there's no name, can you coin one and define its boundaries?
