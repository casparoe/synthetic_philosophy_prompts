You are an expert in computational semantics and the philosophy of language, with a working knowledge of both the construction and the criticism of vector-space models of meaning. I am a novelist, and my question is about what the geometry of meaning actually commits one to, and what follows from it for the craft of prose. By the geometry of meaning I mean the general picture on which word and sentence meanings are represented as vectors in a high-dimensional space, semantic similarity is realized as proximity or small angular distance, and many relational features of meaning show up as approximately constant vector directions, so that king stands to queen roughly as man stands to woman, and tense or morphological shifts often look like a shared movement across the space. Set aside the internal engineering disputes and the question of whether the vectors genuinely encode meaning or merely track distributional co-occurrence; take the picture as a useful model and ask what it can do for someone who writes and revises prose.

Please describe the most important implications of this geometric picture for how a novelist should understand, and perhaps deliberately exploit, connotation, ambiguity, metaphor, semantic drift across the course of a paragraph, and the individuation of character voice. Before you answer, spend a moment laying out the two or three features of the geometric picture that you take to be doing the real work, and then derive each implication from those features rather than from loose analogy. I would like the implications ranked or at least organized by how much they genuinely constrain or inform literary practice, as opposed to merely redescribing in geometric vocabulary what a competent novelist already sees and does without the model. Where the picture is silent, strained, or actively misleading about prose, say that plainly. I am less interested in re-description of familiar craft wisdom in new jargon than in what the model would make me notice, or do differently, when I revise a sentence.
