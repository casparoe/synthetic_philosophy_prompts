I've been going down a rabbit hole on how to score an agent's beliefs, mostly because I keep seeing ML people gesture at proper scoring rules when they talk about calibration, and I wanted to understand the philosophy behind it. I read a blog post that summarized something called accuracy-first epistemology, and I can't tell if its overreaching or if I'm just missing background. The article's summary went roughly like this:

Beliefs are for representing the world accurately, so you can grade a set of beliefs the way you'd grade a weather forecaster. Pick a scoring rule, for example the Brier score: you square the distance between the probability you assigned to an event and what actually happened. A theorem by a philosopher named James Joyce apparently shows that if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is always some other, probabilistic assignment that is more accurate no matter which way the world turns out. So incoherent credences are dominated in accuracy, and rationality therefore requires probabilism. The article then said the same framework shows that updating by conditionalization maximizes expected accuracy, so the entire package follows: rationality requires full-blown Bayesianism — real-valued credences forming a probability distribution, updating via Bayes's rule, the works. It ended by saying this proves epistemic rationality just is a matter of accuracy, so truth is the only thing that matters when forming beliefs.

I'm on board with the idea that if your credences are dominated in accuracy, something has gone wrong. But the leap from "don't have credences that are guaranteed to lose on accuracy" to "truth is literally the only epistemic value and rational agents must be full Bayesians" feels like it's extracting an awful lot from one dominance argument. I also vaguely remember reading that there are multiple proper scoring rules and that it isn't obvious which one is the right one, which makes me suspicious of anyone who casually says "the Brier score" like it's the unique measure. I dont have the philosophy background to pin down where this summary goes off the rails, if it does. Can you go through the summary and tell me which parts are accurate, which are subtle overstatements, what someone working in this area would object to, and whether the final conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts?
