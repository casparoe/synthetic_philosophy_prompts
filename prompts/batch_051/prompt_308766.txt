Ok, so I edit the ideas/science section of a magazine, and I keep running into the same argument in social-science papers. Sometimes a finding will explain an outcome by appealing to something above the level of people—an institution, a norm, a culture, an aggregate rate, a network property—and I don't know whether that's a legitimate cause or just a placeholder for a story about individuals that the authors didn't bother to tell. I'd like you to turn this into a reusable editorial procedure, not a philosophy essay: a checklist or decision flow my desk can actually run on a pitch or a preprint.

The issue has a lineage. John Stuart Mill, in Book VI of *A System of Logic*, has a passage I think about a lot. I'm paraphrasing because I don't have it in front of me, but he argues roughly that the laws of social phenomena can be nothing but the laws of individual human nature acting in the social state, because when human beings are brought together they don't get converted into some other kind of substance with different properties. That's the strongest statement of methodological individualism I know, and it's either obviously right or obviously too strong depending on the week. But it's a useful challenge.

So here's what I want: a procedure for deciding, when a social-science explanation treats a group-level or system-level property as doing real explanatory work, whether that's fine for us to describe as a cause, or whether we should send it back and ask the writer to supply the individual-level mechanism. The procedure should be usable by someone with no formal training in statistics or social theory but who reads a lot of papers. It should include:

- the questions to ask, in order
- what counts as a sufficiently answered question
- where the procedure stops with "go back to the author"
- where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of disciplinary style or values rather than facts

Then run it on three things from my actual inbox:

1. A paper says residential segregation persists because neighborhoods "tip" once their minority share passes a threshold. The summary has no model of individual moving decisions.
2. An economic history paper says "institutions cause economic growth," and when asked, gives long-run cross-country correlations and no story about how institutions actually change individual incentives.
3. A sociology paper says a school's "culture of academic excellence" explains its improved test scores. The evidence is mostly interviews with teachers and administrators, and it treats school culture as a cause operating above the level of any one person.

For each case, tell me what the procedure says we should do, and whether the procedure gives an answer you think is wrong and should prompt you to revise it. Also flag the cases the procedure genuinely can't settle—like when a macro pattern is in principle reducible to individual-level behavior but nobody actually has a credible reduction. I'd rather know that than have a rule that pretends to be exact.
