A friend and I have been arguing about how much authority an earlier self should have over a later one, and I want to push the argument past the familiar cases by working through a concrete mechanism. A jurisdiction introduces a standing directive. Any competent adult may file a legally binding instruction covering decisions they specify in advance — medical treatment, major financial moves, residence, employment, or close personal relationships — for a renewable five-year term. During the term, the later self cannot cancel the directive merely by changing their mind. Revocation requires either a formal finding that the later self currently has capacity plus a ninety-day cooling-off period, or proof that the situation falls under an unanticipated-circumstances clause contained in the directive itself, unless the directive named a proxy and explicitly gave that proxy authority over such clauses.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. What would it incentivize in how people draft these directives, in how later selves contest them, and in how the third parties asked to enforce them behave? How would it be gamed, and where would it break down? Which failure modes are design-specific and fixable without gutting the scheme, and which are inherent to any attempt to give an earlier self binding authority over a later one?
