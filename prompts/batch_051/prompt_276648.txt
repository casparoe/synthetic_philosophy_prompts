In our philosophy of computation module the conversation turned into a standoff about what kind of claim the Church–Turing thesis even is, and I keep going back and forth myself. Dana opened by saying the thesis is basically a theorem, or close to one: lambda calculus, general recursive functions, register machines, Post systems — every serious attempt to formalize "algorithm" has turned out to compute exactly the Turing-computable functions, and that kind of independent convergance isn't an accident. Ravi shot back that the equivalence of all those formal models is a theorem, but the Church–Turing thesis proper says the informal notion of "effectively computable by some finite rule" lines up with that class, and since one side of that bridge is informal, there's no theorem to prove. Dana replied that if it were just a stipulation or a definition, it couldn't be wrong, which doesn't fit how the thesis actually functions — it substantively constrains what anyone could even mean by an algorithm and survived a century of people trying to dream up weird procedures that break it. Ravi then asked: a claim about what, exactly? If it's about human computers following rules, then it's an empirical claim and a sufficiently strange procedure by a person could in principle refute it; if it's about every physical process, it's a much stronger empirical claim, and some physicists and philosophers think spacetime might permit hypercomputation. Dana said now he was changing the subject, because "physical Church–Turing thesis" is a different claim from the one about effective calculability. Ravi said no, Dana was the one changing the subject by pretending an informal-to-formal bridge could be a theorem just because the formal side is settled.

They never agreed on which thesis was even on the table. Can you adjudicate this point by point — which moves from each side are right, which are overreach, and what the epistemic status of each version of the thesis actually is?
