Here's a position I've been defending lately, and I want you to tell me what would have to be true for me to abandon it.

The position is about Haidt-style moral foundations theory and the left/right asymmetry. You know the result: in the usual (mostly WEIRD, self-report) samples, self-described liberals' moral judgments load mostly on care/harm and fairness/cheating, while self-described conservatives also load on loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation. People read that as a win for one side or the other — either conservatives are attuned to a wider range of real moral considerations, or liberals are better at not moralizing fake ones. I think it does neither. The asymmetry is normatively inert: it's a fact about the structure of moral taste, and by itself it's silent on which taste is tracking anything.

What I want: stress-test that position by laying out the conditions under which it fails. Rank the defeaters from the ones that would finish it off to the ones that would merely make it uncomfortable. Keep two kinds separate: first, things that would undercut the asymmetry itself (measurement artifacts, the weirdness of the samples, failures to replicate behaviorally, instability if you add or split foundations); second, things that would license the step from 'moralizes more domains' to 'more likely to have it right' even if the asymmetry is real. For each, say whether it's a defeater my own position has to take seriously, or one only pressed by someone who already rejects the view. I already know the questionnaire is a bit of a mess, so I'm more interested in the second kind of defeater — but still include the first if any of them would be decisive. Then do the self-diagnosis: has 'normatively inert' become unfalsifiable, and is that a bug or the right-size claim for this kind of evidence? Commit at the end: tell me where the position actually breaks and where it survives, not a menu of options.
