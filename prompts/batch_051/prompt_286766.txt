Question: Explain Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment and the conclusion it is meant to establish about the determination of meaning. Then consider the further claim that the experiment shows natural-kind terms such as 'water' are rigid designators. Does the experiment support that further claim? Why or why not?

Student answer: Putnam asks us to imagine a planet, Twin Earth, which is exactly like Earth in all observable respects, except that the clear liquid in its rivers, lakes, and taps is not H2O but a different chemical compound, XYZ, which is indistinguishable from water in ordinary circumstances. The inhabitants of Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of Earth speakers, and they apply the word 'water' to their local liquid just as Earth speakers apply it to H2O. Putnam argues that Earth speakers' word 'water' refers to H2O while Twin Earth speakers' word refers to XYZ, even though the two groups are in identical internal states. The intended conclusion is that meaning is not determined by the speaker's internal psychological state alone; the speaker's physical environment contributes to meaning as well. Since the two words differ in extension while the stereotypes associated with them are the same, Earth's 'water' is synonymous with H2O and Twin Earth's 'water' is synonymous with XYZ, in the way that 'bachelor' is synonymous with 'unmarried man'; the superficial stereotype is only a practical substitute for the real meaning, which is chemical. It also follows that natural-kind terms are rigid designators. If Earth's word 'water' could have referred to XYZ in a possible world, then its extension would not be fixed by what the local liquid really is; but the thought experiment shows that the extension is fixed by the local substance in this way, so 'water' designates H2O in every possible world, with XYZ merely filling the Twin Earth role. Therefore the further claim is supported.
