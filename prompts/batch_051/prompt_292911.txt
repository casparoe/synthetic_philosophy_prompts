At a seminar I attended, a philosopher of cognitive science and a machine-learning researcher argued about whether any system trained only to minimize prediction error on a finite corpus could make a genuine intuitive leap — where that means producing an idea that is not merely a statistically probable continuation of its training data, but that breaks with the local pattern of that data and is then shown, on independent grounds, to be correct or fruitful. I wrote down their exchange afterward as faithfully as I could:

(1) The researcher said no, and that this is almost a matter of definition: the training objective literally penalizes low-probability continuations, so anything that departs enough from the training distribution to count as a "leap" is exactly what the system has been optimized to avoid. A genuine leap would show up in the loss landscape as an error, not as good output.

(2) The philosopher replied that this assumes a sharp inside/outside boundary around the training distribution. In high-dimensional spaces there is no well-defined region with a border; between any two samples there are enormous unvisited regions, and interpolating across distant regions is how any mind, human or machine, produces something that looks novel. If the researcher wanted to call all of that mere recombination, then every human insight is recombination too, since no idea arises from zero precursors.

(3) The researcher answered that the difference is the outer loop. A human can notice that an entire approach is failing and deliberately change the criterion of success, rather than search harder within the old one. A trained system cannot rewrite its own objective; it can only optimize the one it was given, so the standard that decides what counts as good output is frozen at training time. You cannot get a departure from the pattern without some second process that evaluates departures by a different standard.

(4) The philosopher came back that the outer loop is itself just another learned move. Give a model a prompt that asks it to pause, identify where its current approach is failing, and deliberately search for something unlike its recent outputs, and it can — in context — simulate exactly that self-correction. Unless the human's ability to change criteria is a miracle, it too is a pattern acquired from experience, and there is no principled reason a network could not acquire it the same way.

Please adjudicate their argument move by move. Rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say which side has the better of which specific point. End by saying what, if anything, both of them are missing.
