Consider a convention that organises large parts of theoretical computer science and of the philosophy that draws on it. In textbooks, complexity theory, and algorithm design, the line between computationally feasible and computationally infeasible problems is standardly drawn by one feature alone: the rate at which running time grows with the size of the input. Problems solvable in polynomial time — time proportional to n, n log n, n squared, or any fixed power of n — are classed as efficient; problems believed to require time that grows exponentially are classed as intractable. The classification ignores constants, exponents, and the difference between average and worst cases. An algorithm running in time proportional to the hundredth power of n is, by this convention, efficient, while one running in time proportional to (1.000001) raised to the nth power is not, even though for modest input sizes the second is far quicker and for genuinely large inputs both are hopeless in practice. The convention also classifies problems rather than instances: a problem is declared intractable if some contrived instance is hard, even when almost every instance met in practice is easy. And the convention does real downstream work — it appears whenever someone argues that the mind cannot literally perform a certain kind of inference, or that a proposed form of rational deliberation is impossible, because the relevant computational problem is NP-hard or requires exponential time.

I want the implicit philosophy made explicit. What view of feasibility, of computation, and of what counts as a relevant difference between algorithms does this convention presuppose? Does it presuppose that the only properties of an algorithm that matter are asymptotic; that worst-case performance is the right normative standard; that the proper object of study is the problem type rather than the distribution of instances an actual agent faces; or that feasibility is a natural kind rather than a pragmatic boundary drawn for proof-theoretic convenience? Or is the convention best understood as a useful idealisation, a structural claim about the mathematics of resources, or a bet that worst-case growth tracks practical difficulty well enough in the long run?
