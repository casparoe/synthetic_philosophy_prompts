Hey — I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on Bayesian epistemology lately. The thing I can't get past is the problem of old evidence. The classic version is the precession of Mercury's perihelion: people had those measurements for decades, and then general relativity comes along and explains them, and it feels obvious that the old measurements should count as points for GR. But the way Bayes theorem is usually written, if P(E) is already 1 because you've known E for years, then P(E|T) can't be any bigger than that, so you get no confirmation out of it at all. That seems like a weird hole in the whole framework.

I actually hit a version of this at my job: there's an intermittent latency bug we've known about forever, nobody's pinned it down, and now someone has proposed an architectural explanation that predicts the bug's weird failure pattern almost exactly. By the letter of Bayes, should I be telling myself that bug doesn't count as evidence for their theory? Or is the actual lesson something about pretending I don't already know the bug, or about updating on the fact that the theory would have predicted it even though I already new it? More generally, what are the most important practical implications of the problem of old evidence for how a regular person should weigh "this explains something we already knew was true" versus "this predicts something new"?
