We're doing a unit on sovereignty and membership in my international law class — customs unions, confederations, what it means to actually "join" something rather than just sign it — and a friend in the same study group (law student, talks like a treaty) walked me through this argument and now I can't find the exit.

(1) A state is sovereign only if there is no authority above it that can make rules binding on it or change its policies without its consent. (2) In a customs union, members hand the common external tariff, rules of origin, and the power to negotiate trade deals to a collective body, and an individual member can't later set its own tariffs or sign its own free trade agreement, even if the common tariff is hurting it's economy. (3) If some body can set rules that bind you and that you can't change on your own, then that body is an authority above you — otherwise "above" has no content. (4) So joining a customs union puts an authority above you, and a state with an authority above it is no longer sovereign. (5) You can't say it stays sovereign except on trade, because sovereignty doesn't come in sectors: if the state isn't the final decider in one domain, it isn't the final decider anywhere. (6) The same logic then applies to any treaty-based body — the WTO, a bilateral FTA, even a river-management commission — since every treaty binds you to rules you can't unilaterally rewrite. (7) So almost no actual state is sovereign, which is either absurd or a reason to think "sovereignty" was always a useless fiction.

I'd like to keep the ordinary idea that Britain or France or Japan is sovereign, while also saying joining a customs union changes something real about who decides. But every move I can think of — deny 3, deny 5, distinguish being bound by your own prior consent from being ruled by a superior, distinguish pooled sovereignty from transferred sovereignty — feels like it just redefines sovereignty to make the problem disappear. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Or if the argument is valid as it stands, which premise is the least costly one to give up?
