I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a long piece about strict liability in criminal law — the fact that people can be punished without the state having to show they intended the harm, knew about it, or were even careless. The philosophy-of-punishment literature mostly treats mens rea as close to sacred, which makes strict liability look like a standing scandal. But when you look at actual legal systems, strict and near-strict liability are everywhere: felony murder, statutory rape, a lot of regulatory and environmental offenses, some drug- and gun-possession rules. I dont want to write the millionth piece that lands on 'mens rea good, strict liability bad.' I want to write about the genuinely unresolved parts.

So here's what I need. If you take the philosophical case for mens rea seriously — not as dogma, but as a live argument — which of the seeming implications actually carry weight for how criminal law should change, and which are overrated or don't follow at all? I'm thinking of stakes like: whether felony murder should be abolished or just narrowed; whether strict-liability regulatory offenses should be moved out of criminal court and treated as civil fines; what happens to negligence — whether it's a defensible middle ground between intention and strict liability, or just strict liability with better PR; and what any of this says about addiction, intoxication, or people acting under extreme stress. Rank five or six candidates, defend the ranking, and say which two are worth leading a 4,000-word piece with. My readers are smart nonspecialists, so some of the ranking should turn on how much work the philosophy actually does once you strip away the jargon — not just what sounds most dramatic. Tell me the criteria your using, where you'd trade importance against tractability or against how many other questions depend on the issue, and what would have to be true for the ranking to flip.
