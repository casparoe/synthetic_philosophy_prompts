In my undergrad philosophy of mind class I keep coming back to that old story from Dean Wooldridge about the sphex wasp. As I remember it (and I probably have the details slightly wrong), the female wasp stings a cricket to paralyze it, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside for a quick inspection, comes back out, drags the cricket inside, and only then lays her egg. The odd bit is that if you move the cricket a couple inches while she is inside doing her inspection, she comes out, drags it back to the exact same threshold spot, and then goes back inside to inspect again. You can do this over and over and she never skips the inspection step and just drags the cricket straight in; she is stuck in the loop. Dennett and others have used this to make a point about what free action isn’t—free agents aren’t suposed to be rigid like that. But I want to pressure the analogy. How far does the sphex story actually hold as a way of understanding human mechanical rigidity, habit, and the difference between flexible and inflexible behavior? Lay out what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, what features of the wasp’s situation the analogy quietly imports into human cases that aren’t there, and whether the usual conclusion—that freedom involves something like sensitivity to the whole situation rather than fixed subroutines—survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Also tell me if there is a better analogy for the kind of rigidity that supposedly threatens free will.
