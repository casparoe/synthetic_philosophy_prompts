I recently got into an argument with a colleague about whether thought experiments in science establish facts about the world or only expose inconsistencies within a theory's own assumptions, and I'd like to work through a controlled variation on Galileo's falling-bodies argument to clarify this. The canonical argument runs as follows. Assume, with the Aristotelians, that heavier bodies fall faster. Tie together a heavy stone H and a light stone L of the same material. The composite is heavier than H, so it should fall faster than H; but L, falling more slowly, should retard H, so the composite should fall slower than H. Contradiction; the assumption is rejected, and the argument is commonly taken to show that bodies of the same material fall at the same speed regardless of weight. Now vary the theory behind the argument. Suppose a modified opponent holds that fall-speed is an increasing function of the ratio of weight to surface area, not of weight alone. Let H be compact, with great weight and small surface area, and let L be porous or loosely shaped, with less weight but much more surface area; H then falls faster and L slower. When the two stones are tied together, the composite's weight-to-surface-area ratio is a weighted average of the two components' ratios, so it lies strictly between them rather than exceeding both. How does the analysis of the tied-stones argument change under this modification? Does the contradiction still arise, or does one horn stop applying? If the argument no longer refutes the theory, what exactly did the original version establish—a fact about falling bodies, or only the inconsistency of a package of assumptions in which the relevant quantity is additive under composition—and what does that reveal about how thought experiments in science can yield knowledge?
