I've been trying to think about the Löbian obstacle for AI self-trust and I want you to do some counterfactual intellectual history for me. The background: Löb's theorem says that a formal system rich enough to talk about its own proofs can prove "if I prove φ then φ" only when it can already prove φ, so the system can't bootstrap a general guarantee of its own soundness. People now use this to argue that a self-improving AI can't formally certify its own reliability. Gödel thought hard about provability, self-reference, and minds versus machines—and he was a platonist who insisted that mathematical truth outruns mechanical provability—but the Löbian obstacle as an argument about AI only came up after he was gone, so he never adressed it directly. What do you think Gödel would have made of it? Would he have seen it as a real constraint on any machine's self-trust, or would he have said it only shows that "self-trust" can't be formalized, and that a mind sees the relevant truths from outside the system? Reconstruct his likely view as best you can, and be explicit about how confident you are and which part of your reconstruction you are least sure of.
