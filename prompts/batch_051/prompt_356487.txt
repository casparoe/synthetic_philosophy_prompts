I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, so I'm gonna mangle the standard labels, but I've been chewing on a variant of the old experience machine thought experiment and I want it worked through carefully rather than gestured at.

The original as I understand it: you can plug into a machine that gives you any experiences you want for life, and the usual anti-plugging arguments say you still shouldn't, roughly because (1) you want to actually do things, not just have the experience of doing them, (2) you want to actually be a certain kind of person, not just have the experience of being one, and (3) you want contact with a deeper reality, not just one that some engineer rigged up for you. I'm paraphrasing from memory, so treat these as the target positions rather than an exact quote.

Now change two things. First, the outside world has fully automated every job: the careers, crafts, and professions that used to give people a sense of meaningful work are all gone, and everyone is kept alive and comfortable by machines but has no real work to do. Second, the machine on offer is not a whatever-you-want pleasure machine. It's a work simulator: it gives you a long, coherent, appropriately difficult working life where you learn a skill, struggle, solve problems that feel real, get recognized by your simulated peers, and finish things that feel like yours. From the inside its indistinguishable from a real working life.

Run the three anti-plugging considerations through this version one at a time. Do they all still cut against plugging in, or do some of them weaken, flip, or stop applying because the real-world alternative is now automated idleness rather than a real working life? Be precise about where the difference comes from: is it that the relevant good (real achievement, really being a worker, contact with reality) is now impossible outside the machine, or that the machine changed from a pleasure machine into an achievement-feeling machine, or both? And say whether any new reason for or against plugging in shows up only in this post-automation version, and whether the overall verdict changes.
