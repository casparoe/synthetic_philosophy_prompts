I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of science course, and we have just spent two weeks on the problem of induction and Goodman's new riddle. I am trying to make precise the notion of a projectible hypothesis, and I would like you to propose a working definition and then test it against the cases below. By a projectible hypothesis I mean, roughly, a general claim whose observed positive instances genuinely support its unobserved instances—a claim we are entitled to project to new cases, rather than a universally quantified statement that merely fits the data so far.

Let t be a future time, say 1 January 2100. Define 'grue' as follows: an object is grue just in case it is first examined before t and is green, or it is first examined at or after t and is blue. Define 'bleen' analogously: an object is bleen just in case it is first examined before t and is blue, or it is first examined at or after t and is green.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify. I take (1)–(3) to be clear cases of projectible hypotheses, (4)–(6) to be clear cases of non-projectible hypotheses, and (7)–(12) to be the hard cases.

(1) All emeralds are green.

(2) All electrons are negatively charged.

(3) All samples of copper conduct electricity.

(4) All coins currently in my left pocket are made of copper.

(5) All emeralds are grue.

(6) All emeralds are bleen.

(7) All emeralds first examined before t are green.

(8) All ravens are black.

(9) All non-black things are non-ravens.

(10) All swans are white.

(11) All samples of compound X, a newly synthesized molecule, dissolve in water; five of five samples tested so far have dissolved.

(12) All spheres made entirely of pure uranium-235 have a diameter of less than one mile.

Please propose a working definition of 'projectible hypothesis.' Then run the definition against each of (1)–(12), saying for each case whether the definition classifies it correctly, incorrectly, or gives no determinate answer. Where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. In particular, say how your definition separates (1) from (5) and (6), how it handles (7) given its relation to (1), how it treats the logical equivalence of (8) and (9), and whether it counts (12) as projectible even though its support comes from physical theory rather than from any large observed sample.
