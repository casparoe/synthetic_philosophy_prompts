I keep running into this line in econ twitter comment sections and on substack: "printing money doesn't create wealth, it just redistributes it." Last week someone used it to shut down an argument about whether a central bank buying government bonds during a recession leaves the country better off, and it just ended the conversation even though I don't think it actually settled anything.

Could you seperate the distinct claims hiding in that sentence? For each reading, say exactly what "wealth," "create," and "redistribute" would have to mean, and whether the reading is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false. Then tell me which reading (or readings) the slogan would need to be true in order to settle the bond-buying argument, and which readings are doing the rhetorical work even though they don't survive inspection.

I'm especially interested in three cases. First, readings where it's false because wealth isn't a fixed pile—where injecting money or reserves can change spending, investment, or output enough that the pile actually grows. Second, readings where it's true only by defining "wealth" so narrowly that the person using the slogan would disown the definition if you showed it to them. Third, whether the slogan means something different when "printing" is literal cash versus reserves versus the deposits that appear when banks lend, and whether it matters who gets the new money first. If some readings can't be told apart without more information, flag that instead of pretending the slogan is more precise than it is.
