Act as a philosopher of mind who works on the debate over cognitive penetration of perception. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I tend to think about the mind in architectural terms; correct me where that framework misleads.

My working position is that perception — or at least early vision — is a cognitively impenetrable module. It takes sensory input and returns a perceptual representation, and while my beliefs, expectations, desires, and values can influence what I attend to, how I interpret the output, what I remember, and how I report it, they do not write directly into the experience itself. The fact that the Müller-Lyer illusion survives even when I know the lines are equal looks to me like encapsulation. Against my view, I have seen ambiguous figures that flip with expectation, and studies in which hills look steeper to people wearing heavy backpacks, golf holes look bigger to people who are putting well, and spatial targets look different depending on a person's ability to act. My current suspicion is that those can be explained by attention, judgment, response bias, or demand characteristics rather than genuine penetration.

I want a committed answer, not a survey. Start by brainstorming the strongest candidate defeaters for my position and pressure-testing them before you commit. Then give me a ranked map of what would genuinely count against cognitive impenetrability: specific phenomena, experimental designs, and theoretical arguments, ordered from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a sophisticated defender of my view would concede that it counts against the position and how they would deflect it. Flag the one that should move me most, given that I will be suspicious of anything explicable as post-perceptual judgment. End with a definite verdict on how exposed my position actually is, and tell me whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — for example, by defining direct influence so narrowly that no experiment could ever detect it — and whether that should worry me.
