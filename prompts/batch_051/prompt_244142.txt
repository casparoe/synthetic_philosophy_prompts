I maintain a live probability model of field conditions for an aid organization, built from reports whose quality varies: a guard says a bridge is out, a clinic worker says cases have doubled in one village, a driver says a market closed early. My epistemic policy is never to move on the content of such a report as if I had learned it outright. Instead I treat every report as uncertain evidence: I fix a small partition of the possibilities the report bears on, set new probabilities on its cells based on my read of the source's reliability, and update the rest of the model by Jeffrey conditionalization—even when those reliability numbers are little more than a guess. I have caught myself using the policy to avoid large, abrupt updates that a more naive reading of the report would force. Is this policy defensible, or is it a conservative bias dressed as method? When is it actually equivalent to conditioning on the fact that the report arrived, and when does it instead require rigidity, screening, or independence assumptions I have not earned? How should the policy be restated to keep what is good and drop what is ritual, given that I must hand the model to colleagues who need to see why it moved? Please distinguish what is settled about this from what is actively contested among people who work on uncertain evidence, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
