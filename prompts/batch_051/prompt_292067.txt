I got talked into teaching a session for my cousin's high-school debate club; they're prepping a resolution about determinism and moral responsibility, and their advisor wants some philosophy-for-debaters. I want to use the first half to get them tangled in the old sea-battle problem: if it was already true yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle starts to look inevitable, and you get fatalism before anyone mentions gods or neuroscience. I know the Aristotle discussion well enough myself, but I keep realizing I don't know what has to come before what for kids who are sharp but have no formal logic, and who tend to argue by picking whichever definition wins them the round.

So don't explain the issue to me — plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the question even bites: the idea that truth is seperate from what we can know or prove, the law of excluded middle, the difference between an event being caused and a statement being entailed? In what order do I introduce those so the fatalistic conclusion feels forced rather than like a trick? What should be my one carrying example — I'd rather not open with the sea battle itself because to them it sounds like stale ancient trivia; I'm considering a sealed envelope with yesterday's prediction about a coin flip, or next week's weather forecast. What must that example have and not have? Which misconceptions should I expect — 'if we don't know yet, it isn't true yet,' 'the sentence only becomes true when the event happens,' 'if someone could predict it, it must be necessary' — and in what order will they actually come up, and where in my sequence should each one get defused? I especially want to isolate the move from 'p is true' to 'p is necessary,' since that seems to be the whole engine of the thing. What should I openly leave out or refuse to get into (my guesses: the full modal-logic machinery, and the theological-foreknowledge version), and what's one question I could ask at the very end that would tell me they got the distinction between the truth being settled and the event being already fixed, rather than just leaving them spooked?
