My political theory seminar got onto Kierkegaard's reading of the binding of Isaac — what he calls the teleological suspension of the ethical — the claim that Abraham is not a murderer because the duty to Isaac belongs to the universal ethical sphere, and Abraham suspends that whole sphere for the sake of a higher, absolutely private relation to God that he cannot make intelligible to anyone. We ended up arguing about whether the idea can be given institutional form, and someone proposed a genuinely concrete version. A small self-governing town would create a Suspension Court. After a person is conviced of violating a standing law, they could petition this court to have the law suspended in their case, retroactively, without the law being changed for anyone else. To enter the plea, they must waive all ordinary defenses and admit the act was illegal under the law as written. The court would then have to be convinced of three things. First, the person took themselves to be under a higher duty they could not fully state without distorting it, so the court may not demand a complete articulation of what the duty commanded or why. Second, the person did not adopt the duty for any calculable benefit, and following it cost them something they genuinely valued. Third, the person still accepts the original law as binding on everyone else, and would accept the full penalty if the suspension is denied. If suspension is granted, the conviction is erased, the files are sealed, the statute stays in force, and the grant can never be cited as precedent in any future proceeding.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a proposal you're being asked to endorse or reject morally: I want to know how it would actually behave if it ran for, say, thirty years. Who ends up using it, and what do they start optimizing for? Trace a few of the most likely gaming strategies and the court's likely defensive adaptations, and say what those adaptations would leave of the original idea. Then talk about the two features that seem least stable to me: the ban on requiring the duty to be articulated, which might make the whole hearing into a test of acting skill, and the no-precedent rule, which might break down in practice since people will know grants happen and lawyers will mimic whatever pattern they can infer. Where does the thing fail first, and is any of it fixable without turning it into just an ordinary sentencing hearing with extra steps?
