I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar on statehood and international order, and I'm writing the final. I want an essay question on sovereignty and membership — specifically, on the difference between joining a customs union and joining a confederation. I keep finding that students can parrot the line that a customs union is "only" economic while a confederation is political, but they can't actually say what joining does to a state's sovereignty in either case. I was talking to a colleague about this, and we couldn't even agree on which arrangement is the bigger concession of sovereignty, which is probably a sign that it's a good exam topic. So I'd like a question that forces students to work through a concrete case rather than recite definitions.

Maybe use a fictional state, call it Meridia, that has just left a federation and is now choosing between two options. Option A is joining a customs union with a common external tariff, a joint trade-negotiating body, and a binding dispute panel. Option B is joining a loose confederation with a shared defense and foreign-policy council that can, by a two-thirds vote, commit all members to a common position, but with no common market and no common tariff. The foreign minister claims the customs union is the smaller surrender of sovereignty because it only touches trade, while the confederation gives away the "core" of sovereignty because it touches security and diplomacy. The question should ask students to evaluate that claim and to say what picture of sovereignty it presupposes.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B answer. The question is for a 90-minute in-class essay. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points, total 20 marks, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. Also, the question shouldn't require any knowledge of the actual European Union or any named historical confederation, so students can't coast on factual recall.
