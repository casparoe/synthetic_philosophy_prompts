I am a novelist at work on a book set in an enclosed seafaring order whose theology is strict on God's omniscience and immutability; I have had to imagine its rule of life down to the level of its canons. One canon, on petitionary prayer, will govern the climax of the book, and I want to know whether a scene I have drafted turns on a real ambiguity or on a mistake of mine. The canon runs: "It is lawful to ask God for what is not yet, and to lay before Him any need whose hour lies ahead; but it is not lawful to pray that what has already been be otherwise, or to ask that He unwork what His will has done — for He does not turn, and the suppliant who prays backward contends with His immutability."

The case is this. A novice's brother was due to fly from the mainland on a small plane that has now gone down in the sea; the passenger list has not yet been recovered, and no one knows whether he was actually aboard. The novice is found in the chapel praying, "Lord, let it be that he was not on that plane, or that he is alive somewhere on the water." A senior sister tells her afterward that the canon plainly forbids the prayer, since the flight's fate is already behind them and she is asking that a settled thing be one way rather than another. The novice answers that she is not praying backward at all: the hour she is laying before God — the hour of the news and of enduring it — is still ahead, and she is not asking God to alter anything He has decreed, only that what He has decreed prove bearable. I am fairly sure the canon's drafters, writing long before wireless or air travel, had chiefly in mind prayers that a known grief be reversed, and did not consciously legislate for the interval between a catastrophe and its report.

Is the canon violated? What do the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable sister would take the rule to mean each require here — and which reading is the most defensible? If the question is badly framed, for instance because the forward/backward distinction the canon assumes cannot survive once the God to whom the prayers are addressed is already known to be omniscient and immutable, or because my wording makes the case trivial, say that plainly rather than answering it as if it were well made. I would rather revise the scene than receive a tidy answer to a false dilemma.
