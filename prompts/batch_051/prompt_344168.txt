I keep running into people saying the ancient Babylonians were doing mathematics, and I realize I don't actually know what would settle that. So I want a working defintion of 'mathematics' (or 'doing mathematics,' whichever you think is cleaner) that sorts the following cases. I've ordered them roughly from 'obviously not mathematics' to 'obviously mathematics,' but I'm not confident about the middle:

1. A toddler counts out four blocks and says 'four.'
2. A shepherd around 4000 BCE cuts 23 notches into a stick to record how many sheep went up the hill this morning.
3. The Ishango bone — a 20,000-year-old notched bone from what's now the DRC, covered in clusters of tally-like marks. Some people think one column lists the prime numbers between 10 and 20; others think it's a lunar calendar, or just scratches made for grip.
4. Plimpton 322 — a Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 BCE with fifteen rows of numbers that line up as Pythagorean triples, i.e. whole-number solutions to a² + b² = c², though nobody really know what it was for.
5. A scribe in ancient Mesopotamia using a standard multiplication table to convert weights of silver.
6. A Babylonian school exercise asking a student to find the side of a square of area 60.
7. Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes.
8. A modern weather model running on a computer.

Give me a definition, run it against all eight, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or just goes silent. Then either revise the definition until it handles the cases you're sure about, or bite the bullet and defend any surprising verdicts. I suspect the word is doing two jobs — one about what the person is actually up to (counting, record-keeping, computing) and one about proof, generality, and abstraction — so if you end up with two definitions that assign the cases differently, say which cases move between them and why the two-job reading isn't just hedging.
