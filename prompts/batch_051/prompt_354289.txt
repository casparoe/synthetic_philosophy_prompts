I spent my working life in a physical-chemistry laboratory, where a recurring discipline was distinguishing the quantity one wished to measure from the reading on the instrument, and distinguishing an instrument's evidential value from its usefulness as a target. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of well-being, and an economist friend recently pressed on me the following argument about the World Happiness Report. He insists it shows both that the rankings really measure happiness and that a well-intentioned government should try to climb them. I suspect he is confusing a measure's evidential value with a reason to make the measure move, but I want a precise, step-by-step diagnosis instead of the vague unease I keep reaching for.

His argument runs:

(1) The World Happiness Report bases its country rankings on a single life-evaluation item, the Cantril Ladder, administered in the Gallup World Poll: "Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. The top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?"

(2) This ladder score is the nearest thing we have to a country-level measure of how happy a country's people are: it is broadly comparable across countries, reasonably stable, and tracks the sorts of things — health, income, social support — that a happiness measure ought to track.

(3) If a measure is the nearest thing we have to a measure of national happiness, then changes in that measure are the best available evidence about whether national happiness has changed.

(4) If changes in a measure are the best available evidence about whether national happiness has changed, then a government that wants to increase national happiness should make increasing that measure one of its policy goals; it cannot sensibly aim at happiness directly rather than at what tracks it.

(5) Therefore, a government that wants to make its citizens happier should set itself the goal of raising its country's average ladder score — and, since the rankings order countries by that score, it should try to move up the happiness rankings.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step licenses the invalid move? If it is valid but unsound, which step should be rejected, and what is the strongest corrected version of that step on which the conclusion would follow? I also have the nagging sense that step 5 slides from raising a score to outranking other countries, but I cannot decide whether that is the same error or a further one.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one for each of the five steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; item 5 should state the overall verdict on the argument and on the move from score to ranking.
