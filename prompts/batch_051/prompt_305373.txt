A friend and I have spent the last several evenings arguing about whether the formal apparatus of algorithmic information theory — Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff induction, and its convergence guarantees — genuinely justifies Occam's razor, or whether it merely formalizes an attractive coding convention that happens to reward shorter descriptions. We have settled into two positions on the question.

Call the first the formalist position: Solomonoff induction is the real vindication of Occam's razor, because it shows that simple hypotheses carry more prior probability in a sense defined up to a benign constant, and because a predictor built on it learns any computable data-generating process with total error bounded by the complexity of that process. Call the second the pragmatist position: this is mathematics about an uncomputable, machine-dependent prior, and it gives a finite, computationally bounded agent no reason to believe that simpler hypotheses are more likely to be true of the actual world — at most it shows that a certain notion of short description is useful, and only in the limit.

We want you to draft the document that an adversarial collaboration between these two positions would produce. Do not survey the field and do not adjudicate who wins. We want a text both sides could sign, with the remaining disagreement made so exact that each side would accept the wording attributed to it.

Structure the document in four sections.

Section 1 — Jointly accepted claims. A numbered list of claims both positions accept, covering at least: (a) the invariance theorem, stated precisely — Kolmogorov complexity is well-defined only up to an additive constant depending on the reference universal machine, so the induced probabilities of Solomonoff's prior shift by at most a multiplicative constant under a change of machine; (b) the uncomputability of the prior, though it is lower-semicomputable (approximable from below); (c) the fact that the prior assigns positive weight to every computable hypothesis, so no computable truth is ruled out a priori; and (d) the convergence guarantee, in a form both sides accept — for data generated by a computable measure, the cumulative prediction error of Solomonoff's predictor is bounded by a constant multiple of the Kolmogorov complexity of that measure. For each claim, specify what is a theorem, what is an idealization, and what quantity is being bounded.

Section 2 — Precise residual disagreement. A disagreements table with columns: point at issue; wording the formalist would sign; wording the pragmatist would sign; and a note classifying the disagreement as formal, empirical, or conceptual. The table must cover at least these points: (i) whether the machine-dependence of the prior is benign or fatal to the claim that the razor is objective; (ii) whether the convergence bound carries justificatory force over to the finite-sample, computationally bounded inferences scientists actually make; (iii) whether the sense of "simple" involved in real theory choice is the same thing as short Kolmogorov complexity or merely analogous to it; (iv) what the razor is being asked to justify — identifying true structure, or minimizing expected prediction error — and whether the two sides are answering the same question when they use the word "justify"; and (v) whether the particular 2^{-K} weighting is doing the justificatory work, given that many positive mixtures over computable hypotheses would also converge in the limit and differ only in rate, or whether the worst-case rate bound is what makes Solomonoff's weighting uniquely defensible.

Section 3 — Cruxes. For each residual disagreement in Section 2, state the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side, and record in advance what each side would count as evidence against its own view. Be concrete where possible: a formal bound on the constants across a defensible family of reference machines; a pair of equally defensible machines that reverse some ordinary finite-sample model comparison; an argument that the data-generating processes of the actual world are not computable; or a non-circular mapping from scientific simplicity onto program length. For each crux, state whether it is formal, empirical, or conceptual.

Section 4 — The verbalness diagnosis. Say which parts of the dispute, if any, dissolve once the positions are stated this explicitly — for instance, if the formalist's "justify" means something weaker than what the pragmatist needs in order to deny it. If part of the dispute is verbal, identify the words doing the work and give the restated claim each side would accept. Do not force reconciliation: if the disagreement survives restatement, say so and locate the surviving residue in the cruxes of Section 3.

Throughout, the test on every numbered claim and every cell of the table is this: it could be read aloud to the side in question and accepted without hedging. A formulation that one side accepts only under the other side's gloss should be revised until it passes this test.
