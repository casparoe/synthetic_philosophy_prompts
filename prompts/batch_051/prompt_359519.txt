In my debate club we're prepping a case about whether it's fair to say a chatbot "lied" to a user, rather than just saying it made a mistake. Somebody brought up Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio), and the part that stuck with me is this, from section 3: "For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie." His whole defenition is basically that a liar says one thing while having another thing in mind, and wants to deceive. But a chatbot doesn't exactly have a mind to keep things in, and it doesn't seem to want anything, so I can't tell if calling it a liar is just sloppy metaphor or if there's a real version of the claim.

I want to get past just vibes, though. Suppose I set up a serious test between these two readings: (1) when a chatbot confidently and repeatedly says false stuff, it's genuinely lying or deceiving in whatever sense matters, and (2) that whole way of talking is misplaced, and the chatbot is more like a paper that happens to have wrong facts and should just be called unreliable. What would a strong test or experiment actually look like? Be concrete about the setup, say what result would push toward each reading, and then say what a positive result would still not settle—especially whether "lying" under Augustine's definition ever actually gets off the ground for a system like that.

Could you write the answer as exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion?
