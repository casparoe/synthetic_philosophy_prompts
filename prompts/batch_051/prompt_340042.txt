I'm on the curriculum revision committee for my district's high-school world history sequence, and we're redoing the unit on the early Cold War. For years the unit has been built around a final essay: "Who started the Cold War — the United States or the Soviet Union?" Students have to pick one and assign blame. The committee is split. Some members (the traditionalists and the revisionists among us, who normally disagree about everything) want to keep the question because it makes students write sharp, evidence-based judgments; others (the post-revisionists, mostly) want to scrap it entirely because they think the form of the question does bad work — it sneaks in a criminal-law picture where history has a culprit, a victim, and a single moment of ignition, and that picture survives even if the kids end up blaming the other side. I lean toward the "kill the question" camp but I want something I can actually get passed. Draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday: it should recomend a concrete replacement framing for the unit (the actual essay question or questions), make a real decision rather than splitting the difference, give the strongest version of the losing side's argument, and justify the recommendation by saying what a history course is for — not just what makes good pedagogy. And if the memo keeps blame in play in some form, say exactly how so it doesn't become the same mock trial with fancier words.
