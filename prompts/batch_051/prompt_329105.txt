I work for a constitutional-design unit that advises a federal country currently renegotiating the allocation of powers between national and regional governments. My staff reviews recurring proposals to shift a specific function upward or downward—teacher certification, energy subsidies, environmental inspections, family-law appeals, tax collection—and our existing framework is too coarse. It asks whether the function is “national in scale,” “homogeneous,” or “closest to citizens,” which usually produces one argument for centralization and one for decentralization and leaves the decision to whoever has the louder voice in the room. I need a reusable decision procedure my staff can run in half a day, without legal research, on any proposal to move a function between levels of government.

Ground the procedure in this passage from Federalist No. 51 (Publius, 1788):

“May not this defect of an absolute negative be supplied by some qualified connection between this weaker department and the weaker branch of the stronger department, by which the latter may be led to support the constitutional rights of the former, without being too much detached from the rights of its own department?”

Read this passage as a claim about how to protect the constitutional position of a weaker actor by giving a branch of a stronger actor an institutional self-interest in defending it. I want the procedure to test whether a proposed vertical allocation of a function would create an analogous “qualified connection” between levels of government: one in which the level exercising the function has both the competence to perform it and a durable self-interest in keeping the other level within its constitutional bounds, rather than merely a policy preference for doing so.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions with decision rules. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, when the procedure stops with a no, and when it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Among the questions, include at least these: Which actor will be harmed or constrained if the level assigned the function exceeds its authority? Does that actor have both a mechanism and an institutional motive to resist in real time, before the encroachment becomes entrenched? Is the likely resistance a defense of constitutional structure, or just ordinary policy disagreement between governments? And if a “qualified connection” would exist, would it protect the weaker level without making the stronger level’s branch so attached to the weaker level that it neglects its own constitutional role?

Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals:

(a) moving primary-school curriculum authority from regional governments to the national education ministry;

(b) moving tax collection for a nationally defined income tax from the national revenue agency to the regional governments;

(c) creating a joint national–regional environmental enforcement board with power to investigate both national and regional agencies.

For each case, show the questions asked in order, the answer the procedure returns, and the exact step at which it stops or defers. I should disclose that I expect, on subsidiarity grounds, that the procedure will usually favor decentralization. If it does not for one of these cases, say so directly and explain which step forces the result I did not want.

End by stating the limits of the procedure: what kinds of allocation disputes it cannot settle, where empirical facts would need to be checked before it can be run honestly, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Do not provide citations; I will add the legal references later.
