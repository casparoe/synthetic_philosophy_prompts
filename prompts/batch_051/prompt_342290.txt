Consider a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between two artificial agents. Each agent's choice is determined by a program. Before either agent chooses, each program is given a formal description of the other program, and each is allowed to condition its output on that description. Under these conditions, mutual cooperation can be a stable equilibrium without repetition, reputation, or external enforcement: a program can be written that cooperates exactly when it detects that the other program is the sort that will cooperate. Each side's cooperation is conditional on the other's, and the condition is satisfied. Call this the program-equilibrium explanation of cooperation.

Two rival readings of this phenomenon are possible. On the first, the interaction is in an attenuated sense social: each agent can see that the other will cooperate, and they reach cooperation through something like mutual responsiveness or proto-agreement. On the second, the social vocabulary is a metaphor laid on top of a purely computational fact: each program is a function of the other's code, and words like "see", "trust", and "agree" add nothing. I am inclined toward the second reading, but I do not want to decide the matter by temperament.

Your task is to design the best test you can of the claim that program-equilibrium cooperation involves something properly describable as mutual responsiveness rather than merely a self-referential computational mechanism. Be concrete: specify the agents, the conditions or manipulations you would compare, the outcome measures, and the results that would count for and against the claim. You may alter the setup—for example by degrading the code each agent sees, adding noise, allowing programs to simulate one another, or introducing populations that evolve—provided you hold fixed the claim under test. Then say what even the cleanest positive or negative result would and would not settle. In particular, consider whether any behavioral test could in principle distinguish "the agent cooperates because it identifies the other as a cooperator" from "the code branches on a syntactic feature of the other program and thereby happens to cooperate", and whether the residue that no test can reach is a limitation of experimental design or a defect in the very idea of "more than mechanical".
