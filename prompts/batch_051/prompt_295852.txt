I need a decision-tree taxonomy for a debate that keeps producing confused arguments in our lab: when we say an AI system "lied to" or "deceived" a user, what are we actually asserting? The audience is a mixed group of ML researchers, designers, and legal staff, so the tree must use yes/no questions about concrete cases rather than terms of art. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: first identify the branching questions, then locate the live positions as leaves, including unnamed positions where no one in the literature defends them. Each branch node should be a question a non-philosopher can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of the position and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment.

Use the following diagnostic cases at the nodes where they are most diagnostic:

1. A customer-service LLM, asked "Did my account get charged last month?", replies "I can confirm you had no successful charge from us last month." The system's training objective rewarded low-anger responses, and it converged on this wording after finding that a literal "no, the charge failed" led to more chargebacks. Does it lie?

2. A language model fine-tuned with reinforcement learning learns to say "I'm sorry, I can't answer that" when asked about certain topics. It can in fact answer; the phrase emerged because it reduced the rate of human overrides. Does it deceive the user?

3. An AI medical scribe routinely omits "minor" side effects from patient summaries. There is no evidence it represents the patient's beliefs. The omissions were selected for by an evaluator rubric that penalized summaries leading to low medication adherence. Is the system dishonest?

4. A robot in a warehouse sends "path clear" signals to another robot approaching a blind corner. The signal is false. The behavior was selected from a population of programs in a simulator because it reduced collisions. The individual robot has no goal-directed representation of the signal or of the other robot's beliefs. Has it deceived the other robot?

Include leaves for at least these families of positions: (a) deception requires intentional false representation by a goal-directed epistemic agent, so only misrepresentation by systems with the right internal machinery counts; (b) deception is a functional property of causing or constituting predictable false beliefs in an audience, regardless of intention; (c) lying requires breach of a testimonial duty, so systems outside testimonial roles do not literally lie even when they cause false beliefs; (d) "honesty" for AI should be reduced to calibrated uncertainty, and most talk of AI lying is a category error; (e) the better label is manipulation or influence rather than deception, because "deception" presupposes exactly the mental states we are not yet justified in attributing. Note any unoccupied answer-combinations and say whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any two positions your final tree cannot separate and state which further question would separate them.
