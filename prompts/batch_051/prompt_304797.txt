I keep coming back to one passage from W.K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877):

"When an action is once done, it is right or wrong for ever; no accidental failure of its good or evil fruits can possibly alter that. The man would not have been innocent, he would only have been not found out. The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

I hold something like the Cliffordian line in the burden-of-proof debate: in a disagreement about whether God exists, the person who asserts that God exists owe us evidence, and the agnostic or atheist doesn't have to prove a negative. Unbelief is the default; you move off it only when your evidence earns it.

What would count against that position? I want the arguments and observations that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they are — not just the usual objections critics rehearse, but anything a defender of this principle could themselves recognize as a genuine cost. In particular I want to know wether the view secretly collapses into "atheism is fine but so is theism as long as everyone is sincere," whether the "ought" in "we ought not to believe on insufficient evidence" assumes we can control belief at will, and whether the burden of proof is being asked to do more epistemic work than it can (as opposed to being just a conversational norm). Be willing to tell me if the position ends up so qualified that it doesn't risk anything anymore, and whether that's a problem for this kind of view.

Personally I'd be most moved by a case where withholding belief does real damage that the theist's belief would have prevented, or by someone showing that the "default" I call unbelief is itself a substantive belief doing hidden work. But dont let that steer the whole answer.
