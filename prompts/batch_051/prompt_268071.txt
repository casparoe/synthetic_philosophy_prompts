Read Christopher Nolan's film Dunkirk through the philosophical idea of the lost cause and the national myth: the process by which a military disaster gets converted into a narrative of collective character, providential rescue, or moral vindication, as in the Confederate Lost Cause, the siege of Masada, or the mythologized Battle of Kosovo. The film is widely taken to be a deliberately mythic rendering of the evacuation rather than a historical reconstruction: it interweaves land, sea, and air; it builds toward the civilian flotilla; and it closes with the returning soldiers' shame dissolving into public welcome and Churchill's rhetoric of defiant survival. Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions. Where does the film participate in the mythologizing of Dunkirk, where does it resist or draw attention to its own myth-making, and what would an honest critic have to concede about what the film omits, aestheticizes, or distorts—the French rearguard, the scale of the military collapse, the losses, the aftermath? Does the film ultimately reveal something true about how such national myths are made that a straightforward debunking would miss? Please write your answer in American English.
