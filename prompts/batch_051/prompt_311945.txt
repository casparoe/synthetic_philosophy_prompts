I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been turning over an argument that a psychologist colleague thinks is a knockdown case against moral objectivity. I'll reconstruct it in numbered steps, because I suspect an error but can't locate it. You are a philosopher of cognitive science who works on moral psychology and on the methodology of cross-cultural research; please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming candidate diagnoses before settling on one.

The colleague's initial argument runs like this:

(1) Well-replicated research comparing WEIRD populations with a range of non-WEIRD groups shows that moral judgment varies not just in which acts are condemned but in its underlying structure. For instance, whether accidental harm deserves punishment, whether purity violations count as moral wrongs independently of any harm, and whether one's moral obligations extend to anonymous strangers all vary systematically with cultural context.

(2) The best explanation of this deep structural variation is that the relevant features of moral cognition are acquired from the local normative environment, rather than being the outputs of a shared, culture-independent detector of moral facts.

(3) If the structure of moral cognition is acquired locally in this way, then our moral judgments carry no objective warrant: they report the conventions we were taught, not the deliverances of a capacity that tracks a mind-independent moral order.

(4) Therefore, moral objectivity is indefensible.

What bothers me is that the same argument seems to generalize beyond morals:

(5) The same research program has documented comparably deep variation in what communities treat as a good reason or a credible source of belief — for example, in how much evidential weight they give to controlled comparisons versus first-hand testimony, to consistency across contexts versus deference to authority, or to observable causes versus unseen ones.

(6) Applying the same move as in (3), those epistemic norms are locally acquired rather than objective, so no community's standards — including the standards of WEIRD experimental psychology — are objectively better than anyone else's.

(7) But premises (1) and (2) are themselves claims warranted by precisely those WEIRD epistemic standards. If those standards are no more objective than the moral conventions the original argument debunks, then the evidential case for (1) and (2) is just another local cultural convention, and the argument undercuts the very data it needs.

(8) So the argument proves too much: it cannot coherently both rely on the cross-cultural evidence and debunk the norms that license it.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, and I also take (8) to be unacceptable. Where exactly does this chain go wrong? If no individual step is simply false, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon, and what exactly abandoning it costs.
