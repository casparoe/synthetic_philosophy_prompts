As a novelist, I write scenes in which characters make flat assertions about matters they only half know, and I cannot reliably predict when a reader will treat the assertion as blameless rather than defective. A philosopher friend tells me the rule of assertion is knowledge: one should assert only what one knows. I suspect ordinary practice is weaker—that only belief is required, or perhaps only truth. I would like to know which of these three norms, if any, actually governs assertion. How could one bring evidence to bear on that claim? Design a concrete experiment or set of observations—for example, vignettes in which a speaker asserts a true proposition on a lucky guess, a justified false proposition, and a known proposition—and specify which pattern of results would support the knowledge norm, the truth norm, and the belief norm. Then say what the test could not settle, including whether it would reveal the norm people actually rely on in conversation or only the norm they report, and whether a positive result for any one norm would show that norm to be correct rather than merely customary.
