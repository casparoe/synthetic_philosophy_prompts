I work on the philosophical implications of computational complexity, and I have been trying to settle a question about one of the field's founding inferences. The classic argument runs roughly as follows. NP-completeness is a worst-case property: assuming P ≠ NP, no efficient algorithm solves every instance of an NP-complete problem, and on this basis it was long held that NP-completeness gives strong reason to expect practical intractability for real, substantial instances. The last few decades of SAT and SMT solver engineering appear to challenge the practical half of that inference, since modern solvers routinely settle large industrial satisfiability instances drawn from hardware and software verification, planning, and scheduling by exploiting structure that the contrived worst-case instances used in completeness proofs lack. Does the original argument survive this development, and if so in what repaired form? Identify the precise premise on which solver success bears, whether the inference can be restored by restricting its conclusion to worst-case instances, average-case hardness, or parameterized structure, and what the surviving claim about NP-completeness and practical computation should be. If the original argument does not survive, say what should replace it as a guide to which problems are tractable in practice.
