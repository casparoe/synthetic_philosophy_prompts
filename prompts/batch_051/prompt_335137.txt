I am taking a seminar on anthropic reasoning and observer selection effects, in which the doomsday argument has been a recurring topic. The argument, as we have studied it, starts from the premise that one's birth rank is a random draw among all humans who will ever live and concludes that one's having been born this early is evidence that the total number of humans will not be vastly greater than the number already born. I already know the standard objections and do not need them restated: (1) the self-sampling-assumption objection, which denies that one's birth rank should be treated as such a random draw; (2) the self-indication-assumption response, which holds that one's own existence is itself evidence for more observers and that this roughly cancels the doomsday shift; (3) the reference-class problem, which notes that the conclusion changes drastically depending on whether the reference class is humans, observer-moments, persons, or something else, and that the argument gives no principled rule for choosing among them; and (4) the proves-too-much objection, which points out that an ancient human applying the same reasoning would have confidently predicted an early extinction and been wrong. I am looking for an objection to the doomsday argument that is not on this list and is not a mere rewording, special case, or recombination of any of these. State the new objection, explain how it differs from the listed ones, and then tell me honestly whether it is any good as an objection. Please answer in American English.
