I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I follow a certain epistemic policy that I want someone to test. Here is the policy: when a scientific thinker argues for a substantive claim by appealing to a thought experiment rather than to measurement, experiment, or observation, I discount that appeal to nearly zero and wait for the actual data. I treat thought experiments as vivid illustrations that help me grasp a theory after I already have independent reason to believe it, not as themselves supplying new reason to believe it.

The case I keep getting stuck on is Isaac Newton's bucket in the Principia. I have only read discussions of it, but as I understand the reasoning: a bucket hangs from a rope and is made to spin. At first the bucket spins relative to the water while the water surface stays flat; later they rotate together and the water surface becomes concave. Newton argues that the concavity cannot be explained by the water's motion relative to the bucket or other ordinary bodies, because the water and bucket are at rest relative to each other at the relevant moment. So he takes the thought experiment to support the existence of genuine rotational motion with respect to absolute space, not merely relative motion among ordinary objects.

I want to know whether my policy is defensible when faced with this example. Should I treat Newton's bucket as merely an illustration of absolute space, which I should accept only if there are independent empirical or mathematical grounds, or does the thought experiment itself do real epistemic work? More generally, is my discounting rule a reasonable division of labor for someone without my own means to test the physics, or is it a bias against a legitimate source of scientific knowledge? Give me an honest verdict on whether I should keep the policy, abandon it, or refine it, and state the refined version if so. Please answer in American English.
