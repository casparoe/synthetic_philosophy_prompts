I work in the emergency management office of a mid-sized city. Every budget cycle we make decisions about preparedness spending and restrictive measures — buying out flood-prone lots, stockpiling ventilators, closing parks or public housing during heat waves, ordering a short citywide shutdown early in a novel outbreak — where we genuinely cant assign probabilities because the events are to rare or too novel. We need a reusable procedure my staff can run in about a day, without formal decision theory training, that turns the maximin versus minimax regret distinction into a practical tool, and that also tells us when the Harsanyi-style expected-value way of thinking would pull against the maximin way of thinking about the worst-off. For any given proposal, it should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person because it has become a values call, and where maximin and minimax regret would point in opposite directions. My director signs off on the big ones but wants to see the reasoning in plain language. Then run the procedure on three cases: spending a large share of the budget to protect a small number of medically fragile residents from a possible heat emergency this summer, buying out a low-income neighborhood in a floodplain at high cost, and imposing a week-long citywide shutdown early in a respiratory outbreak whose severity and transmissibility are still unknown. I want at least one of these to come out with an answer my office won't like, and I want you to be explicit about what the procedure can't do. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — just paragraphs like an email.
