I'm a journalist working on a piece about how old philosophical puzzles about the future hold up now that we live in a world of prediction markets, superforecasters, and probabilistic models. Here's the setup. Aristotle's sea battle argument, in its standard form, runs something like this: take a claim about tomorrow's contingent event — say, that there will be a sea battle. If its already true today, then the battle is already settled and can't not happen; if it's already false today, then the non-battle is already settled. Either way, the future looks inevitable. Aristotle's response is to deny that such claims are true or false before the event. Modern forecasting culture mostly refuses to say a future event will or won't happen; it assigns probabilities, like a market putting the chance of a sea battle at 62 percent. So my question: does Aristotle's argument actually survive the move from bivalent assertion to graded probability, or is it just dodged? I want the argument traced, not the history. Say which premise the probabilistic practice genuinely puts pressure on, and whether a repaired version of the worry comes back at the level of the probability assignments themselves. Keep the answer in American English.
