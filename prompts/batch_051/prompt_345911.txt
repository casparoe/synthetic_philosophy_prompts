I keep going back and forth between the thought that beauty is just taste and the thought that there is a real standard of taste. The question is whether the verdicts below can all be held together under one principle, and if not, which of them I should give up.

Here they are.

1. I don't think "beautiful" reduces to "pleasant to me." I enjoy a warm bath more than I enjoy some late Beethoven, but I would never call the bath beautiful; much of what I find most beautiful was grim or even unpleasant on first exposure. Yet I also can't make sense of beauty existing in a universe with no possible observer. If nothing could ever respond to a thing, I don't know what it would be for the thing still to be beautiful.

2. When a friend calls a song beautiful that I find cloying, I don't treat her as simply mistaken the way I would if she said Vienna is the capital of Germany. But I also don't treat it as a bare preference like chocolate versus vanilla. I argue with her, and something about the song itself feels at stake in a way it isn't in the ice-cream case.

3. I think taste can be trained, and that this is improvement rather than mere change. A critic I respect once saw beauty in a poem that left me cold. I took her judgment as evidence that I was missing something, not as one more vote in a tie of preferences. After I spent years learning the poem's tradition, its meter, and its allusions, the poem did come to seem beautiful to me, and I would say my taste got better.

4. But I refuse to accept that the trained judge's verdict settles the matter. If I have genuinely attended to the poem and still find it dead, my "I don't find it beautiful" is not a mistake in the way "I don't see the train coming" is a mistake. There is no fact I have simply failed to detect, and I don't owe the critic a retraction.

5. Two equally seasoned critics can disagree about a new work, and I find it plausible that neither need be wrong. Their disagreement doesn't collapse into mere preference, yet I wouldn't say one of them has misperceived the work.

6. I can find beauty in a Noh mask or a Balinese gamelan piece without knowing the conventions of those traditions, so beauty doesn't seem to depend wholly on cultural initiation. But I hold back when a work's point depends on conventions I can't decode: if a dance is praised for flouting a rule I don't know, I don't claim to judge whether it is beautiful.

7. Some cases don't look perceptual at all. I want to say a particular mathematical proof is beautiful, and I don't think this is metaphorical. There is no object to look at, no palpable sensory pleasure, and yet I am more certain of that verdict than I am about most paintings I like.

The best principle I have been able to articulate is this: an object is beautiful if and only if it is such as to produce felt aesthetic approval in suitably prepared judges under suitably favorable conditions. But I can't make that principle respect (4) and (5), and (7) sits badly with its appeal to felt approval.

Is there one principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures these verdicts better, even if it means revising some of mine? State it as precisely as you can, show how it handles each verdict or which ones it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it says about a case I haven't settled: a painting I found beautiful that is later revealed to be a skilled forgery—does it lose beauty, or only something else, and why? Give me a committed answer: pick a definite position and defend it, rather than surveying the options and leaving me to choose.
