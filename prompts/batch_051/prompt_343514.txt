I write a column on the ethics of automation for a general-interest science magazine, which means I ended up covering grown-up trolley problems for a living. Every few weeks some hospital, AV company, or public-health office pitches me a line like "the system never intends to kill anyone; it only foresees the death as an unavoidable side effect of doing the most good." My editor will run those lines if I sign off on the final wording, and my readers mostly trust me to have checked them against the doctrine of double effect. I know just enough about the doctrine to know I should have stopped winging it a while ago — especially after I realized it was built for a single human agent deliberating about a plan, while the people I cover are committees, optimization functions, and protocols. So: turn the doctrine of double effect into a decision procedure I can actually run in about thirty minutes before deadline. I didn't train in philosophy, so the procedure has to be usable by someone who can't reconstruct the debates from memory. I need the questions in order, what counts as a sufficent answer to each question, where the procedure should stop and say "this is not a mere side effect," and where it has to hand the call to a person because the step is a value judgment rather than something a checklist can settle.

Then run the procedure on three cases. A hospital ICU triage protocol that deprioritizes some patients and says it doesn't intend their deaths. A self-driving car that steers toward a motorcyclist to avoid a school bus. And a public-health official who says lockdowns foresee job losses and suicides but don't intend them. In at least one of those, make the procedure hand back an answer I'm not going to like. And be honest about where the translation breaks down: a committee or a trained neural network doesn't literally intend or foresee anything, so your procedure should say whose "intention" we're even testing — the designers, the operators, the algorithm, the institution — and whether that choice does any work.
