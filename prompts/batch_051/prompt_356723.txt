I run a reading group of about a dozen people, mostly programmers and designers who read philosophy on the side, and we have a 90-minute session coming up. I want an activity about orphaned software and abandoned work: what happens to a thing when the person who made it, or the person who was keeping it alive, is gone and nobody is clearly in charge. The idea I want them to feel rather than hear me explain is that one person often occupies several different roles at once — the creator, the holder of legal standing, the maintainer who actually pushes fixes, the person with the authority to say yes or no — and when that person disappears these roles don't automatically pass to the same successor. They come apart, and different people grab different pieces for different reasons. I want the activity to make the group stumble into the question of who gets to decide before the question has a name.

Please design a runnable role-play or structured deliberation for one 90-minute session, 10-14 adults, no legal background assumed. The case should be a small open-source tool or abandoned app the group can understand without knowing copyright law — something like a little utility a lot of people depend on, whose sole maintainer has died or vanished. Put a concrete decision in front of them: fork it under a new name, archive it as-is, rewrite it, let it die. The rules and role cards should be arranged so they can't just take a vote on that decision in the first ten minutes; some built-in constraint has to force the standing question (an heir with unclear moral authority, a security flaw, a contributor who has most of the code but no clear legal claim, an archive that wants to preserve it, a licence term nobody's sure how to read now that the author's gone). The blockage should come from the rules and roles, not from me stepping in and explaining. Materials should be copy-pastable: a one-page scenario, role cards, and either timed stages or an event deck. No real money; use points, chips, or in-fiction stakes if you need incentives.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea — whatever distinctions you build into the roles and rules — and be explicit about at least one feature the activity cannot reproduce (my guess is the actual felt weight of legal liability, and the way real orphaned projects stay unresolved for years) that I will have to supply in words afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g., the group agrees to quickly and papers over the standing question, or a loud person gets treated as the owner just for being loud — and how to fix each thing.

Structure the response in this order, with these section names:

- The activity: rules, materials, setup, role cards — around 600 words.
- How it teaches the idea: which rule does which bit of the conceptual work — around 200 words.
- Debrief: a script I can actually read from — around 300 words.
- Things that will go wrong: and the fixes — around 200 words.

Keep the prose casual, like this message. Feel free to leave the printable bits in plain text I can paste into a handout.
