I am a science journalist preparing a long-form piece on the case that the Neolithic transition from foraging to farming was, on balance, a bad trade for human welfare. In my notes I already have six standard arguments. The first is skeletal: early farmers were shorter and carried more signs of nutritional stress and infectious disease than the foragers who preceded them. The second is epidemiological: sedentary, crowded settlements and close contact with domesticated animals created the conditions for epidemic disease. The third is labor: farming demanded longer and more repetitive work than foraging, and foragers are plausibly thought to have met their needs with many fewer hours of subsistence effort. The fourth is social: stored grain surpluses allowed would-be elites to control the food supply and gave rise to durable hierarchy, property, and subordination that mobile foraging bands largely avoided. The fifth is food security: dependence on a narrow range of crops exposed farming populations to catastrophic harvest failure and famine. The sixth is ecological: early agriculture already brought deforestation, soil erosion, and, in irrigated regions, salinization.

I want a seventh argument for the same position that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or simple combination of any of the six. I believe the six above already capture the standard quality-of-life arguments, so the one I am asking for should differ in logical structure rather than adding another item in the same key. Give me that argument and say what it contributes to the overall case that the other six do not.
