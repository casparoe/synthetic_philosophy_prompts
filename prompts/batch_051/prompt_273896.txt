I am a teaching assistant in an upper-level philosophy of economics course, and I am grading final-exam essays from our unit on the meaning of utility and the revealed-preference program in consumer theory. One of the short-essay questions on the exam read as follows:

"Paul Samuelson introduced revealed preference theory as a way of rebuilding consumer theory without postulating unobservable, introspectively felt utility. Explain (a) the constraint this program places on the meaning of utility, and (b) whether it follows that utility is nothing over and above observed choice behavior. In particular, assess the claim that, because revealed preference theory defines preferences in terms of choices, the hypothesis that consumers maximize utility has no empirical content. Refer to the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP) and the Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP) where relevant."

A student submitted the following answer:

"Samuelson's revealed preference approach starts from the fact that markets give us choices, not reports of inner satisfaction. It therefore replaces the older idea of cardinal utility — a measurable quantity of pleasure or desire — with the idea that a consumer prefers bundle x to bundle y just in case the consumer would choose x whenever both are affordable and y is not chosen. Utility then functions only as a numerical index representing this preference ordering: saying that u(x) is greater than u(y) says nothing beyond the fact that x would be chosen over y, and any increasing transformation of u represents exactly the same choices. The constraint on meaning is accordingly severe: no introspective intensity, no cardinal units, and no interpersonal comparison.

Against that background, I agree that utility is nothing over and above choice behavior, and I think it also follows that the utility-maximization hypothesis is empty. Since preferences are defined through choices, any collection of choices whatsoever can be rationalized by some utility function — one can always assign numbers afterwards that make the chosen bundle look optimal. The claim that consumers maximize utility is therefore not an empirical discovery about the mind but a convention for labeling what people do. WARP and GARP are the real content of the theory, because they are genuine regularity constraints that some actual behavior satisfies and other actual behavior violates. But even where they fail, we can simply describe the agent as having intransitive or context-dependent preferences and assign a local utility index to each situation. Thus preference and choice turn out to be interchangeable, and the correct conclusion is that the utility-maximization hypothesis adds nothing beyond the consistency axioms."
