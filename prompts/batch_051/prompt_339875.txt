I keep trying to make liberal neutrality and public reason talk to the AI governance literature, and it's coming apart on me. Here's the problem. The alignment crowd says we want AI that acts on human values or preferences. But liberal neutrality, at least on the reading I work with, says that coercive state power can't be justified by appeal to a contested comprehensive doctrine; you need public reasons, or at least reasons no one can reasonably reject. Now suppose we put a high-stakes AGI in charge of something like triaging benefits or allocating organs or setting bail. It is making decisions that the state would otherwise have to justify. If the system is aligned to aggregate preference satisfaction, that's already a controversial theory of value, never mind the interpersonal comparisons. If it's aligned to some thicker account of human flourishing, that's even more clearly a comprehensive doctrine. So my question: is there a principled way for a value-aligned AI in these roles to be neutral in the liberal sense, or is alignment just value-laden all the way down? I suspect the clean answer is that neutrality is a constraint on state justification, not on technical artifacts, but then what do we say to people who insist the algorithm is the justification when it's doing state-like allocation? I'd like you to do two things: tell me where my framing is wrong or at least contested before I build a chapter around it, and separate what is actually settled among people working on this from what is genuinely up for grabs. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. Also, have I commited the classic mistake of treating the AI as a mini-sovereign?
