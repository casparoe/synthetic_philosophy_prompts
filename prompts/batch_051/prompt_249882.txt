I am trying to work out what I actually think about how far an author's intention determines the meaning of a text, going by my gut on particular cases.

A novelist writes a character who is, in every scene, petty and cold. In a later interview the novelist says the character is generous underneath, but no detail in the novel so much as hints at this. I say the novel does not mean that the character is generous; the later intention cannot do that work.

A poet describes the enormity of the night sky, having always believed that enormity means great size. I say the line does not mean what she intended; it means what the word in its context conveys, and I am uneasy about this.

After a novelist's death, a notebook is found, dated during the writing of her last book, in which she records that the final chapter, which the published text leaves genuinely ambiguous, is meant to be a dream sequence. I say the notebook settles the matter: the final chapter means a dream sequence.

A screenwriter and a director co-create a film. One later says a particular line was written as a joke; the other says he shot it deadpan because he took it as sincere. The finished scene itself gives no cue either way. I say neither of them simply gets to decide; the line is, as it stands, indeterminate.

A translator renders a poem into another language, altering its imagery to preserve its rhyme. I say the translation does not mean what the original means, and is not simply the original poem's meaning in new words.

I keep going back and forth on one further case. A living poet publishes a poem that readers and most critics read as a straightforwardly nationalistic statement. The poet says it was intended as parody, but the poem contains no signal of parody, and no one who had not heard her say so ever read it that way. Is there a principle that captures cases one to five, or nearly all of them? State it as precisely as you can, say which case motivates each part of it, and tell me what it says about the parody poem. If no one principle captures all five, which verdict should I give up, and why that one rather than the others?
