I'm taking a philosophy of technology seminar and for my final paper I want to write on 2001: A Space Odyssey, but not the tired 'HAL is a warning about AI alignment' reading. What interests me is the teleological shape of the thing: the monolith shows up among the early hominids and seems to nudge them toward tool use, a second one is buried on the moon and points toward Jupiter once we've gotten that far, and the film ends with Bowman becoming the Star Child after HAL, the one genuinely new mind the humans built, fails or is broken. I want to read the movie through the idea that biological humanity is a stepping stone in a longer history of intelligence, and that this history has something like a telos — a direction, if not a designer — that hands off to something post-biological. But I dont want to flatten the film into propaganda for that view, and my instructor has a habit of circling everything that doesn't fit.

So: where does 2001 actually support the teleological reading, and where does it resist or complicate it? Is the Star Child really a successor, or just a person transformed by contact? Is the arc shown as guided by the monoliths, or just triggered by them? Does the film endorse the handoff as good, or does it linger too much on what's lost for that? And does the film point to anything that philosophers defending an AI-succession thesis tend to miss or want to avoid? Point me to specific scenes rather than re-telling the plot, and be honest about the places where the lens stops fitting.
