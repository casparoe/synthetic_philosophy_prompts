I'm a high school student, and in my philosophy club we were given a puzzle that I have been unable to stop thinking about. I know it is a known thought experiment, but the way it was presented to me turned it into an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the weak point. I would like a careful diagnosis.

Here is how I understand the puzzle:

(1) A person cannot rationally form a genuine intention to do something they are certain they will not do. To intend is, at least, to be settled on doing the thing; if you know you won't do it, you can at most wish you would do it.

(2) Suppose a billionaire offers you one million dollars, which will be wired to you tomorrow at 9 a.m., on one condition: at midnight tonight you must genuinely intend to drink a harmless but nauseating toxin tomorrow at 5 p.m. Whether you actually drink has no effect on the payment, and drinking brings a cost (a miserable evening) with no further benefit.

(3) Therefore, at midnight tonight you are in a position to be certain that tomorrow at 5 p.m. you will not drink. By then the money will already be yours, and a rational person does not voluntarily take on a cost for zero benefit.

(4) From (1) and (3), at midnight tonight you cannot intend to drink the toxin. So you cannot meet the condition, and you do not receive the million.

(5) But the same reasoning generalizes to any commitment whose reward arrives before the performance and whose performance is net costly at the time of acting: a contractor paid before finishing the job, a student who receives a grant before writing the required report, a friend who gets what they want before keeping their side of a bargain, a borrower who faces only a mild penalty for defaulting. In each case, parallel reasoning says the person cannot form the intention to perform, so the commitment is either impossible or possible only through self-deception.

(6) That conclusion is unacceptable. Commitments of this kind are made and kept constantly, and any account of practical rationality that rules them out as impossible is clearly too strong.

So at least one step must be wrong. Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Please answer as a professional philosopher working in the philosophy of action and decision theory. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinct places where the argument could fail before you commit to a diagnosis.
