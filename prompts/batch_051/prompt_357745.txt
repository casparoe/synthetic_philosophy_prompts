I got into it with a friend who says she might never have existed if her parents hadnt met, and instead of proving her wrong I ended up with a argument that seems to prove everything that exists had to exist. That cant be right, but I cant see which step is doing the damage. Heres the thing I wrote out afterwards:

(1) Take anything that exists, like this coffee mug. It could not have been something other than itself.
(2) A thing also could not fail to be itself. There is no possible situation in which some thing is not itself.
(3) If a thing is itself in a possible situation, then that thing is there in that situation.
(4) So each thing there is is itself in every possible situation.
(5) So each thing there is is there in every possible situation.
(6) So everything that exists exists necessarily, and there could not have been any things that do not actually exist.

I flinched at (6) as soon as I wrote it, because it seemed like my friend could have failed to exist even though I cant point to a non-actual her. But going back through, every one of the first five looked fine on its own. Id rather not give up (1) or (2) if I dont have to, since they just say a thing is itself. Where does the argument go wrong, or if its valid which premise is least costly to reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it? If theres a hidden slide between two of the steps, tell me exactly what that slide is.
