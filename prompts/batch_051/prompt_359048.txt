There's an old line of thought, associated with Montesquieu and Smith and a lot of eighteenth-century writers, that commerce civilizes people: when you trade with strangers repeatedly, you have to become punctual, trustworthy, agreeable, and willing to stick to your word, because the next deal depends on the other party remembering who their dealing with. Self-interest gets channeled into decent behavior, and the market softens manners. The mechanism pretty obviously runs on repeated dealings between people who can identify each other and make each other answer for past behavior.

Now suppose most of the commerce a person actually does is one-click buying from anonymous sellers on a platform, where the only durable memory of your trading character is a reputation score the platform computes. And suppose further that the score is cheap to game or buy, gets chopped up across shell accounts, and is rarely read by buyers until something has already gone wrong. Does the civilizing argument survive this? I can see a case for saying it's stronger than before, because the platform keeps a bigger ledger than any village could; I can also see a case for saying the premise that the counterparty will remember you has been silently cut out, so the mechanism stops doing its civilizing work even if prices stay low. I want to know whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or is just about a different situation now. Pick out which premise the new setup bears on and whether it can be restored.

And tell me what would change your mind: what fact about the new setup would flip your answer, or where in your reasoning you're least sure.
