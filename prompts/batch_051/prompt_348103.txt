I coordinate a volunteer flood-rescue unit in a region that gets a few bad seasons a decade. When a call comes in, whoever is on scene has about ten minutes to choose which site to reach first, and the wrong choice means people die. Our standing rule has always been that when you can reach only one group, you go to the larger one. I have come to believe that rule was never actually justified to us. I can see the point of saving as many as possible when our resources can be expanded, but in a forced choice between one person and five, I cannot see how the four extra lives make the five's claim any stronger. Each one of them needs rescue exactly as much as the one does, and the one's need does not shrink because five other people are somewhere else. So I am drawn to the view that the numbers do not by themselves count. But I am also uneasy, because a procedure built on that view would sometimes leave a child behind while I saved one adult, and I would have to stand in front of her family and explain it.

I am asking for a reusable decision procedure for my team, built on the commitment that each imperiled person's claim counts equally and that the bare number of claimants is never a reason. It has to be something a tired volunteer with no philosophical training can run in ten minutes in the field, with me not there. It should set out the questions in order, say what counts as an adequate answer to each, say where the procedure ends in a definite instruction — go to group A, go to group B, draw lots — and say where the procedure stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person because a value judgment cannot be avoided. This procedure will be what we defend afterwards, when a family asks why their person was not chosen.

The procedure must be honest about the hard cases, and I know most of them already: unequal chances of success, such as six people panicking in fast water against one person on a stable roof; special relationships, such as a rescuer whose own family member is in the smaller group; partial information, where a group may be larger than reported or may already be dead; and cases where we cannot identify the individuals before we choose. But the hardest point for me is this. If I really think each person has an equal claim, I cannot tell whether that forces drawing lots between the groups, so that the one is just as likely to be saved as the five, or whether it forces giving each individual an equal chance, so that the five win five times out of six. Those are different instructions, and one of them seems to let the numbers back in by the side door. The procedure has to settle that and explain why the settled answer is not just counting heads in disguise.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing the reasoning step by step and the instruction each case produces.

First, five strangers and one stranger, in different directions, equally reachable, all equally likely to survive if reached, and no special tie between any rescuer and any of them.

Second, the same situation, except that one of the five is the sister of the rescuer on scene, and the team has an informal norm that rescuers do not abandon family.

Third, a case of your own choosing in which the procedure, run honestly, gives an answer I will not like — for example, instructing the team to leave a larger group to die in order to save a smaller one, or telling a rescuer to set aside the fact that their own family member is one of the people at risk.

Finish with a blunt assessment: which step of the procedure carries the real philosophical weight, what the procedure cannot settle no matter how carefully it is written, and what a reasonable colleague who believes we are always obliged to save the greater number would say is wrong with it.
