I've been stuck on this claim that floats around philosophy of biology: a genuinely explanatory account of a biological phenomenon—how a protein folds, why a neuron fires, whatever—has to pick out the actual mechanism, the entities and activities organized to produce the thing. A model that just nails the input-output mapping, or a bare regularity, is supposed to be less explanatory even when its predictions are perfect. That sounds like a claim about what explanation is, so on its face no experiment can touch it. But I keep thinking you could at least put pressure on it, e.g. with a case like AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures extremely well while giving you very little of the folding pathway or the forces involved, and biologists still argue about whether it explains folding at all. If I wanted to test the mechanism requirement rather than just argue about the word explain, what's the best test you can design? Be concrete: what would you vary or measure, what pattern of results woudl count for and against the requirement, and what part of the question would even the best-designed test leave unsettled?
