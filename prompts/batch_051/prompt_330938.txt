Two people in my philosophy of pain seminar started arguing after class yesterday and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. The debate was about pain asymbolia, which we'd just been reading about — it's the condition where people with damage to part of the insula will say a pinprick hurts and can locate it and rate its intensity, but they dont wince or withdraw, and they report that it doesn't bother them. The whole argument was over what this shows about whether pain is intrinsically bad.

Ana opened by saying the standard view we'd been assigned holds that pain is essentially or intrinsically bad — that a pain, just by being a pain, gives you a reason to avoid or stop it. And asymbolia looks like a counterexample: the patient reports "it hurts, but it doesn't bother me" and acts that way. So the same pain seems to be present without the badness.

Ben said she was moving too fast. In asymbolia, he argued, what the patient has is closer to a bare noxious sensation with the awful part removed. If pain is partly defined by its awfulness, then what's left in asymbolia just isn't pain — so the case can't show that pain can occur without badness.

Ana called that a no-true-Scotsman move. The patient had pain before the lesion, knows what it's like, and now says the same sort of thing is happening but it doesn't bother them anymore. If we can't take a report like that at face value, she said, what evidence could ever show pain and badness come apart?

Ben pushed back that he wasn't redefining pain on the fly. There's independent evidence that pain has two sides: a sensory-discriminative side that tracks where a stimulus is and how intense it is, and an affective-motivational side that gives it its urgency and awfulness. Asymbolia patients seem to have damage to the second side while the first keeps working. But that's exactly what you'd expect if "pain" names the whole package — the badness isn't just a reaction to pain, it's partly constitutive of it.

Ana then granted the package view for the sake of argument. Even so, she said, the components come apart, and when they do, only one component is doing the bad-making work: the affective-motivational one. So the badness isn't intrinsic to the noxious sensation itself — it's contributed by the motivational system.

Ben answered that she still hadn't refuted the intrinsicality claim. If the affective component is a constituent of pain, then pain is still essentially bad; she'd only shown that the badness lives in one constituent. To refute it, she'd need a case of the full package without badness, not a case where a part is removed and the remainder is called pain.

Ana came back that this is precisely the dispute. The patient calls the remaining sensory core "pain" and rates it the same intensity as before. If Ben insists the word only applies to the full package, then their disagreement is about labels, not about whether the bad-making component is separable. Either way, she said, the badness tracks one component rather than pain as a whole.

Ben's last point was that the label does real work: if ordinary "pain" refers to the package, then asymbolics don't have pain full stop, and Ana's claim that "pain can occur without badness" is false; if "pain" refers to the sensory core, then she's changed the subject.

I tried to referee and genuinely couldn't. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each argument goes wrong, and what both of them are missing? And, if you had to, who is more right overall, or is this a genuine tie?
