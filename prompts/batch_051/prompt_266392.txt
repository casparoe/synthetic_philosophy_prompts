I spent my career in sensory physiology, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of mind seriously. The problem I want help with is not the neurology, which I think I understand, but the structure of a philosophical dispute that seems to me to have stalled: the dispute over what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain.

Pain asymbolia is a neurological condition in which a person responds to noxious stimulation in a strikingly divided way. Asked about a pinprick or a hand immersed in painfully cold water, the patient can locate the stimulus, rate its intensity, and describe its quality as sharp, burning, or aching, yet denies that it is unpleasant, does not withdraw or grimace in the usual way, and may even laugh while the stimulus is applied. In ordinary language: the person seems to have pain without the hurt.

The philosophical debate, as I have reconstructed it, runs as follows. One camp holds that a pain's badness is internal to the pain itself: an experience that is not in some way bad, unwanted, or aversive is not pain in the full sense. On this view the asymbolic patient is either not feeling genuine pain at all or is feeling a truncated, defective fragment of pain, a bare damage signal from which something essential has fallen away. The other camp holds that normal pain is a composite of two dissociable components: a sensory component of location, intensity, and quality, and an affective-motivational component of unpleasantness and the impulse to stop or escape. On this view asymbolia is exactly what the composite picture predicts, and it shows that the badness of pain is not an intrinsic feature of the sensation itself but something normally, and contingently, welded onto it.

The clinical facts are not, as far as I can tell, in dispute. Both sides cite the same dissociation and draw opposite lessons, and neither has moved. My working hypothesis is that this is less an empirical disagreement about the clinical picture than a disagreement about what the word "pain" is being used to pick out, the narrow sensory episode or the whole normal package including motivation, and, beneath that, about what it takes for a property such as badness to be intrinsic to an experience rather than a constant companion of it. I want that hypothesis tested.

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the dispute substantive or verbal? Are the two camps answering different questions under one heading? Is a hidden commitment in the word "intrinsic" doing the work? If my hypothesis is wrong, say where the real disagreement lives. I am not after a historical survey or a list of references; I want the internal structure of the disagreement worked out. After the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a reframing of the question, or an argument that progress should not be expected.

Please organize your reply under the following headings, in this order:

1. The impasse (roughly 200 words): restate the two positions precisely and explain why the asymbolia evidence fails to discriminate between them.
2. What each camp must assume (roughly 300 words): identify the background commitments about "pain," about "intrinsic," and about what a dissociation can show, that keep each position stable.
3. Diagnosis (roughly 350 words): defend your account of what kind of disagreement this really is.
4. What would count as progress (roughly 200 words): given the diagnosis, say what would move the debate, or argue that it should not be expected to move.
