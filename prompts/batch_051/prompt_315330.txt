I haven't spoken to my brother since last fall, when he said I was "choosing strangers over family" because I supported resettling more refugees while our cousin had been out of work for a year. The fight itself I could get over. What I can't shake is how differently I treat the reasons on each side. I think a refugee policy should be about who is harmed and whether the burden is shared fairly, and I regard those as real moral considerations anyone has to answer to. When he talks about loyalty to the country, or the feeling that something sacred is being lost, I don't hear a different weighing of the same stuff; I hear preferences dressed up as morality.

Then I started reading about moral foundations theory, and now I'm worried I've just been flattering my own settings. The version of it I've seen claims people build moral judgments on a few different foundations—care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation—and that liberals like me lean mostly on the first two while conservatives spread across all of them more evenly. That sure fits my brother and me.

So here's the asymmetry I want you to either justify or kill. I treat care and fairness as public reasons: I expect an objection like "people will get hurt" or "the rules won't apply equally" to carry weight even with someone who doesn't share my politics. I don't treat "it's disloyal" or "it's a desecration" that way; I file them under taste or rationalization. Is that asymmetry defensible, or does moral foundations theory commit me to saying my brother and I just have different moral taste buds, neither one more correct than the other? And if I can't justify the asymmetry, does that force me to treat his loyalty- and sanctity-based objections as being on a par with my harm- and fairness-based ones, or is there a third option I'm missing?

If I'm setting this up wrong—like if I'm asking a descriptive theory to do normative work, or the asymmetry isnt really an asymmetry—please just say that instead of politely answering the bad version of the question.
