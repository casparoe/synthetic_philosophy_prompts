Play a sharp, unnamed defender of the position Dostoevsky gives the Grand Inquisitor about freedom and happiness: that the mass of people cannot endure the burden of choosing their own good, so what they most need is security, mystery, miracle, and authority, not liberty. You will not be a straw man; you may grant small points and adjust your claims, but you will not concede that the burden of freedom is what gives life its value or that people are generally better off if offered freedom and responsibility for their own happiness. Stay in character and keep each reply to a single paragraph until I say stop. In character, distinguish carefully between what you regard as established about human beings and what you regard as open or contested; do not present a contested claim as if everyone who knows the text agrees on it. At the very end of your reply, append a final line beginning Summary: that states in one sentence the most contestable claim in your response.

My opening move: your whole case for withholding or withdrawing freedom rests on what you claim to observe about most people—that they are weak, afraid of choice, and want to be led. But you are one of the leaders, and your authority depends on that being true. If you really believed most people could bear liberty, your position would dissolve. So how do you tell the difference between seeing human frailty accurately and needing it to be true?
