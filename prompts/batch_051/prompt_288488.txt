After retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I began sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and we have lately been working through the familiar argument against eternal conscious torment. A punishment is just only if it is proportionate to the offense. Any sin a finite creature commits is finite in gravity, since it proceeds from a finite will and produces finite harms. Eternal conscious torment, by contrast, is infinite in severity: a torment that never ends has no upper bound on the total suffering it inflicts. And a finite offense cannot stand in proportion to an unbounded severity. Therefore no sin could justly merit eternal conscious torment, and the only coherent alternatives would be annihilation or universal reconciliation.

I am sympathetic to the intuition that the claim about infinite severity is doing the real work, but I have begun to suspect that the phrase infinite in severity hides an ambiguity about how severity aggregates over time. To expose it, I offered the following parallel argument, and my group has not been able to resolve it.

(1) Take an ordinary finite punishment: one hour of continuous pain at constant intensity.
(2) On the usual mathematical treatment of time as continuous, that hour contains more than countably many instants, and at every such instant the subject is in pain at positive intensity.
(3) The total severity of a punishment is the sum, across the instants at which it occurs, of the pain present at each instant.
(4) A sum over more than countably many positive quantities is infinite.
(5) Therefore one hour of constant pain is infinite in total severity.
(6) If infinite severity cannot be proportionate to a finite offense, as the anti-hell argument holds, then one hour of constant pain is no more proportionate to any finite offense than eternal torment is.
(7) That conclusion cannot be accepted: it erases the very distinction between a finite sentence and an infinite one on which the debate over hell depends.
(8) So something is wrong either with my parallel argument or with the original inference from endless duration to infinite severity.

Where does the parallel argument go wrong? Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, if the faulty step concerns how pain is summed over instants, does the corrected account of aggregation also vindicate the original anti-hell argument, or does it leave unsupported the original claim that eternal torment is infinite in severity? I would like a precise statement of how severity should be aggregated over time, one that makes an hour of constant pain come out finite while making clear exactly which feature of eternal torment, if any, makes it infinite.
