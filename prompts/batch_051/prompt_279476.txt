The handout reads: "The three classical schools can be put briefly. Logicism says mathematics is reducible to logic: every mathematical truth can be expressed in purely logical vocabulary and proved from purely logical axioms. Frege and Russell tried to carry this out, but Russell's paradox showed the logicist idea was hopeless, since the set theory it required was inconsistent. Formalism says mathematical symbols have no meaning in themselves, and doing mathematics is just the manipulation of such symbols according to stipulated rules; a mathematical statement is true just in case it can be derived by those rules. Gödel's incompleteness theorems refuted this picture, because they show that no consistent formal system can prove all arithmetical truths. Intuitionism says mathematical objects are mental constructions, so a proposition like Goldbach's conjecture has no truth-value until someone has actually proved or refuted it; and because truth just is proof, intuitionists reject the law of excluded middle, which assumes every proposition is already determinately true or false independently of any proof. The three schools are thus rival answers to the question whether mathematics needs foundations in logic, in rules, or in the mind."

Go through this claim by claim and tell me what a careful philosopher of mathematics would accept, what they would treat as a popular simplification, and what they would call wrong. I am particularly unsure about two points: whether Russell's paradox really showed logicism was hopeless, and whether Gödel's theorems refute formalism itself or only a specific formalist program. I am also inclined to think the final sentence's "logic, rules, or the mind" framing is too neat, but I cannot say what it gets wrong.
