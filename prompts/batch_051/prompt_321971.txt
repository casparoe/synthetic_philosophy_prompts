Last term I co-taught a short unit on the 'AI debate' proposal with two colleagues from the machine-learning group (I was the philosopher in the room) — the idea that if you set two AIs to argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge, the judge can often recognise which side is right even when they couldn't verify the reasoning from scratch themselves. We kept getting stuck on the institutional version of the idea rather than the formalism, and I'd like you to work through a clean counterfactual for me.

Suppose that, starting next year, an international standards body imposed debate as mandatory for any AI system above a capability threshold that is answering a "high-stakes" question. The system may not answer directly. It must instead output a transcript of two debater AIs, one arguing for and one against, and the only people who read the transcript are a panel of five randomly chosen adults with no relevant expertise, no internet access, one hour, and a small budget for follow-up questions. Their verdict is final and binds whoever asked the original question. Predict how this regime changes the epistemic landscape over ten years. Which sorts of question does it make more reliable, and which does it make less reliable? Who accumulates power under the arrangement — the people who train the debaters, the people who write the capability-threshold definitions, the people who select and breif the judges? What new forms of gaming emerge, and under what conditions does the whole thing collapse into an elaborate rhetoric contest rather than a truth-tracking mechanism? I am less interested in a yes/no verdict than in the chain of reasoning, so mark clearly which predictions you think are robust and which depend on assumptions you would want to see tested before betting on them.
