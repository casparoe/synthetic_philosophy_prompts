A student has sent me the following passage from a popular biology textbook and has asked me to help her decide how much of it to trust, since it sits uneasily with some philosophy of biology she has been reading. I would like you to produce the assessment I would give her. The passage reads:

"Living things are standardly identified by a cluster of features—organization, metabolism, growth, reproduction, responsiveness, homeostasis, and adaptation. But the cluster resists being turned into a definition: mules are alive yet do not reproduce, fire grows and spreads but is not alive, and viruses replicate and evolve only by commandeering the machinery of a host cell, which is why they are generally placed on the nonliving side of the line. Because every proposed definition of life has run into counterexamples, many scientists have concluded that there is no fact of the matter about what counts as alive: 'life' is not a natural kind but a pragmatic category, and how we draw the line in borderline cases is ultimately conventional. The working definition favored in parts of astrobiology—life as a self-maintaining chemical system capable of evolution by natural selection—is now the closest thing biology has to an official definition, and it neatly explains why mules count as alive while viruses do not."

Please separate your assessment into three parts. First, identify what in the passage is accurate or at least defensible. Second, identify what is a common simplification that would be acceptable in an introductory setting but requires qualification. Third, identify what a philosopher of biology or a biologist working on the definition of life would likely reject or regard as overstated, and explain the underlying conceptual issue. The student does not need a bibliography; she needs to understand which claims the passage has earned and which it has not.
