I am a novelist. My working vocabulary is the vocabulary of narrative craft—interiority, point of view, free indirect style, subtext, narrative distance, showing versus telling—and I think fluently in terms of whether a character is legible or opaque to the reader. Lately I have come to suspect that the craft ideal of legibility, according to which a character's motives ought to be made available to the reader and opacity is treated as evasion or failure, is really a moral demand in stylistic disguise. I would like you to take the philosophical debate over whether there is a duty to be legible to others—whether sincerity and transparency are owed to other people, such that one's beliefs, motives, and commitments should be rendered interpretable to them, or whether opacity and privacy are legitimate and worth defending—and restate it systematically in my craft vocabulary. Translate each main position as faithfully as that vocabulary allows. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which cannot be expressed in the vocabulary of narrative craft at all. End by saying what you think the residue indicates—a limitation of my vocabulary, a confusion in the philosophical claims, or a genuine blind spot in the philosophical debate.
