Two people at a party last night got into an argument I couldn't settle, over a scenario one of them had read online. A man has been sleeping with a woman for months while concealing from her that he is married: he keeps the ring off, never brings her to his place, and invents excuses about weekends. He knows she would never have slept with him if she had known. The question was whether her consent to the sex was valid. I'll reconstruct the back-and-forth as close as I can. Please referee it move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and why; then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing.

1. Iris said valid consent has to track what you are actually agreeing to. She consented to sex with a single guy, not to sex with a married man hiding a family. He withheld the exact fact he knew would flip her answer, so her consent is not genuine.

2. Sam said that standard proves too much. People conceal dealbreakers all the time during early intimacy: that they are broke, that they are more religious than they have let on, that they are also seeing someone else. If every withheld fact that would of changed someone's mind invalidates consent, a huge share of ordinary seduction becomes nonconsensual. That trivializes the concept.

3. Iris came back that this is why we need a line, and there is a familiar one: deception about the nature of the act or serious risks — who you actually are, whether you have an STI — invalidates consent, while deception about merely collateral matters does not. But you have just asserted marriage is collateral. From her perspective the person's availability is part of the core of the act, not a side detail.

4. Sam replied that it has to be collateral. If consent could be invalidated by any undisclosed fact the other person happened to care about, there would be no privacy left: you would have to disclose your whole history and psychological file before anything counts as consensual. That can't be the norm. At some point the person with the dealbreaker has to ask.

5. Iris said but he did not just fail to volunteer; he actively concealed — ring off, fake excuses, never going to his home. Active concealment of a known dealbreaker is deception, not privacy. And she did not need an all-or-nothing verdict: consent can be defective in degrees. This might be much less serious than force, but it is still a defect, because the yes was extracted under a false description of the situation.

6. Sam said "defective in degrees" is doing all the work. Once you give up the clean line between the nature of the act and collateral details, you have not given a replacement criterion for which defects count and why; you have just moved the vagueness. The old common-law line at least has the virtue of being administrable.
