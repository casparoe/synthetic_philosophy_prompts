Here are my verdicts on when a belief is properly justified, and I would like to know what account of justification they add up to, if any.

When a theorem in arithmetic is proved from the standard axioms and rules of inference, I count that as a conclusive justification even though the axioms themselves are not derived from anything further. I do not feel that arithmetic is left in doubt merely because its starting points are laid down rather than proven.

But when someone maintains that a certain event will happen because a guru he trusts said so, and, when pressed, answers only that the guru's word cannot be questioned, I regard the belief as unjustified. Confidence and composure do not by themselves make a stopping point legitimate.

If I try to justify my trust in perception by pointing to its past reliability, and then justify my trust in memory by pointing to my perceptual experience of remembering, I notice that the two supports only lean on each other. I do not think this small circle, by itself, gives any support to either faculty.

Yet in a criminal trial, when a dozen independent strands of circumstantial evidence—a witness's timing, cell-tower records, a receipt, a scratch on a car—interlock so that each strand makes sense in light of the others, I think the package is strongly justified, even though no single strand would do much alone. There the mutual support seems to be doing genuine work.

I also think an ordinary visual belief, such as that a cup is on the table in good light, is justified as it stands. If someone keeps asking why, I can fall back on how things look to me, and I do not think I first need an argument establishing that perception is generally reliable.

Against that, I do not think a claim becomes justified just because one can keep producing a new reason for each reason, so that the chain never ends. I feel no better about an argument that runs A because B, B because C, C because D, and so on forever: the endless staircase never seems to reach the ground, and at a certain point I suspect the chain is not doing any work.

Finally, a friend of mine holds an elaborate, internally consistent conspiracy theory in which every official statement is planted disinformation and every apparent coincidence is a clue. However much evidence the web absorbs, I do not think he is justified, because the web is consistent only at the cost of never being allowed to fail.

I clearly think some beliefs are justified. But when I reject endless chains, I seem to be left with stopping points about which I am picky and circles about which I am pickier. Is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all these verdicts? Which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up? And what does the principle say about a case I cannot settle: someone who justifies a claim by a chain of reasons that could in principle be extended forever, but in practice stops at a link he treats as good enough for now?
