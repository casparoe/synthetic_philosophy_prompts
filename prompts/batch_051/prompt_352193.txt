I'm a philosophy professor who teaches applied ethics, and a former student of mine—now a technical lead at a small custom-software agency—asked me for something I couldn't just improvise on a call. Her team builds tools for clients (logistics dashboards, fintech, marketing automation, that sort of thing), and every few months a contract comes in where the client's own business is legal but the feature they want clearly greases the wheels of something shady. Her examples: a payday lender who wants a "re-engagement" flow aimed at people who've already defaulted twice, and a landlord who wants to scrape tenant data. She wants a decision procedure her engineers can actually run in under an hour, before they agree to build anything, so the decision doesn't fall entirely on whoever happens to be estimating the job. Not a lecture, not "follow your conscience"—something with steps and a stopping point.

I'd like you to write that procedure. Ground it in whatever the philosophical work on complicity actually gives us—the difference between causing a harm and enabling one, sharing a purpose versus merely selling a tool, whether the contribution is fungible or bespoke, whether you're lending credibility or legitimacy, whether you benefit from the wrong, that sort of thing—but translate it so the team never has to read the literature to use it. It should be a checklist or decision tree in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure should stop with a hard no, and where it should escalate to the team lead or the whole team because the question has become a values call rather than a factual one. Then work it through two or three cases: (1) the payday-lender re-engagement feature, (2) a hospital that wants an internal dashboard which could also be used to track which nurses file the most complaints, and (3) something a few removes back—a scheduling app for a company whose parent corporation is involved in something ugly, the kind of case where your never quite sure whether it's your problem. Please make sure at least one worked case lands on an answer the team won't like, so I can tell whether the procedure actually bites. And be honest about where it would fail: the genuinely complicit work it might still wave through, and the harmless work it might block.

No bibliography, no reading list, no footnotes—my friend wont use them. If you're relying on a named view or argument, put it in your own words.
