I'm a novelist and I've been turning over Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma for months, trying to figure out how to get it into a story without turning it into an essay. You know the setup? Twenty people are each told that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon tomorrow, that person gets a billion dollars; if nobody sends, or more than one sends, nobody gets anything; and they can't communicate with each other. The pull of the thing is that ordinary rationality leaves you with nothing solid to stand on, and the only move that feels right is to randomize as if the other nineteen are doing the exact same arithmetic, sending with probability one in twenty. Then there's the Luring Lottery, which Scientific American actually ran in the eighties: you mail in a postcard saying how many entries you want, the prize is a million dollars divided by the total number of entries and awarded to one randomly chosen entry, so a bigger number improves your odds but shrinks the prize for everyone, including you. If every person reasons "I'll just enter a little more," the whole thing runs away, and people really did mail in googolplexes, and the prize became worthless. I don't want a story that explains either of these. Write me a short story, around 1,200 to 1,800 words, where the characters are inside a Luring-Lottery-like situation — a small-town raffle, an online bet, a family inheritance scheme, anything — and the real drama is a character slowly realizing that acting well means treating her own reasoning as a shared object, something she should expect others to follow exactly because they're her in the same position. The realization should land like vertigo, not like a lecture. No one should every say "superrationality" or "Platonia." And don't end it as a puzzle with a clever twist; end it in the feeling of trusting that strangers will do the same delicate arithmetic you did.
