Last night me and a friend got into a long argument about whether sphexishness and out-of-distribution brittleness in ML are the same thing, and I still can't decide. You know the digger wasp routine: it paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow entrance, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and only then drags the cricket in. If you move the cricket a couple inches while the wasp is inside, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the threshold, and goes through the whole inspection again — over and over, indefinitely. The eerie thing is that it looks like it "knows what it's doing" until a tiny perturbation shows it's just running a fixed subroutine.

My friend's claim was that a model that nails it's training distribution and then collapses on slightly reworded or shifted inputs is doing exactly the same thing: the competence is real enough for the narrow environment, but the failure reveals it was a rigid policy rather than a flexible grasp of the task. I wanted to say no, the ML case is statistical generalization and the model is still doing something flexible between the failure points, whereas the wasp is an evolved fixed action pattern with no learning involved. He said that's a difference in origin, not in structure: both are systems that track a goal without representing it. We went in circles for an hour.

So: is this parallel deep or superficial? If it is deep, does anything transfer back the other way — say, from the ML distinction between memorization and generalization to the free-will debate that sphexishness usually gets dragged into? Or does the analogy break somewhere specific, like the wasp having nothing analogous to a loss function, or the model being able to notice its own failure in a way the wasp can't?
