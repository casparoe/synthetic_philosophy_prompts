I've been turning over a puzzle in my spare time, and I keep arriving at a view I can half-defend in conversation but not against a really sharp opponent. It concerns words like "heap," "bald," and "fluent." No single grain of sand seems to make a pile into a heap, no single hair seems to make someone bald, and no single vocabulary word seems to make a learner fluent; yet we use these words all the time and argue with them as though they mark real divisions in the world. My settled inclination is that this is because such words genuinely leave the borderline cases open: when a case sits right in the middle, the sentence is not secretly true and not secretly false — our language simply has not decided it. The thing that unsettles me is that the opposite view, though it sounds almost mystical, might actually be stricter and harder to escape than mine.

Please play a committed, resourceful defender of that opposite view, and stay in character until I say "stop." You hold that every vague word does have an exact boundary in the borderline region: for every step-by-step accumulation of grains, there is a precise last grain — the one that turns the pile into a heap — and likewise for any vague word along any gradual series. You also hold that this boundary is unknowable in principle, not merely undiscovered; no amount of inspection, measurement, or testing could reveal it. You accept this as the price of keeping ordinary logic intact — in particular, the principle that every statement is either true or false — and you suspect that any attempt to introduce genuine truth-gaps for vague words only postpones the difficulty, because a line between the settled cases and the unsettled cases would then itself have to be drawn. You are not a caricature: you may grant that your position sounds strange and that you owe a story about why the line is unknowable, but you will never concede the central claim that the line exists, and you will not break character. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so the exchange feels like a conversation, and begin by giving your best response to the following worry.

Here is my worry. Imagine two piles of sand side by side, identical to every eye and every instrument, which differ from each other by a single grain; on your view, one of them sits just below the line and the other just above it, so one is a heap and the other is not, though no possible test could ever tell them apart. If the boundary is unknowable in principle, in what sense is it a fact about either pile rather than an invention we posit to keep our logic tidy? My view seems to respect the evidence better: I say "heap" simply does not settle such close cases, so the sentence "this pile is a heap" is neither true nor false in the middle. What is wrong with that?
