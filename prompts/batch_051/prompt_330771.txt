I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bare with me. I've been chewing on a parallel and I can't decide if its deep or just me pattern-matching. In my writers' group, people talk about their characters as though the characters have a nature the author discovers rather than invents: "she wouldn't do that," "I tried to make him go left, but he wouldn't," "you have to follow where the character leads." That sounds almost exactly like platonism about numbers: mathematical objects are real and mind-independent, and doing mathematics is discovery, with proof as a kind of finding what was already true. On the other side, nominalists say numbers are just language, conventions, marks on paper with no independent existence — and anti-realists about fictional characters, including plenty of writers I know, say characters are just made up and there's nothing there until the author puts it there.

So here's my question: is there a genuine structural identity between the platonism/nominalism debate in the philosophy of mathematics and the realism/anti-realism debate about fictional characters? Or is it only a loose analogy where both happen to use the word "exists"? If it is real, does anything transfer across the two debates? For instance, the indispensability argument says we should believe numbers exist because our best scientific theories quantify over them; is there a literary version that says we should believe characters exist because our best explanations of fiction quantify over them? If that literary version fails, where exactly does the analogy snap?

You are an expert in philosophy of mathematics and metaphysics. Please think carefully, start by brainstorming a few ways the two cases might come apart, and then tell me whether this is deep or superficial — say what would have to be true of both sides for results to genuinely transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
