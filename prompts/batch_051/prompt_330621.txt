I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how people at work talk about insiders who report wrongdoing. When the reported wrongdoing belongs to the organization — a scheme, a policy, a cover-up — people call it whistleblowing and treat the reporter as a hero even though they've burned their team. When the reported wrongdoing belongs to a peer acting on their own — stealing meds, padding expenses, drinking on shift — people call it snitching, and the same disclosure to the same manager becomes a betrayal that should have been "handled inside." I get that the two sorts of case usually differ in scale and in whether your punching up at the institution or sideways at a coworker. What I want to know is whether the distinction survives once those are controlled for. So here's the ask: give me the strongest principle you can find that makes reporting an institution's wrongdoing different in kind from reporting a peer's — and don't let it quietly presuppose that institutional wrongdoing is just bigger. Then run that principle against a case where the peer's misconduct is at least as harmful as the institutional misconduct, like a nurse who reports a colleague diverting patients' pain meds versus one who reports her hospital systematically understaffing. Be honest about the verdict: is the whistleblower/snitch asymmetry something we should keep, abandon, or keep only as a rough heuristic for when reporting is actually useful?
