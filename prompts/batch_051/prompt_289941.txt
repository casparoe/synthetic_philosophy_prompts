I am a retired geochemist who has recently begun reading philosophy seriously, mostly around questions of character and self-knowledge because they remind me of how one thinks about measurement error and calibration. I attend a reading group whose most formidable regular is a former trial judge with unusually stable views about humility and modesty. I have been keeping a record of how she rules on particular cases. Her verdicts, in paraphrase:

1. A Nobel laureate, asked to account for his success, says sincerely that he was in the right place at the right time and that his postdocs did the decisive work. He does not deny being a fine scientist, and what he says is true. She calls him genuinely modest.

2. A young scientist knows she is the strongest statistician at her institute, and she is right. She never mentions the fact, does not resent the greater visibility of others, and does not think her ability makes her a more important person. The judge calls her modest.

3. The same young scientist, asked directly by a hiring committee whether anyone else at the institute can run a specific analysis, replies plainly that she is the best qualified and gives the evidence. The judge says this is not immodest: a true comparative judgment, offered when it is directly relevant and without claiming standing beyond the case, is compatible with modesty.

4. A scientist says he is hopeless at a task while not believing it, in order to elicit reassurance. The judge calls this a servile form of vanity, not modesty.

5. A scientist genuinely believes she is among the worst in her field although the record clearly shows she is among the best, and she turns down collaborations for that reason. The judge says this is not modesty; it is a failure of self-knowledge and of proper self-respect.

6. A scientist knows he is the most accomplished person in the room and treats that fact as a reason others should defer to him even on matters outside his expertise. The judge calls him immodest even on days when he never says anything about it.

She treats intellectual humility as related to but not identical with social modesty. In one discussion, she said that a retired physicist in our group who routinely says he does not know enough to have an opinion when he is in fact the most informed person in the room, out of discomfort at being the expert, is showing intellectual servility rather than intellectual humility.

The case on which I want a prediction is this. Call the researcher N. N has scrupulously accurate beliefs about her standing in her subfield. She keeps a private record of how she ranks against each colleague, feels a quiet satisfaction when she overtakes a rival, and would be distressed if a close competitor passed her. None of this shows in her conduct: she shares credit, treats colleagues with ordinary respect, never asks for deference, and volunteers nothing about her rank. Like the statistician in cases 2 and 3, she would answer a direct professional question about her qualifications plainly and accurately.

What would the judge say about N? Is N modest, and would the judge regard N's modesty, if she finds any, as a virtue? Which earlier case is the closest analog, and where does the analogy break down? Please set out the rules that fit the judge's track record — I can see at least three she might be running: modesty as accurate self-assessment; modesty as a social virtue of not presenting oneself as above others; and modesty as an attitude of equal regard for persons irrespective of their merits — as well as a modesty-as-underestimating-one's-merits view that her verdict in case 5 appears to reject. Say which rule or combination best predicts her ruling on N, which alternative rules also fit the earlier verdicts, and what further case would most cleanly discriminate among the leading candidates. I am not looking for a literature review or reading list, only the reasoning from the cases as I have given them.

Please attach a rough confidence to the prediction and say which additional fact about N would most change it. And in doing all of this, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present a contested account as if it were the consensus.
