For a graduate-student workshop's closing session, I am organizing a Q&A with a philosopher who defends a Held-style political ethic of care: she maintains that the caring relation, not the autonomous individual, is the basic unit of moral and political analysis, and that care is a comprehensive normative framework, with justice treated as a subordinate value that has a legitimate but limited place within a wider care ethic. She is attentive to the familiar objections and does not want to spend time restating her framework.

Draft ten questions that push past exposition into contested territory, attaching to each a brief follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply and aims to block it. Include at least one question on each of the following:

- how a framework built from particular, affectively grounded relations accounts for obligations to distant strangers without implicitly importing impartiality through a back door;
- whether the view can identify bad care — care that is exploitative, smothering, or responsive to deformed needs — using standards internal to care itself, rather than borrowing from justice, rights, or autonomy;
- whether making the caring relation basic rather than the individual yields the wrong verdicts in conflicts where sustaining a damaging relationship benefits third parties or preserves a valued social role;
- whether care ethics can answer the suspicion that care, as historically feminized and devalued labor, carries the marks of subordination, and specifically whether it has a non-question-begging test for distinguishing caring practices worth endorsing from those worth rejecting.

End with one question about what observable pattern in a society's care practices — such as a durable gender division of care labor, the low pay of care work, or the routine sacrifice of caregivers' participation in public life — would count as evidence against treating care, rather than justice or capability, as the framework's basic normative concept.
