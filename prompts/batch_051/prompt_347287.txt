I've ended up defending expressivism in ethics, and I want the view stress-tested rather than given the usual sympathetic treatment. My position is: saying "Murder is wrong" isn't reporting a moral fact; it expresses a con-attitude, roughly being against murder. And I think the Frege-Geach problem gets far more credit than it deserves. The standard objection is that "if murder is wrong, then encouraging murder is wrong" embeds the first bit unasserted, so the expressivist can't say what the antecedent contributes or why modus ponens works. My line is that this is just the composition problem everyone faces: truth-conditional semantics owes a compositional story too, and expressivists can tell one where complex sentences express complex attitudes — a "being for" style account on which negation and conditionals are attitudes toward attitudes or pairs of attitudes. For basic logical embedding that seems good enough to me, and I suspect the remaining hard cases are no worse than the hard cases for truth-conditional accounts. I'm tired of people treating this as a decisive refutation. Play it straight: what would have to be true for my position to be wrong? Give me specific embeddings, inferences, or results in logic or philosophy of language that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which criticisms a committed defender should concede are real costs and which are cheap shots. For context, the cases that already worry me are attitude reports like "he believes stealing is wrong" and quantifiers over moral judgements, not the plain modus ponens case — so dont spend the whole answer there. Also say honestly whether this position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's a problem for a semantic research programme as opposed to an empirical theory, or not.
