A friend and I had a long argument yesterday about when Latin stopped being Latin and started being Italian, and it left me with an argument I cannot find the flaw in. Reconstructed, it runs: (1) There is an unbroken chain of speaker-to-speaker transmission from the Latin of the late Republic down to present-day Italian, each generation acquiring the speech of the one before and being understood by it. (2) For any two adjacent stages in that chain, the speakers were mutually intelligible enough that neither side would have judged the other to be speaking a different language. (3) If two stages are mutually intelligible in that way, then their speakers speak the same language. (4) Sameness of language is transitive: if the speakers at stage A speak the same language as those at stage B, and those at B the same language as those at C, then those at A speak the same language as those at C. (5) So by repeated application of (3) and (4), the Latin of the late Republic is the same language as present-day Italian. (6) But the same reasoning, run along the equally unbroken chain that ends in Paris rather than Florence, shows that the Latin of the late Republic is also the same language as present-day French. (7) So present-day Italian and present-day French are the same language, and by extension any two languages joined by a continuous chain of transmission would be one language. (8) Italian and French are not the same language. Something has gone badly wrong, and I am strongly inclined to keep the historical premise in (1) and the plain fact reported in (8). I want to know which step to reject, why it looked so plausible, and exactly what it costs to give it up. In particular, I worry that whichever step we reject, we may be left either with no sense in which a language is a single thing that persists over time, or with the conclusion that Latin and Italian differ only as arbitrary end-points of a continuum. Is there a third option? Please answer in continuous prose, as in an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
