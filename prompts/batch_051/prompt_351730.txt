I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether a government may defensibly discount the interests of people who will live in the distant future. My friend held that any positive pure rate of time preference is simply temporal discrimination: it treats a death in the year 2400 as less important than a death now, without any reason that would survive scrutiny. I replied that the discount rates used in public cost-benefit analysis are not only about impatience; they also incorporate expected growth in per capita consumption and the declining marginal utility of additional income. We could not agree on what a public agency should actually do, so afterward I looked up the manual our state's environmental review office uses.

Its chapter on long-range evaluation contains the following rule:

"Future costs and benefits shall be discounted to present value at a real annual rate of three percent. The three percent figure combines the pure rate of time preference with the expected rate of growth in per capita consumption, adjusted for the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption. The same rate applies in each future year, however far in the future the cost or benefit occurs."

A case has now arisen that the manual seems not to have anticipated. The office must evaluate a proposed geological repository for high-level radioactive waste, whose hazard profile extends on the order of one hundred thousand years. All of the manual's worked examples concern bridges, treatment plants, and similar infrastructure with lifetimes between thirty and sixty years, and nothing in it discusses horizons beyond a century. At three percent annual discounting, a serious accident at the repository in year one hundred thousand would have a present value vanishingly close to zero — less, in fact, than a single minor administrative cost incurred today.

I want to know whether the quoted rule, properly interpreted, requires the office to treat that distant harm as negligible, or whether the rule should be understood not to cover this kind of geological-timescale case at all. Please consider how the language itself, the purpose of the rule, and what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked each bear on the question, and indicate which of these should prevail for an official who must actually decide.
