Longtime lurker on the alignment side of rationalist-adjacent forums, and there's a split I keep noticing that I'd like someone to actually lay out. In security-flavored discussions everyone agrees average-case guarantees are basically worthless: if you show me a system that behaves well on 99.9% of a test set, I'll just say an adversary is going to spend all day in the remaining 0.1%, so we demand worst-case behavior or at least guarantees that survive a competent adversary. Fine. But these same people, when they're presenting there own results, report benchmark accuracy, mean reward, or pass rates on held-out evals — all numbers that presuppose a fixed distribution and treat the test inputs as a random sample. Red-teaming gets tacked on at the end as qualitative evidence, not as the thing that licenses the safety claim.

What I can't figure out is what view of "safety" or "guarantee" this mixed practice actually presupposes. Does it presuppose that there are two different senses of safety — one about ordinary deployment and one about adversarial pressure — and that they call for different standards? Or does it presuppose that the deployment distribution is stable enough that average-case performance is a decent proxy for worst-case, even though the rhetoric denies that? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, and is the practice coherent at all? And if we really did adopt the worst-case-view as the only legitimate kind of guarantee, what would a benchmark, an eval, or a section of a paper have to look like before it could claim that a model is safe?
