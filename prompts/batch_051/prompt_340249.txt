In my graduate seminar on mathematical concept formation this term, I have found myself pulled in two directions about what it means for a definition to be the right one. On one side is a deflationary instrumentalism I can argue for cleanly: definitions are chosen because of the theorems they make possible, and there is no further fact of correctness over and above that. The standard definition of a topological space, as a set with a collection of distinguished subsets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, earns its keep by being exactly what makes continuity, compactness, and convergence work in the abstract; a rival definition that closed the collection only under countable unions would not, on this view, be a false definition but a less fruitful one, if it failed to deliver those theorems. Group axioms, similarly, are shaped to capture the notion of symmetry of a structure, and a slightly different system of axioms that carved out the same class of examples would be an equally good choice — asking which was really right would be like asking whether a choice of coordinates is really in the manifold.

And yet I cannot shake a contrary conviction. When I seriously contemplate the countable-union rival, I do not merely notice a loss of theorems; it seems to me that it would miss something about what continuity is, gerrymandering the boundary between the local and the global. Nor does altering one of the group axioms feel like taking up a different but equally serviceable instrument; it feels like changing the subject. I am not asking for a verdict. I want to know what it would take for the instrumentalism to give way, what it would take for the correctness intuition to be debunked as an artifact of habit or pedagogy, and whether there is a stable intermediate position in which a definition can be genuinely right, but only as a compressed record of successful mathematical practice. Walk me through the costs of each move.
