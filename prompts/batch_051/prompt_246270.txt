Okay so for my math club I'm doing a little talk on whether math is discovered or invented, and I read this article that sums up Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations. It went roughly like this: Lakatos showed that math isnt really different from natural science. You don't establish a theorem by deducing it from fixed axioms; instead you put a conjecture and a proof out there and test the proof by hunting for counterexamples. When a counterexample shows up, mathematicians don't reject the theorem, they just renegotiate the definitions so the counterexample gets excluded — thats what all the stuff about monster polyhedra and Euler's formula is about. So on Lakatos's view, whether a theorem is true is just a matter of which definitions the mathematical community happens to accept, and since definitions can always be revised to save a theorem, no mathematical claim is ever genuinely refuted. Mathematics is therefore quasi-empirical: its like an experimental science except the experiments are counterexamples instead of lab observations, and its truths are conventions rather than necessary truths.

This feels a little off to me but I can't say exactly where. I get that Lakatos was pushing back against the idea that math is just deducing from fixed axioms, but the article makes him sound like he thinks definitions are totally arbitrary and mathematical truth is just a social consensus about how to use words. Could you go through the summary and tell me which parts a person who actually follows Lakatos would accept, which parts are overstatements or conflate two different claims, and whether the final conclusion — that mathematical truths are basically conventions, like the rules of a game — actually follows from the accurate parts?
