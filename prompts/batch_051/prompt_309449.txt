A foundation proposes to replace managerial hierarchy with a system of continuous mutual observation, on the hypothesis that discipline and knowledge about work should be produced horizontally rather than imposed from above. The proposed rules are these.

Every member of the institution keeps a public, continuously updated work log. Each day they must add one entry stating a single concrete task they completed and the number of hours they claim for it. All logs are visible to everyone in real time.

At the end of each week, the platform randomly assigns each member five colleagues. For each assigned colleague, the member must read that colleague's week of log entries and attach one of two tags: "load-bearing" (the logged work is something others actually depend on) or "exhibitionary" (the work is legible but does not plausibly bear load). Tags are public; the one-sentence justification accompanying each tag is visible only to the tagged colleague.

The platform converts these tags into a public score for each member: the trailing four-week count of "load-bearing" tags minus the trailing four-week count of "exhibitionary" tags. Scarce resources—travel, release time from administrative duties, priority on equipment—are allocated each month strictly in descending order of this score. There is no manager override, and there are no formal instructions about what should count as load-bearing or exhibitionary; the tags themselves are the only training data the categories ever get.

Finally, any member whose trailing score is in the bottom tenth for two consecutive months enters an audit: the whole institution reads that member's full log and all justifications in an open meeting, then votes by secret ballot on whether the member retains access to the platform and to the resources it allocates.

Analyze this institution as a working system rather than as a question of whether surveillance is good in the abstract. What does it incentivize members to write, to read, to tag, and to leave out of their logs? What kind of "knowledge" about work does the platform produce, and how does that knowledge feed back into who has power? Where does it get gamed first, and what would sophisticated gaming look like? Trace the likely second-order effects on collaboration, information sharing, and log-keeping itself. Does the system distinguish productive work from merely legible self-presentation, and if not, what does it actually discipline? Conclude by identifying one failure mode that could be fixed by a small rule change, and one that is probably intrinsic to the design.
