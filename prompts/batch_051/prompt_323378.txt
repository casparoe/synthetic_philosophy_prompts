I've taught a unit on blackmail for years, and I still find myself staring at the board every time I try to state what exactly is wrong with it. Take the clean case. Alice knows that Bob cheated on his taxes — or on his spouse, say. She says: "pay me five thousand or I'll tell the relevant people." By everyone's lights that's blackmail, and in most places it's a crime. But the two ingredients are each perfectly legal on their own. Alice is free to tell the relevant people (and might even be praised for it, if the tax thing is bad enough), and she's free to ask Bob for five thousand dollars with no threat attached. Yet put them together as a conditional — payment in exchange for silence — and we suddently have a felony. Why should a legal act plus another legal act sum to a crime?

The asymmetry gets stranger when you put it next to nearby practices. A lawyer can write "pay my client or we'll sue" — that's the entire business of settlement — and nobody says extortion, even when the lawsuit is mostly leverage. A tabloid can pay somebody for a story, but somebody who says "pay me and I'll stay quiet" looks like a blackmailer. The usual line-drawing candidates — the threat is coercive, the blackmailer creates no value, the secret isn't hers to sell, blackmail is a kind of private taxation — all seem to either sweep too wide or too narrow once you push on them. So I'd like a menu, not a sermon.

The ask, then. State the asymmetry precisely enough that we'd know what a justification would have to explain. Then take the serious candidate justifications (coercion, unproductive exchange, privacy, third-party effects, whatever you think the real contenders are) and test each against the settlement-letter case, the paying-for-a-story case, and any other hard case you think cuts. Say which asymmetry that principle would actually license, whether it draws the line where the law seems to draw it, and whether the blackmail asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present one contested theory as if it were the consensus. Keep it under 700 words; I want to hand it out in next week's seminar.
