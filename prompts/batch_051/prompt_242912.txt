I've been taking a class on ancient moral psychology, and we spent a while on Aristotle's account of akrasia in the Nicomachean Ethics—the idea that the weak-willed person acts against their own correct judgment because appetite or passion drags them around, as opposed to the intemperate person who doesn't even have the correct judgment to begin with. He also draws a distinction between the impetuous person, who acts before deliberating, and the weak person, who deliberates but fails to stick with it. Around the same time I fell down a rabbit hole reading about hyperbolic discounting and the β-δ model of present bias, where people make patient plans (finish the paper, save for retirement, don't text your ex) but then flip when the smaller-sooner reward is right in front of them, even though nothing about the underlying options actually changed.

What I'm trying to figure out is this: if Aristotle somehow got a look at the modern experimental literature on present bias—delay discounting curves, preference reversals, sophisticated vs naive agents, commitment devices like the one Ulysses used—what would he make of it? Would he file hyperbolic discounting under akrasia as he described it, or would he say the model misses the phenomenon entirely? Specifically, would he treat the preference reversal itself (rather than the failure to resist it) as akrasia, or would he say that at each moment the person is just doing what they most want, so it's not really acting against their own judgment? And would he think the sophisticated agent who precommits is more or less virtuous than the one who just... doesn't need the precommitment? I keep going back and forth, and my proffessor is no help because she thinks bringing discount functions to Aristotle is anachronistic, so I'd love a careful reconstruction of what Aristotle would probably say, including where you think he'd push back on the whole economic framing.
