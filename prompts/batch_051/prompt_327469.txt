You are an expert in formal epistemology, especially accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory, and you also have experience designing classroom games that let students discover technical ideas through rules and payoffs rather than through lecture. I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar of fourteen students, mostly philosophy majors who have taken one logic course, and I have ninety minutes to work with. I want to run a hands-on session on the central thought behind accuracy-first epistemology: that rational degrees of belief can be modeled as the credences of someone who values accuracy, or closeness to the truth, and who grades her own credences by a strictly proper scoring rule. I tried a version last year and opened with the formalism, and the room went dead; this year I want the ideas to arrive through what the students do.

Design a runnable ninety-minute activity in which the students come to feel three things: (1) why some scoring rules make honesty a bad strategy, so that a linear payoff for reporting a probability rewards bluffing or extremism, while a proper rule such as Brier or logarithmic scoring makes reporting one's true credence the strictly best response; (2) the jolt of seeing probabilistically incoherent credences get accuracy-dominated by a coherent shadow assignment that outscores them in every possible world; and (3) a clear-eyed sense of where this framework leaves off, in particular that it takes the agent's standards for expected accuracy as fixed and does not by itself tell anyone what the evidence supports. The machinery should be concrete: an urn of colored chips or a card deck with a known composition, small real stakes, roles for players, a scorer, and perhaps a shadow bookie who constructs the dominating coherent assignment. Give the exact payoff formulas and how to introduce them without lecturing, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a rule that the term accuracy-first epistemology is not spoken until the debrief.

Think carefully about which theoretical moves can be carried by the rules, payoffs, and roles, and which must be handled afterward; then write out the full activity as the instructor should run it. In the debrief, map specific moments in the game onto the theoretical claims, predict two or three likely failure modes, such as students not trusting the scoring formula or a group refusing to report credences that are not already coherent, and explain how to handle each. Be explicit about one feature of the real theory that the game cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply in discussion. The activity should stand on its own, so do not pad it with reading lists or citations. Please write your answer in American English, with the logistics spelled out precisely enough that I could run it tomorrow.
