I chair the Campaign Finance Board of a large city. We have before us an advisory-opinion request involving a social welfare organization, Citizens for Responsible Growth, that spent roughly $900,000 in the month before a city council election on television and mail advertisements criticizing one incumbent's record on land-use and development votes. The advertisements name the incumbent and show her photograph but never say to vote for or against any candidate. Almost all of the money came from three donors, one of whom has a development proposal currently pending before that same council, and the group's executive director previously ran the incumbent's opponent's campaign. Under our current rules, a group becomes a political committee — and thus subject to contribution limits, source restrictions, and disclosure — only if its major purpose is expressly advocating the election or defeat of candidates; communications that discuss public issues are treated as issue advocacy and left unregulated unless they explicitly call for a vote. Our enabling statute directs the Board to safeguard both the integrity of the electoral process and the free speech rights of speakers.

The Board is divided. One faction holds that the point of campaign finance regulation is not, or not only, to stop explicit quid pro quo exchanges but to prevent a political order in which a small donor class can purchase outsized access, agenda-setting power, and differential responsiveness; on their view this case is exactly the kind of circumvention that the political-committee definition should catch, and we should reclassify the ads so that limits and disclosure attach. The other faction holds that spending which stops short of express advocacy is protected political speech and association, that influence is not the same as corruption and cannot be regulated without a principled stopping point, and that voters are better served by more issue discussion even when interested money pays for it. Please draft the Board's decision memo for this case. It should recommend one of three actions — issue the advisory opinion, decline it, or issue it with conditions such as disclosure-only treatment — state the standard the Board should use to distinguish regulable election spending from protected issue advocacy in future cases, justify that standard with reasons both factions could recognize, engage seriously with the strongest argument for the losing side, and end with an actionable bottom line the Board can vote on.
