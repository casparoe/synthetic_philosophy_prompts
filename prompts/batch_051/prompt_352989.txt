I keep noticing that my moral arithmetic changes with the size of the group, and I can't tell whether it is tracking something real or just a quirk of how I picture things. When a close friend tells me she and her husband plan to have a second child, and I have good reason to think the child will be healthy and reasonably happy, I treat that as straightforwardly good news. I say the family will be better off, and some part of me thinks the world will be better off too, not simply different: there will soon be another person leading a good life, and that feels like an improvement. But if you asked me whether a city of two million people, all reasonably happy, is a better city than one of one million people who are exactly as happy, I would not say yes. I would resist treating the sheer number of residents as making any moral difference at all. What matters, I'd insist, is how well each of them is doing, not how many of them there are. I have something like the same hesitation about species. I would be sorry if elephants disappeared, but I do not think elephants matter more than pandas because there happen to be more of them.

So the same thing, the existence of an additional happy life, seems to count as an improvement when it happens inside a family, but not when it happens inside a city or a species. I'm not even sure where the switch happens: between a third and fourth child? Between a village and a metropolis? Is there a principle that makes sense of saying a bigger family is better but a bigger city is not, and a bigger species is no more valuable than a smaller one? Or am I simply being sentimental at one scale and clear-eyed at another? Please find the strongest justification you can for holding both judgments, or argue that one of them should be given up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, and then give your reasoning, without preamble and without restating the question.
