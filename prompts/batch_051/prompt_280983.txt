I am a student in a course on biography and moral formation, where we have been contrasting a Plutarchan, hagiographic use of lives—biography as an instrument of moral improvement even at the cost of strict factual completeness—with a truth-first use that treats accuracy to the historical record as a precondition for any moral value in a life. We have also been discussing a related idea from exemplarist virtue ethics: that readers imitate persons by taking up a coherent moral identity, and that a deliberately idealized life may be more imitable than a messy and accurate one. Please construct a thought experiment in which the hagiographic approach produces a genuine improvement in a reader's moral conduct that an accurate biography of the same person would not produce, even though the reader knows the life has been idealized. Constraints: the setting should be realistic (a school, a hospital, a congregation, or a community organization), the scenario should be no more than one paragraph, both biographies must be equally well written, and the improvement must be behavioral rather than merely emotional. After the scenario, show why the idealized life yields the improvement and why the accurate life would not; identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work; state what would happen if that feature were removed; and explain the strongest objection a defender of the truth-first approach would raise, along with the best reply available to the hagiographic side.

Please present the response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should be the scenario; item 2 should give the reasoning for the hagiographic approach; item 3 should give the reasoning for the truth-first approach; item 4 should identify the working feature and what happens if it is removed; item 5 should state the strongest truth-first objection and the best reply.
