I teach a course on the metaphysics of virtual worlds, and I keep returning to a question I'd like to turn into a short-answer exam problem: whether a wrong done entirely inside a game can count as a real harm, rather than merely a fictional event or an offense. I've been using four rough criteria in class. A harms B just in case (i) B has a genuine stake in something — it is tied into B's ongoing projects or what B needs to flourish, not a passing whim; (ii) A's action sets that stake back, leaving B worse off with respect to it than before; (iii) the setback is attributable to A as an agent, not mere bad luck or natural causes; and (iv) B's stake is in something B has a legitimate claim to, so that being distressed by conduct that is within someone else's rights doesn't by itself count.

Here is the case, which I've modified enough that you should treat it as invented. Amara spent four years curating a library in a long-running multiplayer world: thousands of player-written texts, records of in-game events, and unique cosmetic artifacts that existed only because she and others had composed or collected them. The games Terms of Service are the usual boilerplate — everything in-world is a revocable license, the developer owns it all, and anything can be deleted at any time — but in practice the admins never delete non-abusive content. Another player, using a long-known server-replication exploit, deliberately triggered an event that corrupted the account holding the library's core objects; the unique player-created items could not be restored from backup. The exploit gave no in-game advantage, and the player said they did it just to see what would happen. Amara lost something around which she had organized much of her leisure, several friendships, and a real sense of competence, slept badly for weeks, and quit the game for good.

Does Amara count as having been harmed, under the four criteria? Go criterion by criterion; where a criterion leaves the verdict underdetermined, say what would settle it. Then say whether the case puts pressure on the criteria themselves — especially on whether a legitimate stake can survive when a legalistic license insists the objects were never hers. End your answer with a line reading exactly 'Verdict: yes' or 'Verdict: no' (choose one), and nothing after it.
