After a bad year I quit drinking about eight months ago, and now I'm trying to write up what I actually think self-control is -- partly from reading Aristotle, but mostly from arguing with people in recovery meetings. I've ended up with the list below, and I'd like a proper audit of whether it hangs together before I present it to a small reading group at my local comunity centre. These aren't meant to be novel; they're just where I've landed, and I can no longer tell which of them are doing the work.

1. Moment-by-moment willpower is overrated. The real work of self-control happens upstream: not buying the bottle, not keeping it in the house, choosing who I spend my evenings with.

2. There's a real difference between the person whose appetites are already in order and the person who has to wrestle with strong desires every day. The first is what I'd call temperate in the full sense -- I think it's Aristotle's distinction between the temperate person and the merely self-controlled one -- and it's the better state to be in.

3. But I also think an addict who stays sober one day at a time through constant effort is more praiseworthy than a person who simply never had the pull. Stronger: if praise tracks effort, the struggling person is the more admirable one.

4. White-knuckling as a long-term strategy mostly doesn't work and does damage. In that sense I've come to think of real self-control as the opposite of repression, not a version of it.

5. Still, leaning on structures outside myself -- app blockers, not keeping the stuff in the flat, telling a friend to call me when I slip -- doesn't feel like cheating or like proof that I lack the virtue. It feels like self-control, just applied earlier in time.

6. And I think we should go much easier on people for failing in the moment when they're exhausted, grieving, or surrounded by cues; but I also think what makes someone reliable is what they do over months and years, not what they do on one bad Tuesday.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what's the least costly revision to make the whole set consistent? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and tell me which single change would clean up the most. Oh, and could you answer in British English? I'm in London and it's for a local group.
