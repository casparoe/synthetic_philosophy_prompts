I have recently retired from a career in physical chemistry and now convene a small philosophy discussion group at a local institute. My background leaves me with a stubborn sense that when a photograph works, it is not exactly a picture the way a drawing is a picture; it is more like an instrument readout, a sample of the world. I would like my group to examine this intuition rather than simply accept it.

I have been reading William Henry Fox Talbot’s The Pencil of Nature, published in 1844. In the introductory remarks Talbot makes what seems to me a strong claim: the images in the book were produced by the action of light alone on sensitised paper, through optical and chemical means, without the assistance of anyone trained in drawing. That claim has stayed with me because it suggests the photograph is not made by a hand interpreting the world, but by the world impressing itself on a surface. I want the group to feel the pull of that idea before they are told what it is.

Please design a ninety-minute session for twelve to sixteen adults, with no formal philosophy background, in a seminar room with tables, blank paper, and a printer. The session should be a practical workshop, not a lecture. Participants should be doing something within the first ten minutes. The aim is that they discover for themselves the question whether we see through photographs, without my naming the question or any philosophical term until the final debrief.

Here is what I know about my group: they are curious but argumentative, and they tend to collapse questions into sociology too quickly, asking who made the image, for whom, and with what motive. I want to slow that move down and keep them inside the perceptual character of the photograph.

Could you design an activity with three stages?

First, a sorting task using small printed cards. I can print photographs of ordinary scenes, a few drawings, and one or two photorealist paintings ahead of time. Participants should be asked to sort them along a line from something you look at to something you look through. I want the rule to be simple enough that they finish in ten minutes and begin arguing about borderline cases.

Second, a manipulation task in which each pair receives a single photograph and is asked to produce two altered versions: one by cropping, and one by adding a short written caption. They then present both to another pair. The receiving pair is asked what the photograph now shows. I want this stage to make the original sorting principle unstable: cropping and captioning change what is seen even though the photographic trace is unchanged.

Third, a short reconstruction task. Give each pair an unfamiliar photograph with no context and ask them to write down precisely what they take themselves to be seeing: the scene, the object, the moment, the cause of the marks. Then reveal that the photograph has been selected from a larger contact sheet, and show the adjacent frames. Ask the group whether the earlier claim that they were seeing the scene itself survives the news that the photographer chose this frame and not those beside it.

After these stages, I would like a structured debrief of no more than twenty minutes. It should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical problem: where the sorting task encouraged the sense of seeing through, where cropping and captioning made the photograph behave like a manufactured representation, and where the contact sheet raised a question about whether the photograph offers the world or an editorial selection from the world. The debrief should then introduce Talbot’s claim as a way of sharpening what was at stake. It should end by separating two positions the group will otherwise run together: the causal claim that photographs are formed by light from the scene, and the stronger claim that we therefore see the scene itself. The first can be true while the second remains in dispute.

I want you to be candid about what will go wrong. I suspect some participants will say that of course we see photographs, not the things in them. How should I respond without turning the session into a semantic argument? I also suspect someone will object that cropping does no philosophical work because the remaining part is still a genuine trace. How should I use the contact-sheet stage to answer that? And what part of Talbot’s picture can the activity not reproduce at all, because no printed card can reproduce the causal relation between a particular surface and a particular past object? I would rather be told where the activity is weak than have it pretend to settle the matter.

I do not want a list of readings or a historical survey. I want a session plan with timings, exact instructions for each table, and a facilitator script for the debrief.
