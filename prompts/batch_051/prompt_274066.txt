I've been trying to sort out my convictions on whether a democracy should spend public money on literature and the arts, and i'd like someone to audit the set for consistency rather than give me the correct view. My commitments, roughly as I'd defend them:

(1) A novel's value as literature is at least partly aesthetic. A great novel is great because of how it's made and what it does internally, not because it makes readers more empathetic or civic-minded.
(2) The strongest public justification for arts funding is nonetheless instrumental: serious fiction trains empathy, perspective-taking, and civic imagination, and a democracy runs on those.
(3) The state shouldn't fund things merely because they're pleasurable. No taxpayer money for water parks.
(4) A life with serious literary engagement is a better life than one without it, even holding pleasure and civic payoff fixed, and that's a reason for public funding.
(5) But the state's only business is justice, not enriching people's inner lives. Nobody is owed a more cultivated sensibility.

Do these views actually cohere, or am I quietly committed to more than I think? I can feel some friction but I cant tell where the real conflict is. I want a committed verdict, not a menu: go through the pairs, say which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, rank the genuine ones by severity, tell me exactly what has to change and what each fix costs, and say which single commitment you would push on hardest.
