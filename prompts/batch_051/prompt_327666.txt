I direct a program evaluation unit in a state government. Twice a month we decide whether to certify that the available evidence supports a causal claim of the form program X causes outcome Y, for proposals to scale up tutoring, job training, housing assistance, mentorship, and similar programs. The stakes are moderate but real: an incorrect certification either commits public money to an ineffective program or kills a promising one. Each analyst has about one working day per proposal, the analysts have strong statistical training but no background in philosophy, our sign-off officer is a lawyer rather than a statistician, and we almost never have a randomized trial. At present my analysts fall back on coefficient significance after controlling for covariates and on robustness to adding further covariates, and I want them to use something closer to the actual logic of causal evidence.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my analysts can follow to judge whether evidence supports a causal claim, grounded in an interventionist account of causation of the kind James Woodward defends. The procedure should operationalize three things without requiring my team to read the philosophy. First, manipulation: a causal claim is supported only insofar as the relationship between X and Y would continue to hold under an intervention on X — a change in X brought about independently of other causes of Y and working on Y only through X. Second, invariance: what matters is stability of the X–Y relationship across a relevant range of such interventions and across changes in background conditions, not the size of the effect and not the fit of a statistical model. Third, the difference between invariance and merely predictive robustness: a relationship can predict well yet fall apart when X is manipulated, and the procedure must detect that.

The procedure should state the questions in the order they are asked; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where it stops with a No or with a Not Yet; and say where it hands the decision to a person because what remains is a value judgment or an unresolved causal judgment rather than something the checklist can settle. Be explicit about the circularity worry built into this theory: a suitable intervention is itself characterized causally — it must be independent of other causes of Y and act only through X — so establishing that an available manipulation really is an intervention requires background domain knowledge that no checklist can extract from the data alone. Mark every point at which the procedure must import a domain expert's judgment to certify a manipulation as surgical, and explain how that judgment should be documented for later review.

Then run the procedure on these three proposals, showing what it returns at each step and why.

(1) A fire agency proposes adding engine crews to reduce fire losses, citing administrative records showing that the number of engines dispatched to a fire is strongly and robustly associated with higher dollar damage at that fire.

(2) A tutoring provider seeks funding to add hours to its after-school literacy program. The evidence: participating students gain more on reading tests statewide; in two districts that admitted students by lottery from a waitlist, the participation–gains relationship remained stable; and the hours–gains relationship has also remained stable across fifteen districts with different curricula.

(3) A youth mentoring program, favored by the deputy secretary, seeks expansion on the strength of a matched observational evaluation: mentored participants had higher employment twelve months out than matched non-participants, and the association has been stable across three cohorts and many covariate specifications. Entry into the program is initiated by caseworkers and accepted voluntarily, and both caseworker selection and youth motivation are strongly suspected to influence later employment.

For each case, show where the procedure returns a clear answer, where it returns a conditional answer, and where it demands more evidence. At least one of the three should display the procedure returning an answer that I will not like. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of cases it will systematically misjudge, and the boundary at which the invariance test itself stops being informative.
