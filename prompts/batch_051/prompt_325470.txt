I've been trying to build a small forecasting-and-disagreement protocol for a reading group, and it has pushed me into something I can't shake. The setup leans on something like Aumann's agreement theorem: if two perfectly rational people start from a common prior, update on their own private information, and then keep announcing their probabilities back and forth, they can't agree to disagree — any leftover disagreement would have to come from asymmetric information, not from rationally putting different weights on the same evidence. For the protocol, this is gorgeous. It gives a crisp story about why disagreement persists ("someone has evidence the other lacks, so we should trade forecasts until we converge") and a clean account of what a group's opinion even is. I find the argument almost like a proof that rational disagreement is just a transient information-processing bug more communication would fix.

But there's an intuition that refuses to go away. I know two people who look at the exact same public facts — same news, same economic data, same model code — and end up with genuinely different probabilities about whether a recession hits next year, and neither one is being sloppy or hiding a private signal. It feels like they start from different temperaments and hunches, and that their starting points are not just noise; they weigh the same information differently from the outset. If the common prior assumption is right, I have to call at least one of them irrational, or invent a hidden piece of evidence, and both options feel false. I've heard the standard deflections — maybe the disagreement is really over which model to use rather than the data, maybe priors are just a formal device with no psychological content, maybe my intuition is just romanticizing stubbornness. But I'm not sure any of those face the problem head-on. Walk me through which should yield here: should I debunk the intuition and accept that at least one of my friends is being irrational, should I amend or soften the common prior assumption (imprecise priors? ambiguous evidence? something less tidy), or is there a reading on which a common prior and persistent rational disagreement are compatible? And spell out what would actually justify each of those moves, not just which side you prefer.
