I've been kicking around a concept I call "workflow constitutionalism" and I want someone to check it before I embarrass myself in front of the whole eng org again. My claim is that any sufficiently entrenched software system is a constitution in miniature. A constitution doesn't usually tell people what to think or want; it sets up offices, procedures, deadlines, veto points, and standards of review, and it makes some actions easy, some hard, some visible, and some invisible. That's also what a mature codebase plus defaults, permissions, and schema does. For example, a content-moderation queue separates powers between an automated classifier, a human reviewer team, and an appeals process. An expense-approval workflow encodes a theory of who's accountable to whom before anybody writes an HR policy. I got onto this after reading Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" But I think people take the wrong lesson from Winner: they focus on the famous cases like Robert Moses' low bridges or the mechanical tomato harvester, which makes it sound like only some technologies are political. My view is that all robust software has something like a constitutional order, except nobody ratified it and the framers are just whoever touched the repo last. I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, so maybe this is just Winner with a metaphor bolted on. Tell me: is this actually different from what Winner argued? Where does the constitution analogy break? And what's the strongest case where treating software as a constitution would lead me to a dumb design decision?
