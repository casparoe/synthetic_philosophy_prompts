I’m in a philosophy of responsiblity class, and we’re reading Galen Strawson’s paper “The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility.” I want to test the Basic Argument against a case I don’t think he had in mind. The part I’m stuck on is the later step. My notes paraphrase it this way: you do what you do because of the way you are; to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you would need to be truly responsible for the way you are; to be responsible for the way you are, you would have to have chosen how you are, and to choose that you would already need to be some way, so the responsibility just runs back. That’s why he says you would need to be causa sui—the cause of yourself—and nobody can be that.

Now take someone who gets a neural implant that will change a personality trait—say, make them less impulsive or more empathetic. They knowingly choose the implant. On another read, the decision to get the implant was made by their old personality, so the regress just begins one step earlier and nothing changes. Which reading is more faithful to the argument? Is there a way to say the later action is not truly their responsibility without also saying the decision to get the implant was never really theirs either? I care less about whether we should blame them and more about whether the Basic Argument has the resources to draw a line between deliberately changed character and just ending up with a character.
