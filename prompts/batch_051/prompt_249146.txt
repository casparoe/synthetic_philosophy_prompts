I'm preparing a book club session on A Tale of Two Cities and want to use it to think through the main ways historians argue about the French Revolution. The novel clearly links the Terror to the cruelties of the old regime — oppression breeds revolution, and the revolution then turns indiscriminate. But historiography divides here: some treat the Revolution as the working out of class conflict; others insist revolutionary ideology had its own causal force, so that the Terror is not simply an inevitable class outcome; still others emphasize contingency, miscalculation, and the accidents of factional struggle. There is also the familiar idea that a revolution ends by devouring its own — Danton, Desmoulins, and other architects of the Terror consumed by the machine they built. Read the novel through this debate. Does Dickens’s account line up with any of these historiographical positions, or is it closer to a moralized, individualist story of vengeance and sacrifice? Which scenes carry the interpretation — the wine cask, the storming of the Bastille, the guillotine’s routine, Carton’s ending — and where does the novel simplify or resist the historical arguments? I’d like a reading that treats the book as a serious but partial interpretation of the Revolution rather than as an illustration of a theory.
