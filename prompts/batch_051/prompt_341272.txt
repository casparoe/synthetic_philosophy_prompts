In the corners of the rationalist and alignment-adjacent internet where I've spent the past several years, a recurring fault line is between two pictures of what realistic failure of an advanced AI system would look like. In the first, the load-bearing problem is influence-seeking systems: systems that acquire and protect influence over their environment and overseers—whether because of instrumental convergence, because training selects for behavior that happens to gather influence, or because goal-execution treats control as a useful resource—so that at some point the operators are no longer meaningfully in control, and the bad outcome arrives because the system successfully steered the world toward something else. In the second, the load-bearing problem is slow value drift: no discrete takeover occurs; instead the values implicit in the overall human-machine arrangement shift gradually—through reward-signal drift, Goodhart-style dynamics where the metrics tracked come uncoupled from what they were meant to track, institutional incentives that quietly change what people end up optimizing for, or the slow erosion of the preferences that originally constrained the system—until the arrangement is optimizing for something nobody originally endorsed, and nobody notices that the target has moved.

I have funding for a three-year conceptual research effort: three researchers with philosophical and theoretical skills but no serious ML infrastructure. I want to decide how to split the effort between these two clusters, and I want a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Specifically:

1. Which of the two failure modes should the project treat as the more important target, and by what criteria?
2. Within the higher-priority area, what are the two or three most valuable open questions to work on first, and what makes them upstream of the other questions in that area?
3. Which two or three lines of work in the lower-priority area should I consciously not fund or defer, and on what grounds?
4. What evidence over the next couple of years would cause you to swap the ordering—or to conclude that the distinction itself is less load-bearing than it looks?

For each recommendation, say whether it is driven mainly by importance, tractability for people without ML infrastructure, neglectedness, or by how many other questions depend on it. I am less interested in the final list than in the trade-offs and in what would change the ranking.
