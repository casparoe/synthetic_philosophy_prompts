I work for a state law revision commission. Every two years we get a batch of maybe forty statutes that are still on the books but basicaly never enforced — old Sunday trading bans, a loitering ordinance, a rule against putting handbills on cars that hasn't been used in decades. For each one we have to recommend to the legislature: repeal it, leave it as is, or keep it and actually start enforcing it. We're two lawyers and an intern, with about a day per statute, and our recommendation has to survive a public hearing.

From everything I've read, desuetude raises rule-of-law problems: a law nobody enforces stops guiding anyone, officials can reach for it whenever they want to pressure somebody, and the law as enforced drifts away from the law as announced. But I can't tell what that means for our list, because some of these feel harmless to leave on the books and others don't.

I don't want a summary of the desuetude literature. I want a decision procedure we could actually run on each statute: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a definite recommendation, and where it hands the question to elected officials because it's values rather than facts. As you build it, please keep three kinds of consideration separate: rule-of-law considerations (notice, consistency, selective-enforcement risk), moral considerations about whether the conduct should be regulated at all, and practical considerations about enforcement cost and capacity. Mark the points where the procedure will misfire or where judgment can't be eliminated.

Then run the procedure on three examples and show your work: a Sunday trading ban that's unenforced and basically unenforceable; a law against sleeping in public doorways that's mostly ignored but gets used by police now and then to move particular people along; and an adultery law that's never prosecuted but that lots of constituents want to keep as a statement about marriage. Include at least one case where the procedure gives us an answer we won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't do.
