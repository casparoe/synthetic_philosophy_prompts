At a reading group I sat in on, two members got into a long argument over a passage in Samuel Johnson's Preface to Shakespeare. Johnson there makes roughly this point: no spectator at a play is actually deceived into thinking the staged actions are real, and the pleasure tragic drama gives depends on our never losing the awareness that what we are watching is fiction rather than fact. He seems to treat this as no problem for the rationality of our responses, but as part of the explanation of them. The argument went through six distinctive moves; I've numbered them below. I honestly couldn't referee it, so I'd like you to adjudicate.

A1. One of them said Johnson effectively dissolves the paradox of fiction. If the only worry were that audiences hold false beliefs—say, that the actor is really being killed—then Johnson's observation removes the error: audiences know it is a play, so their responses cannot be criticized as belief-based mistakes. The emotions are directed at a represented situation, and they are answerable to whether the representation makes that emotion intelligible, not to whether the represented events exist. On this view, the paradox of fiction is not a real problem; it is just the ordinary fact that people can be moved by stories.

B1. The other replied that Johnson has disposed only of the easy part. The hard problem is not that audiences might be deceived; it is that they are moved despite not believing that anyone is suffering. Pity, fear, and the like ordinarily include something like a thought that their object exists and is threatened or harmed. If you strip that out, the emotion should disappear, but it doesn't. So Johnson has not solved the paradox; he has stated the exact fact that needs explaining.

A2. The first answered that B1 is assuming, without argument, that genuine emotions require existential beliefs. That is false. A parent who vividly imagines a child getting lost may feel real fear without believing the child is lost. A person may pity a merely hypothetical victim while fully knowing there is none. So there is no conceptual barrier to fiction-directed emotion. The rationality question is whether the emotion fits the represented content, not whether that content corresponds to the world.

B2. The second answered that the imagined-child case proves the opposite. The parent's fear is rational only as an alarm response to a scenario that might come about; it is calibrated to a real possibility. In fiction, by contrast, the audience knows there is no such possibility: Lear's daughters are not live threats to anyone. If imagining a scenario can set off pity and fear regardless of what we believe, then our emotions are systematically insensitive to our own take on reality. Calling that "rational" stretches the word beyond use.

A3. The first replied that this treats emotions as if they were beliefs and must track the truth. They are not. An emotion is rational in the relevant sense if it is a fitting response to its object as the subject represents it. The object of fiction-directed pity is the character as represented, not a concrete person. Johnson's point is precisely that tragedy can engage the passions so deeply because we know the object is not a live practical situation: we are released from the demand to intervene and can respond to the representation on its own terms.

B3. The second returned that "fitting to the representation" is not the same as "rational to feel." A hallucinated monster might make fear fitting relative to the hallucinated content, but fear in that condition is not rational. If the best we can say for fiction-directed emotion is that it fits represented content, we have replaced the question with one about internal coherence. The paradox of fiction, so the second argued, is whether being moved by known fictions is rational full stop—not whether the response fits the fiction.

Please structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

Summary (about 120 words): restate Johnson's point and the disagreement between the two members.

Move-by-move adjudication (about 500 words): rate each of the six moves (A1, B1, A2, B2, A3, B3) on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating. Do not merely say who wins; assess each move as an argument.

What each side is right about (about 180 words): identify the strongest compatible insight on each side, even if one side is overall weaker.

Overall verdict (about 150 words): say who is more right overall, and specify what—if anything—both parties are missing.
