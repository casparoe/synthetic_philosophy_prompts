One of my former students — call him Dan — runs the analytics shop at a state health department, and he called me last week with a version of a problem I've been teaching for years. His analysts sit on dozens of data feeds: ER chief complaints, pharmacy sales, school absenteeism, search terms, weather, even truck traffic counts around the regional food hub. Every Monday they triage candidate "leading indicators" for disease outbreaks, and the standing question is whether a pattern they found in last year's data will keep tracking the thing they care about next flu season. He wants something his team can actually use: a reusable procedure for deciding which candidate regularities are projectible and which are just gerrymandered after the fact. It has to be usable by people who never read philosophy, but it can't just be data-science folklore either.

The way I think about it is Goodman's grue. The familiar point is that every emerald observed so far is equally well explained by the hypothesis that all emeralds are green and by the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, where grue means green for anything first observed before some future date and blue for anything not. The two hypotheses fit the same data, so the problem isn't settled by more observation; it's about which predicates we're entitled to project into the future. That's the distinction I want captured, but translated into a checklist, not a seminar.

Write me a one-page procedure (a checklist or a flowchart in words) that a working analyst can run a proposed predictor through in, say, twenty minutes, to route it to yes, no, or bump to Dan. Ground it in the actual projectibility and grue discussion, but make each step operational: what do they ask, in what order, what exactly counts as passing, and what fails automatically. The procedure should catch the standard moves that create grues in the wild — predictors defined with time-indexed categories, thresholds chosen after seeing the data, variables that are really four unrelated things glued together with an or, categories tightened around one anomalous season, and the like. But be honest that every useful predictor is gerrymandered to some degree; the procedure has to separate the harmless kind from the kind that voids the inference, and it has to say where that line is and why its not just a fancier version of their existing gut calls.

After the procedure, give two or three worked examples using realistic signals: a moving average of ER visits for flu-like illness whose case definition changed mid-year, a spike on the first Friday after payday in some pharmacy category that looked predictive last year, and a genuinely seasonal signal that was first found through a messy fishing expedition but is probably fine. Make at least one of them a case where the procedure says to drop a signal the team already loves. Then tell me where judgment cannot be eliminated, where the procedure will likely misfire, and what Dan should tell his people to watch for.

Constraints: the analysts have no philosophical background, they see dozens of candidate signals each week, a false alarm means a costly field investigation, and a missed outbreak can get people killed, so whatever survives has to be defensible to the state epidemiologist and occasionally to a legislature that wants to know why their paying for astrology. I don't want a survey or a bibliography; I want the usable translation, with the hard parts marked as hard.
