I recently retired from a career in the physical sciences and have been spending my reading time on political philosophy and social choice. A line of argument I keep meeting, especially in discussions of futarchy, runs roughly as follows. The information relevant to choosing a policy is widely dispersed among many people, each of whom knows only a small and local part of it. Democratic deliberation is a weak way to aggregate that information: it rewards fluency and coalition-building rather than accuracy, and it gives participants reasons to spin or conceal what they know. A market, by contrast, lets anyone who believes the prevailing judgment is wrong translate that private belief into a wager, and prices move in response to the wagers of those with genuine information. Hence the proposal: let citizens vote on a goal or metric, and let prediction markets determine which policy is expected to best achieve it.

Now suppose a development that struck me as cutting against at least one of the argument's original premises. The traders in the relevant markets are no longer, as the argument pictured, a broad population of independent human actors with scattered private information. Instead, the marginal trader and much of the liquidity is supplied by a small number of automated forecasting systems. These systems are trained on largely overlapping public data, share similar architectures and inductive biases, and are often fine-tuned on each other's outputs. Meanwhile, the human trader whose edge was local or tacit knowledge is increasingly a price-taker or has stopped participating. One might then expect the systems' errors, especially about structurally novel events that fall outside the training distribution, to be correlated rather than independent.

I am trying to work out whether the classic argument survives this. Which premise is actually under pressure: the assumption that policy-relevant information is widely dispersed among many independent judges, or the assumption that markets aggregate information whenever there is money to be made, or something else? If the information-aggregation argument has to be repaired, what does its repaired form still license — for instance, only a weaker claim that a market of correlated but disagreeing forecasters beats deliberation on ordinary questions, and nothing stronger? And if prices can be systematically wrong in just the sort of tail event where policy stakes are highest, does that undermine futarchy's central epistemic claim, or does it merely impose a design constraint on how such markets would need to be subsidized, populated, or supplemented with non-market institutions?

I should add that I am not looking for a bibliography. I would, however, be especially grateful if the response separated what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, rather than presenting one side of the dispute as the consensus.
