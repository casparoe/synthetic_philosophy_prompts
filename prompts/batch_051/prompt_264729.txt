As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am working on a piece about how logic changed in the late nineteenth century, and I want to anchor it in a concrete example rather than broad claims about notation. The older logic often stated its criterion of deductive validity as a rule: "An argument is valid just in case it can be expressed as a categorical syllogism with exactly three terms, each term occurring in two propositions, and with the middle term—the term present in both premises but absent from the conclusion—distributed in at least one premise." A term is distributed in a proposition when the proposition says something about every member of the class the term designates.

Now take an argument that looks impossible to deny: "Every horse is an animal; therefore, every head of a horse is a head of an animal." Take any object that is the head of a horse: it is the head of some horse, and because that horse is an animal, it is the head of some animal. The inference appears to add nothing beyond logical apparatus. Yet the argument has one explicit premise, not two; and if one tries to fit it into the quoted rule, one seems to need four terms—horse, animal, head of a horse, and head of an animal—with no middle term to distribute. The rule appears to have nothing to say about it.

I want the rule treated honestly. Is it false, incomplete, or merely inapplicable to an argument that was never meant to be a syllogism? What exactly has to be added to it to license the inference from "Every horse is an animal" to "Every head of a horse is a head of an animal," and what does that addition reveal about what Frege changed when he replaced the subject–predicate analysis of a proposition with the analysis into function and argument, and introduced quantifiers that bind variables?
