I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about statutes of limitations—not the doctrine so much as the moral logic underneath: when does time actually matter, and when is the whole thing just a legal accident. My gut keeps going in different directions depending on the case, and I'd like to know whether there's one principle behind it, or whether some of my reactions have to go. A 19-year-old robs a convenience store with a fake gun; no one gets hurt. He gets away and then lives an ordinary, law-abiding life for forty years—works, raises kids, helps out in his community. At 59 a cold-case unit matches his old prints. I think they should leave him alone; whatever he deserved in 1985 isn't there to collect anymore. But a 94-year-old former concentration-camp guard is found, who at 22 helped herd prisoners into the gas chambers and then lied about it for seventy years. I don't care how quietly he's lived since: try him, convict him. Time changes nothing there. I know those two pull in opposite directions, but they both feel right.

An accountant steals $200,000 from a small family business over two years and then spends fifteen years forging records and lying so they won't find out. The clock where he lives runs from the theft, so he's clear; I think that's wrong—he shouldn't get credit for years he spent actively keeping the theft buried. Another bookkeeper pockets a similar one-time amount and makes no real effort to hide it afterward beyond the initial false entry; the family just doesn't audit for twenty years. I lean toward letting that one go, even though the act is as deliberate as the first. A woman is sexually assaulted at 20 and doesn't report then, out of fear and shame; at 45 she finally feels able to. The limit ran out years ago. I want to say the clock shouldn't start until reporting is a live option for her, but I can see that this practically abolishes limits exactly where evidence is oldest and memory is most disputed—I'm less sure then I'd like.

So is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? The candidate ideas I keep reaching for—people become different over time; some wrongs are too grave for time; the clock should run only once the wrongdoer has genuinely stopped and stepped into the open; the clock should start when the victim could realistically act—push against each other, especially between the camp guard and the convenience-store kid, and between the two embezzlers. What's the best statement of when time should matter, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what would it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: an 18-year-old who served as a lookout in an armed robbery where someone ended up killed, didn't know that would happen, and is identified 30 years later after an otherwise law-abiding life?
