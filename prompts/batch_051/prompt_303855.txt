I'm a retired physical oceanographer — spent thirty-five years on currents and moorings, not political philosophy — and I've only recently got into the charter cities and seasteading literature. I keep returning to one question: can a person genuinely opt out of a state, or are all these arrangements just the state renting out a little soverignty for as long as it suits it?

For a while now I've been using four tests for whether a scheme is a genuine opting-out, rather than a state-tolerated special regime:

1. The governance provider's authority stems from private agreement or property, not from a grant the parent state can revoke at will.
2. Residents finance it by genuinely optional payments: if you stop paying, you're excluded, but you're not fined, imprisoned, or made stateless.
3. The provider actually supplies the functions individuals cannot easily supply for themselves: security, adjudication of disputes, emergency response.
4. The parent state does not exercise, and has not credibly reserved the right to exercise, the ordinary incidents of sovereignty over the place — taxation, conscription, final criminal jurisdiction, customs.

Now consider a case that sits right on the line. Port Meridian is a charter city on the coast of a small country, created by a 99-year treaty between the host state and a private consortium. It has its own elected council, its own civil and commercial courts, most criminal jurisdiction, a small police force, and its own schools. Residents are admitted under a residency compact; they pay a flat annual service fee in place of taxes, and they're exempt from conscription. The host state's ordinary law is disapplied inside the city except for a short closed list of violent crimes — murder, kidnapping, hostage-taking — and a joint committee resolves disputes about that list. Residents accused of those crimes are arrested by city officers and handed over to the host state. The only road in runs through a host-state customs post. The treaty allows termination for "material breach," with arbitration, but the city now accounts for a sixth of the country's economy, so no government would plausibly end it. Last year the country's supreme court held that the national constitution applies throughout the national territory, including the city, but explicitly declined to say what that means for the treaty.

I'd like you to go through my four tests one by one and say whether Port Meridian passes, fails, or leaves the test underdetermined. Then give an overall verdict: does it amount to opting out of the state in any sense that matters? And tell me whether the case shows any of my four tests needs changing — my own hunch is that the fourth test demands more than even a fully recognised independent state can deliver about its own future, but I can't tell whether that's a problem with the test or with the whole idea.

One epistemic thing: please keep what's genuinely settled about all this separate from what's actually contested among people who work on political and legal philosophy, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. And please write the answer in British English.
