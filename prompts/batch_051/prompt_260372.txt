I classify a value as intrinsic for a group, rather than merely instrumental, only when four conditions are met: members invoke it as a final reason rather than pointing to something further; members accept real costs to honor it even when the usual downstream payoffs are absent; members treat violations as defects in themselves rather than as strategic mistakes; and members refuse to trade the value off against the very goods it is said to serve. Now consider a research institute whose founding charter commits it to report its findings without regard to consequence. Members treat the norm as sacrosanct and stay even when better-funded rivals recruit them; when a colleague buries a finding because publishing it might cause public panic, colleagues describe this as a corruption of the institute, not a miscalculation. Yet in private, senior members defend the norm almost entirely by arguing that only unsparing honesty preserves the institute's long-run funding, reputation, and ability to recruit — and they admit each of these could be advanced in the short run by selective dishonesty. Under my four conditions, is the institute's commitment to truth-seeking intrinsic? Or does the seniors' defense show it is instrumental over a long time horizon and merely feels intrinsic? Does the case show that one of the four conditions is doing too much or too little work in the intrinsic/instrumental distinction?
