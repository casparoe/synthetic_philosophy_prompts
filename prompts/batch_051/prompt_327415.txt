I keep coming back to the sunk cost debate because it feels like everyone involved should be able to settle it, and yet they keep talking past each other. On one side you have the economists' line: the amount you've already paid or invested is gone, so the only thing that matters for a decision now is future costs and benefits; treating the past investment as a reason to continue is an error. On the other side you have a grab-bag of people defending the practice—behavioral scientists who just point out that humans do this constantly (escalation of commitment and all that), philosophers and some economists who say there are respectable reasons to honor sunk costs: keeping a story about yourself as someone who finishes what they start, avoiding the regret of having wasted past effort, signaling to other people that you're committed, and game-theoretic cases where sunk investments are rational because they change what others expect you to do later. The thing is, neither side seems to deny the other's examples. The economists dont deny people do it, and the defenders mostly admit that in a clean one-shot case the money is just gone. So why has the argument not moved in decades?

My working theory is that the disagreement is mostly about what the word 'rational' is supposed to be doing: one side means something like consistency with forward-looking expected utility, the other means something like good practical sense for a temporally extended person with a self-image, social ties, and emotions. But I could also see it being a dispute about which cases are the real ones, or about whether the descriptive fact that we do this is even relevant to whether we should. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on whether sunk costs ever count. And then: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually count as progress, or is this one of those debates where the only honest answer is that people are asking two different questions?
