I'm in a philosophy of moral disagreement class, and we've been doing moral trade for the last couple weeks — basically when two people with different moral views can still make a deal where each gives the other something the other values, without either person changing their mind. My prof has been going through cases, and I've been writing down her verdicts cuz I want to predict how she'll rule on a new case she hinted at for next week.

Her verdicts so far:

- A farmed-animal activist and a malaria-charity volunteer swap two hours of phone-banking for each other's cause. Each thinks thier own cause is way more pressing but the other's cause is decent, so each thinks the hours they're gaining are worth a lot more than the hours they're giving up. She called it basically a perfect moral trade.

- A climate activist and a pro-nuclear blogger each agree to hand out flyers for the other's campus event. Each thinks the other's message is a little off, but neither thinks handing out the flyers is wrong, and each would rather have more people at their own event. She said fine.

- A vegan agrees to buy a leather jacket as a birthday gift for a friend in exchange for the friend donating $25 to an animal shelter. On the vegan's own view, the jacket causes way more harm than the $25 prevents. Prof said no; she kept pointing out that the math didn't work out on the vegan's own view. Someone then asked if it would still be a no if the donation were like $2,000, and she said that kind of case was coming next week.

The new case is this: Maya is a vegan who thinks buying meat is wrong. Her neighbor says he'll donate $400 to the farmed-animal charity Maya considers most effective if she buys him a $15 steak on her next grocery run, and he's reliable. By Maya's own values, the $400 prevents way more suffering than the one steak causes — it's clearly net positive by her own math.

I can think of three rules she might be running: (a) never do something you yourself think is wrong, even for a good trade; (b) it's ok to do something you think is wrong when your own values say the whole trade nets out positive; (c) you can trade away an act whose wrongness comes from its consequences when the trade nets out positive, but not an act that's wrong independently of its consequences, like lying or breaking a promise.

What's your prediction for how she rules on Maya's steak case? Which of those rules (or something else) best fits her earlier verdicts, and what further case would be the cleanest way to tell the remaining rules apart?
