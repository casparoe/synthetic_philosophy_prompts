In many common-law jurisdictions a person can be convicted of murder for a death that was not their aim. Juries are directed that they may find the required intention — an aim to kill or to cause serious harm — only if the death was a virtually certain consequence of the defendant’s voluntary act and the defendant appreciated that it was; foresight of virtual certainty is treated as evidence from which intention may be inferred, not as equivalent to intention. The law therefore keeps the claim that the defendant foresaw death as virtually certain and the claim that the defendant intended death apart, joined only by a permissive inference.

Experimental philosophy’s Knobe effect is often taken to put pressure on that separation in ordinary thought. In the standard pair of cases, a corporate chairman approves a profit-making initiative while saying he cares only about profits and not at all about the environment. When the initiative harms the environment as a side effect, most subjects judge that he brought about the harm intentionally; when the same structure makes the side effect helpful, most subjects judge that he did not intentionally bring about the help — the only changed feature is the moral valence of the side effect.

What theory of intentional action does the criminal law’s treatment of foresight as evidence of intent presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view of what it is to act intentionally that would make the foresight–intention distinction stable and morally neutral, and identify which parts of the practice strictly presuppose that view and which merely fit well with it. Then say whether the practice is coherent if the Knobe effect is taken at face value as revealing the ordinary concept of intentional action rather than as a performance error or a question-wording artifact. If the ordinary concept really does constitutively involve moral evaluation, what would have to change in the virtual-certainty rule — in how juries are instructed, in what the prosecution must prove, or in the law’s distinction between intention and recklessness — and what would it cost to change it? Finally, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, say which rival theories of intentional action would license it and what further commitment would be needed to choose among them.
