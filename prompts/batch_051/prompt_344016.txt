i've been using a pet theory at work and want someone to tell me if it's a real idea or just me projecting software onto thinking. i call it the call-stack model of deliberation, or sometimes thought factoring. the claim is that real deliberation — an expert debugging a horrible production incident, or deciding between two architectures — isn't one continuous act of a unified mind. it's a tree of tiny self-contained questions. each node is small enough that a person could answer it in under a minute with no idea what the overall problem is, so long as the parent node handed down the right context. the intelligence isn't in the answering, it's in the splitting: the person who keeps decomposing "what's wrong with this system" into "check the load balancer logs," "check the db connection pool," "see whether the 3pm deploy touched config." the stronger claim i want checked is that any deliberation can be factored this way without loss, and that an expert's judgment could be compiled into a runbook-tree so that a bunch of much less skilled people, working in parallel, reproduce the expert's conclusion just by executing the tree. the under-a-minute cap is the whole trick for me — it forces each step to be mechanical enough to check and makes the tree parallelizable.

i've used a version of this to onboard juniors: instead of teaching them the whole codebase, i teach them a debuging tree, and it works right up until it doesn't. so — is this an actual theory of what deliberating is, or just a metaphor? i'd bet someone has worked it out already under a name i'd recognize if i saw it; what's the nearest existing idea? and where does it actually break — which kinds of thinking can't survive being chopped into one-minute nodes, and is that a fatal flaw or something you could patch?
