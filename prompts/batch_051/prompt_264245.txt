I am a retired natural scientist who has recently taken up early modern philosophy seriously, and I keep hitting a problem in Descartes’s treatment of the cogito. In the Second Meditation, after running through the doubts about body, sense, and a deceiving God, he reaches the point where he says that “this proposition (pronunciatum) I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind.” That sentence sounds like a proposition whose necessity is established by the meditation: it is put forward as a result that can be stated and defended. But in the Replies to the Second Set of Objections, commenting on the sentence “I am thinking, therefore I am, or I exist,” Descartes says that the person who says this does not deduce existence from thought by means of a syllogism; rather, he recognizes it as something self-evident by a simple intuition of the mind. Those two statements seem to pull in different directions: on one side a proposition that is necessarily true when expressed or conceived, on the other an intuition that is not derived by any syllogism. Since I was trained as a scientist, I am used to distinguishing between a formally proven theorem, an experimentally observed fact, and a precondition for inquiry, and I would like to understand which of these, if any, the cogito is supposed to be.

Then evaluate each candidate. Which reading honestly fits both texts without special pleading?
