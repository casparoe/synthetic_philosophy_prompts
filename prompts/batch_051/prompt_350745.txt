A friend and I recently argued far into the evening about whether the interventionist account of causation associated with James Woodward is objectionably circular. Neither of us moved much, but I came away convinced that the strongest form of each side was considerably better than what either of us had been defending at the start.

The interventionist view, stated loosely, is that a relationship between X and Y is causal just in case it is invariant under some range of interventions on X, where an intervention is an ideal experimental manipulation of X that is independent of other causes of Y and that affects Y, if at all, only through X. The circularity worry is that the account explains causation through a notion of intervention that is itself saturated with causal content: to say that a manipulation is independent of other causes of Y, or that it affects Y only through X, is already to employ the very causal facts the account was meant to analyze.

Write a short dialogue between two proponents of the opposing views. The first should press the circularity objection, arguing that the interventionist account either fails as a genuine analysis or becomes non-circular only at the cost of explaining nothing. The second should defend the interventionist account, perhaps by arguing that the circularity is harmless because a reductive definition was never the goal, or by treating the formal intervention operator as an undefined primitive, or by some other route that engages the objection directly. The two should respond to each other's actual moves rather than talk past each other, and neither should be a strawman. They need not converge, but each should be forced to refine or qualify their position at least once.
