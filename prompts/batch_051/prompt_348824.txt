I started a new job a few months ago helping build an agent-based simulation of crowd flow for large events, and my lead—who is a serious Wolfram-head—said something during a planning meeting that's been stuck in my head since. He claimed our sim is "computationally irreducible," meaning the only way to find out where a dangerous bottleneck forms at minute 90 is to just run the whole thing; no shortcut, no closed-form, no coarse-graining, no learned emulator will ever reliably beat it. I'd read bits of A New Kind of Science years ago and it always struck me as more of a worldview than a claim you could nail down, so I pushed back. I said that's just a guess about our current ignorance—we havent exactly looked hard for shortcuts. But then I realized I'm not sure what would settle the argument even in principle, and neither is he.

Here's the claim as I understand it: for many systems (Rule 30 gets brought up constantly), predicting the state at step n requires on the order of n steps of computation; you cannot jump ahead, and the only way to know the future is to live through it step by step. I don't want the hopeless version of the question (prove from first principles that no algorithm exists), and I don't want a bibliography. I want the best test a working scientist with real computational resources could actually run today. What would the test look like? What result would count for the irreducibility claim, and what would count against it? Then be really honest with me: suppose the test came out "for" irreducibility—what exactly would it have shown, and what would it leave untouched? Doesn't a failed search for a shortcut only ever license "we didn't find one," which is a different proposition from "there isn't one"? At what point does this stop being an empirical hypothesis and become a definition of what we mean by "no shortcut," or just a statement about our tools?

Act as a philosopher of science who also has a decent working knowledge of computational complexity theory. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate tests and their weaknesses before you commit to the strongest one. I'd rather get one well-defined test with a clear failure mode than a list of vague suggestions.
