I'm a retired physical oceanographer, and a few years ago I fell into philosophy through a reading group. I've been reading Nozick, and the Wilt Chamberlain argument keeps coming up in a renewable-energy co-op I help run, so I want to turn the entitlement theory into something our board can actually use.

We're a ninety-household solar co-op in the upper Midwest. Members own shares, but the shares aren't all alike: some people put in more upfront capital, some did installation labor, and some buy and sell small amounts of their generation credits. Most of the board aren't philosophers, and they wont sit still for a long theoretical preamble. Every quarter or so someone proposes to redistribute the credits toward lower-income households, or cap what a high-earning member can receive, or give a bigger share to someone who contributed more labor. The board splits into two camps every time: one says a voluntary transfer is a just transfer, full stop, and the other says the resulting pattern matters and the Wilt Chamberlain story is just a libertarian talking point.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for evaluating any such proposal, grounded in Nozick's entitlement theory as you read it. Commit to a specific reading of Nozick — don't lay out three interpretations and leave us to pick — and tell me where the procedure's verdicts follow from that reading and where they come from your own values instead. I want ordered questions we can ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to the board because the question is one of values rather than theory. Keep it runnable by non-philosophers in a two-hour meeting, with small-dollar decisions that set precedent.

Then run it on these three cases: (1) a member who did extra installation labor wants an outsized share of credits for five years; (2) a proposal to skim credits from members who voluntarily sold theirs at a high price, to fund lower-income members' dues; and (3) a proposal to award extra credits to a member whose great-grandparents were dispossessed of the land under our array in a 1930s tax sale, as a kind of rectification. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us something we'd rather not hear, and say what the procedure cannot settle, where the board will have to make a political decision.

At the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of the procedure's core rule, as the last line, in the form \answer{one-sentence summary}.
