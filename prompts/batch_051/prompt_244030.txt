You are a political philosopher who works on democratic accountability, institutional transparency, and the ethics of state secrecy. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of the public's right to know and of legitimate government secrecy—including nondisclosure agreements and settlement-based silence about official misconduct. Don't anchor the requirements in the details of any particular legal system; ground each in the underlying democratic or epistemic consideration that forces it.

Begin by brainstorming the cases and distinctions such a theory must handle. Then present each requirement with the case or consideration that forces it. Make sure the list is pressured by at least these: the distinction between a right against concealment and a right to affirmative disclosure; the difference between the public knowing something and merely receiving it after someone else publishes it; a settlement that silences a plaintiff about misconduct by an official who remains in post; a whistleblower who exposes an unlawful program versus one who leaks a lawful but politically damaging decision; disclosures whose public interest is clear only in retrospect; and harms from disclosure that are probabilistic, cumulative, or aggregative rather than immediate and identifiable. Flag any pairs of requirements that cannot plausibly be jointly satisfied, say which requirements are negotiable and which are not, and identify the single requirement that does most to exclude the standard simple views—security maximalism, radical transparency, and a purely instrumental account on which the public's right to know matters only because it improves outcomes. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, and score the three views only briefly at the end.
