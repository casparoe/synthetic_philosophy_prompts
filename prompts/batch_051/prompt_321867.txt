Hey—I'm in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility right now, and after reading Strawson on the reactive attitudes my freind and I got into an arguement that seems to prove way too much. I need help seeing which step actually breaks. Can you think it through with me?

(1) On Strawson's view, whether someone is morally responsible is a matter of whether our reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, hurt feelings—are appropriate toward them, and what makes them appropriate gets settled by standards internal to the practice: we drop resentment when the injury turns out to be an accident, we shift into the objective attitude toward young children or the severely mentally ill, and so on.

(2) These standards are not adopted on the basis of a theory. They're just what the participant attitude, the normal human way of being with other people, consists in.

(3) Now imagine a community—call it Vindexia—whose participant attitude includes things we find clearly mistaken: they resent someone for being left-handed, and they hold a dead ancestor responsible for a crop failure in much the way we'd resent a living person. The people there are as naturally inside their practice as we are inside ours, and by their own standards these reactions are appropriate.

(4) If (1) and (2) are right, then there is no stance-independent fact about who deserves blame that our practice latches onto better than theirs does. "Appropriate" just means appropriate-by-the-standards-of-some-practice.

(5) So Strawson's view makes responsibility practice-relative: if a community can stably hold its reactive attitudes without collapsing into the objective attitude, those attitudes are as correct as ours.

(6) But that's unacceptable—a whole practice of blaming can be wrong, and we want a theory that lets us say it's wrong, not just wrong relative to our practice.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2), because I love the anti-metaphysical move that physics doesn't settle blame. And I'm happy to reject (4) or whatever bridge lets (5) follow. I keep feeling like "appropriate" is sliding between two senses, but maybe I'm the one equivocating. Act as a professional philosopher working on moral responsibility and walk me through it step by step: which premise is least costly to give up, why it looked fine, and what Strawsonians have to pay to keep the view from collapsing into relativism. Start by brainstorming the different things "appropriate" could mean here, and tell me exactly where the argument goes wrong before giving your final diagnosis.
