I've got a policy I use whenever someone brings up the pessimistic meta-induction. When they point to phlogiston, caloric, the aether, and the other dead posits as evidence that our current best theories will probably die too, I refuse to update unless they can give me a principled reason why today's theories are relevantly different from the dead ones — not just that we're smarter now or that our instruments are better. This came up again last week when a friend sent me another article claiming the history of science refutes realism, and I found myself giving the same reply without actually reading it. I know there are stronger versions of the argument than the one I keep answering, and I worry the rule is partly a shield that let's me keep my scientific realism comfortable without engaging the history. Is it defensible as a policy rather than a bias dressed up as one? Under what conditions would the historical record actually force someone like me to lower their confidence in current theoretical posits, and is there a version of the rule that keeps the good part, refusing to be stampeded by a lazy induction, without the bad part, dismissing real evidence? When you answer, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. No headings, bullet points, bold, or tables either — just continuous prose like an email.
