A colleague of mine in the philosophy department — she teaches Hellenistic ethics and runs a well-attended evening course on Stoic practice — has a crisp way of sorting cases with the Stoic dichotomy of control, and I have been keeping notes on her verdicts. As best I can reconstruct them, she would judge as follows. Whether her article is accepted by a journal: not up to her. Whether she casts a vote in a departmental search: up to her. Her startle response when a fire alarm sounds in the middle of a lecture: not up to her. The decision to set aside her first impression and reread a disputed student exam: up to her. A flare-up of tendinitis in her wrist: not up to her. The judgment she forms about an argument's validity after working through it step by step: up to her. Her department's refusal to grant her a course release: not up to her. Whether she reads a colleague's insulting email a second time or deletes it at once: up to her.

The case I want a prediction about is this. Last week she received a hostile referee's report on a manuscript, and by her own account she spent the rest of the afternoon angry — not acting on the anger, just feeling it, going over the unfairness, and afterwards insisting that the anger came without any deliberation and that she could not simply have decided at noon not to be angry. I genuinely do not know whether she will call that anger "up to her" or "not up to her," and the question is what she must say if her earlier verdicts are to remain consistent.

Your task is to work out the rule she is implicitly running, not merely the word she would use. It seems to me her earlier verdicts fit at least two quite different rules, and I would like them distinguished before the prediction is made. The first is a classical rule on which what is up to us is the activity of judgment, assent, impulse, and choice — and the actions that flow from them — while externals and reflex-like first movements are not. The second is a more modern rule on which what is up to us is only what can be altered by an immediate act of will, so that unchosen emotions and automatic states fall outside even when they occur "in our heads." Predict her verdict on the afternoon of anger and give your best guess, with reasons; say which rule that verdict commits her to; state why the earlier cases do not by themselves settle between the two; and give the further case that would cleanly separate them if the anger case does not.
