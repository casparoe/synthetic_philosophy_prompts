I'm a novelist. Last month, at a writers' colony, I became the unwilling referee in an argument between two residents that I still cannot settle. A third resident had shared a novel draft in which a character, as an act of political protest, desecrates a consecrated object belonging to a living religious tradition. The argument went like this.

(1) One writer, an essayist on religious ethics, said the scene is wrong in a way that mere offence is not. Insults wound people's feelings; desecration is a wrong against the object itself, or at least against the community in its identity as a community of reverence, not against individuals' sensibilities as such. It is closer to desecrating a grave than to speaking rudely, and the author has a duty to refrain that cannot be redescribed as merely avoiding hurt.

(2) The other, who works in political nonfiction, replied that this is the very move liberal public culture must refuse. No one is obliged to treat as inviolable what only one tradition holds sacred. The idea that a symbol or object can be wronged independently of harm to persons smuggles a religious premise into common morality. What can reasonably be asked is that we avoid harming people or singling them out for abuse; a novel read by choice does neither.

(3) The first answered that this misstates the demand. The faithful are not asking the author to revere the object, only not to degrade it. An atheist tourist who carves his initials into a grave marker in the cemetery of a faith he does not share still wrongs the mourners, even though he shares none of their beliefs about what the marker means. The wrong is relational, owed to the community, and requires restraint rather than belief.

(4) The second replied that the grave analogy carries the argument but proves too little. Human remains and private burial grounds have secular justifications that a generic category of the sacred lacks. Once the object's claim is detached from personhood and property, the only available criterion for what counts is one community's say-so, which is just what a pluralistic public order cannot arbitrate. If desecrating this object wrongs its community, then every satire of a cherished value wrongs someone, and an appeal for restraint collapses into a demand for a veto.

(5) The first said the demand for a neutral criterion proves too much, since every accepted limit on expression — harassment, defamation, incitement — rests on lines that are contested and administered politically. The second writer himself treats certain secular memorials as beyond degrading treatment, not because he shares the victims' beliefs but because some things have acquired a status that makes their desecration wrong. The real question, she said, is whether religious communities may claim the same protection, not whether the category of desecration exists.

The two then asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate the argument move by move. Give exactly five numbered points, one for each move above, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; use the fifth point to state the comparative verdict and anything both parties are missing.
