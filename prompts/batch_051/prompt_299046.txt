I work for a small ceasefire monitoring body. We get daily incident reports from field observers in a couple frozen conflicts, and every few days we have to decide whether to recommend that a party has engaged in an act that makes future mutual confidence impossible—our board uses that recommendation to decide whether to suspend support for a peace process. The board isn't philosophically trained, and our field staff aren't either. I was reading Kant and got stuck on this line from the sixth preliminary article of Perpetual Peace: "No state at war with another shall permit such acts of hostility as would make mutual confidence impossible in a future peace." He then names assassination, poison, breach of surrender, and inciting treason in the enemy state as the core examples.

I'd like you to turn that princple into a decision proceedure we can actually run on incident reports: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the process stops with a no, and where we should hand it to a values-led committee because the principle genuinely runs out and judgement can't be eliminated. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a unit fakes a white-flag surrender to lure an enemy patrol out and then shoots them; (2) a government quietly offers an enemy general amnesty and safe passage if he defects, using family members as intermediaries, with no public acknowledgement. I expect the proceedure to catch the first and probably clear the second, but if the principle forces a different answer on either, say so. Keep it under 800 words, and write it so someone who has never read Kant can use it.
