I'm in a reading group that has spent the last semester tearing itself apart over the question of whether we should want to be moral saints. Two members have to be physically seperated at this point. One of them — the Perfectionist — holds that the more morally good a life is, the better it is, full stop, and any limit on moral striving is just a concession to human weakness. The other — the Critic — holds that a maximally moral life would be a bad human life: flat, humorless, without art or deep partial attachments, because non-moral values are not mere extras but part of what makes a life worth living. We've convinced them to write a joint document instead of arguing past each other for another nine weeks.

Please draft that adversarial-collaboration statement. Open with a short statement of each position, worded so its own holder would accept it. Then give a numbered list of claims both can sign; these shouldn't be platitudes, they should do real work — covering whatever they agree on about how moral and non-moral reasons relate, whether the disagreement is about duty or about an ideal, and what would not settle the debate. I suspect one crux is whether love for particular people is itself a moral phenomenon or a separate source of value, and another is whether a life's being good and a person's being morally good share a single scale of better and worse. After the agreed claims, produce a disagreements table: for each remaining disagreement, state it neutrally, give the version each side would accept, and identify the crux that would actually move the debate. For each crux, propose a concrete case or test both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. If any part of the dispute looks merely verbal to you, say which and why. No bibliography or citations — I want the ideas, not the references.
