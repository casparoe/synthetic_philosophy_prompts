A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether the gaps in the historical record are neutral absences or something more active. His view was that a missing document is simply a missing document: the archive can tell you only what survives, and any story about why something does not survive is speculation dressed up as history. He granted that records are sometimes destroyed, but he insisted that destruction is an event we can document, while the resulting absence is not itself a thing we can study. I resisted that, but I found I could not say exactly what turns a bare absence into a silence worth theorizing. We eventually agreed that the entire dispute turned on what one means by an archival silence.

Could you give me a working definition of an archival silence — a meaningful absence in the historical record — that lets us mark the difference between a mere gap and something done by, or to, the archive? Please test the definition against these cases:

1. a nineteenth-century census that records enslaved people by age and sex but not by name;
2. a married woman who appears in land records only as "the wife of" her husband;
3. an underground political party that kept no written minutes for fear of police seizure;
4. a colonial administration that burned its files in the weeks before independence;
5. a community whose histories were preserved orally and never written down;
6. a private diary that its author destroyed before death;
7. a person whose letters survive only because an heir published a heavily edited selection.

I suspect the concept does two jobs at once: it describes a state of the surviving sources and makes a claim about how that state came about, and those jobs can come apart. Give me the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it yields the wrong answer or no answer, revising it if necessary. I am less interested in a definitive classification of each case than in a definition that does not reduce every gap to either pure chance or deliberate conspiracy.
