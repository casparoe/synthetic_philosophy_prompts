I work as a software engineer, with no formal background in economics or political philosophy, and I've been pulled into a running argument with a friend in local government about a universal-basic-income mechanism for a mid-sized city that has a large commuter workforce and heavy tourism. The design is this: the city eliminates its existing cash income-support programs and sets its sales tax rate so that the expected monthly revenue from that tax roughly equals the cost of the programs it replaced. Every month, the entire sales tax revenue is paid out the following month, in equal shares, to every adult who has been continuously registered as a city resident for at least twelve months. There is no work requirement, no asset test, and no clawback as earnings rise; in any given month everyone gets the same amount, and the amount rises and falls with the city's total sales tax collections. Commuters and tourists pay the tax whenever they buy inside the city, but they receive no payment unless they are qualified residents.

I'm not asking whether UBI is a good idea in general. I want a concrete account of how this particular mechanism would actually behave: what it does to residents' spending and work decisions, how the residency rule gets gamed and by whom, what businesses do with pricing, location, or payment collection, how the monthly feedback between tax collections and payouts affects the city's budget and local prices, and where the whole thing breaks first. Then tell me whether the most serious failure can be fixed without reintroducing means-testing, income thresholds, or work conditions, and what the fixed version would do differently.

Please commit: take a definite position on the dominant failure mode and on whether the mechanism is salvageable, and argue for that position rather than presenting a balanced list of possibilities.
