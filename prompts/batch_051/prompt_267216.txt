Two people in my reading group got stuck on Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy, Book V, the bit just after Philosophy defines eternity as holding all of your life at once, not spread out along a timeline, and then says God's knowledge is not really foreknowledge because all times are present to Him from a kind of high vantage point. Chris said this solves the problem: if God sees my future action as already present to His gaze, it no more forces my choice than my own seeing what I'm doing right now forces my hand. Dana said that's just relabeling. She said from where we sit, God's cognition is still there before the action, and if it's infallible then the action is fixed before I get to it; moving the knowing outside of time doesn't touch the fact that at any earlier time I couldn't do other than God atemporally knows I do. Chris came back that this objection assumes God is believing in time, but Boethius denies that: there is no "at any earlier time" from God's point of view, so the phrase "before the action" has no grip on His knowledge. Dana replied: but it has a grip on me, since I deliberate in time; and if His atemporal knowledge includes the truth of my tomorrow's choice, then at any earlier point in my calendar the world already contains an infallible state that entails my choice. Chris said she's smuggling in temporal language and then complaining; the whole point of Boethius's two-necessities distinction is that inferences like "if God knows it, then it will happen" impose only conditional necessity, not simple necessity, so the action can still be free. Dana said conditional necessity with an eternal and immutable antecedent is just as bad. I honestly couldn't sort it out. Please adjudicate: who's right about which specific moves, and does Boethius's solution survive Dana's charge that it relocates rather than removes the problem?
