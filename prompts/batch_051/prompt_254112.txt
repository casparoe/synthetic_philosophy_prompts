Twice in the 2010s, a majority of the United States Senate used the so-called nuclear option to change the effective threshold for ending debate on presidential nominations without amending the Senate's written rules. In November 2013, the Democratic majority did this for most executive and lower-court nominations; in April 2017, the Republican majority extended the same approach to Supreme Court nominations. The mechanism in each case was this: a senator raised a point of order asserting that cloture on those nominations required only a simple majority; the chair ruled the point out of order; the majority appealed and overturned the chair's ruling by simple-majority vote; and the chair then announced a new precedent that would govern thereafter. The written cloture rule was left in place, and the Senate never went through the ordinary process for amending the Standing Rules, a process that itself requires a two-thirds vote to end debate.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the theory of self-amending rule systems: the study of systems, such as the game Nomic and the puzzles of legal self-reference, in which the rules determine how the rules themselves may be changed. Work out the formal anatomy of what happened by distinguishing the first-order procedural rule (the sixty-vote threshold), the higher-order rule of change (the amendment procedure, protected by its own supermajority requirement), and the move the majority actually made (obtaining a ruling that effectively changes what the first-order rule is taken to mean). In the theory's terms, was the nuclear option an authorized change, an unauthorized change that nonetheless became binding, a retroactive reinterpretation of the old rule, or a move that exposes an ambiguity in the line between applying a rule and changing it? Then identify what this framework explains about the episode and what it misdescribes or leaves out: for example, the role of precedent as a parallel system of rules, the fact that the maneuver became available to either party once it existed, the persistence of the filibuster for legislation, the partisan and repeated-game incentives at work, and the dependence of a rule's force on enforcement and acceptance. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction. Does the episode show that the puzzles of self-amendment are genuine features of real rule-governed institutions, or does it suggest they are artifacts of treating rules as a formal system detached from the practice of interpreting and applying them? Where the theory is silent about a feature of the case, say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak.
