Lately I've been trying to get straight how languages live with homonymy. You have forms like pitch that can mean the black stuff, a throw, a musical property, or a sales talk, and somehow nobody's head explodes. The story I keep hearing from linguists is that context does the disambiguating: syntactic frame, topic, register, collocates, and in tone languages the lexical tone, in others pitch accent or stress. I want to see that story translated into information-theoretic vocabulary, piece by piece. Homonyms as a code that maps one signal onto several messages; context as something like side information or channel state; frequency asymmetries as something like a non-uniform source distribution; the different disambiguating cues as different correlated channels or extra dimensions of the signal. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the linguists' story translate cleanly, which only fit if you quietly stretch the math, and which have no expression in information theory at all. And be honest about what the residue means — is it a defect of the information-theoretic lense, or is its a sign that the linguists' story was doing some hand-waving I shouldn't have trusted? I'm not looking for an analogy; I want a translation that keeps the math honest and treats what doesn't survive as the interesting part.
