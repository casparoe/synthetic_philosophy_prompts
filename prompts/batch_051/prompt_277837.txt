Immanuel Kant's Toward Perpetual Peace (1795) rests on a small set of commitments. Among them: durable peace requires every state to have a republican constitution; the international order should be a voluntary federation of free states rather than a single coercive world state; foreigners have a cosmopolitan right of hospitality, though not a right of settlement; standing armies should in time be abolished; and no state may forcibly interfere in another state's constitution or government.

Now consider three developments that postdate him. First, the United Nations system: a standing international body whose Security Council can authorize military force, but whose five permanent members hold a veto. Second, the European Union: a voluntary association of constitutional democracies in which supranational law overrides national law in some domains, with its own court and parliament. Third, armed humanitarian intervention in the style of the Responsibility to Protect: outside states using force inside another state, without its consent, to stop internal atrocities.

For each development, reconstruct the verdict that follows most faithfully from Kant's stated principles: which would he have welcomed as an imperfect realization of his project, which would he have judged a direct violation, and which would have forced him to revisit his own distinctions — for example between a voluntary league and an embryonic world state, or between non-interference and the demands of hospitality? Be explicit about which of Kant's commitments drives each verdict, and mark the places where the principles underdetermine the answer and you must reason beyond them.
