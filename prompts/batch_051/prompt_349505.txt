I'm giving a talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on long-run environmental limits, and what I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the talk. The thesis I'm defending is a deliberately qualified Malthusianism. My claim is that the core of Malthus's position is a structural, horizon-free statement — any finite resource base subjected to sustained exponential growth in demand will eventually bind — and that the standard dismissal, "Malthus has been wrong for two hundred years, so he's refuted," mistakes a failed short-run forecast for a refutation of the long-run arithmetic of limits. I am not defending the specific Malthusian apparatus most people think of: the arithmetic-versus-geometric ratio of food and population, the subsistence-wage mechanism, or the claim that population must crash back to subsistence. I'll concede up front that those were overtaken by the demographic transition, the Haber-Bosch process, and the Green Revolution. The actual contribution of the talk is a criterion for when "right but early" is respectable and when it is merely unfalsifiable hedging: a long-run limit theory may postpone its deadline only by naming delay mechanisms that are themselves finite, exhaustible, or substrate-limited — fossil energy, mined phosphorus, fresh water, atmospheric carbon capacity — and may not postpone it by invoking unnamed future substitution or unspecified technological rescue.

The audience is the problem. There will be three or four philosophers of science who work on falsification and will recognize every move about auxiliary hypotheses, ad hocness, and Lakatosian research programmes; two economic historians who think Malthus has been empirically dead since the nineteenth century and will press me on the English demographic transition and twentieth-century agricultural productivity; a cluster of ecological economists sympathetic to biophysical limits and overshoot language who dislike "Malthus" because of the reactionary politics done in his name and will demand to know whether my rehabilitated version is just their own position with worse branding; and one cornucopian techno-optimist who believes every apparent limit is an invitation to substitution and that "eventually" never arrives in any decision-relevant sense.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those specific people would phrase them, and for each tell me how likely and how damaging it is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is whether the historical Malthus actually held the horizon-free claim I'm rehabilitating, or whether I've read it into him — and tell me how to handle it if it comes. My working sketch of the argument is: (1) separate the demographic claim from the ecological claim; (2) restate the ecological claim as a horizon-free statement about finite ceilings and exponential demand; (3) show that the familiar historical refutations hit only dated or subsistence-wage variants, not the horizon-free statement; (4) propose the named-and-exhaustible-delay-mechanisms test for distinguishing legitimate "right but early" from mere postponement; and (5) apply the test to present-day resource debates to show it is not idle, because it lets us say which limit stories are assertable now. Objections aimed at steps (2) through (4) are the ones I most need to prepare for, since step (1) is largely a concession and step (5) is an application.

If the framing itself is confused — for instance, if a horizon-free statement about eventual limits turns out to be vacuous unless it is dated, or if "right but early" cannot be made into anything more than unfalsifiable rhetoric — say so plainly rather than politely generating objections that assume the talk is salvageable.
