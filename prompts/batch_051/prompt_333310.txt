I have a settled epistemic policy for reading work on the origins of farming, and I want a verdict on whether the policy is defensible or is just a bias wearing a methodology.

The policy concerns declension claims about the agricultural revolution: the claim, associated with Jared Diamond and many others, that the adoption of farming was on balance a mistake — that hunter-gatherers had achieved a kind of affluence for comparatively little effort, and that farming exchanged health and leisure for larger populations, harder labor, and the first durable inequality. When I encounter such a claim, I do not begin from neutrality. I begin from the assumption that I am probably reading a projection of modern discontent onto prehistory, and I impose three demands before I will treat the claim as history.

First, I require the comparison to be prospective and local. The claim should concern a particular population that actually made the transition, so that health can be compared before and after in the same place — teeth, stature, bone stress, workload — rather than by contrasting one region's foragers with another region's later farmers. Two populations differ in too many other respects for that kind of cross-sectional comparison to bear the weight.

Second, I require a stated counterfactual. The author has to say what the realistic option of continued foraging was for those particular people at that time, and why they nevertheless passed a point of no return if the new equilibrium really was worse. A one-time mistake is not the same as an ongoing trap; the author has to show the ratchet, not just the fall.

Third, I treat the moral timbre of the narrative as evidence about the author. To the extent that a text reads as an indictment of farming, sedentism, or civilization generally, I lower my credence in its empirical claims. I justify this to myself by noting that our own disaffections are exactly what would manufacture a lost golden age, and that the older story of farming as pure progress is now too naive for serious people to assert, so the romantic inversion meets almost no critical resistance.

The effect of this policy is that I find most of the farming-as-trap literature unconvincing, and I am content to remain agnostic about the whole question on the grounds that the evidence that would settle it is mostly not the kind that survives burial.

My question is whether this policy is tracking the evidence or merely guaranteeing a conclusion. Is it defensible as a standing habit? If parts of it are worth keeping, what is the restated version that would keep me from being taken in by declension stories while still leaving me open to the real possibility that a worse equilibrium can defeat a better one and then lock itself in?

Please give me a committed answer, not a survey of options. Say whether the policy, taken as a policy, should be kept, abandoned, or amended in a specific way, and defend that verdict.
