I spent forty years in a lab where if a category couldn't be operationalized it didn't go in the paper, and now that I'm retired I keep tripping over social-science words that everyone uses and nobody defines. The one bugging me lately is "generation" in the Boomers/Millennials/Gen Z sense. I want a working definition of that term — not the family sense (parents, children, grandchildren) but the big birth-cohort sense — that gets the following cases right.

- The Baby Boomers (in the US, the post-1946 through 1964 group, or whatever the real boundaries are). Clearly a generation if anything is.
- A purely administrative five-year birth cohort, say people born 1990–1994. Clearly not a generation, I'd have thought; its just a statistical bucket.
- People born in 1964 or 1965, right on the Boomer/Gen X border. I have no idea weather there's a fact of the matter about which side they fall on, or whether the border is just a marketing artifact.
- The "Greatest Generation," shaped by the Depression and WWII. If that's a generation, it's defined by an event rather than by birth years, which feels like a different kind of thing.
- A child who immigrates to the US in, say, 2005 at age eight. Birth year puts them in Gen Z, but their formative context isn't the same as a native-born kid's. Are they in the generation or not?
- The "Xennial" micro-generation (late 70s to early 80s) that supposedly sits between Gen X and Millennials. Is that a generation, a sub-generation, or just a label for people who don't want to be either?
- Kids whose schooling was disrupted by COVID. I hear people call them "the COVID cohort." Generation or not?

I've also been reading about the age-period-cohort problem in demography: you can't cleanly separate the effect of being a certain age from the effect of being born in a certain year from the effect of the particular year it happens to be, because age plus birth year equals the current year, or something like that. It makes me suspect that "generation" is doing a lot of causal work under the table, as if naming a cohort settles an identification problem by fiat.

So here's what I'd like. Propose a definition of "generation" (the sociological kind), run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. If some cases force a split — one descriptive, one causal/historical — give me both definitions and say which cases move between them. And then tell me straight: does the term pick out a real kind of thing, or is it a filing system we've mistaken for a natural category? I genuinely don't know the answer, so if your definition makes you change a verdict, say so.
