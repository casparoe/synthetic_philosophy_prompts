I'm an SRE (well, sort of a platform engineer) at a company that runs a bunch of customer-facing services, and I keep hitting the same wall whenever I have to give a risk estimate. The number changes completely depending on which bucket of similar past situations I drop the thing into. If I want the probability that next quarter's deploy of the new database connection pooling layer causes an outage, I can look at all deploys to this service this quarter (basically never breaks), or all deploys that touch connection pooling specifically (breaks a lot), or all Friday afternoon deploys by small teams (somewhere in between), or deploys where the author hasn't touched that module before (much higher). None of them is obviously wrong, and people in review meetings just pick whichever bucket supports their position and then wave it around as "the data." I've heard this has a name, the reference class problem, and I want to turn whatever the philosophers actually know about it into something my team can actually run.

So here's what I'd like: a reusable decision procedure I can use every time we have to put a number (or at least a defensible range) on a low-probability, high-cost software event. This comes up maybe a dozen times a quarter: deploy outage risk, capacity planning misses, a migration going way over budget. Something an engineer without any philosophy or stats background can run in maybe half a day, with me as the lead having to sign off on the final estimate. It should be a step-by-step thing: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and says don't estimate this, and where it hands the final call to a human because it's a values call rather than a calculation. I specifically want the hard cases handled: when the past data is too thin for any bucket, when two reference classes both look legitimate but give wildly different numbers, and when someone wants to use a bucket that contains the thing being assessed but has a sample size of like three.

Then run the procedure on two or three concrete cases so I can see it in action, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure spits out an answer I'm not going to like (higher risk than I'd want to admit, or "you can't estimate this, you're guessing"). Finally, be straight about what the procedure can't do for me, where I'm still just making a judgment call and should say so in a meeting.

Context that might matter: the stakes are real but not life-and-death; a bad estimate means an outage or two months of wasted engineering time, not someone getting hurt. We usually have some historical data but its always sparser than you'd want, and the thing we're estimating is usually not exactly like anything we've logged before. Also I don't care about the philosophical terminology, I need something I can paste into our team wiki and make a junior engineer follow.
