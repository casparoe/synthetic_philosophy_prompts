I chair the clinical technology review committee of a nonprofit hospital system. We must decide whether to make permanent a machine-learning symptom triage system for our nurse advice line, after a six-month pilot on weekend calls. The pilot's first-order outcomes are readable: call lengths fell about 12 percent, the proportion of callers directed to emergency care dropped slightly, and no serious mis-triages were reported. But the second- and third-order consequences are genuinely opaque to us. Triage nurses may gradually lose the skill to challenge or override the system; regular callers may learn to phrase their symptoms to get the disposition they want; the vendor will update the model quarterly in ways we cannot validate; and the tool will change what our system counts as an emergency, which changes the data, which changes the model — a loop none of us can trace with any confidence.

Our committee's remit says we should approve a technology only when its expected effects on patients, staff, and the system are on balance beneficial. That framing is exactly what makes this hard. One faction argues that because we cannot see the long-run consequences, we lack what we would need to justify adoption, and we should stick with the status quo. Another faction replies that the status quo is equally opaque in its long-run consequences, and that refusing to adopt is itself a choice with unknowable effects; so our ignorance cannot by itself decide the matter.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — with a time frame, and it should honestly engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than treating the decision as a calculation we are simply failing to finish. Close with a candid note on how confident you are in the recommendation and which of the opinion's claims you are least sure of.
