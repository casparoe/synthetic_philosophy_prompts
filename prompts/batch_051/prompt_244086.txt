I spent my working life as a research physiologist and have only recently begun reading the philosophical literature on probability. I am to give a departmental seminar to applied statisticians and data scientists, with a few philosophers of science in the room. My thesis is that the reference class problem is not a defect in particular models but a permanent feature of single-case probability: for any individual event, the probability is well defined only relative to a reference class, and the choice of reference class is fixed by the purpose of the enquiry rather than by any fact about the individual. I intend to open with a close reading of the passage in John Venn’s The Logic of Chance where he observes that the same individual belongs to many different series, and that the probability we assign depends upon which series we take it as a member of, illustrating the point with the chance of a man dying before a certain age depending on whether he is classed as an Englishman, a clergyman, or a member of a particular family. The audience is mostly frequentist applied statisticians who think of probability as a property of well-conducted procedures rather than of single cases; they know Venn’s name but have no patience for radical conventionalism. Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet in British English. State the eight or so objections I am most likely to receive in the question period, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary these statisticians would actually use, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say what I should concede up front to stop the discussion sliding into a general anti-statistics argument.
