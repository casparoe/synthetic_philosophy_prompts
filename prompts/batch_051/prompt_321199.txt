Question: Using a single example, explain what a conversational implicature is and how it differs from (a) what the sentence literally means and (b) what the speaker says. Then say which Gricean maxim does the main work in your example, and explain whether the implicature can be canceled.

Student answer: I'll use "Some of the students passed the exam." The sentence literally means at least some, possibly all, of the students passed. For purposes of the question, (a) and (b) are basically the same here: what the sentence means and what the speaker says are both just that at least some passed. The not-all reading is something the speaker communicates beyond that literal content. A hearer figures this out by reasoning that if the speaker had been in a position to say that all the students passed, then saying merely "some" would have been less informative than the speaker could have been, so the hearer infers the speaker is not in a position to assert that all passed. Since this extra bit attaches to the conventional meaning of the word "some," I'd classify it as a conventional implicature. The maxim doing the work is the maxim of relation—the speaker is expected to be relevant, and the not-all reading is the most relevent one. And because the implicature is tied to the word "some" itself, it can't really be canceled: once you say "some," the not-all reading is already there.
