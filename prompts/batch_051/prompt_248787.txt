A friend and I keep coming back to an argument that seems to show, too cleanly, that no finite sequence can be random; if it is sound, the question "can this finite sequence be random?" has a trivial answer and we should stop asking it. I do not believe the conclusion, but I cannot locate the step that should be rejected, so I am looking for a precise diagnosis.

Take a fair coin flipped twenty times. The sequence HHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH strikes us as non-random, while HTTHHTHTTTHHTHTTHHTH strikes us as random, or at least as far more random. The argument runs:

(1) The two sequences differ in an intuitive property: the first is non-random, the second is random. I want to keep this.
(2) Under a fair coin, every particular sequence of twenty outcomes, all heads included, has probability 1/2^20. The two sequences are exactly equally likely.
(3) If two sequences are exactly equally likely, then they cannot differ in how random they are; randomness, if it belongs to a sequence at all, can only be a matter of how probable that sequence is.
(4) So the intuition in (1) is mistaken, and no finite sequence is more random than any other.
(5) If no finite sequence is more random than any other, then "random" does not describe the sequence itself; it can describe the process that produced it, or our information about that process, but not the sequence.
(6) Therefore a finite sequence is never random. Randomness is a property only of processes, and the appearance that one outcome is more random than another is something we project onto it.

Each premise feels plausible as I read it, yet the conclusion would force us to give up the practice of calling an experimental result, a password, or a purportedly random sequence "suspiciously orderly" in an objective sense. Premise (2) seems to me beyond reproach, and I would like to keep (1) if I can; I have no strong attachment to the rest. Which step should be rejected, why did it look fine, and what do the main ways of resisting the argument each have to give up? I am after the diagnosis at the level of the inference, not just a restatement that the probabilities are equal.
