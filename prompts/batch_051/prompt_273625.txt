A friend of mine has been reading around constitutional theory and has convinced himself that "interregnum" is basically an empty category. His claim is that once you grant that a constitution can be replaced by something not authorised by the old order, you lose any principled way of calling a coup during a collapse illegitimate. I think he's overreached, but I couldn't pinpoint the move. Here's the argument as he lays it out:

(1) An institution governs with legitimate authority only if its authority is conferred or validated by an existing legal rule.
(2) In a true interregnum, no such rule exists for anyone: the old order's offices, succession rules, amending formulas, and emergency powers have all run out.
(3) So whoever steps in during an interregnum lacks legitimate authority.

(4) But consider a constitutional order that came about through a genuine break — a revolution or a total collapse — rather than through the old order's own rules of amendment or replacement. Some such orders are now legitimate, or at least capable of being legitimate.
(5) At the founding of such an order, no existing legal rule conferred or validated the founders' authority to make it.
(6) So by the standard in (1)-(3), the founders lacked legitimate authority at the founding.
(7) Yet the order they founded can count as legitimate, so legitimate governing authority must be able to arise without prior legal authorisation.
(8) And if that's right, then during an interregnum the fact that a person has no legal warrant is not, by itself, a deciding reason against their legitimacy.
(9) Some other basis for legitimacy must therefore do the work — acceptance, stability, rights protection, the quality of the order produced, whatever it is.
(10) But that basis is available to a soldier or faction that seizes power in a collapse just as much as to the founders, as long as they eventually secure order, a working constitution, or whatever the test turns out to be.
(11) So the distinction between a legitimate founding and a coup during an interregnum shrinks to a post hoc judgement of outcomes, and we can't say the coup-maker was illegitimate at the time — which is exactly the judgement I'd wanted to keep.

I want to hold on to both thoughts: that a new constitutional order can become legitimate after a genuine collapse even if the old rules didn't authorise it, and that not every armed group that fills the vacuum becomes legitimate just by hanging on. He says I can't have both once I abandon (1). Which step of the argument should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
