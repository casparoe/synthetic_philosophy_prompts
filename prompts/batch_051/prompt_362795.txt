Question: In chapter 3 of On Liberty, Mill argues for individuality and what he calls "experiments in living." Here is one of his statements of the case. Mill writes:

"It is not by wearing down into uniformity all that is individual in themselves, but by cultivating it and calling it forth, within the limits imposed by the rights and interests of others, that human beings become a noble and beautiful object of contemplation; and as the works partake the character of those who do them, by the same process human life also becomes rich, diversified, and animating, furnishing more abundant aliment to high thoughts and elevating feelings, and strengthening the tie which binds every individual to the race, by making the race infinitely better worth belonging to. In proportion to the development of his individuality, each person becomes more valuable to himself, and is therefore capable of being more valuable to others."

Using the passage: (a) reconstruct Mill's argument for protecting individuality, and (b) consider this objection — if the worth of a way of life is only shown by its being tried, then any freely chosen way of life is as good as any other, so Mill's view collapses into relativism about the good life. Would Mill have an adequate reply?

Student's answer:

Part (a). Mill's main argument is against the pressure to wear everyone down into uniformity. He thinks a society that grinds away individual differences loses something important. That's where the idea of "experiments in living" comes in: since we don't know in advance which ways of life are best, people should be allowed to try out different plans of life, and the worth of those ways of life gets shown by actually living them. The passage he gives us says that by cultivating individuality rather than wearing it down, human beings become "a noble and beautiful object of contemplation" and human life becomes "rich, diversified, and animating." The structure here is basically the same as Mill's argument for free speech: we protect different opinions because we're fallible and need disagreement for the truth to emerge, and we protect different ways of living because we're fallible about how to live and need the experiments to learn it. The passage also puts a limit on all this: the cultivating happens "within the limits imposed by the rights and interests of others." That's the harm principle in different language.

Part (b). The objection is better than it looks, but it doesn't quite land. It's true that Mill thinks the worth of a mode of life can only be proved by trying it, but that doesn't make him a relativist, because he does have a standard: an experiment is off the table when it harms others. Inside that boundary, though, Mill really doesn't compare one experiment with another for goodness. The passage itself gives the relevant standard as cultivation and diversity — becoming "a noble and beautiful object of contemplation" — not any particular view of what the good life is. So among non-harmful ways of living, Mill has no resources for saying one experiment is better than another. What makes a way of life worthwhile for him is just that it is freely chosen and expresses a person's own character. That isn't relativism exactly, because the harm principle rules some experiments out, but it does mean his view sits closer to the objection than he'd like to admit.
