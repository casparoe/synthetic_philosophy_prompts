Last night I spent most of a dinner party arguing with a friend about a single sentence — the one about the present King of France being bald. He insisted it was simply false; I kept wanting to say that it does not receive a truth value at all, because there is no such person for the sentence to be about. When we moved on to a borderline heap, and then to a prediction about tomorrow's coin toss, our answers shifted in ways neither of us could fully defend. I came away convinced that plenty of otherwise thoughtful people hold an unexamined grab-bag of commitments about truth-value gaps, and that it would be useful to have an instrument that surfaces those commitments for a general-audience discussion group I help run — bright and argumentative, but with no formal training in logic.

I would like you to write that instrument: a decision tree (or, if the tree becomes unwieldy, a short sorting quiz with a scoring key) that takes a group member from their answers about concrete cases to a named position about bivalence and truth-value gaps. Please build it around four diagnostic cases: (1) a heap from which grains are removed one at a time, with the question whether some intermediate stage is neither a heap nor not a heap; (2) the sentence about the present King of France being bald, together with its negation; (3) a sentence uttered now about a not-yet-determined future event, such as whether a particular tossed coin will land heads; and (4) a self-referential sentence that says of itself that it is false. You may add one or two further cases if the tree needs them, but every branch question should be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the technical labels. The leaves should include at least: a classical bivalence view, including the epistemicist version on which apparent gaps are really ignorance or hidden falsehood; a Russellian view on which the King-of-France sentence is simply false because its existence claim is false; a supervaluationist view on which borderline vague sentences lack a truth value while classical logical truths and inferences are preserved; a truth-functional three-valued, strong-Kleene-style view on which the negation of a truth-valueless sentence is itself truth-valueless; a presupposition-failure view that locates gaps in failed existential assumptions rather than in vagueness; and a glut or dialetheist view on which some sentences, such as the self-referential one, are both true and false.

Please keep it to roughly seven or eight questions and at most seven leaves. Since the point is to map the logical space rather than reproduce a list of labels, also note where a combination of answers has no corresponding position — for example, someone who treats vagueness as gappy but future contingents as bivalent — and briefly diagnose in each case whether that cell of the space is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Begin your response with the instrument itself, with no preface or commentary, and put any caveats, scoring notes, or further commentary after it.
