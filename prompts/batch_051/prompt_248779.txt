My cousin, who's doing a psych phd, got annoyed when I called evopsych a pile of just-so stories. His defence was: if you found some weird gadget in the desert, you wouldn't say any story about it is as good as any other; you'd take it apart, figure out what it seems built to do, and then test that. He says the mind is the same kind of thing — a box of parts shaped to solve recurring problems in the ancestral environment — so the stories evopsych tells are just hypotheses you can check by asking whether the trait really does the job it looks designed for. From this he draws a strong conclusion: we should default to treating even weird or unpleasant psychological traits as adapted tools rather than bugs or leftovers, and anyone who shrugs and calls it a just-so story is being lazy.

I dont find this convincing but I can't quite say why, and I suspect the gadget picture is smuggling in a lot. Can you audit the analogy properly? Give me the correspondence table — what maps to the gadget, its parts, the toolmarks, the manual, the environment it was built for, and the act of testing it. Then go through the places the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that actually matter for the just-so charge from the ones that are side issues. Say what the analogy quietly imports about design and about how you'd know a trait had one function. If it helps, run one concrete trait through it — say a fear of snakes or sexual jealousy — to show where the mapping holds and where it gives out. And finally tell me whether his conclusion, that reverse-engineering function from form lets evopsych escape the just-so story problem, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was the gadget picture doing the persuading. If the analogy is bad, what better analogy should he have used?
