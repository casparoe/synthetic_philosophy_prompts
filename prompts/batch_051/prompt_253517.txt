I'm in a seminar this term on blame and moral responsibility, and last week we read Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" — well, the excerpts about reactive attitudes, the participant stance, and the objective stance you fall into when you stop treating someone as a person to be reasoned with and start treating them as a problem to be managed. Since then I can't stop noticing how uneven actual legal practice is. In US sentencing hearings, a defendant who shows genuine remorse often gets a lighter sentence; judges mention it constantly, and parole boards basically require it before they'll release anyone. So the system seems to think the offender's own emotional stance toward what they did matters for how much punishment is appropriate. But in the same courthouse, a defendant found not guilty by reason of insanity, or described by a psychologist as psychopathic, stops being a target of blame entirely and gets talked about as a risk to be managed. And even for ordinary defendants there are dangerousness assessments at sentencing where the judge sounds like an actuary, not someone responding to a person.

What theory of moral responsibility does this uneven practice actually presuppose? It looks like it's halfway committed to Strawson's picture — blame as a two-way exchange of reactive attitudes, where remorse matters because the offender is re-joining the community that resents them — but it also keeps slipping into the objective stance whenever a defendant is hard to read as a participant. Reconstruct the practice's implicit theory, be clear about what it strictly presupposes versus what just fits with several theories (could a pure deterrence theory explain the role of remorse? a desert theory?), and then say what would have to change in sentencing and parole if we took the reactive-attitudes picture all the way seriously. Also, tell me how confident you are in your reading and flag the piece you're least sure about.
