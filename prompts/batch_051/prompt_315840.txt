I teach criminal law, not philosophy, and I think most naturally in the vocabulary of actus reus and mens rea, inchoate liability, attempt, intention, knowledge, recklessness, negligence, strict liability, and the line between motive, intention, and mere fantasy. I would like you to restate Abelard's position that sin consists wholly in consent to the act, and not in the outward deed itself, as faithfully as that legal vocabulary allows. Please be specific about the distinctions he draws: the initial suggestion or temptation, the pleasure or inclination taken in entertaining the deed, and the consent that completes the sin; and what he says about the person who consents but never acts and the person who acts without prior consent. Then give me a residue report: which parts of Abelard's view translate cleanly into the categories of modern criminal law, which translate only if I distort the legal concepts, and which parts have no counterpart at all. I want to know whether Abelard is best understood as an early mens rea theorist whose insight has been absorbed into modern doctrine, or whether he is defending a view the criminal law is structurally unable to express. Please write your answer in British English.
