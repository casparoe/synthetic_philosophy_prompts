In Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, a long sorites is run from a case in which a subject clearly feels cold to a case in which she clearly does not, through adjacent cases so close that she cannot discriminate between them. The argument says that if the condition of feeling cold were luminous — whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know it obtains — then, because knowledge requires a margin for error, knowledge at one step would guarantee that the condition still obtains at the next step; luminosity would then yield knowledge at that next step, and so the condition would be forced to propagate step by step until it reached the clearly non-cold case, a contradiction. The argument appears to depend on the underlying condition varying over something like a continuum, without sharp boundaries between adjacent cases. Now suppose, for the sake of argument, a changed assumption: the mind is realized by a classical digital computer, so its total state space is discrete. Between any two distinct total machine states there is a sharp, hardware-fixed transition, and a monitoring subsystem can read its own state register, so that the current machine state is directly available to the system with no gradual borderline region between states. Does Williamson's anti-luminosity argument survive this changed assumption? Does it still show that the relevant mental conditions are non-luminous and that the KK principle — if one knows p, one is in a position to know that one knows p — fails for such a system? If the argument loses its foothold, say whether this rescues the full KK principle for the system or merely makes some surrogate condition, such as being in machine state S, luminous. Could the argument be repaired by constructing a discrete sorites, for instance over a sequence of machine states differing by one register bit at a time, and what would that repair have to assume? Identify exactly which premise of the original argument is under pressure.
