Before I read any more proposals about which traits deserve heightened legal protection, I want the specification of what an adequate theory of suspect grounds has to deliver. The one-factor accounts I keep meeting — immutability, visibility, political powerlessness, historical subordination, choice — each look plausible in the cases they were built for and unconvincing one step away. Blood type and height are as immutable as race but attract no suspicion; religion is partly chosen and often invisible, yet sits among the most protected grounds; sex receives intermediate scrutiny even though women are an electoral majority; and a group can be marked as subordinate on the basis of an arbitrary ancestry that tracks no deep human difference. So write me the desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and which distinction it forbids a theory to collapse. I at least need a theory to keep apart: an irrational classification and a suspect one; a protected characteristic and a ground that triggers strict scrutiny; an immutable trait and a suspect ground; and a class being disadvantaged and a ground being an impermissible kind of reason for decision. Flag any subsets of the desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and state which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories. Then provide only a brief scorecard of the main families of theory — immutability, choice or autonomy, political process, anti-subordination, and pluralist multi-factor views — against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them, not on the scorecard. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding about one of the contestable grounds, such as religion, sexual orientation, disability, or body size, would most likely make you reweight the desiderata, and where your verdict is least stable. Write in American English.
