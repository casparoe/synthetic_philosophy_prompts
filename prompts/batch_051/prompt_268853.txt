I am a retired natural scientist, and after spending most of my life in the laboratory I have recently begun working through political philosophy with a reading group. Over the past several months we have been collecting the strongest objections to the claim that citizens in a large democracy have a moral duty to vote, and I have reached the point where nearly every new article I read seems to restate an objection I already have on my list. I would be glad of any further objection that is genuinely distinct from the following six, which I give in my own words rather than as quotations.

The first is the inefficacy objection: an individual vote has a negligible chance of deciding the outcome, so nothing about consequences can ground a duty to cast it. The second is the opportunity-cost objection: the time and attention required to vote responsibly could almost always be spent on other activities that do more good. The third is the liberty objection: even a purely moral duty to vote would apply pressure to perform a political act that a person may find alienating or dishonest, and that pressure sits uneasily with respect for individual agency. The fourth is the competence objection: many citizens are badly informed about the candidates and the issues, and it is strange to claim that such people are morally required to add their unreflective judgment to the total. The fifth is the expressive objection: since a vote is widely read as an endorsement, a duty to vote would force people to affirm positions they reject, while abstention can itself be a meaningful form of protest. The sixth is the complicity objection: in voting for a candidate one takes on some responsibility for the wrongs that candidate later commits, and a general duty to vote would therefore require citizens to dirty their hands whenever the available options are all morally compromised.

I would be especially interested in an objection that does not turn on the smallness of a single vote's causal effect and does not turn on the voter's ignorance, since those underlying ideas are already represented above. The objection should also not be a restatement, sharpening, special case, or conjunction of the six I have listed. After setting it out, please tell me honestly whether it is any good: whether it can survive the obvious replies and whether it really adds something that the six do not. I would rather you conclude that a candidate objection fails than dress up a familiar point as a discovery. Continuous prose only, please, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
