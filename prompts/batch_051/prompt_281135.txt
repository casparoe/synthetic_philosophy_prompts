I'm a science journalist at a mid-size digital outlet and I'm in a running argument with our standards editor about the video team's new workflow. The relevant bit of our newsroom's editorial guidelines says: 'Content must not be presented in a manner designed to mislead an audience about the strength of the evidence, the certainty of a source, or the reliability of the information.' Boring, standard, fine. The new thing is we've started using this retention-optimizing tool that analyzes the first minute of our explainer videos and suggests trims. Its scary how consistent it is: nearly every suggestion is to delete a hedge — 'though this is disputed,' 'so far the data's mixed,' 'one study, not a consensus' — and push the caveats a minute or two later, because people click away otherwise. So the opening now states the claim much harder than the scientists themselves would. Nothing in the video is false, and the caveats are all still in there; they're just buried after the hook. Our editor says this is a clear breach of the clause, because we are now designing the presentation to mislead people about how settled the evidence is, even if only for forty seconds. I say it can't be a breach: every sentence we air is true, the hedges appear in the same video, and moving them around is just structuring information persuasively — the same thing a good lede does. She says the lede defense is exactly what the clause exists to police and that the tool just does it too well for us to notice. So I want someone to work through it properly. Please go through it in terms of the usual approaches to interpretation: the literal wording, the purpose of the clause, what the people who wrote it would plausibly say if we showed them the tool, and what a reasonable viewer takes the first minute to be telling them. Then tell me whether the workflow breaches the clause, and more generally whether optimizing for watch-time is just modern persuasion or tips into manipulation when the design choice is made by an algorithm rather than an editor. I keep flip-flopping on that last part and I'd like the answer tied to the actual rule, not a general essay on clickbait.
