I'm in a politics and religion reading group, and last week we spent three hours on a city council case that I still can't make up my mind about. The way I was taught it, a reason offered in public political debate counts as a public reason only if it meets four conditions: (a) accessibility — a reasonable person who doesn't share the speaker's religion or worldview could see how the reason has force; (b) sincerity — the speaker actually endorses the reason, rather than treating it as cover; (c) reciprocity — the speaker would accept reasons of the same general kind from people with rival worldviews; and (d) scope — the decision concerns something all citizens are bound by, not just a private association's internal affairs.

Here's the case. A city council is deciding whether to renew a predictive policing contract. One member, a devout Catholic, wants to vote no. In the chamber she gives only a secular argument: the system has racially disparate false-positive rates, the vendor won't explain the model's coefficients, and when the state coerces people it has to be able to say why — due process can't run on a black box. Privately she tells me afterward that her actual ground is theological: each person is made in the image of God, and treating someone as a bundle of demographic risk scores is a kind of idolotry. She says she left that out because it wouldn't persuade anyone, but she also would have voted the same way even if the secular argument had collapsed.

Does her argument, as actually offered in the chamber, qualify as a public reason? Run it through the criteria one by one, and say where the biggest ambiguity is. I keep flip-flopping between 'obviously qualifies, the stated argument is secular and strong' and 'obviously fails, she's feeding the chamber a reason that isn't actually doing the work for her.' Is the real problem with her, or with criterion (b) as stated? Does the case show that one of the four criteria is doing all the work, or that sincerity is being asked to carry too much weight?
