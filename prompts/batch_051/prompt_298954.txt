Next month I'm giving a paper to a philosophy of religion colloquium defending a reading of Wittgenstein on religious belief. The audience is mostly analytic philosophers of religion who treat theism like a big explanatory hypothesis—the sort who think the interesting question is whether the evidence raises or lowers the probability of God. There's also two Wittgensteinians in the room who hold the standard fideist reading, the one where religious belief is cut off from rational criticism entirely. My thesis is that both sides misread him. Wittgenstein isn't saying religious belief is a hypothesis you assess from outside, and he isn't saying it's immune to criticism either. He's relocating where the criticism has to happen: inside the life the belief shapes. So the usual evidence-for-God debate is a category mistake, but the belief still has internal standards—it can be shallow, self-serving, fall apart under pressure, fail to orient a life. Compressed argument: the contrast he draws between religious conviction and ordinary empirical belief isn't a difference in how much evidence you have for the same kind of claim; it's a difference in what kind of commitment it is.
