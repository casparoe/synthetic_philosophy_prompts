I'm a novelist, and I've gotten obsessed with an idea from Wang Yangming—that knowing and doing aren't two separate things, and that whatever you "know" but never act on isn't really knowledge yet. It's messing with how I write characters who say one thing and live another. I've got gut verdicts on a bunch of cases; I want the principle, if there is one, that generates all of them.

A son says, with total sincerity, that he knows he should visit his mother in the hospital, and he never goes. I want to say he dosn't know it, not in any sense that matters. A recovering alcoholic says he knows one drink will wreck his life, then takes one. I still think he knew; his body just overruled him. But I can't see why failing to act proves ignorance in the first case but not the second.

A chess player blunders under time pressure but can point to the winning move afterward—he knew it all along. A woman raised by racists can recite all the right anti-racist things and sincerely beleives she's changed, but she still flinches around Black strangers and can't say why. Does she know what she claims, or only know that she ought to say it? A child shares her lunch with a new kid and can't justify it beyond "he looked hungry"; I want to say she knows generosity better than an adult who can lecture on it. A sleepwalker navigates the house, unlocks the door, steps outside, and remembers none of it—he didn't know what he was doing, full stop. An actor plays a grieving father flawlessly, and I don't think that means he knows grief.

What principle about knowing and doing gets these all, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the character I'm stuck on: a man who acts bravely under fire and afterward says, honestly, "I don't know what courage is." If the principle ends up far from the unity idea I started with, say so, and tell me which verdict is doing the damage.
