I've been arguing with a couple of friends about whether your gut reactions can ever be moral evidence, and I keep running into a flip that nobody will own up to. Take my friend: he has a strong, not-especially-articulable aversion to eating octopus — not a worked-out animal-suffering view, just a deep 'something is off with that' — and he's happy to treat that feeling as a moral clue, a genuine preference or intuition registering something real that he hasn't managed to state. But when his coworker reports the exact same sort of not-especially-articulable aversion to his adult son bringing a boyfriend to family dinners, my friend immediately files it as bias: something the coworker picked up from his upbringing, not a barometer of anything out in the world. Same kind of psychological state, two labels. I notice I do this too, and I'd bet most people do.

I want to know whether anything justifies that flip, or whether 'bias' is just what we call other people's unshared intuitions and 'intuition' is what we call our own. Try to state the strongest principle you can that sorts gut-level aversions into moral intuitions worth taking seriously and biases worth discounting — and stress-test it against cases where the easy answers stop being easy (aversions to eating dogs, to watching boxing for fun, to interracial marriage, to line-jumping, that sort of thing). Then give me an honest verdict: is the asymmetry something we should keep, reverse, or give up?
