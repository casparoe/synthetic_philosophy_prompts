Lately I've been going back and forth about whether my partner and I should have kids, so I started writing down my gut reactions to a bunch of scenarios, like test cases. They feel like they follow some consistant rule, but I can't extract it, and two of them might be giving different answers to what is secretly the same question. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'm probably missing an obvious framework, but I'd rather you derive the rule from my cases than quote one at me.

Here are my five cases:

1. A couple who would be loving, stable parents decides not to have children. The children they would have had would very likely have had happy lives. My verdict: not wrong. I don't think you owe it to a possible person to bring them into existence, even a good existence.

2. A couple learns that any child they conceive now would have a condition that causes severe, near-constant pain and death around age two, with only brief calm periods. They conceive anyway, saying the child will still have some good moments. My verdict: wrong. Giving a mostly miserable life a few okay minutes doesn't cancel the misery.

3. A couple learns their next child would be born with a condition that makes life substantially harder — ongoing pain and social exclusion, but still an overall life the person is glad to have, maybe a 6/10. They go ahead and conceive naturally. My verdict: not wrong, though I'd understand if they chose not to.

4. The one that trips me up: same basic setup, but now the couple is doing IVF and must choose which of two embryos to transfer. Embryo A would develop into a child with the 6/10 condition from case 3. Embryo B would develop into a different, healthy child with a 9/10 life. Everything else is equal. My gut says choosing A is a mistake — you should pick B. But I can't say why without implying that the 6/10 child in case 3 would have been harmed by being born, which I don't actually believe. So cases 3 and 4 feel like the same situation and I answer them differently.

5. The core weirdness I keep noticing: when I imagine a happy person who never exists, I don't feel any loss or badness; but when I imagine a suffering person whose existence was avoided by a decision, I feel like something bad was successfully prevented. I care about missing bads but not missing goods, but only across the line between existing and never-existing. Is that a real principle or just a mood?

My half-formed rule right now is something like: don't create new bads, but you're not required to create new goods. Except that version seems to rule out every birth, since every life contains some bad parts, and it isn't my actual rule because I already said case 3 is okay. Maybe the line is "overall good vs overal bad"? But then why isn't case 1 wrong — the overall-good child never shows up? I can't make all five cases compile.

Can you help me find the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits these five verdicts? Please state each candidate as precisely as you can; go case by case and say which candidate handles it and whether it handles it cleanly; be explicit about which of my verdicts the best candidate can't keep as-is; and apply the best candidate to two further cases I'm stuck on:

- New case A: a couple already has one child, a second child would have a happy life, and the first child's life would be much better with a sibling. Is creating the second child merely allowed, or is there something close to an obligation?
- New case B: a couple could conceive this month and produce a child with a 6/10 life because of a temporary health factor, or wait one month and produce a different child with a 9/10 life. I can't tell whether not waiting is wrong, and I especially can't tell how that answer should fit with my answers to cases 3 and 4.

- Candidate principles (the 2-4 rules that best fit, stated precisely) — about 200 words.
- Case-by-case fit (for each of my five cases, which candidate handles it and how cleanly) — about 350 words.
