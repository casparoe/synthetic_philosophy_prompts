I retired from soil chemistry eight years ago, and since then I've been reading moral philosophy in the evenings. I'm still more comfortable with a mass balance than a modal operator, so I'd like help with this argument I built up about frugality. It feels like it proves too much, but each premise looked plausible to me as I wrote it.

(1) If frugality is a virtue, it is the virtue of handling scarce resources without waste.  
(2) Waste means using a resource in a way that produces less good than some feasible alternative use of that same resource.  
(3) A virtue is not just a useful habit; it carries a norm of blame. If someone fails in honesty or justice, we don't just say it was suboptimal—we say they did wrong. So if frugality is a virtue, avoidable waste is also a wrong.  
(4) Most ordinary nonessential spending is avoidable waste in the sense of (2). The same money could have produced more good elsewhere—say, transferred to people who lack clean water or basic medicine—than it produces when spent on the third espresso machine or a book club purchase of a book I won't reread.  
(5) Therefore, if frugality is a virtue, most nonessential spending is morally wrong, and most people in rich countries are constantly failing in their duties.  
(6) But a virtue that makes ordinary life into a near-continuous moral failure is not a virtue in any recognizable sense; it also destroys the difference between extravagance and merely not maximizing.  
(7) So either frugality is not a virtue of the kind that carries moral blame, or it stops being a personal ideal and becomes an impersonal demand that resources always be allocated to maximize good—in other words, we should promote efficient use rather than be frugal ourselves.

I want to keep (1) and (6), because I was raised with both: waste is bad, and a life that treats every unmaximized dollar as a sin is also bad. But I can't tell whether the problem is in the definition of waste at (2), the move from virtue to blame at (3), or somewhere in how (4) characterizes alternatives. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
