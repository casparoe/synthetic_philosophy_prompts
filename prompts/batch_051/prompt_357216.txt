I run a weekly one-hour reading group for mathematics PhD students and postdocs. They are technically strong and broadly interested in foundations, but none have worked through academic philosophy, and several arrive with the assumption that the field is either obvious or verbal. Next week's session is on Benacerraf's dilemma. I do not want you to explain the dilemma to me; I want a teaching plan for the session. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what, the single worked example or case that should carry the hour and the properties it needs to have and to lack, the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than argued with after it has hardened. I already anticipate several specific traps worth mapping: treating the causal constraint on knowledge as a straw man to be dismissed, thinking provability in some formal system already satisfies the semantic demand, treating formalism or structuralism as a cheap dissolution of the tension, and conflating the dilemma with Benacerraf's other argument about why no single set-theoretic reduction of the numbers is privileged. I also want one question I can pose in the final five minutes whose answer would tell me whether they have understood the dilemma as a structural tension between two independently plausible demands rather than as a quick argument against platonism, and I want to know what should be deliberately left out of the hour and how to mark it as omitted. Throughout, separate what is the settled statement of the dilemma and its standard objections from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mathematics, and do not present a contested resolution as the consensus.
