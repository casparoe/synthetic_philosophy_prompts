I came to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, and I have been reading the literature on scientific realism. I keep returning to the pessimistic meta-induction. Rather than try to refute it outright, I have been using a distinction I call the coincidence criterion. Looking back at theories that were once empirically successful and later abandoned, the pieces that were discarded seem to me to have been overwhelmingly supported through a single channel of experimental access: one family of instruments, one measurement tradition, one technique interpreted within a single theoretical framework. The pieces that survived into the successor theory, often in recognizable or limiting form, were those that had to satisfy two or more experimental traditions that were developed and justified independently of each other at the time. Call a posit coincident when it is forced on us by at least two such independent channels. My proposal is that the pessimistic induction should be read as a localized warning rather than as global antirealism: past success misled us chiefly about single-channel posits, while coincident posits are where realism remains warranted. I am aware this sounds close to Whewell's consilience of inductions, to Hacking's entity realism, and to the selective realists' working-posits maneuver, and I am unsure whether my notion of independence between experimental channels is sharp enough to distinguish my view from all three. I would like an honest assessment. Is the criterion coherent? Does the historical pattern hold, or is it an artifact of the cases I happen to know? And what is the strongest counterexample — a coincident posit that was nonetheless abandoned, or a single-channel posit that survived — and would that counterexample be fatal or merely force a refinement?
