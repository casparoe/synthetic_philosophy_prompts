I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who works on the ethics of testimony, and I came away unsure whether she is running one coherent principle or several. Over the past few months she has handed down a fairly stable set of verdicts, which I wrote down to keep her honest. She is certain that a doctor who waves off a Black woman's report of chest pain as anxiety, muttering that patients like that always exaggerate, commits a clear testimonial injustice, and she says the same about an all-male committee that dismisses a graduate student's calm explanation of why her experiment failed as defensive and emotional while taking the word-for-word identical explanation from her male colleague at face value. She also counts it as testimonial injustice when a homeless man is disbelieved about owning his dog until a housed passer-by repeats the claim, and when an editor sets aside a Japanese-American scholar's policy analysis as obviously biased purely on account of her surname. But she is equally firm that there is no testimonial injustice when an air-traffic controller doubts a trainee's altitude reading because the trainee has failed the required simulation three times that week; that, she says, is mere calibration on a track record, not a credibility deficit aimed at who he is. And when I described a woman in the early 1960s who could not get her workplace to understand the harm of persistent sexual touching and comments because no shared vocabulary for sexual harassment yet existed, she called it an epistemic injustice of another kind: a gap in shared concepts rather than deflated credibility.

Tonight the fight was about a new case. A welfare officer disbelieves a Roma applicant's self-reported income, not from any personal animus but because his office's standard risk profile says applicants from her postal district, which is heavily Roma, misreport income at a higher rate; he would have accepted the same form from an applicant outside that district, and the applicant is in fact telling the truth. I said that on her own pattern this must be testimonial injustice. She kept insisting it is only ugly statistical discrimination, not an epistemic injustice, though she could not articulate what distinguishes it from the cases she does count. Before I concede, I want a neutral audit of her track record.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working on social epistemology and the ethics of belief would: begin by brainstorming the candidate rules or principles that fit all six of my friend's earlier verdicts, say which candidate rules classify the welfare officer's decision which way, and then give a careful prediction of what she would say if she were forced into consistency. If her earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say exactly what commitment each resolution would add, and describe the further case I should press her on to settle which rule she actually runs.
