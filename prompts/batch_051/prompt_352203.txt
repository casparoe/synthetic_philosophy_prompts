There's an asymmetry in how we treat states versus persons that I can't quite resolve. We regularly expect, sometimes demand, that a state apologize for wrongs committed by a previous regime, by officials long dead, or even by a colonial administration from a century ago. People treat a nation as the "same" moral agent across generations: the current government is supposed to say sorry, and possibly pay reparations, for things done before any living citizen was born. But if you asked an individual to apologize for what his great-grandfather did, we'd find that bizzare or offensive—even when the person has inherited property or a family business from that ancestor, and even if he quietly benefits from the wrong. So states get treated as continuous moral persons for purposes of apology and restitution, while actual persons don't.

Maybe the answer is that a state is a corporate agent that persists through time as a legal entity. But its membership turns over completely across a century, just as an individual's body does; the thing that persists looks like an abstraction, not like a person with a conscience. I want to know whether this asymmetry is actually defensible or just something we've kept because it's awkward for governments to refuse. If you think it's defensible, give the principle that does the work and test it against the individual case: make sure it doesn't imply that individuals should apologize for their ancestors too. If you think it isn't, say what we should give up instead. And please tell me what would change your mind, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail if I pressed on it.
