My grandmother is eighty-two and has moderate-to-advanced dementia. Twenty years ago, while fully competent, she signed and notarized an advance directive stating that if she ever reached a point where she could no longer recognize her immediate family, she would refuse antibiotics and artificial nutrition for otherwise life-threatening infections. Last week she developed pneumonia. She does not recognize my mother most days; once or twice a week she calls her by name, but the rest of the time she treats her as a kind stranger. She is not in pain, smiles at visitors, seems absorbed by music, and clearly takes pleasure in eating. Her physician reads the directive as triggered and as requiring antibiotics to be withheld; my mother argues that would be wrong, because the woman in front of us now seems to want to go on living and still has ordinary enjoyments. I have to help my family decide, and I do not want to rely on vague intuitions. In my philosophy of medicine course we have studied the debate over advance directives, but I cannot figure out how to apply the competing principles to this exact situation. Should we honor the earlier written refusal or act on the present apparent interests and wishes of my grandmother, and what should decide the matter?
