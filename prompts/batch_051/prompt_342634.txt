I'm a philosophy major taking an epistemology course, and we just did the dogmatism paradox. I also work as the news editor at the campus paper, and I keep catching us doing a sloppy version of the exact same move in the newsroom. As I understand it, the paradox is: if I know some claim p, then I also know that any evidence against p is misleading, so it seems like I can rationally set aside counterevidence without even looking at it. That's obviously a terrible rule for a paper, but its weirdly hard to say exactly which step is wrong, and something like it sneaks into how we handle readers or sources challenging something we "know" is true.

Could you turn whatever epistemology actually knows about this into a decision procedure I could run as news editor? For context, I get maybe four or five challenges a week, often on deadline with about fifteen minutes to decide, and my editor-in-chief has to sign off on any correction. The procedure should be a checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it tells me to run a correction or update versus hold the story, and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a values judgment rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a reader emails a scan of a state charter that contradicts the founding year we print on the masthead, (b) a student senator writes in that our story saying a bill passed unanimously is wrong because she voted no, and (c) right after we ran a profile saying the provost has no record of misconduct, an anonymous tip alleges misconduct at her previous job. I especially want to see a case where the procedure forces me to do something I really dont want to do, plus a clear statement of what the procedure cant settle.
