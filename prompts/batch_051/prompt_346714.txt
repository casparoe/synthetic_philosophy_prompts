I've been rereading Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and the section on learning machines near the end has been bugging me. He says something like: instead of trying to program the full adult mind directly, you'd build something more like a child's mind, give it sense organs, and then teach it. He talks about punishment and reward, and about the whole process being more like education than engineering. I got into an argument with a friend about whether training a modern LLM with RLHF counts as what Turing was imagining, and obviously we couldnt even agree on what teaching means in the first place.

So give me a working definition of teaching that gets these cases right. A grad student walking another grad student through a derivation. A parent correcting a toddler's over-regularized past tense. A convnet being optimized by stochastic gradient descent on ImageNet. A parrot trained with food rewards to say hello. Someone working through a textbook and its exercises on their own. A school system drilling kids for standardized tests. RLHF on a language model. A child in a boarding school made to recite nationalist slogans. A coach demonstrating a tennis serve and correcting the student's grip.

I think the first two are teaching and the third and fifth are not, but I'm not sure about the parrot, the RLHF case, or whether indoctrination should be a subspecies of teaching or a different thing entirely. Propose the definition, run it through all nine, and say where it surprises you or where you have to revise to keep it from collapsing.
