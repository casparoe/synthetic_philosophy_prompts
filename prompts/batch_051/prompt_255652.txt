I sit on the Probabilistic Risk Assessment Committee of a national energy-safety regulator. We license advanced reactor designs whose novel components can fail in ways for which little or no directly relevant operating history exists. Licensees must submit a quantified risk model as part of their safety case, and our current guidance says only that they must use "a defensible prior" over unknown failure probabilities. It does not say what makes a prior defensible, and reviewers have been inconsistent.

The committee is now split, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo that will set a binding standard.

One faction wants our guidance to require the maximum-entropy prior. Where a licensee can justify only certain constraints — say, a failure rate bounded within some interval, or a mean failure rate from accelerated bench testing — the unique distribution that maximizes Shannon entropy subject to those constraints is, they argue, the one that assumes the least beyond the evidence. Any tighter or differently shaped prior imports information the licensee does not have. On this view, maximum entropy is not one allowable prior among others but the rationally mandated starting point, and it is what "defensible" should mean.

The other faction replies that the maximum-entropy prior is less neutral than it looks. Its shape changes depending on whether one takes the unknown to be a failure rate or a reliability, even when the same information is supplied; different reference measures yield different least-informative distributions. They deny that there is a uniquely rational prior here, and say that pretending otherwise gives false precision. They want the guidance instead to require licensees to report how the bottom-line risk metric changes across a prescribed ensemble of meaningfully different priors, and to defend decisions against the worst plausible case in that ensemble, with no prior privileged as canonical.

Draft the decision memo. It should recommend adopting the maximum-entropy standard, adopting the prior-ensemble standard, or adopting some third rule. Give a clear recommendation that our staff can turn into review criteria, with reasons each faction could actually respond to and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. The memo should also address what reviewers should require when a licensing decision ultimately needs a single risk value rather than a range, and it should be candid about the question underneath the dispute: whether there is a uniquely rational prior at all, or only priors that are more or less defensible as judgments.
