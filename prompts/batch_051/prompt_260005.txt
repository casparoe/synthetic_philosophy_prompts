I have settled into a standing rule for explaining apparently intelligent or designed-looking behavior. Whenever I encounter a system whose outputs look purposeful — a neural network producing fluent prose, an ant colony building a nest, a market setting prices, an organism solving a problem — I default to the competence-without-comprehension explanation: the competence is generated by a blind, uncomprehending process such as variation and selection, gradient descent, or trial and error, and no component of the system understands what the whole is doing. I admit genuine comprehension into my explanation only on positive evidence, never merely because the output looks clever. I adopted the rule after being persuaded by the Darwinian and Turingian inversions of reasoning, which suggest that design-like and intelligence-like results repeatedly arise from mechanisms that understand nothing. But I have begun to suspect that I have turned an antidote to the design intuition into a bias of my own, one that is in practice unfalsifiable and systematically unfair to cases where understanding does real explanatory work. Is this standing default defensible as an epistemic habit? When does it serve me well, and when does it fail? What would a better-calibrated version say about where the burden of proof should fall, and should the default differ across evolved organisms, trained models, and human institutions?
