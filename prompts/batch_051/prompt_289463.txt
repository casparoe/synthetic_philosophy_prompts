I am a retired atmospheric chemist who came to philosophy late, and I now convene a continuing-education discussion group at our community centre: fourteen to sixteen adults, a mix of scientific and non-scientific backgrounds, meeting for ninety minutes. I would like you to design a structured activity, with rules and materials, that makes the group feel the force of an idea in the ethics of risk imposition without consent. The idea is this: a risk that appears morally negligible when one assesses it as an outside planner — a small probability of serious harm distributed across many people — can abruptly stop looking negligible when one is asked to accept it as one of those people; and a favourable aggregate expected value does not by itself answer the question whether one party may impose the residual risk on another party without that party's consent. I want participants to run into the gap between the planner's perspective and the exposed person's perspective before the facilitator names it.

Please design the activity for a ninety-minute session that requires no equipment beyond printed cards and tables, and moves through at least three phases. A first phase might have small groups act as a permit board or safety committee, deciding on a set of proposals of the kind that arise in ordinary municipal life — a composting facility, a delivery service, a festival, a medical study, a school ventilation change, and the like. Each proposal should have explicit numerical risks, identifiable benefits, and a clear distribution of who gains and who is exposed; at least two proposals should place the benefits and the risks on different people, and at least one should place them on the same people, so the debrief can examine whether that symmetry matters. A second phase should redistribute the roles so that participants discover they are now among the exposed parties and must say what, if anything, would lead them to accept each proposal. A third phase should force the two perspectives into a constrained negotiation or deliberation, where the planners may not justify a decision solely by aggregate numbers and the exposed parties may not veto a proposal merely by saying they dislike it — both must give reasons of a specified kind.

Please supply complete rules, a timeline for the ninety minutes, the full text of the proposal and role cards, any decision criteria or lightweight scoring that keeps the deliberation from becoming diffuse, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the underlying philosophical points about consent, expected value, risk transfer, and the difference between accepting a risk for oneself and imposing it on another. I would also like your honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it: who will try to philosophise before the intended moment, where the numerical frame will threaten to swallow the normative question, and how a facilitator should respond without turning the session into a lecture. Finally, please be candid about which feature of the ethical problem the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, living with a risk over years rather than weighing it for twenty minutes — and what the facilitator will need to supply afterwards in discussion.
