I'm teaching Middlemarch and want to frame it through George Eliot's religious thought, specifically the Feuerbachian humanism she did so much to introduce to English readers: the idea that God is the projected sum of human excellences, that theology is disguised anthropology, and that the strongest religious feelings are really directed at the human lot and what we owe one another. Read the novel through that lens for me. Where does it dramatize the movement from a supernatural faith to a humanized ethic of sympathy — I'm thinking of Dorothea's evangelical ardor and her two marriages, Casaubon's desiccated scholarship as a way of mistaking dead tradition for living duty, Bulstrode's fall, and Farebrother's lukewarm but honest ministry — and where does it complicate or resist that reading? Does the novel end up endorsing a project of living well without a supernatural object, or is it more skeptical than that about whether human feeling can bear the weight that religion once did? Which scenes would you point a student to, and what does the fiction show that the philosophical statement of the view leaves out?
