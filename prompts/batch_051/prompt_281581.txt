I've been rebuilding a version of the following argument for years, and I still can't tell whether it is a reductio of computer-assisted proof or of my own picture of what a proof is.

(1) To know a theorem by proof is to survey the proof: to take in its structure and see why the theorem follows, not merely to accept that each line is valid. (2) In the computer-assisted proofs of the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture, no one has that kind of survey of the computational phase. The four-color proof depends on an exhaustive check of an unavoidable set of configurations, and the Kepler proof on the verification of a vast collection of numerical inequalities; the work is delegated to a machine, and in the formal verification of the Kepler proof the only part of the proof object a human can realistically take in is the proof-checking kernel plus a description of the search, while the millions of low-level steps remain opaque to any reader. (3) What one acquires from such a computation is justified trust in the reliability of hardware, software, and the people who built them. (4) Justified trust in an instrument or another party is empirical knowledge, not mathematical proof. (5) So the four-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture are not known by proof in the traditional sense; they are known, at best, the way one knows a reported fact. (6) But the surveyability criterion cuts far more widely. A large share of published mathematics consists of proofs no single person has surveyed as a whole; referees split the load, and every advanced result rests on earlier results almost no one re-checks. Applied consistently, the criterion makes most of mathematical knowledge a matter of testimony and social trust, with only a small core of genuinely proved theorems. (7) That is a strange and revisionary picture, and perhaps it is simply true.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the defense is that a computer proof can have a surveyable high-level architecture with delegated, mechanically checked cases, I want to know whether that distinction does real epistemological work or is special pleading for exactly the feature that (1) was meant to rule out.
