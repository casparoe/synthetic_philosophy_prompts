I've been reporting on a case where a county signed off on a renovation that later collapsed, and the family whose kid was hurt can't sue the county because of sovereign immunity — in the state where this happened you can only sue the government when a statute specifically allows it, and the waiver here didn't cover this kind of failure. What got me is that the same county is out there filing suits against contractors, fining homeowners for code violations, and enforcing leases as a landlord. So it can be a plaintiff, impose obligations, collect money, but when the tables are turned it talks as if the injury wasn't the sort of thing a court can even recognize. The county attorney was almost apologetic about it, like the law just didn't have a slot for the claim.

I want to write a piece on what a doctrine like that presupposes about whether a government can do wrong at all. I'm not after the boring legal mechanics of consent and waivers; I'm after the philosophy underneath. Does sovereign immunity only make sense if you think the state is not a moral agent — that individual officials can do wrong but the state itself can't, and the state is just a vessel for them? Or does it presuppose that the state can wrong people but has some special standing that makes being sued in its own courts somehow incoherent? Or is the doctrine philosophically empty — just a political shield for the treasury that doesn't commit you to any particular view of whether the state does wrong or can owe repair? Reconstruct the implicit view that would make the practice hang together, separate what that view strictly requires from what is just historical baggage, and say what would concretely have to change in how governments operate if we replaced it with the view that a government is a moral agent like a corporation — capable of wronging people and owing them repair.
