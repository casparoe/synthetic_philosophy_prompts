I've been trying to formalize the familiar post-war argument that a present nation cannot be guilty of its historical crimes, and I keep getting tangled in Jaspers's distinction among four kinds of guilt: criminal (culpability for one's own unlawful acts), political (the liability each citizen bears for what their state does), moral (responsibility for one's own chosen acts), and metaphysical (co-responsibility arising from standing by while wrongs were done that one could have resisted). Here is the cleanest version of the argument I can produce.

(1) To be guilty of a wrong, an agent must be culpable for it: the agent either did it, enabled it, or could have prevented it but failed to.
(2) A nation is not an agent with intentions and hands over and above its members; it is a changing collection of persons who happen to share institutions and territory.
(3) The persons who did the wrong, or who could have prevented it at the time, are now mostly dead; their successors either did not exist when it happened or could not have intervened.
(4) Therefore no member of the present nation satisfies (1), and the nation itself is not the kind of agent to which (1) applies.
(5) So a present nation cannot be guilty of its historical wrongs; talk of national guilt is either false or a misleading metaphor.

I can't tell whether this argument is valid but merely shows that criminal guilt does not transfer, or whether it commits the old trick of defining guilt in (1) as individual culpability and then using (5) to dismiss every other sense of the word. Tell me: is the argument valid as written? If not, identify exactly which step fails, on what reading of it, and state the corrected version of that step. Then say which of Jaspers's four kinds of guilt, if any, survive the corrected argument and which the argument genuinely rules out. Keep your answer under 450 words.
