I've been reconstructing the Thomson's lamp argument with a colleague, trying to put it in a form where each step can be checked, because I can't tell whether it proves too much. Here is the version we settled on. (1) An ordinary two-state lamp is off at t = 0. (2) It is switched at every time t_n = 1 − 1/2^n for n = 1, 2, 3, ... : on for n = 1, off for n = 2, and so on, alternating. (3) Every switch therefore occurs before t = 1. (4) At every instant, including t = 1, a lamp must be in some definite state: on or off. (5) Since all the switches have occurred by t = 1, the state at t = 1 is the state produced by the completed sequence of switches. (6) If the lamp is on at t = 1, then it must have been turned on by some switch and then left on by all later switches. (7) But there is no last switch, since every switch is followed by another. (8) The same reasoning shows it cannot be off at t = 1. (9) Therefore at t = 1 the lamp is neither on nor off, contradicting (4). So the supertask is impossible.

I have a nagging suspicion that the trouble is in step (5) or step (6), and that the right response is not that the lamp is impossible but that the instructions fail to determine a state at t = 1; but I want a precise diagnosis. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what is the correct thing to say about the lamp's state at t = 1, including how a discontinuous or arbitrary state could be consistent with the switching rule? In your answer, please separate the settled mathematical and logical facts about the sequence from the genuinely contested philosophical questions about whether such a supertask can be performed in any possible world, and do not present a contested view about supertasks as if it were the consensus.
