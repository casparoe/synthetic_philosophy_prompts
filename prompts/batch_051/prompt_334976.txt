I retired a few years back from a career in condensed matter physics, mostly soft matter and mesoscale modeling, and I've been reading around in philosophy of science as a retirement project. The reductionism/emergence debate has me stuck, and not because I can't follow the arguments. It's because the thing seems to run in place: it's been running since the 1920s British emergentists and their critics, the same two camps keep re-forming under new names, and neither side ever seems to land the kind of blow that would end a normal scientific dispute. What I'd like is a diagnosis of the deadlock itself, not a verdict on who's right.

Let me lay out how the two sides look to me, in case I'm already misreading them. The reductionist says the world is nothing over and above the fundamental physical stuff and the laws. Higher-level facts — about liquids, organisms, markets, minds — are fixed in principle by lower-level facts plus the relevant bridge principles. Higher-level talk is shorthand, or a compact way of tracking patterns that are ontologically parasitic. On this view the only respectable emergence is weak emergence: higher-level patterns that are unexpected or too complicated to derive in practice, but still derivable in principle. Strong emergence, with its new causal powers or downward causation, is either incoherent or just a name for a derivation nobody has managed yet.

The emergentist says that last move is exactly the cheat. They don't usually deny that the world is made of microphysical stuff. Their claim is that organization and history produce genuinely new properties, constraints, or causal powers — superconductivity, the rigidity of a crystal lattice, perhaps the content of a thought — that are not just in-principle consequences of microphysics, or at least cannot be expressed in the lower-level vocabulary without losing the thing you were trying to explain. Some want full downward causation; others only want the higher level to be explanatorily autonomous in a way that is not a confession of ignorance.

What bothers me is that both sides seem to agree on the microphysical basis and on the practical indispensability of higher-level descriptions. The fight is entirely over what to make of notions like in principle, nothing over and above, novel, and causal power. That makes me suspect it's partly verbal. But I also keep feeling there is a real disagreement buried in there, because the two camps seem to live in different epistemic worlds.

My own hypothesis, offered just as a hypothesis, is that reduction is doing at least three jobs at once: intertheoretic derivation, part-whole metaphysics, and explanatory completeness. The debate stalls because each side picks one of those as the real question and treats the others as beside the point. I've also seen people suggest the real disagreement is about modal force, or about whether higher-level properties have independent causal relevance — which would be a scientific question disguised as metaphysics.

So here's what I want: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement the strong-emergence fight actually is. Weak emergence feels settled, maybe wrongly — tell me if that's part of the problem. Is the dispute substantive, verbal, about which cases count as data, about standards of evidence, or several different disagreements under one label? Are the two sides answering different questions? Is there a hidden empirical or formal claim doing the work? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves part of the dispute, a formal result, a specific experiment, a change of question — or is the smart conclusion that the question is ill-posed and nobody should expect progress? I'd realy appreciate it if you'd write the answer in American English.
