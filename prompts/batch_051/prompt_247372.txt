In Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute (On Old Age), Cato is made to answer the objection that old age brings a failing memory. His reply is not that memory remains fully intact, but that whatever declines should be blamed on failure to exercise the mind rather than on old age itself: those who keep exercising their mental powers tend to retain them, while those who let them lie idle should not charge nature with what is really negligence. This is part of a larger strategy in the dialogue, in which much of what people attribute to old age is recast as a defect of character or habit rather than of the years themselves.

I want to know whether this argument survives the modern picture of normal cognitive aging. We now have good evidence of fairly general, gradual, age-related declines in processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory, beginning well before the kind of advanced old age Cato is defending, and present even in people who remain mentally active. Does this evidence refute the Ciceronian claim outright, or does it only force a sharper distinction between capacities that are largely preserved by use and capacities that decline regardless of use? What premise in Cicero's argument is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version of his defense of old age that remains defensible once that premise is adjusted?
