I have been unable to get a particular parallel out of my head, and I want it checked. In the theory of computation there is a fundamental distinction between a sequential process, in which operations are totally ordered in time, and a concurrent process, in which several operations can be underway at once. Most of linguistics, even where it does not say so explicitly, seems to assume something like the sequential model for language: structure is built along a linear string ordered in time, morphemes concatenate, and the objects syntax manipulates are ordered trees read off an ordered string. Spoken languages fit this picture quite naturally.

But sign languages sit uneasily with it. A single sign can carry several morphemes at the same moment — handshape, location, and movement bound into one form, with a facial or head movement layered over it — and a signer's two hands can in effect run two morphological processes in parallel. Part of the grammar of a signed sentence looks genuinely concurrent rather than sequential. My parallel, then, is this: the sequential/concurrent distinction in computation corresponds to a real distinction between spoken and signed grammar, and the definition of language inherited from spoken languages has a serial bias that sign languages expose and correct.

Is this a deep structural analogy or a loose one trading on the word parallel? If it is deep, what exactly would transfer from concurrency theory to sign-language grammar — composition rules for simultaneous processes, a notion of interference or race conditions, a distinction between safe and unsafe interleavings? If it is only superficial, then signed and spoken languages are different surface encodings of the same underlying linear syntax, and I want to know why that package is the right one. I would also appreciate at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false — my own suspicion is that grammatical simultaneity is disciplined in a way raw concurrency is not, and the analogy may collapse at exactly that point.
