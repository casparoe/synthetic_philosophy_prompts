The constitution of a federal republic, drafted after a civil war to accommodate the country's three recognized national communities, contains this article:

"Article 14. A people that has historically inhabited a defined territory, possesses a common language and culture, and has maintained its own institutions of self-government may, by a free and democratic referendum, determine its political future, up to and including secession."

A fourth community now petitions the constitutional court. Its roughly three hundred thousand members use a sign language as their primary language, share a common culture, and run their own schools, cultural institutions, and mutual-aid societies, but they are dispersed through the republic's cities and have never inhabited a defined territory. They do not ask to detach any land from the republic. Instead, they seek a referendum on non-territorial self-government: their own bodies would govern their schools, family-law disputes, and cultural institutions while they remain geographically inside the republic.

Does Article 14, read as its drafters would have read it, apply to this community? Does it apply if read as a general principle about the self-determination of peoples? If those two readings diverge, which should the court follow, and what does the choice reveal about whether self-determination is fundamentally a territorial idea?
