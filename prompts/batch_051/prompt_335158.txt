In my philosophy of biology seminar we have been working through the tautology objection to natural selection. I want a definition of fitness that does not collapse into realized reproductive success — which would make the claim that the fittest survive empty — but that also does not float free of how organisms actually fare. Please provide such a definition and apply it to the following six cases:

(a) two genetically identical annual plants in the same soil, one killed by a drought two weeks before flowering and the other producing a full seed set;
(b) a sterile worker ant that rears many fertile siblings;
(c) a mutation that doubles an insect's egg count but halves each egg's probability of surviving to reproductive age;
(d) a trait with higher expected reproductive success that is nonetheless lost in a small population by chance;
(e) a shore plant whose seed-dispersal traits suit today's coastline, even though that coastline will predictably be submerged within a generation; and
(f) a trait that is currently selectively neutral but would raise reproductive success if the local climate became wetter.

I am drawn to the propensity interpretation — fitness as a probabilistic disposition to leave descendants — but I want to know exactly what the disposition is a disposition to do, over which stage of the life cycle, and relative to which environment, and whether the drift case and the two environmental cases force it to collapse back into actual outcomes. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me which verdict is least secure.
