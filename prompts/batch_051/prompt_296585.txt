I'm a student in an animal ethics course, and we spent a week on freeganism, specifically the idea that consuming animal products recovered from waste is permissible because it doesn't add demand to the system that breeds, confines, and kills animals. I've been trying to turn that intuition into a general principle I call the inert-recovery principle: an act of using something that came from an animal is permissible just in case (a) the act does not, either by itself or in aggregate, make it more likely that any animal will be bred, confined, or killed, and (b) the act does not express the attitude that animals are resources. I added condition (b) to handle a case someone raised: eating a leftover steak in front of friends who are deciding whether to go vegan. The act seems causally harmless, but it plausibly communicates that animal bodies are still food, and that message may do its own harm.

The principle has been strained by byproducts of rescued animals. A local sanctuary keeps rescued hens whose unfertilized eggs they ignore; the eggs rot if not collected. Condition (a) seems to permit eating them, since the hens were rescued rather than bred for laying and no future breeding follows from eating the eggs. But condition (b) cuts both ways: eating them in front of volunteers might normalize treating hens as productive bodies, yet refusing to eat them seems to grant an unfertilized egg a moral status I do not actually believe it has. The same problem arises for a rescued sheep that a veterinarian says must be sheared for its health; using the wool feels like a pure surplus, but it also feels like the animal remains a standing source of goods.

I would like an honest assessment of the principle, not a defense of it. Please reconstruct it in its strongest form, say whether it or something close already exists under a name I should know, and identify where it breaks or stops being distinctive. In particular, I want to know whether condition (b) does independent moral work or collapses into condition (a), and whether the principle can distinguish the rescued-hen eggs from the leftover steak without leaning on an unstated premise about the difference between waste and surplus.
