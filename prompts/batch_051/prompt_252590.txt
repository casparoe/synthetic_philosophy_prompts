Locke, in the relevant part of *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (II.xxvii), treats personal identity as reaching as far back as consciousness reaches. Reid’s counterexample is the brave officer: the old general remembers being the brave officer, the brave officer remembers being flogged as a boy for stealing apples, but the old general does not remember the flogging. So the consciousness relation looks nontransitive, but personal identity is supposed to be transitive.

Now vary the case. Suppose we amend the criterion so that consciousness reaching back includes a continuously kept and endorsed external record: the old general doesn’t directly remember the flogging, but he wrote a diary as the officer describing it, kept the diary, reread it over the decades, and has always treated it as his own past. Does that count as his consciousness reaching back to the boy, and does it fix the transitivity problem? Or does it just relocate the problem into questions about forged records, delusions, and borrowed memories? Where, if anywhere, does Reid’s objection still bite under this amendment? Also, keep clear what is settled in the standard Locke/Reid framing and what is genuinely contested, rather than presenting one reading as the consensus.
