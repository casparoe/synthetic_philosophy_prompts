I run the quarterly onboarding for new data scientists and product people at a food-delivery app, and a few weeks ago a model our team trusted blew up in our face. The model was supposed to learn what couriers "really want" from their accept/decline behavior — basically inverse reinforcement learning over trip offers — and then the dispatcher started optimizing for those inferred preferences. Couriers were mostly choosing the least-bad option under constraints (distance from home, time of day, how surge pricing interacts with tips), and the model read that as "prefers short trips near downtown" when it was mostly constraints. We had a small walkout in two markets. I now have to run a 90-minute session for this quarter's new starters (around 18 people, mostly ML engineers but a few product managers and one designer) and I dont want to give them another slide deck about revealed preferences being tricky. I want a game or structured activity where they actually get burned by the problem themselves before the debrief names it.

Design the activity. The core idea I want them to feel is that the same observed behavior is consistent with a bunch of different underlying reward functions or preferences, and choices can only tell you what someone did under their constraints, not what they wanted or valued. The rules, roles, and incentives should force that discovery to happen in the room — for example, some participants get a secret scoring rule and others have to infer it from their choices, and at least one round should be engineered so two different hidden rules are equally consistent with everything the inferrers saw and only diverge on a later choice. No lecture up front, and dont say "inverse reinforcement learning" or "underdetermination" until after they've hit the problem themselves.

Give me: the setup and materials, the exact roles, rules, and scoring, a running timeline for the 90 minutes, the rounds or twists that make the ambiguity show up rather than being asserted, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this — participants trying to game it, demonstrators getting sloppy, a team getting confident too early — and how I should handle each. And be honest about one or two features of real preference inference the game cant reproduce and that I'll have to supply in the debrief because the game makes them look simpler or different than they are. I'd like them to leave suspicious of any future dashboard that claims to have learned "what users want" from behavior alone.
