I teach an undergraduate jurisprudence course and am preparing a unit on the Hart–Fuller debate. Instead of lecturing through the standard positions, I want to give students a sorting instrument: a decision tree or “which position do you actually hold?” quiz that starts from their reactions to one concrete case and ends by assigning each of them to a named legal theory. The case should be the post-war prosecution of a German woman who, during the Nazi period, denounced her husband to the authorities for remarks against Hitler; after the war, a court held her guilty of unlawfully depriving him of liberty under the very Nazi-era statute she had relied on, reasoning that the statute was so contrary to sound conscience that it was not law at all. I want the instrument to be anchored in a specific passage from H. L. A. Hart’s essay “Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals” (1958), where Hart discusses this case. To keep the prompt self-contained, here is a paraphrase of the passage I have in mind: Hart argues that the court’s way of reaching its result is both analytically confused and morally dangerous, because it blurs the distinction between what the law is and what the law ought to be. If a statute is iniquitous, we can condemn it morally and still say it was law; indeed, that combination is what allows us to speak honestly about the moral choice of whether to obey it, whether to punish those who relied on it, and whether to use retroactive legislation. Denying that the iniquitous statute was law, Hart suggests, conceals those choices behind a pretense that the statute never existed.

The instrument should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of labels. I want it to run through four diagnostic questions, each phrased as a concrete case or choice that students can answer without knowing the theories, and each splitting the positions on a distinction that actually matters: (1) Was the Nazi statute law at the time, and what makes it such? (2) If the post-war court should have convicted the woman, was that because the statute was not law, or because a later law justly reached back? (3) Is a rule’s formal enactment by a legal system sufficient for legality, or does something about its content or its internal procedure matter? (4) Is the defect of Nazi law primarily that its content was wicked, or that it was not genuinely law-like in the way law must be to guide conduct? The leaves should include at least Austinian command positivism, Kelsenian grundnorm positivism, Hartian inclusive legal positivism, Razian exclusive legal positivism, Radbruch’s post-war natural law, and Fuller’s procedural natural law. For each leaf, give one sentence stating the most awkward commitment the position has to accept. Also flag any pairs of leaves that the tree cannot separate, and any answer patterns that leave a student between positions. The point is to sort students by what they say about the case, not by which label they find attractive, so no node should simply ask whether they are a positivist or a natural lawyer.
