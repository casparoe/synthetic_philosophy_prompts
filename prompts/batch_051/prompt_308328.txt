So there's a running argument on my team about whether to build what a user literally asks for or what they'd ask for if they actually understood our system's constraints and what it could do. Most of us think the second answer is right most of the time — a user files a ticket asking for a CSV export button, but what they actually need is a scheduled report; we call it the X-Y problem, and we treat the literal request as noisy evidence about some cleaner underlying job the person is trying to get done. Part of the job is reverse-engineering that cleaner need out of messy, sometimes contradictory tickets.

Then I was reading about Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition, and it sounded like the same move but at civilization scale. As I understand it, the idea is that you shouldn't build an AI that does what people currently say they want, because actual stated preferences are full of ignorance, bias, impulsiveness, and plain inconsistency. Instead you aim the AI at the preferences people would have if they were much better informed, thought a lot faster, were more like the people they'd want to be, and had hashed things out together for long enough. You treat actual preferences as noisy data about an idealized object, and you hope that idealized object is coherent enough to act on. That feels exactly like what good requirements discovery is supposed to do: the stated request is the raw input, and the "real requirement" is the cleaned-up, extrapolated version.

But I cant tell whether this is a deep structural parallel or me pattern-matching on the phrase "what they really want." The obvious break is that in software there's an external check: we eventually see whether the feature made the work faster or cut errors, and when stakeholders disagree we can escalate to some business outcome or metric to break the tie. CEV doesn't seem to have that external ground truth — the idealized preferences are the ground truth, or they're supposed to be, and I don't see what the tiebreaker is if different idealizations pull in different directions. So: is requirements elicitation actually doing the same kind of preference idealization CEV is talking about? If the analogy is deep, what would have to be true on the software side for CEV-style claims to transfer — especially the claim that the extrapolated thing converges to something coherent across lots of different people? And where does the analogy break worst; what does it predict about reconciling contradictory feature requests that turns out false in practice?
