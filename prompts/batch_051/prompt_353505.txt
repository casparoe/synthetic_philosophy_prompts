I've been arguing with friends about moral sainthood — the ideal of a person whose every choice, plan, and disposition is aimed at maximizing moral goodness — and whether that ideal is really a rational one or a quietly impoverished one. The skeptical thought is familiar: single-mindedly optimizing moral value crowds out non-moral excellences such as wit, aesthetic achievement, idle curiosity, and partial attachments like friendship when they compete with impartial duty. Lately, though, I've been struck by how much the moral saint resembles a greedy algorithm. A greedy algorithm takes the locally optimal step at each iteration, the move that best improves the objective function right now, and exactly this can trap it in a local optimum on a non-convex landscape. Techniques such as simulated annealing and the exploration/exploitation trade-off in reinforcement learning exist because sometimes you must accept locally worse moves to reach a globally better state. Translated into ethics: a life that always takes the most morally valuable available action looks like the greedy policy, and the non-moral goods the saint forgoes look like the locally worse moves needed to reach a better overall life. On this reading, the standard anti-sainthood argument is a special case of a general truth about local optimization under a single scalar objective.

I can't tell whether this is deep or just pattern-matching. The analogy needs moral value to be a scalar, additive quantity, needs a life to have something like a fixed non-convex objective landscape, and needs moral agency to be a form of optimization — none of which I accept without reservation; a deontologist or virtue ethicist would reject the setup outright, and even a consequentialist might say the right comparison is to satisficing or constrained optimization, not greedy search. I want a careful verdict on whether there is a real structural commonality such that results from optimization theory — local optima, exploration bonuses, the value of accepting short-term losses — transfer to the ethics of moral sainthood. Please respond as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The list should collectively deliver this verdict: the strongest version of the mapping, where it holds, where it breaks, and at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false on the ethics side.
