I am developing a document for a course on the structure of value. Write the joint statement that two philosophers should produce after an adversarial collaboration on whether human values are many or one thing in disguise. One party, the value monist, holds that there is exactly one irreducibly distinct final value, and that the apparent plurality of human values consists in specifications, components, or context-bound manifestations of that single value. The other party, the value pluralist, holds that there are at least two irreducibly distinct final values, and that available attempts to reduce the many to one either relabel the many or systematically misrepresent what people value for its own sake. The document must define the positions by the claims they accept, not by historical labels, and each position should be presented in its strongest defensible form.

Throughout the document, keep three questions distinct: (a) how many final values there are; (b) how many properties make things finally valuable; and (c) how many distinct fitting evaluative attitudes there are. No claim should be allowed to do duty for a different one of these questions than the one it actually answers.

The document should have four parts. First, a jointly signed, numbered list of agreements. This must include working definitions of final value, derivative value, apparent value, reduction, irreducibility, and incommensurability, stated without prejudging the outcome. It must also include at least three adequacy conditions on any proposed monist reduction, such as conditions ruling out mere relabeling, requiring that a purported master value explain rather than postdict patterns of choice and regret, and requiring that a reduction display how the appearance of many values arises and why that appearance would be systematically misleading. Second, a disagreements table with four columns: the exact claim at issue in neutral wording; the monist's preferred formulation of that claim; the pluralist's preferred formulation; and what each side says the other must show in order to win that row. Include at least four rows, covering at least the following matters: the number of irreducible final values; whether a shared source without a common currency counts as a reduction; whether context-dependent trade-off ratios favor pluralism; and the burden of proof each side bears.

Third, a cruxes section identifying at most five sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, record what each side predicts about it, and state what pattern of outcome would move one side and what would move both. Somewhere in the document, the following distinctions and cases must be worked through: the distinction between two values sharing a common source and their being exchangeable on a common currency; the distinction between a consideration being outweighed and its being silenced, that is, made irrelevant in a context rather than traded against a stronger consideration; the problem of a retrospective covering value that can explain any choice after the fact but predicts little in advance, and whether such a value counts as a genuine monist reduction; and a case of apparent novel value recognition, in which a person seems to come to value something for its own sake in a way not expressible as a recombination of values they already recognized.

Fourth, a closing note distinguishing the disagreements that are merely verbal from those that are substantive, including any substantive disagreement that currently cannot be settled by the cruxes. The entire document should use wording each side would accept when describing its own view and the other's, should not adjudicate the dispute, and should be a text both parties could put their names to.
