I have spent the past few months helping a small grant-making body compare two proposals: one would prevent a small number of episodes of extreme, fully lucid suffering, and the other would prevent an astronomically large number of mild, fleeting, barely conscious discomforts. Preparing that comparison has forced me to make explicit a theory of value I had long used without stating it. I am drawn to a multiplicative account: the value of an experience is the product of its degree of consciousness and its hedonic valence, how good or bad it feels, and the value of a world is the sum of those products across experiences, subjects, and durations. The theory has systematic virtues I do not want to surrender lightly. It is continuous, it gives a determinate answer to every comparison, and it avoids drawing arbitrary thresholds between supposedly serious and supposedly trivial states.

But the comparison has also surfaced a conviction that contradicts the arithmetic. On the model, a discomfort with one-hundredth the intensity and one-hundredth the valence counts for one ten-thousandth as much, so ten thousand such episodes must be exactly as bad as one severe episode, and a sufficiently large collection of them must be judged worse. Yet when I hold the two proposals side by side, I find no number of barely conscious discomforts that I would treat as more important to prevent than one episode of the worst agony, and no number of faint contentments that would justify accepting such an episode. The multiplicative view cannot say this, because it treats intensity and valence as commensurable dimensions that trade against one another at a fixed rate.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to understand what each resolution would require. Is the intuition a debunkable artefact of vivid case thinking, scope insensitivity, or a failure to imagine very large numbers? Or is it tracking a real structural feature of value, such as lexical dominance, a discontinuity, or a nonlinearity the product model cannot express? Is there a reading on which both survive, for example one in which the two dimensions do not combine as independent factors at the extremes, or would that merely reintroduce the arbitrary thresholds the theory was designed to avoid? Please present both sides at their strongest and explain what would justify giving up the intuition, amending the theory, or regarding the two as compatible. Please write your answer in British English.
