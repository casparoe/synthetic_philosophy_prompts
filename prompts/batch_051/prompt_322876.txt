I would like you to translate a philosophical debate into the vocabulary I actually use, and then tell me what fails to survive the translation.

The debate is the problem of logical omniscience. In epistemic logic, knowledge is usually modeled with an operator K governed by something like: if an agent knows φ, then it knows every logical consequence of φ, and it knows every logical truth. The familiar objection is that this makes the modeled agent logically omniscient — it knows all of mathematics and every consequence of anything it knows, which no finite reasoner could. The standard repairs are, roughly, these three: evaluate knowledge over "impossible worlds" where classical constraints can lapse; add an "awareness" function so that consequences count as known only when the agent explicitly entertains the relevant sentences; or replace the semantic story with a syntactic or algorithmic account, in which knowledge is whatever a bounded procedure can actually derive.

Please restate, one by one, the closure idealization, the objection to it, and each of the three repairs in the vocabulary of static analysis and formal methods — for example, soundness versus completeness of analyzers, decidable fragments, abstraction, and the distinction between what a type system accepts and what it can prove about a program. Then give me the residue report: which pieces translate cleanly, which pieces you had to distort, and which pieces have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. I also want a verdict: is the whole dispute really about choosing a tractable approximation to an ideal reasoner, the way a static analyzer approximates an undecidable program property, or are the philosophers pointing at something my vocabulary cannot express — a first-person epistemic state, perhaps — and is that inexpressibility a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that their claims were never fully precise?
