A colleague of mine has been pressing the old saying that music is a language of the emotions, and she uses it to support a strong claim. She argues that when a passage of music is described as sad, the sadness is genuinely in the music itself — carried by its contour, tempo, harmony and dynamics — in roughly the way the meaning of a spoken sentence is carried by the words rather than projected onto them by a listener. On her view, the fact that people from different backgrounds can largely agree that a piece sounds sad rather than cheerful is evidence that music possesses something akin to a semantics of feeling. I suspect the language analogy is quietly doing more work than she acknowledges. Could you audit how far it holds? Please set out the correspondence table first: what is meant to correspond in music to vocabulary, grammar, compositionality, speaker, content, and the distinction between what is said and how it is said. Then identify where the mapping breaks and separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from those that are merely superficial. In particular, which features of language does the analogy silently import that music lacks, and do those absences undermine the claim that sadness can be a property of the sound itself? Finally, if music is not best understood as a language of the emotions, is there a better analogy for what is happening when a passage is sad, and how much of her conclusion would that substitute analogy still support?
