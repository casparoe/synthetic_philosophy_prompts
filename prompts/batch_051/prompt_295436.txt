I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether prioritarianism fully captures what matters about equality, and I left less settled than I expected. I am attracted to prioritarianism because it has real theoretical virtues: it gives extra weight to benefits to the worse off, yet it locates the reason for that extra weight in the absolute level of a person's well-being rather than in any relation between persons. That allows it to explain why we should prioritize the badly off without implying that there could ever be anything good about leveling down — making the better off worse off while helping no one. The view is clean: the betterness relation can be represented as the sum of an increasing concave function of individual well-being, with no separate term for inequality itself.

Still, I cannot shake the intuition that a pure gap can matter. Compare two societies in which the worst off are equally badly off; in one, the best off are only moderately well off, and in the other, the best off are enormously well off. Stipulate that the larger gap in the second society has no downstream effect on anyone's absolute well-being — no envy, no domination, no positional competition, no effect on self-respect, no informational significance. Prioritarianism must say that, in every respect, the two societies are equally good. I find myself resisting that verdict: I want to say the first society is better in at least one respect, and that the relation of inequality itself is objectionable independently of any effect on how well anyone's life goes.

My friend kept insisting that this residual thought is just egalitarianism in disguise, and that the leveling-down objection exposes it: if the smaller gap is better for no one, then preferring it is a preference for no one's good, exactly the sort of thing the objection rules out. I suspect the residual thought may instead be deontic rather than telic — a claim about which distributions are consistent with treating people as equals, not a claim about the betterness relation. But I am not sure that move works either.

I want a negotiated verdict rather than a declaration of victory. Present the strongest case for debunking my intuition — as status-quo bias, a scope error, or an aesthetic preference for symmetry dressed up as morality — and the strongest case that prioritarianism must be amended or abandoned to make room for a non-instrumental objection to inequality. Is there a reading on which both survive, for example one in which prioritarianism is the full story about betterness while a separate fairness constraint does non-teleological work? Say what would justify each move, and tell me which you would in the end give up.
