ok so in my value theory class we've been doing population ethics, and the prof gave us three tests for whether an outcome counts as an overall improvement in welfare: (1) total wellbeing goes up, (2) average wellbeing doesn't drop, and (3) nobody who already exists ends up worse off. Here's the case I can't settle. A couple with two kids has enough resources and attention for a third. The two existing kids are at 8/10 wellbeing, the parents are also at 8/10, and a third child would be at 7/10 — happy, just slightly below everyone else because theres less money and attention to go around. The existing kids and parents stay exactly where they are, and the third kid is clearly glad to exist. Total wellbeing goes from 32 to 39, so the total test says definite improvement. But average wellbeing goes from 8 to 7.8, so the average test says the family is worse off. So two of the three tests say adding the third child makes things better, and one says it makes things worse. Is adding the third child actually an improvement, or does that drop in average show that just summing wellbeing misses something? Is the average condition doing real work in the definition, or should it be dropped? And does your answer shift if we scale the same numbers up to a city (a million people at 8, then a big wave of newcomers at 7) or all the way to a whole species — is a bigger population ever better when it lowers the average, even though no one is made worse off and every new life is worth living? I'm trying to figure out whether bigger is basically better as long as no one gets hurt, or whether the lower average should flip it.
