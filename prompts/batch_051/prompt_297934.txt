Commentators on environmental and common-property problems often reach for game theory and describe the tragedy of the commons as an N-person Prisoner's Dilemma. On this gloss, each herder has a dominant strategy of adding another animal to the pasture, because the herder captures the full private benefit while the cost of degradation is spread over all users; everyone defects into overgrazing even though all would prefer the outcome under mutual restraint. The analogy is then made to carry a practical conclusion: common-pool resources are doomed under individually rational choice and therefore require either privatization or imposed regulation from outside. I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Give the correspondence table first: what plays the role of a player, cooperation, defection, temptation, and punishment in the pasture case. Then identify the places where the mapping is strained, for instance whether adding another animal really is a dominant strategy at every level of stocking, whether a many-user resource with continuous choices is best treated as a one-shot, anonymous, two-strategy game, and which assumptions about open access, the absence of communication and institutions, and the herders' preferences get quietly imported along the way. Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not, and offer a verdict on whether the conclusion that individual rationality leads inevitably to collective ruin survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy would be less misleading.
