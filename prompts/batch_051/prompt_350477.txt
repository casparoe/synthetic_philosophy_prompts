I've been trying to think through something about word embeddings—the way people represent words as points in a high-dimensional space and then talk about that space as if it were a map of meaning itself. I want to write a short essay arguing that this is a case where a graphing convenience has quietly hardened into a theory, and I'd be grateful for comments on the idea before I write it.

The core of the essay would be that an embedding gives you a record of which words show up in the same kinds of stretches of text, and the distances in the space initially encode just that—interchangeability in context, not the kind of likeness people mean when they say two words share a meaning. I'd point to things like antonyms sitting closer together than many unrelated words because 'hot' and 'cold' occur in the same contexts, and to the familiar analogy arithmetic, king minus man plus woman landing near queen, as something that impresses us only because we've already understood it semantically before we ask the geometry for anything. I'd also lean on a quirk I keep returning to: if you walk in a straight line between two unrelated words, you pass through points that don't correspond to any meaning at all, and yet the geometry treats that trajectory as though it were a path through conceptual space. My suspicion is that the tidy visuals—gender directions, plural directions, clean clusters—are partly an artifact of projecting a few hundred dimensions down to the two or three we can look at, and partly our habit of telling region-and-direction stories about any continuous space.

What I'd like to know is what you would flag if you were commenting on this as an essay idea. Is the line I'm drawing between co-occurrence and meaning drawn too sharply, or is that distinction itself the thing that needs defending rather than being assumed? Is the antonym point actually an objection, or does it just show that the distance measure is tracking something broader than similarity, which I've then mislabeled as a failure? Would the king-queen example read as an outdated strawman, and should the critique instead be built around what the newer, context-sensitive kinds of embeddings do and don't fix? And is the honest conclusion that the geometry is real but partial—a genuine structure for some relations and a projection for others—rather than the stronger claim I'm currently leaning toward?
