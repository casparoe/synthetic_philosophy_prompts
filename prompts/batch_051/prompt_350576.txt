I'm a retired geologist, and I've gotten myself into Locke on personal identity. I keep loosing track of what I should give up when I think through Reid's brave officer, so I'd like someone to walk me through the costs.

Here's what attracts me to the memory criterion. It doesn't posit any immaterial thing. It makes the question whether someone is the same person an empirical question about what they can recall. And it captures why I care specially about my own past but not about the past of some stranger who happens to share my atoms. I like definitions you can operationalize, and memory at least points at something you could in principle test.

Now the brave officer. A boy is flogged at school. Years later he's a young officer who remembers the flogging. Decades after that he's an old general who remembers a brave exploit as a young officer, but has entirely lost the memory of the flogging. By the memory criterion, officer equals boy, because he remembers; and general equals officer, because he remembers; but general doesn't equal boy, because he doesn't remember. But identity is transitive: if A equals B and B equals C, then A equals C. My whole training says a relation that isn't transitive can't be an identity. So something has to go.

I'm not asking who's right, Locke or Reid. I want to know what each surrender costs. What exactly do I lose if I keep the memory criterion and accept that personal identity isn't transitive? Could personal identity be a similarity relation rather than an identity, and is that even intelligible? What do I lose if I keep transitivity and amend the criterion to overlapping chains of memory, and does the amended view still do the work that attracted me in the first place, or have I quietly smuggled in a further fact? Is there a reading on which the brave officer isn't a counterexample at all but just shows Locke should have said continuity, not direct memory?

I'd also like you to say how confident you are in what you claim, and to single out the point you're least sure of.
