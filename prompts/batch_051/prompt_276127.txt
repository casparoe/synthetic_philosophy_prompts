I run an undergraduate philosophy club of about eighteen students; we meet for ninety minutes, and after several weeks on decision theory the members are comfortable with simple payoff structures and with Newcomb's problem. A few weeks ago the topic stopped being academic for me: I had to decide whether to keep a promise to a colleague who had already moved abroad and would never learn whether I kept it, and I noticed I was reasoning not about what my choice would cause but about what I expected her to have done in my place. I want a single session whose real subject is that kind of reasoning: cooperating, even trading, with someone whose choices you cannot causally influence in any way, because each of you does better if each correctly anticipates that the other will choose the same way. I have seen this discussed under the name acausal trade, and the nearby idea of superrational cooperation, but I do not want to begin from the labels, and the students should not hear the name until the end.

The difficulty is that on the page the idea sounds like a logical trick, and my students shrug it off as empty, because nothing one person does reaches the other. I want them to feel its pull before they are allowed to form an opinion. So I am asking you to design a classroom activity for this club: a real game they play under real though small stakes, in which doing well depends on making exactly this kind of move toward someone they will never meet and cannot affect, and in which they notice what they have done only afterwards. It should be a game, not a lecture. Please build in a physically obvious absence of any causal channel, for instance separate rooms, sealed envelopes, or asynchronous turns, while still making mutual cooperation pay.

The design must fit ninety minutes including the debrief, work for about eighteen students, and use nothing beyond index cards, envelopes, and slips of paper or poker chips; the stakes can be points or a small prize rather than money. Please give me the complete design: the setup and materials; rules that a first-year can follow in under two minutes; the precise decisions the players make and the order in which they make them; a timeline for the session; and a structured debrief that walks them back through what they did before it names the idea. The debrief should connect what happened in the game to the familiar set-up in which two agents each hold something the other wants and only a courier passes between them with no communication, to Newcomb-like cases where a choice is evidence about a partner one cannot influence, and to the standing objection that the player is merely betting on resemblance to himself rather than trading with a real person. I also want an honest note on which features of the real idea the game cannot reproduce, especially that in the philosophical discussions the cooperating agents usually share a decision procedure or an implementation while my students do not, and on the failure modes I should prepare for, such as a student who defects and then insists that defection was the rational play. Please write all of this as a long email to me: continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
