This is maybe a weird one but it keeps coming up. I'm on the board of a small housing co-op, four buildings, about fifty units, and our bylaws are a mess. They were writen in the 80s and don't cover half the situations we actually hit. Last month the president resigned and the vice president just left with no notice, and the bylaws say the board fills vacancies but at that point there were only two board members left and you need three for a quorum, so we were kind of in a loop. It got me reading about the Roman thing where there's an interregnum (sorry if I have the name wrong) and about the general problem of what happens when the rules run out, and I realized we have no way of thinking about this. We've got an election coming up and the same thing could happen again.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run, not a theory. Like a checklist for the board: here's how to decide who, if anyone, is allowed to act when the bylaws are silent, contradictory, or say something must happen but the procedure for doing it is impossible. It has to be usable by five people with no legal training, in a two-hour meeting, maybe a few times a year, and a lawyer is too expensive for most of it. Whatever we decide has to pass a board vote and might get challenged by angry members at the next general meeting, so the procedure has to give us something we can defend, not just something we feel good about. The stakes: we're talking about a couple hundred thousand dollars in reserve funds, leases, and sometimes an emergency repair where a pipe is actively leaking into someone's kitchen.

I need it to have: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure says "stop, you can't do this," and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a judgment call rather than a rule. Then run it on three of our actual situations: the no-quorum board-filling problem above; an emergency repair where the only person allowed to sign checks is in the hospital and the rules have no backup signer; and a case where two different parts of the bylaws point opposite ways about whether non-resident owners can vote. Please make one of the worked examples come out with an answer we won't like, because if the procedure just tells us we're allowed to do what we wanted to do anyway I won't trust it.

Also, at the end, say how confident you are in the procedure overall and what the single point is that you're least sure about.
