I've been roped onto a district planning committee and there's a policy tangle I cant get out of my head. Last year we cut morning recess to squeeze in more "instructional minutes," but this year we bought a math app where kids earn badges and climb a leaderboard, and the principal keeps calling it "learning through play." At the same time, one of our elementary schools is piloting a forest kindergarten where the teachers are given an almost opposite set of instructions: don't structure the activities, don't assess anything, don't redirect the kids toward something "educational," and don't ask them what they learned. The grant narrative for that pilot keeps saying the point of play is that it is not for anything. Nobody on the committee sees a conflict, and I can't figure out if that means I'm confused or they are. Some members say play is fine whenever it eventually produces learning, another says play is most valuable exactly when it has no goal, and a third says it all depends what we mean by "play," at which point the meeting ended.

What I want is a careful account of what each of these three practices—cutting recess, dressing up math review as a game with points and badges, and the deliberately unassessed forest kindergarten—actually presupposes about what play is and why it matters. Is the district committed to an inconsistent view, or is there a defensible position that underwrites all three? Where are these policies genuinely forced by a theory of play, and where are they just compatible with several theories? And if we really adopted the view that play and leisure are valuable for their own sake, not mainly as tools for learning or health or "engagement," which of these practices would have to change, and which could be kept but for different reasons? I'd also like some way of telling whether the argument in our meetings is about facts, about values, or just about the word "play" stretching too far.
