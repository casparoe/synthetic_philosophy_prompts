You are a professional metaphilosopher — someone who works on whether and how philosophy makes progress. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so think of me as a smart layperson who reasons in terms of specs, requirements, and acceptance criteria. My hunch is that the whole does-philosophy-make-progress debate never gets anywhere because everyone has a different defintion of progress and they just talk past each other. I don't want a yes/no answer, and I don't want a literature survey. What I want is the requirements document: what would an adequate theory or metric of philosophical progress have to get right before we can even apply it to the actual history of philosophy?

Give me the list of requirements. For each one, say what it is and why it earns its place. The list has to handle some fairly obvious cases (the shift from Aristotelian term logic to modern formal logic; the fact that lots of people worked on the Gettier problem without universal agreement on the end result; the fact that some big problems just persist for centuries) and some trickier stuff, like the distinction between progress in methods, progress in settled answers, and progress in asking better questions. There are also distinctions it shouldn't collapse: consensus vs. truth, dissolving a problem vs. solving it, a question getting answered vs. the discipline just moving on, progress within a philosophical tradition vs. progress across traditions. And it can't simply ignore the fact that whole fields have split off from philosophy and then run successfully, like physics — either that counts as philosophical progress or the theory has to say why not.

Then turn the requirements back on themselves. Which requirements are probably non-negotiable, and which ones are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a neutral constraint? Is any subset of the list jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, what does that itself tell us? Pick the single requirement that does the most work eliminating popular pictures of philosophical progress, and say why. Don't spend too much time scoring named theories against the list — the criteria are where the thinking is. A table for the requirements is fine. Start by brainstorming a few candidate requirements and then trim them hard; I'd rather have ten sharp requirements than thirty vague ones.
