I'm on the membership committee of a small land co-op, and I work remote as a software engineer, which is how I got pulled into this. Our founding covenant from the 1970s says: "Members shall be self-sufficient in their material needs and shall not depend upon external systems for their livelihood." The clause was put in when the founders were mostly worried about the electricity grid, supermarkets, banks, trucking—big centralized systems you couldn't do much about.

One member, Elena, has a solar-powered homestead, grows most of her own food, and makes her cash selling saved seeds online. But the store is hosted on a cloud platform, payments run through Stripe, customers mostly find her through a search engine, and she files her taxes through some web app. She says shes not actually dependent on any of it because it's all swappable: if Stripe dies she'll use another processor, if the host goes under she'll move the store, and if the internet itself went down she'd still eat. Her claim is that her dependence is optional in a way that buying food from a supermarket isn't.

Her case doesn't fit the examples the rule was written for, and I keep going back and forth. I've heard people read the clause by the plain words, by the purpose, and by what the founders would have said if they'd been asked about the internet. I want to know what the clause actually requires here: is she in breach on the words? Does the purpose even reach her? What would the founders have said, if that's the right test, and which approach should win? Also, is the real problem that the clause assumed external systems are monolithic and inescapable, while modular cloud services are neither—and if so, what should "self-sufficiency" mean for the rule to stay coherent without just counting everyone out? I care less about the practical outcome than about what the rule could even mean now.
