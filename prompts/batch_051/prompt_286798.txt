I sit on the professional-conduct committee of a small learned society, and a case has just reached us that I want to think through as a philosopher of language rather than merely as a committee member. Our society's code contains a rule whose exact wording is this: "Members shall not knowingly assert any false statement in the course of the Society's business." The rule was drafted many years ago, and I suspect its authors had in mind the straightforward case of a member standing up at a meeting and relating something false about a colleague's record or the Society's accounts.

The case before us is this. At a meeting of the Society's Council, the members were deciding whether to elect Dr. S. to Fellowship at a time when the stated criterion was a substantial and continuing record of published research. A senior member, Dr. M., who opposed the election, knew two things: that Dr. S. has a strong publication record in other venues, and that Dr. S. has never published in the Society's own journal. Knowing that her colleagues would hear the fact as damaging, she said: "For the record, Dr. S. has never published in the Society's journal." The sentence is true. But the other members, reasoning that she would not have volunteered a fact irrelevant to the matter before them, took her to have conveyed that Dr. S.'s record is too thin to merit election—a proposition Dr. M. knew to be false and intended them to accept. In Grice's terms, what she said was true, and what she conversationally implicated was false and part of what she meant.

I would like a careful view of whether Dr. M. has violated the rule. Does the word "assert" reach only what is said, or does it reach what a speaker means, implicatures included? Please work out what each of four approaches yields—the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the original drafters would have said had this case been put to them, and what a reasonable member reading the rule today would take it to forbid—and then say which approach ought to govern and why. If you think the rule should be amended, propose a wording that would catch Dr. M.'s conduct without making members liable for every unintended inference a hearer might draw, and explain whether Grice's distinction between saying and implicating can survive the amendment, or whether the distinction is precisely what created the loophole.
