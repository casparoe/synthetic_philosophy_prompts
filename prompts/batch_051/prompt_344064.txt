I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on whether disputes in metaphysics that run for decades are genuine impasses or failures of translation between rival frameworks. For that feature I want to reconstruct, as a document rather than a retrospective summary, an adversarial collaboration between a committed counterpart theorist and a committed defender of transworld identity about de re modal attribution. By counterpart theory I mean the Lewisian view on which possible worlds are concrete and pairwise disjoint, no individual exists in more than one world, and a de re modal claim — for example, that a particular losing candidate could have won the election — is made true by a counterpart of that individual in another world, one that resembles the original in contextually selected respects and represents the original de re. By transworld identity I mean the view that possible worlds are total ways things could have been, that one and the same individual features in more than one of those ways, and that de re modal predication is assessed by tracking that single individual across worlds rather than by trading in a surrogate.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith effort to state, without fudging, exactly where they part ways. Do not adjudicate, and do not manufacture disagreement where their views already coincide. Structure your response as follows.

First, provide a numbered agreed-claims section. The claims should be substantive rather than vacuous: they should state what any semantics for de re modal sentences must deliver; which informal modal judgements and which inferences involving existence, identity, actuality and nested modal operators both camps preserve; and what their disagreement is not about. Do not settle for formulations so thin that each side reads into them whatever it likes.

Second, produce a table of residual disagreements. For each row, state the precise question at issue, then give the formulation of the transworld identity theorist's answer in wording that camp would accept, then the counterpart theorist's answer in wording that camp would accept.

Third, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and, for each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. The cruxes should plausibly include: whether the Humphrey objection is better met by relinquishing transworld identity or by showing that it poses no real threat to an identity-based semantics; whether accidental intrinsic properties, such as an object that actually has five digits but could have had six, favour world-bound individuals over a single individual featuring in several worlds; whether the context-sensitivity and inconstancy of the counterpart relation is a defect or a strength, and whether the identity-based account carries any lighter contextual load; and whether the necessity of identity, together with the semantics of actually, imposes asymmetric constraints on the two views. Do not confine yourself to these if you find deeper cruxes.

Fourth, close with a note on which parts of this long-running dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, and why.

Please write the response in British English.
