I'm a philosophy PhD student working on moral responsibility and the insanity defense, and I keep getting stuck on the M'Naghten rule. The House of Lords' judges put it roughly this way: someone is excused only if, through a "defect of reason, from disease of the mind," he either didn't know "the nature and quality of the act he was doing," or, "if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong." Here's the case I can't stop thinking about: a defendant with severe psychopathy — two clinicians agree on the diagnosis — no delusions, no intellectual impairment, fully able to say what he did, that it was illegal, and that other people think it's wrong. What he seems to lack is any grasp of moral wrongness as something binding on him; he can't feel or appreciate it the way an ordinary person does, he just registers that others disapprove. Does the rule, read literally, cover him? Does "wrong" mean legally wrong or morally wrong here, and what turns on that? I'd like you to try the text, the purpose of the insanity defense, what the 1843 judges would have said if they'd had the modern psychopathy literature, and what a reasonable reader at the time would have taken the rule to mean. Then tell me which approach handles him best and why.
