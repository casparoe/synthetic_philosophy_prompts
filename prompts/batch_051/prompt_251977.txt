I would be grateful for comments on the following short essay, which I am drafting as the germ of a longer piece on narrative and the epistemology of coincidence. I am less interested in whether the prose is polished than in whether the central argument can be made to hold.

---

The Coincidence Budget

A novelist quickly learns that there is a double standard about coincidence. In life, a striking coincidence is treated as evidence that something is going on. If I dream of a friend I have not thought about in years, and the next morning he writes to say he is dying, I will not simply file this under chance; I will feel the pull of explanation. If I meet a stranger at a party in another country and we discover that we once lived in the same small building in different decades, the meeting feels charged. We do not say: with enough parties and enough strangers, such overlaps must happen. We say: what are the odds. Yet in fiction, the same event, introduced by an author to move a plot, is usually felt as a cheat. The novelist who resolves a story by having two separated lovers board the same train by accident is accused of lazy construction, unless the story has already trained us to read such events as fate or design.

I want to try to account for this asymmetry, because I think it exposes something about the epistemology of anecdote that the standard base-rate lesson misses.

The usual way of debunking a coincidence is to enlarge the reference class. The birthday paradox is the cleanest example: with twenty-three people in a room, the probability that at least one pair shares a birthday is greater than half, even though the probability that any particular pair shares a birthday is small. What feels like an event aimed at us is often an event aimed at no one, visible only because some pair or other had to collide. Applied to anecdote, the lesson is that we undercount the number of opportunities for a coincidence and overcount the specificity of the one we noticed. The friend who dreams of a plane crash and then hears of a plane crash is like the pair in the room who share a birthday: selected after the fact from a large field of unremembered non-events.

I think this is true but incomplete, and I want to isolate what it misses. The birthday paradox works because there is a well-defined ensemble of equally possible outcomes. We can say what the space of alternatives is: the set of birthday assignments across twenty-three people. But most of the coincidences that move us in life have no such ensemble. The dream of the dying friend is not drawn from a known distribution of dreams and outcomes. We cannot say how many possible dreams there were, or what the chance of any one of them matching a subsequent event would be, because the content of a dream and the content of a later event do not come pre-classified into countable cells. The base-rate critic pretends they do, or else he dissolves the event into a generic category, such as some friend being dreamed about around the time of some death. That generic event is indeed common. But the felt coincidence is not generic; it is the exact conjunction of this friend, this night, this tone, and this news. The question is whether its specificity is itself a product of after-the-fact selection, or whether specificity is what we mean by the event.

This is where the novelist enters. In a novel, we do have something like a known ensemble, because we know there is an author who could have chosen otherwise. The reader's complaint about a coincidence is not that the event is improbable in the world of the story; it is that the author has selected an improbable event from a large space of possible authorial choices, and the selection is too legible as convenience. The coincidence is not discovered; it is made. In life, by contrast, we take ourselves to be in the position of the reader who does not know whether there is an author. A coincidence feels meaningful exactly when it presents itself as found rather than made, as something that happened to us rather than something we assembled.

I therefore want to draw a distinction between found coincidence and made coincidence, and to suggest that the epistemology of anecdote is really an epistemology of selection. The base-rate critic is right that found coincidences are often made covertly, by memory's pruning and attention's spotlight. But he is wrong to treat the birthday paradox as a general solvent, because it requires an ensemble that many of the most gripping cases lack. The novelist's craft is a controlled experiment in this distinction. A good coincidence in fiction is one that can be made to feel found: it must appear to emerge from the world of the story rather than from the author's need. A bad coincidence is one whose made quality remains visible. Readers are not rejecting improbability as such; they are rejecting a visible act of authorial selection where the story has not earned it.

I am not sure this distinction holds. It may be that found and made are the same relation viewed from two different distances, and that what the novelist manipulates is only the reader's access to the act of selection. It may also be that the birthday paradox applies more widely than I allow, because even unique events can be re-described into ensembles once we allow coarse-grained categories. I am also unsure whether the concept of an authorial choice space can be made precise enough to explain why some made coincidences are satisfying and others are not.

I would welcome comments on the following. First, is the distinction between found and made coincidence doing real argumentative work, or is it a metaphor that collapses under pressure? Second, does the birthday paradox in fact undercut the anecdote in a way my essay underestimates, or does my criticism of it rest on a mistaken view of what a reference class requires? Third, where is the argument most vulnerable to a hostile reader? Please keep your comments to at most 600 words.
