My friend and I have been running a Nomic-type game for a few months (in case you don't know it: it's the game where the players can propose and vote to change the rules, and changing a rule is itself a move that's valid only if the current rules make it valid). We are stuck on our amendment rule. We wrote it as a single catch-all rule, R: "Any rule may be repealed or replaced by a two-thirds vote of the players." My friend claims R is either self-applying or it isn't, and both ways lead somewhere he thinks is absurd. Here's his argument.

(1) Either R applies to itself or it doesn't.
(2) Suppose it does. Then two-thirds of the players can validly vote to replace R with a new rule R* saying that no rule may ever be changed again. The vote meets the threshold R itself sets, so R* takes effect validly.
(3) Once R* is in force, no later vote can repeal it, since a repeal would itself be a rule change, which R* now forbids.
(4) So a one-off two-thirds majority can permanently switch off the only thing that made the game self-governing. The game has used its own amendment power to cancel amendment forever, so whoever first realizes this can entrench whatever rules they like and the rest of us are stuck.
(5) Now suppose R does not apply to itself. Then R can't be amended by the game's own amendment procedure, which means one rule was always beyond amendment. That's exactly the kind of hidden fixed point R was meant to avoid, and it makes the word "any" in R false.
(6) So either the game can be frozen by a single valid vote, or one rule was never really amendable. Both contradict the claim that the game is fully self-amending from the inside.
(7) Therefore a rule like R cannot govern its own amendment; any working game has to rest on something outside the rules themselves, like a rule of recognition or a practice the players just accept and never amend, to decide which changes count.

I keep going back and forth on which step to reject. I'd rather keep the game self-contained than admit an outside rule, but every objection I raise he says is special pleading. Wich step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's a third position between "R applies to itself" and "R doesn't," I'd like to know what it is.
