I keep getting stuck on a tweaked version of the grandfather paradox and I'd like a proper diagnosis of what the tweak does to the standard analyses. In the classic case you travel back and kill your grandfather before he meets your grandmother, so you're never born, so you can't have gone back. The usual moves are: the timeline is fixed and self-consistent, so something always stops you (the gun jams, you slip, you have a heart attack); you branch into a new timeline when you interfere, so you kill someone else's grandfather; or time travel to the past is simply impossible.

Now change the weapon. Instead of trying to kill him, you go back and shoot your grandfather in the kneecap at point-blank range, with no intention to kill — you just want him bedridden for a few weeks so he misses the specific party where he met your grandmother. On the fixed-timeline view, the universe doesn't need to stop the bullet; it only needs to get him to that party anyway, which means the apparent contradiction gets resolved by a limp, an unusually fast recovery, or sheer stubbornness. My suspicion is that this is the same paradox wearing a less violent costume, but I'm not sure: the original paradox blocks your existence outright, whereas this one only requires a seemingly unlikely sequence that is nonetheless physically possible. So here's what I want to know. Does the kneecap version actually distinguish the something-always-stops-you view from a subtler view on which the timeline only forbids outright contradictions and otherwise just lets improbable things happen? And do any of the standard analyses (fixed timeline, branching, impossibility) get ruled out or rehabilitated by it — or does it just make the fixed-timeline view look like it's smuggling in fate under the name of self-consistency?
