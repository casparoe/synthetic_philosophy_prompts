I recently got drawn into a zoning fight in my city and have agreed to give a short talk to my neighborhood association in support of an upzoning ordinance. I came to this through political philosophy rather than housing economics or land-use law, so I can state and defend the abstract position, but I do not trust my sense of how this particular room will attack it. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a speech.

The thesis I will defend is this: current residents have no moral right to veto changes in who may live near them, because the opportunities a city confers—jobs, schooling, networks, safety, longer life—are not a common asset owned by incumbents; the right to the city belongs as much to would-be residents as to present ones. In concrete terms, I am arguing for an ordinance that would allow fourplexes by right in every residential district (six units on corner lots), remove discretionary design review for buildings under ten units, and grant height-and-setback relief to projects that include below-market homes.

My argument in compressed form: the usual objections to new housing—neighborhood character, congestion, parking, property prices—are aesthetic and pecuniary interests of people who already have secure access to the city's advantages, and such interests cannot justify excluding others from those advantages; and local veto powers, whatever their democratic surface, have in practice functioned as the successor to the exclusionary covenant. I am not claiming upzoning alone solves the housing shortage; my claim is narrower, that the veto itself is an unjust closure of opportunity.

The audience: roughly fifty or sixty residents at a monthly meeting of an old, high-cost neighborhood in a West Coast city, mostly homeowners who have lived there for decades, left-of-center and quick to describe themselves as environmentalists and supporters of racial and economic justice, yet reliably hostile to new housing in their own district. There will be a few renters, a retired land-use attorney who chairs the meeting, one architect, and no developers or professional housing economists. The commitments I expect to be working against include the preservation of neighborhood character, property values as retirement security, environmental quality (trees, shade, stormwater), traffic and parking, the legitimacy of local self-governance, and an articulated fear of displacement and gentrification.

Prepare the sheet as a list: for each objection, give me the wording I should expect in the room, then an estimate of how likely and how serious it is, then a reply that could be delivered in about two minutes, then a short private note to me on where the reply is thinnest and what concession it would cost me to make it honest. Order the list so that the most dangerous objections come first, and keep it to the objections this audience will actually raise rather than the ones I would meet at a housing-policy conference. Add, at the end, the question I should be praying nobody asks.
