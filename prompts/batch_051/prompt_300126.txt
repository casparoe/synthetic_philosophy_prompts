I cover psychology and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I've been working on a feature about whether emotions are part of a well-lived life or mostly internal noise. I've been shadowing an emotion researcher who won't give me a theory, just case-by-case verdicts. Her calls so far: a woman's grief in the year after her father died, part of a good life; a commuter's daily fury at traffic that he forgets within minutes, noise; a man's outsized fear of flying that eventually kept him from visiting his grandchildren, noise; a carpenter's quiet satisfaction after a day of precise work, part of a good life; a grad student's envy of a friend's richly deserved book deal, which she's ashamed of and never voices, noise; an organizer's anger at a rigged vote in her building, which she used to mobilize her neighbors, part of a good life; and an old man's bittersweet ache at hearing his father's favorite songs, part of a good life.

I keep comming back to two principles that both fit this record: (a) an emotion is part of a good life only if it's a fitting, proportionate response to something real and genuinely worth caring about; (b) an emotion is part of a good life if it's sincerely felt, genuinely hers, and makes her inner life richer, whether or not its object is strictly real. They come apart on the case I want to ask her about next: a woman who weeps, deeply and sincerely, at the death of a beloved character in a novel, knowing the character is invented, then closes the book and goes about her day unchanged. Predict how she'll rule on the novel-death case, say which principle (or a third one) best fits her track record, and tell me what further case would cleanly decide between the fittingness principle and the authenticity principle.
