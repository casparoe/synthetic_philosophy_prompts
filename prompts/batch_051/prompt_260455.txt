I am writing a paper on low-impact AI and I want the full specification of what a theory of an agent's impact on the world has to deliver before I look at candidate measures. Write the desiderata for a theory of side effects, where adequate means: it lets an agent avoid side effects without being given a full specification of human value, while the agent still knows its assigned task. The motivating problem is this. An agent is told to accomplish something narrow — deliver the coffee — and is also told to leave the rest of the world as it would have been. But we cannot give it a utility function over world states, because that would presume the value specification we are trying to avoid. A theory of impact therefore has to say what prevailing as it would have been means and which aspects of physical change count as impact, without silently becoming a theory of value.

The requirements list should cover at least the following pressure points. For each, give the case or consideration that forces it and mark it non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference:

1. The baseline problem. Impact is relative to a counterfactual, and agent-absent, agent-inert, and default-policy baselines can disagree about which changes count as side effects.
2. The intended-effect problem. The measure must penalize side effects without penalizing the assigned objective, which requires separating doing the job from disturbing the world without already knowing what is valuable.
3. The butterfly problem. Nearly any action changes vast numbers of microscopic details. The measure must be able to treat some physical differences as negligible, on a principled basis.
4. Goodharting resistance. If the measure is ever used as a constraint or objective, the agent will exploit its gaps. The theory must say how it behaves under optimization pressure and why that does not make it useless.
5. Value-encoding transparency. The theory must state which valuations it cannot avoid making (which features enter the state representation, which changes count as resources, which state variables are kept or discarded) and defend why those are not already the full value specification.
6. Reversibility and persistence. It must say whether changes the agent can undo count for less than permanent ones, and why.
7. Temporal depth. It must specify how far down unbounded causal chains it traces consequences and how it avoids regress.
8. Estimability. A bounded agent with observational data — not a omniscient world model — must be able to approximate the quantity the theory defines, or the theory must say what approximation buys it.

Add any requirement I have missed if a concrete case forces it.

Then identify which subsets of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and give the argument. My suspicion is that the deepest tension is between the no-full-specification demand and the intended-effect requirement, since separating unwanted disturbances from the desired outcome seems to require ranking outcomes after all; a second candidate is the tension between butterfly-indifference and sensitivity to genuine catastrophes. If the real conflict is elsewhere, say where.

Close with a brief scorecard of the main candidate approaches: penalizing divergence from an inaction baseline; counting only persistent changes; attainable utility preservation (penalizing interventions that shrink the set of goals an agent could still achieve from the resulting state); task-relative side effects (measuring deviation from a default trajectory that would have accomplished the task); and any other candidate you regard as serious. Keep the scorecard brief; the criteria are the deliverable.

Follow it with a short prose section on which subsets of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. Then give the scorecard as a second table with columns: Approach | One-line mechanism | Where it fails the requirements — and keep each row to a sentence. Write in American English.
