I'm a journalist who covers the science-and-ideas beat, and I've been reading the more quantitative end of history lately. Across a bunch of different old agrarian states -- Han China, Rome, early modern France and England, maybe Mamluk Egypt -- I keep seeing the same cycle described. Population grows, the economy grows. But the number of people with elite status, or elite pretensions, grows way faster than the number of positions that can support them. So you get more and more titled or educated people fighting over a fixed number of plum slots. They demand exemptions and stipends from the state, which hollows out state finances. Meanwhile ordinary peoples' wages or land access erode, because there are more laboring people competing for the same land and work. Then some wave of internal war, rebellion, or state collapse hits, the population drops, the elite ranks get thinned out, land gets cheap, and the whole thing starts over.

I don't have a word for this, and I'm not sure the people writing about it do either. Is there an established term for this specific mechanism -- the elite glut doing the political work -- or is it normally treated as just one gear inside a larger population-and-instability story? How would you separate it from a plain Malthusian cycle, where the whole population, not just the top layer, overshoots the food supply? And how is it different from the old "states rise and fall" talk, which doesn't really have a mechanism at all? I'm not just after a label; I want to know what the core mechanism is supposed to be, where its edges are, and whether the apparent couple-century cycle is a real thing or just our pattern-matching.
