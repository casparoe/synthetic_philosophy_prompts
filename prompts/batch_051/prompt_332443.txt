I have just come from a long argument with a friend about whether a larger population is better, and I left unsure how much my position genuinely risks. I had been defending a totalist axiology: other things equal, a larger population is better when the additional lives are worth living, because the value of a state of affairs is the sum of well-being it contains and every life worth living adds positive well-being. It follows, as a matter of axiology rather than of policy or individual obligation, that there is pro tanto reason to favour larger populations of such lives over smaller ones, however strong the practical constraints may be.

My friend pressed Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion, and I found myself saying that I can learn to live with it rather than treating it as a counterexample. That response made me suspect my position may have been insulated from refutation, so I want an honest map of what would count against the view as I have stated it. Please sort the considerations by how decisive they should be, and distinguish what would be decisive from what would merely be awkward. Among the things I want examined: whether the neutrality intuition—that adding a life worth living is neither good nor bad—should move me, and how I could resist it without special pleading; whether the asymmetry between our reasons to create happy lives and our reasons to create miserable ones shows that the total view has the wrong shape of value; whether critical-level and variable-value views account for the same range of cases better than the sum-of-well-being view; whether average-sensitive views are genuine rivals or merely answers to a different question; and whether my 'other things equal' clause is doing illicit work by screening off density, resource, environmental, and social objections that any real-world pronatalism would have to answer. For each consideration, include the reply I am most likely to give and the follow-up that would press past it. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and whether, in axiology, being insulated in that way is a defect or simply the ordinary condition of a first principle. Of the things that would count against it, I expect to be most moved by cases in which the total view commends a future I find not merely unintuitive but unacceptable.
