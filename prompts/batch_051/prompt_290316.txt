I've been trying to sort out when I think mandatory arbitration is fine and when it's a quiet form of injustice, and my gut keeps pulling in different directions. I'd like you to test whether there's a principle underneath these verdicts, or whether I need to give some of them up.

Here's where I land, honestly:

- Two midsize companies with actual lawyers negotiate a supply contract and agree to arbitrate disputes before a commercial arbitration firm they both know and respect. Fine. Actually preferable to court.
- A credit card company buries an arbitration clause and a class-action waiver in the fine print of a new card. Later the company admits it wrongly charged a $29 overdraft fee to about a million people. My gut says the clause shouldn't stop a class action, even though each individual loss is tiny.
- A warehouse worker signs an arbitration agreement as a condition of getting the job, and it waives class actions. Later it turns out the company shorted everyone's overtime. Not fine. The waiver is what bothers me.
- Same setup, but the claim is sexual harassment, and the agreement says the company picks the arbitrator and pays their fees. Definitely not fine, even without a class issue — it just looks rigged.
- A union and an employer negotiate a grievance-and-arbitration system for discipline disputes as part of a collective bargaining agreement, with a jointly picked arbitrator. Fine — honestly I think its better than court.
- Two neighbors, after a fence dispute has already started, both agree to let a retired judge arbitrate. Totally fine.
- An elderly woman is admitted to a nursing home, and her daughter signs the intake forms under pressure, including an arbitration clause. The mother is later injured by neglect. I keep flip-flopping. Part of me says she never really consented; part of me says adults sign contracts like this all the time.
- A gig-work app's terms of service include an arbitration clause for all disputes, ban class actions, and let the app pick the arbitrator from a list it maintains. You can't use the app without accepting. I think this is not fine, but I can't articulate exactly why it's worse than the two-company case.

Is there one principle — about consent, power, the public role of courts, the difference between genuinely adjudicating a dispute and shunting it aside — that captures all or most of these? If the principle forces me to drop one or two of my verdicts, tell me which and why. Then run the principle on two cases I genuinely can't decide: (a) a public school district includes a mandatory arbitration clause in its enrollment packet, including for special-education disputes; (b) a startup offers binding arbitration by a language model with no human review or appeal for all disputes under $500, and agreeing to its terms is the only way to use the app. I don't need case law or citations; I want the principle and how you'd defend it.
