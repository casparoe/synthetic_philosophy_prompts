I keep reaching for the phrase naturalistic fallacy and then noticing that I use it for at least three different moves: the supposed impossibility of deriving a normative conclusion from purely descriptive premises, Moore's argument that evaluative terms like good cannot be defined in non-evaluative terms, and the popular thought that what is natural is thereby good. I would like a working definition of naturalistic fallacy — or, if you think the phrase names more than one defensible thing, a small set of definitions — that I can actually use to sort cases.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

1. The inference from pain is intrinsically unpleasant to we ought not to cause pain in others, with no stated bridging premise.
2. The claim that eating meat is permissible because humans evolved as omnivores.
3. Moore's open-question argument against defining good as pleasant: it always remains open whether pleasant things are good.
4. A utilitarian who says that wrongness just is the property of failing to maximize expected well-being, and treats this as a substantive theoretical identification rather than a dictionary definition.
5. The argument from revealed preference: people consistently choose A over B, so A is better for them.
6. The argument that because moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection, and natural selection tracks reproductive fitness rather than moral truth, our moral intuitions are unreliable.

I think 3 is the paradigm of the fallacy and I am fairly sure 2 should count as well, though they look like very different moves. I am inclined to say 4 is not the fallacy but a substantive moral theory, and that 1 is better diagnosed as a hidden premise than a violation of a gap. On 5 and 6 I keep changing my mind.

Please do not give me a survey of positions. Take a definite stand: give the definition or definitions you would actually use, run them through all six cases, mark the cases on which the definition gives the wrong or surprising answer, and say whether you revise the definition or defend the verdict. I also want to know which of the six verdicts you find least secure, and why.
