You are a specialist in the epistemology of legal proof and in evidence law. I want a rigorous consistency audit of a set of positions I have accumulated, before I present them to a seminar. Please work through them carefully; it may help to begin by mapping the pairwise relationships among the commitments and sorting them into real tensions, merely apparent tensions, and pairs with no tension, before writing your final ranked assessment.

My commitments are these:

1. In civil cases, the standard of proof is, and should be, the preponderance of the evidence, and I understand that standard as a probability threshold: the claimant should win when, on the total evidence, the claim is more probable than not.

2. In the blue bus scenario—a pedestrian is injured by a bus on a route where the Blue Bus Company operates 80 percent of the buses and the Red Bus Company operates 20 percent, and there is no eyewitness or other identifying evidence—the pedestrian should not recover against the Blue Bus Company.

3. In the gatecrasher scenario—one thousand people attend a rodeo, 499 purchased tickets, 501 entered without paying, and the organizer, unable to identify which people did not pay, sues one attendee selected at random—the organizer should not recover against that attendee.

4. In criminal cases, proof beyond a reasonable doubt is not reducible to any fixed probability threshold. It requires a kind of epistemic warrant that cannot be represented as a number, and a very high probability alone would not, in principle, satisfy it.

5. A cold-hit DNA match can properly support a conviction. A defendant identified only because a database was searched, with a random match probability of 1 in 10 million, may be convicted, and the statistical character of that evidence is not by itself an objection. I would resist calling such evidence naked statistical evidence in the objectionable sense.

6. Lost-chance recovery in medical malpractice is justified. If a physician's negligence reduces a patient's survival probability from 45 percent to 25 percent, the estate may recover a proportional share of the loss, even though the patient was more likely than not going to die absent the negligence.

7. Market-share liability is justified. When many firms manufactured a fungible product and a victim cannot identify which firm made the unit that harmed her, each firm should pay in proportion to its market share, even though the probability that any particular firm caused the harm may be far below 0.5.

8. I reject the use of pretrial actuarial risk scores as a primary ground for detention. A score that classifies a defendant as high risk is group-level statistical evidence, not evidence about what this particular person did or will do.

I do not know where the stress points are, and I do not want a lecture on why each position is individually defensible; I already know each has defenders. Please deliver a consistency audit: (a) identify the real tensions among these commitments, ordered by severity; (b) for each real tension, say precisely what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs; (c) identify any pairs that look inconsistent but are not, and explain why the appearance is misleading; and (d) tell me whether some single distinction or principle would reconcile most of the set, and if so, state it and test it against all eight; if not, say which single revision would do the most repair.
