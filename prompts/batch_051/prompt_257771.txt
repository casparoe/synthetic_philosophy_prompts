For an upcoming meetup I host — a dozen adults, mostly with technical backgrounds but almost no formal probability theory, two hours on a weekday evening — I want a hands-on exercise rather than a talk. The idea I want them to feel in their bones before it is named is the one behind Bertrand's paradox: the principle of indifference is not a complete rule, because "equally likely" and even "a random X" have no meaning until a procedure or measure is specified, and equally intuitive specifications can disagree. By the end, I want participants to have moved from the cheap lesson ("the problem was underspecified") to the harder question: what does it take to specify it, and can any specification be called the right one?

Design an activity along these lines. Split the participants into small teams, and give each team a different but comparably "obvious" mechanical rule for generating one random chord of a circle (for example: pick two endpoints uniformly on the circumference; pick a random point in the circle's interior as the chord's midpoint; pick a random point along a random radius and draw the chord perpendicular to that radius). Have each team generate enough chords — suggest a feasible number given the two-hour window — to get a stable frequency for the event "the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle," and then have the teams compare numbers. The three procedures I listed should land near 1/3, 1/4, and 1/2 respectively, so the disagreement between teams should be unmistakable and should surface before anyone says "Bertrand" or "measure." Give me the concrete setup: what to supply (pre-printed circles? protractors? dice? random-number tables?), exact instructions per team so one team cannot cheat by quietly importing another team's uniformity assumption, how trials are recorded, and how to sequence the reveal for maximum cognitive friction. Include a timed plan.

Then give a debrief in bullet form that (1) has the participants themselves articulate where the teams diverged, tabooing the words "probability," "random," and "uniform" until they've first described what each team actually did; (2) maps each moment in the activity onto a part of the idea — different teams were indifferent over different quantities (angles, midpoints, points along a radius), and uniformity in one parameterization is not preserved under the transformations connecting them; (3) pushes past the standard rationalist dismissal I expect from this crowd — "it's just underspecified" — by getting them to see that "underspecified" still leaves open which specification to use when a real problem arrives, and that resolving the paradox requires an argument for why some procedures are ruled out (invariance or symmetry constraints, say) rather than a shrug; and (4) leaves at least one version of the question unresolved out loud rather than pretending the paradox has a single accepted dissolution.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong and how to recover: a team executing its procedure sloppily so its frequency drifts toward a neighbor's answer; sample sizes small enough that the three answers blur together; a participant announcing early that the problem is ill-posed and deflating the exercise; a team believing its own procedure is the only natural one and treating the other teams as making a mistake. Also be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the measure-theoretic statement of the problem, the fact that the three answers are exactly 1/3, 1/4, and 1/2 under the corresponding measures rather than just "around" them (empirical frequencies will be noisy), and the role of the facilitator in supplying the history and the modern resolution attempts. Where should the debrief stop and leave the matter unresolved rather than resolving it by force?

Finally, be self-critical about the design itself: what would a pilot run have to show for you to change your mind that this teaches the intended lesson rather than merely teaching participants to distrust applied probability, and which part of the design is most likely to fail?
