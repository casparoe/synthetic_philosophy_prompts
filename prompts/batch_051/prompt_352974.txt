Here's a mechanism I want you to actually work through, not just give me a verdict on. A national arts endowment gets tired of the whole 'is beauty objective or not' fight and decides to just build an institution modeled on Hume's true judges — the idea being that some people really are more competent to judge than others because they've practiced, made lots of comparisons, and shed their prejudices, so a consensus of such people is our best shot at tracking real beauty. The trouble is figuring out who those people are and keeping them honest.

So the endowment sets up a certification program. Trainees make blind judgments on pairs of artworks. The pairs are split into a training set (old stuff where posterity has apparently converged — you get points for matching the canon) and a probe set (recent, still-contested stuff where there's no settled answer yet). You need a high training-set score to get certified. Your probe-set calls get sealed, unsealed decades later, and then they determine your pension and whether you keep your judging license. Once certified you sit on panels that control all public art funding and acquisitions for the national collection, and a unanimous panel verdict has legal force. If the panel rejects your work, you get your deposit back if a review board later finds the rejection was procedurally sloppy or against the panel's own precedents — and when that happens the panel loses pension points.

Walk me through what this actually does in practice. Who ends up certified, and is the training-set score selecting for good judgement or just for being well-socialized into the canon? What happens to genuinely new work that would have looked wrong by the old canon? How do artists, curators, and art schools start gaming it — do we end up with a monoculture, or with funded provocation aimed at probe-set payoffs after the artist is dead, or with capture by whoever controls the training set? Does the unanimity rule make the system more conservative or just push the real fights into the review board? And whether their is any fix you can think of — paying judges to disagree, adding non-experts to panels, rotating or anonymizing the training set — that would make this track something like a real standard of taste rather than a manufactured consensus, or whether the design just collapses that distinction.
