I'm a philosophy professor, and I've got a first-year seminar on rationality where we've just done a week on instrumental rationality and expected utility. Next session I want to do the sunk cost fallacy, but I dont want the standard line that sunk costs are irrelevant, full stop. The question I actually want them to sit in is whether honouring sunk costs is always irrational, or whether a rational person (or a rational policy) can sometimes let sunk costs matter. I care less about the usual bias lecture and more about the would-be exceptions: sunk costs as evidence about future payoffs, honouring them as a commitment device or piece of self-binding, the thought that the regret over wasted resources is itself a future cost, and the purely psychological pull that students will mistake for one of those. I want them to be able to sort those cases from each other, not just nod along when I say the slogan.

So rather than write the lecture for me, I'd like a plan for the session's explanatory arc. In particular: what idea needs to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, and what the order of ideas should be (what depends on what); which single example or case would carry the whole session and what it needs to have and to lack; the misconceptions they'll arrive with or form partway through, in the order they're likely to surface, and how the sequence should absorb each rather than just refute it; how I'll know by the end whether they've understood the question itself rather than learnt a new slogan; and what to leave out at this level and say so. Please write the whole plan in British English.
