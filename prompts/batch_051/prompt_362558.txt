As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am planning a long essay on what Gödel's incompleteness theorems have been taken to show outside mathematics, and I want to be able to judge those uses rather than merely be suspicious of them. People have built ambitious conclusions on the theorems: that the human mind cannot be captured by a machine, that artificial intelligence has principled limits, that no legal or constitutional system can be complete or justify itself, that physics will always leave some questions open, that there are truths no system can ever prove. Some of this may be right and some is almost certainly overreach, but I need a principled way to tell the difference.

Write me the desiderata that any responsible application of the incompleteness theorems to a subject outside mathematics must satisfy before its conclusion should be accepted. By desiderata I mean the requirements such an application has to meet: the distinctions it must respect, the hypotheses it must establish about its target system, the results it must remain consistent with, and the hidden premises it must bring into the open.

Include, with the consideration behind each requirement, at least the following: the formal hypotheses actually needed for the theorems — consistency, an axiom set that can be mechanically listed, and the ability to encode enough elementary arithmetic; the differences between provability and truth, between incompleteness and undecidability, and between a statement's being unprovable in one system and being unknowable outright; the fact that the theorems are relative to a system and that extending the system can often decide the leftover sentence; and the extra step required to move from any of this to claims about human thought outpacing machines. Please distinguish where the first and second theorems impose different obligations.

For each requirement, say whether it is a non-negotiable precondition of the mathematics or a preference of a particular camp that I could soften. If some pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied by the same argument, flag that as a finding in its own right. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in screening out bad Gödelian arguments — the one I should apply first when I read a claim in the wild.
