I'm taking an upper-division course that sits between philosophy of mind and clinical psychology, and we've spent the term arguing about when self-examination helps and when it harms. Our seminar has split into two stable camps. One holds that turning attention onto one's own thoughts, feelings, and motives is broadly truth-conducive and self-correcting, and that the activity's bad reputation comes from conflating it with depressive brooding or anxious worry. The other holds that there is a real, well-established division between constructive self-reflection and maladaptive rumination, and that much of what gets called self-examination in ordinary life is the latter: repetitive, abstract, negatively focused, and reliably associated with poorer problem solving, lowered mood, and interpersonal strain—including co-rumination, in which two people jointly rehearse and deepen the same negative self-focused material.

Our instructor has asked us to stop arguing and instead produce the document that an adversarial collaboration between the two camps would yield, and I'd like you to draft it. The document should include: (a) a numbered list of claims both camps accept, worded so that neither side would object to any clause—covering, at minimum, the basic capacity for self-focused attention, the relevance of negative versus positive content, the distinction between processing a problem's concrete features and processing its abstract causes and meanings, and what kinds of evidence could in principle move either camp; (b) a disagreements table giving, for each row, the exact point of disagreement in neutral language, together with separate formulations each side would endorse as its own position; and (c) the true cruxes, framed as sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one camp, with merely verbal disagreements identified and set aside. For each crux, specify a concrete manipulation, longitudinal design, or ordinary-life case that both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. The instructor will judge the result by whether a member of either camp could read it and say, yes, that is my view stated exactly as I would state it, and these are the things that would need to happen for me to change my mind.
