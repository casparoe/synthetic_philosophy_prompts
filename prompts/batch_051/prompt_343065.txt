An intro epistemology book I read this week gives this summary of Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, and I'm not sure how much of it to trust:

"Williamson argues that there can be no luminous mental states. A state is luminous just in case, whenever you are in it, you are in a position to know you are in it. He imagines a person slowly warming up at dawn, so gradually that she can never tell the exact moment she stops being cold. Since she can always be wrong about whether she is cold, she never really knows she is cold, even when she is. And since luminosity is basically the idea that if you know something you are in a position to know that you know it, the same argument also refutes the KK principle. The upshot is that introspection gives us no better access to our own minds than observation of other people does."

I have a hunch the last two sentences are overreaching, but I don't know the details well enough to say exactly where. Could you go through it piece by piece and tell me what Williamson would accept, what he'd qualify or reject, and whether the book's upshot actually follows from the accurate parts? I'd also like the answer in American English, and ideally phrased so I could pass a corrected version along to a classmate without having read Williamson myself.
