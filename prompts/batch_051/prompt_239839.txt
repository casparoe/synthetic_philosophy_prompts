For a class on population ethics I've been reading around David Benatar's view that coming into existence is always a serious harm, and a friend who hasn't read the original work summarized it for me as follows: "Benatar compares two scenarios. If you exist, your pain is bad and your pleasure is good. If you never exist, the absence of your pain is good, but the absence of your pleasure is nothing—not good, not bad, just a blank. Since every life contains at least some pain, nonexistence wins the comparison: it has all the advantages of existence and none of the disadvantages. The pleasures you would have had simply don't count against it. So Benatar concludes it would have been better for each of us never to have been born, that having children is always a net harm to the child, and that accepting the argument makes suicide the rational choice for everyone." I've read enough to suspect that the first few sentences are a fair compression but that the last part slides into something Benatar doesn't say—I've heard he distinguishes between lives worth starting and lives worth continuing and denies that his view recommends ending existing lives. But I'm less sure about the middle, especially the claim that the absence of pleasure is "not good, not bad, just a blank," and whether "nonexistence has all the advantages of existence" is a defensible restatement. Could you give me an accuracy audit of my friend's summary, sentence by sentence? I want to know what the asymmetry actually states, why the absent-pleasure case is supposed to bear less weight than the absent-pain case, where popular glosses of this argument usually get sloppy, and which of the practical conclusions about procreation and suicide actually follow from Benatar's premises once the distinctions are kept straight.
