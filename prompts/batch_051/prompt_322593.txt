I'm working on a short essay about why the argument over whether war games glorify war never seems to budge, and I'd like comments on the core idea before I expand it. The usual positions are that these games are just toys with no serious content, or that any game set in a real conflict is inherently disrespectful because it turns war into entertainment. Neither feels right, but I also dont think defenders can just say "it's only a game." My thesis is that the dispute stalls because everyone uses "fun" to mean too many things at once: (a) the fun of competitive mastery—learning maps, optimizing loadouts, outplaying opponents; (b) the fun of spectacle—dramatic, even beautiful-looking destruction; and (c) something closer to absorption or inhabiting a role, which isn't really pleasure at all. A war game can be criticized when it funnels the war through (a) or (b), because then the war gets reshaped into sport or a fireworks show; but the more serious treatments people praise tend to depend on (c). The problem is that critics and developers keep attributing the entire game to whichever sense of fun they personally felt most strongly.

Before I write the whole thing, I'd like comments: does the three-way distinction actually do any work, or does it just relabel the old disagreement? Is it plausible to locate the glorification problem specifically in (a) and (b), or am I already smuggling in my conclusion by defining them as "sport" and "spectacle"? I'm also unsure whether the essay needs concrete case studies—like a shooter about a recent war and a strategy game on the Eastern Front—or whether the diagnosis can stand on its own. I'm a bit worried the taxonomy is too tidy and the essay will end up just sorting game mechanics into boxes.
