I'm a PhD student working on a metaphysics paper on persistence, and I keep getting stuck trying to figure out whether my own case judgments commit me to endurantism, perdurantism, or maybe one of the stage-theory variants. I was rereading Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds, where he sets up the distinction roughly like this: an object endures when it is wholy present at each of the times it exists, and it perdures when it persists by having different temporal parts at different times. That distinction is clear enough on paper, but my gut reactions to cases just don't line up neatly.

Here are my verdicts, pretty much as I'd defend them pre-theoretically.

1. Clay statue. On Monday an artist shapes a lump of clay into a statue. On Tuesday she squashes it flat. I think the statue is destroyed on Tuesday, while the lump of clay survives. I also think that on Monday the statue and the lump are two distinct objects exactly occupying the same place, because they have different persistence conditions.

2. Ordinary childhood. When I consider my own ten-year-old self, I don't think that's a different temporal part of me or a counterpart of me. It was literally me, the whole object, at age ten, and I'm the same whole object now. That feels endurantist.

3. Time-travel self-visitation. Suppose a 40-year-old woman travels back to visit her 10-year-old self, and they sit at the same table. I think it's right that the visitor is the same person as the 10-year-old, but I also don't want to say one thing is wholly present in two places at once. I find myself reaching for the idea that there are two different stages of a single four-dimensional person, and the person persists by having these stages. That sounds perdurantist.

4. Amoeba fission. An amoeba divides into two daughter cells. I deny that either daughter is identical to the original amoeba; the original ceases to exist when it divides. But I'm not sure whether that's because no thing can be wholly present in two places, or because fission is just destructive.

5. Restored statue. Suppose the squashed clay from case 1 is later reshaped into a statue of the same shape. I think a new statue is created; the old statue does not come back into existence. The clay lump remains the same throughout.

What I want to know is whether there is one account of persistence that delivers all five of these verdicts—ideally stated in terms of whether ordinary objects are wholly present at times, have temporal parts, or are momentary stages linked by counterpart relations. If no single clean account does the job, I want to know which verdict(s) I should be most willing to give up, and why those are the least costly. I'd also like the account to predict a new case I haven't settled: a man undergoes gradual total replacement of his body while his memories and personality are preserved; later, out of the collected replaced matter, a duplicate of his original body is built. Which, if any, of the two is the original man on your account, and does the account say "person" works by being wholly present or by having temporal parts?
