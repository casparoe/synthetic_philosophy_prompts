A colleague of mine works on anti-corruption reform, including in places where gift-giving and patronage are the ordinary idiom of politics, and he walked me into the following argument, which I cannot find an exit from.

(1) Whatever else corruption involves, it involves a public official distributing benefits to particular persons or groups in exchange for political support, rather than according to impartial rules. (2) A legislator who steers a hospital, a bridge, or a bloc of public jobs to her own district is likewise distributing particular benefits to particular people, and she does so because they support her or because she needs their support. (3) So if the party machine that hands out patronage jobs and favors to voters is corrupt, the legislator is corrupt on the same principle. (4) But this kind of constituency-directed distribution is not a deviation from representative democracy; it is much of how districts are represented and coalitions are held together. (5) So the principle in (1) proves too much: it converts normal democratic politics into corruption. (6) Yet when we try to narrow (1) to avoid this, by requiring that the exchange be secret, or illegal, or personally enriching, we seem either to let the open and legal machine off the hook for doing much the same thing, or to make corruptness turn on the accident of how a transfer is formally booked. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I most want to keep the thought that corruption names a real difference between crony politics and ordinary democratic politics, and not merely politics we happen to dislike.
