I've been going down a rabbit hole about Fomenko's New Chronology and I keep flip-flopping on what exactly makes it pseudohistory rather than a radical but maybe legitimate revision. In case you don't know the case: it's a research program mostly associated with the Russian mathematician Anatoly Fomenko and G. V. Nosovsky. The core claim is that the standard timeline of antiquity and the middle ages is a kind of repeated mirage — that ancient Greece, Rome, Egypt, and the events of the Bible are really scrambled and duplicated retellings of medieval events, and that the timeline got locked in by early modern chronologists like Joseph Scaliger. A consequence they draw is that the so-called Mongols and the Russians were actually the same empire, and everybody else's history is a reflection of it. They lean on statistics, alleged astronomical retro-dictions, and textual parallels. Mainstream historians think its crank stuff; its partisans see themselves as Galileos ignored by a guild.

Here's what I want: analyze the New Chronology as a test case for the demarcation problem, the problem of separating science (or here, history) from pseudoscience. Take Popper-style falsifiability, Kuhn-style normal science and paradigm shifts, and any other demarcation idea you find useful — but don't try to cover everything; pick the two or three that do the most work. For each, say whether New Chronology comes out as pseudohistory or as a legitimate research program, and be explicit about what feature the criterion is tracking. Then push back on each criterion: does New Chronology actually behave the way the criterion says a pseudoscience behaves? For instance, are its claims unfalsifiable, or are many of them perfectly specific and testable, just tested and failed? Does it resemble a Kuhnian paradigm crisis, and does that show the criterion is too permissive? What I most want to know is whether New Chronology is pseudohistory because of something in its content, or because of how its practitioners handle evidence — cherry-picking, rescuing the thesis every time a prediction dies, immunizing the core claim — and which demarcation criterion captures that difference best. If you think none of them does, say what a better criterion would look like and how it would classify the case.
