People have disputed for centuries whether to read Scripture as history — to be reconstructed with the ordinary tools of textual criticism, archaeology, and comparative religion — or as revelation, whose meaning is available only from inside the community that received the text as Scripture. The puzzle that interests me is that the dispute does not seem to reduce to a factual disagreement: the historically sophisticated critic and the historically sophisticated believer can accept the same findings about redaction, parallels, and gradual canonisation, yet the critic takes these findings to dissolve the text's claim on us, while the believer takes them as the medium through which revelation is given. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about what the text is but about what it is to understand a text — whether understanding means explaining how the text came to be, or being able to inhabit the role it plays in a continuing tradition. Diagnose this: is it a verbal disagreement, a difference about standards of evidence, a difference about which considerations count as data, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about authority that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a reframing, a change of question — or whether the impasse should be expected to persist and why. If the framing itself is confused, say that outright and tell me what question I should have asked instead.
