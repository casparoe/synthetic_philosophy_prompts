The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) appears to hold two commitments about worship that have struck many readers as difficult to square. In chapter 21, section 1, the confession teaches that the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by God himself and so limited by his own revealed will, with the consequence that God may not be worshipped according to human inventions or in any way not prescribed in holy Scripture. This is often called the regulative principle: whatever is not commanded is, for worship, forbidden.

But chapter 1, section 6, after affirming that nothing may be added to Scripture, says this:

"Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding of such things as are revealed in the Word: and that there are some circumstances concerning the worship of God, and government of the church, common to human actions and societies, which are to be ordered by the light of nature, and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word, which are always to be observed."

The apparent conflict is easy to state. The first passage says God may not be worshipped in any way not prescribed in Scripture. The second says some circumstances concerning worship are to be ordered by the light of nature and Christian prudence. But practically every element of worship has a "circumstantial" description: the time of the service, the language, the seating, the use of a building, whether to kneel at a particular moment, whether to sing from a psalter or a hymnal, whether to use musical instruments, whether to observe a fixed liturgical year. If enough is classed as a circumstance, the regulative principle seems to collapse into a weaker rule that merely excludes what Scripture forbids. If too little is classed as a circumstance, the worship life of the church becomes oddly arbitrary.

I want a careful account of whether these two passages can be reconciled, and at what cost. Please address at least these questions.

First, what principle of the confession, if any, distinguishes those things that God has to prescribe from those "circumstances" that fall to human prudence? Give the principle with enough precision that it could be applied to new cases, not just by matching known examples.

Second, run your proposed principle through these cases: a congregation decides to meet at 11 a.m. rather than 10 a.m.; a congregation replaces all sung worship with unison psalmody; a congregation uses a piano to accompany singing; a congregation reads a non-inspired prayer composed by its minister; a congregation sits rather than kneels for prayer. Which of these are regulatively permitted? Which does your principle classify as sinful? Which does it leave undetermined?

Third, say whether the reconciliation you propose is real or whether it is in effect special pleading — a line drawn where the confession's authors needed it to be drawn, but not one that can be defended from the principles they state. If you think the two passages cannot be reconciled without changing the meaning of one of them, say so and identify which one gives way.

One more thing: if my framing here depends on a mistaken understanding of what the regulative principle actually requires, or on the assumption that the confession's authors meant to apply chapter 21 to particulars like these when they may not have, tell me that directly instead of building an elaborate reconciliation on a bad premise.
