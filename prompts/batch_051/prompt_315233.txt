I keep hearing friends in ML say that training a model is basically like raising a child: you can't just write down the rules directly, you have to expose it to examples, give it feedback, shape its habits over time, and eventually you just have to trust the result. The conclusion they draw is that alignment work should be less about specifying the right utility function up front and more about good "upbringing" — curriculum design, data diet, the right kind of correction. It sounds plausible, but I got to thinking about Locke, who in *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* says something like — and I'm paraphrasing because I don't have the text in front of me right now — that children don't learn by rules, which they'll always forget, but by practice, and that the mind is to be moulded early like wax before it hardens. He also has this whole bit about how you shouldn't beat a child for a fault that isn't wilful obstinacy, because that just teaches them to fear the rod rather than to love the right. Anyway, my sense is that Locke's picture is actually a lot more directive than the relaxed ML folks are imagining: the educator in Locke is constantly manipulating the child's environment, associations, habits, even their reputation, not just providing a beneficial environment and hoping the kid comes out okay. So here's the question: how far does the training-as-parenting analogy really hold? Give me a correspondence table — what maps to the parent, the child's nature, the curriculum, the discipline, the eighteen years — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Specifically, does anything in Locke's account of education transfer to model training in a way that should make people either more or less relaxed about alignment? Or was the analogy quietly doing all the work for the relaxed conclusion? And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually think about this; don't present one school's take as if it were the consensus.
