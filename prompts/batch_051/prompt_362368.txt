I teach a graduate philosophy of science seminar and we've been stuck for two weeks on Milton Friedman's old F-twist from his essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics" — the idea, roughly, that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant so long as the model yields sufficiently accurate predictions for the purpose at hand. One of my students keeps saying this is just an unfalsifiable methodological slogan, while a colleague in the economics department insists it's a substantive claim about how good economic models actually are. I'm tired of the usual talking points.

I'd like you to act as an expert philosopher of economics and scientific methodology, someone who actually knows the difference between predictive and explanatory adequacy. Consider the specific class of idealizing assumptions in economics like perfect competition, zero transaction costs, or common knowledge of rationality. The conceptual claim on the table is this: if a model that incorporates such blatantly false assumptions nonetheless predicts observed market aggregates well, then those assumptions are not, by themselves, a legitimate ground for criticizing or distrusting the model. Your task is to design the best empirical or historical test you can to distinguish that claim from a rival view, call it assumption realism, which holds that predictive success alone is insufficient and that at least some degree of realisticness in core assumptions is required for a model to be trustworthy for novel interventions or explanations. Be concrete: what data would you collect, what comparisons would you run, and exactly what outcome would favor each side. Then tell me what the test would not settle even if it came out cleanly — for instance, whether the real dispute is about explanation versus prediction, or about extrapolation to new regimes. Start by brainstorming two or three possible tests, then pick the strongest one and defend it.
