I've been reading a nineteenth-century essay by T. H. Huxley defending the view that animals—and human beings—are conscious automata, and there is a passage I keep returning to without being able to settle what it really commits him to. As I understand it, Huxley compares consciousness to the steam whistle of a railway engine. The whistle is produced by the engine's machinery, but it does not drive the wheels; it is a byproduct of the real causal work. In the same way, he suggests, states of consciousness are generated by the nervous system but play no role in determining what the organism does. Even our sense of willing an action, on this picture, is an accompanying symptom of neural activity rather than a cause of the movement.

What I find puzzling is not the analogy itself but the position it leaves Huxley in when he then makes claims about consciousness. If my feeling of pain is an inert byproduct of the brain processes that also cause me to wince or to say that I am in pain, then the feeling appears to be causally irrelevant to the report. The belief that I am in pain would be caused entirely by neural machinery, not by the pain itself. But then in what sense is my report about the pain? Is there a way to read Huxley's view so that it does not undermine the reliability of our judgments about our own conscious states? I would like help reconstructing the argument in the passage and seeing whether an epiphenomenalist can consistently claim to know, or truthfully speak about, the very consciousness that is supposed to be causally idle.
