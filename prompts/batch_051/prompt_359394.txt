Part of my beat is the way legal doctrines quietly encode contested philosophical assumptions, and I am currently working on a piece about what statutes of limitations assume about time and wrongdoing. The usual justifications are pragmatic: evidence decays, memories fade, and a legal system wants repose. But I want to pressure-test a deeper question—whether the passage of time itself has any intrinsic moral significance—by staging an adversarial collaboration between two camps. Call the first camp time-neutralists: they hold that temporal distance is never by itself a basic ground of moral difference, and that if blame, punishment, or a demand for repair becomes less apt decades after a wrong, it must be because of something that merely correlates with time, such as decaying evidence, the wrongdoer's changed character, social reconciliation, or the victim's changed stakes. Call the second camp temporalists: they hold that bare temporal distance can itself do moral work, so that the relation between a wrongdoer and an old wrong, or a community's standing to condemn, can change simply because of how much time has passed, over and above any non-temporal changes. To keep the discussion from collapsing into familiar points about evidence and reliability, stipulate throughout a discovery scenario: the wrong occurred decades ago, but its discovery and the identification of the wrongdoer happened only recently, the evidence is strong, and the psychological continuity of the parties can be varied case by case, so that what is being isolated is what bare time contributes.

Draft the joint statement such a collaboration should produce. Include (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, written to be neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side; (2) a disagreements table giving the precise residual difference, with each position stated as its own proponents would accept; (3) a list of cruxes—sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side—and, for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where part of the dispute looks merely verbal, say which part and why. Begin with the joint statement itself, with no preface or commentary.
