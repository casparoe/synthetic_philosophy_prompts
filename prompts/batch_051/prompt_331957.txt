writing this down partly to get it straight myself and partly because i need a second pair of eyes. my current commitments on care ethics, more or less:

(1) care ethics is a full normative theory on its own, not just a corrective to kantian or utilitarian approaches
(2) the main object of moral evaluation should be caring relations and the practices that sustain them, not discrete acts or character traits
(3) partiality can be morally proper — a parent owing special attention to their own kid isnt a bias to overcome, its kind of the point of caring
(4) but institutions like schools, hospitals, and courts ought to be impartial, and care ethics should be able to say why favoritism there is wrong without just declaring justice a separate domain
(5) im drawn to moral particularism: there are no context-independent rules that settle what's right, and judgment is always local
(6) genuine caring is only complete when it lands with the cared-for — their uptake is part of what makes it care, not mere well-intentioned effort or skill
(7) still, care ethics should offer a criterion of right action, something like: the right act is the one that, in its context, best sustains or deepens the caring relations at stake

i honestly cant tell which of these are in real tension and which only seem to be. can you go through them and say which pairs actually pull against each other and which only look like they're in tension, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each one tell me what the cheapest fix would be? and if a single change would buy me the most coherence, point me to it — including what that change would cost.
