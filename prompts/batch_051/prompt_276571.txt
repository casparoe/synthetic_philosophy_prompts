I've been chewing over the ethics of biopics and historical fiction, and Peter Shaffer's Amadeus (the play and the Milos Forman film) keeps coming back to me as the hardest test case. The film deliberately gives us Antonio Salieri as a musically second-rate court composer whose envy of Mozart curdles into something close to a plot: he anonymously commissions the Requiem, exploits Mozart's poverty and illness, and more or less works him into his grave, with the whole story framed as old Salieri's confession from an asylum. The real Salieri was by all accounts a hugely successful and respected composer and teacher (Schubert, Liszt, and for a spell Beethoven all studied with him), and the idea that he actually brought about Mozart's death is rumor that the film inflates into drama. I want a reading of Amadeus through a single question: what, if anything, do we owe the real dead when we dramatize them? Take both the film and the philosophy seriously. Does the film wrong Salieri, and what sort of wrong is it—defamation of a man who can't sue, an injury to a reputation that the living still hold in trust for him, an offense against the truth itself, or something else? Does the asylum-confession frame let the film off the hook, since what we're getting is a jealous man's remembered version rather than an omniscient narrator's, or does it end up smearing him just the same? And I want the reading to be honest about the places where the film resists the moralizing: where it's not just a libel but is doing something artistically real that a scrupulously accurate biopic might miss. End with a verdict, even a conflicted one.
