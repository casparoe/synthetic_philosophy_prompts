I have no training in philosophy; I run a small family firm and have spent years watching a particular dynamic repeat itself, and I want to know whether the field has a name for it.

Two examples, so you can see the pattern I mean. In my firm, for a long time my uncle was the one whose opinions mattered. My sister, who manages the day-to-day work, would constantly ask what he thought, repeat his judgments to customers, and arrange things so that he was the one who had to sign off. He almost never asked what she thought, and when she proposed something he treated it as a request for permission rather than as a judgment to be weighed. The striking thing is how this ended. Over the years she became quietly self-assured, because she was the one actually holding the place together, while he became brittle and could not make a decision unless she was there to notice and approve of his choice — even though, on paper, she was the subordinate and he was the one being looked up to.

The second example is from a voluntary association I belong to. A long-standing officer was treated with great deference by a younger member: the younger member always quoted her, always asked for her view before speaking, and never received the same treatment back. Again the subordinate one gradually became the person others actually relied on, and the esteemed one became thin-skinned and dependent on the continued deference of that one follower.

So the thing I am trying to name is not, or not mainly, the whole famous story of master and servant. It is the lopsided arrangement itself — one side doing the recognizing, the other side existing as the recognized one — together with the reversal in which the recognizing side seems to gain its independence while the recognized side is hollowed out. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration, and is the reversal a separate phenomenon or part of the same one? If the nearest concept is the master–servant (or lord–bondsman) dialectic, I want to know how the thing I am describing differs from other parts of that story, and whether the specific asymmetry I keep seeing has a name of its own. I am after the carving, not a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are. If there is no established name for this exact piece, say so, and propose one with a definition and boundary cases.

I later came across a sentence in Hegel's Phenomenology of Mind (1807; the early twentieth-century English translation by J. B. Baillie), in the part of "Self-Consciousness" usually called "Lordship and Bondage," at §185:

"It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes."

I read "one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes" as close to the lopsided arrangement I described, and I want this sentence unpacked carefully. In plain language, what is the "middle term," what are the "extremes," and are the "two" two people, two roles, or two phases? Does Hegel mark off this asymmetry as a stage with its own name or concept, distinct from what comes later in the section? Is it fair to read it as a relationship between two people, or is he describing something more abstract? And does the reversal I have seen — the recognizer growing and the recognized one becoming dependent — match what Hegel himself says later, or am I importing a modern, psychological reading into an older text? Finally, please separate what someone who teaches this text would state as plainly correct from what is genuinely disputed among careful readers, and tell me where your own reading is a contested one rather than presenting it as the consensus.
