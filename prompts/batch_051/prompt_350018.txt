I've got this little theory about letters I've been poking at for a while, and I'd like someone to be honest about whether its doing any real work. I call it tolerance budgets. The thought is: a letter is not a shape but a region in a space of deformations. Slant it, fatten the strokes, raise the x-height, add serifs, tighten the curves -- none of that changes the fact that you're looking at an 'a'. But push too far and you've crossed into a 'd' or a 'q' or an 'o'; the boundary is just wherever the identity flips. Letter identity, on this view, is path-connectedness: two shapes are the same letter iff you can deform one into the other continuously without ever leaving the region. Style is what happens when you apply the same deformation to every letter in a font at once, moving the whole alphabet together and keeping each letter inside its private budget; a font breaks when exactly one character gets pushed over its boundary, which is why a bad 'g' ruins an otherwise lovely typeface.

The problem is that the theory dies on my two favorite cases. First, the single-storey and double-storey 'a'. There's no path from one to the other that stays an 'a' the whole trip -- at some point you're holding a shape that is neither version, or not an 'a' at all -- yet we all treat the two as the same letter, in roman and italic and handwriting alike. So either path-connectedness is too strong or I have to say they're two separate letters that happen to share a keyboard slot, which feels like admitting defeat. Second, I can't actually name the dimensions of the deformation space, and I suspect the 'boundaries' are just my own faimilarity with a few hundred typefaces. Maybe tolerance budgets is family resemblance with nicer geometry. So: is this already a thing under a better name, where exactly does it break, and is the path-connectedness part worth salvaging or should I bin it?
