I've been trying to think clearly, on my own, about why desecrating something sacred can seem wrong even when no one is hurt or offended. I'm writing a short essay for a general audience defending the idea that some wrongs are not just harm or offence, and I want a case built to order, because the cases I come up with always turn out to have a harmed or offended person hidden somewhere in them.

The case should be one paragraph or less and completely realistic. A person, acting alone, deliberately treats an object that is sacred to a living community to which they belong with genuine contempt — the sort of act a sanctity-based view would call desecration. Use an ordinary kind of sacred object: a consecrated vessel, an icon, a copy of scripture, something like that. Avoid human bodies, graves, and remains, and do not make the moral judgment depend on God, supernatural punishment, or an afterlife.

The scenario must meet all of these conditions at once. No one else witnesses the act or ever learns of it. No one's feelings are hurt and no one is offended. No one's plans, relationships, or practices are disrupted or exposed to any risk. The object is left physically unchanged in every observable respect. The person is not caught and is not made worse off — no guilt, no distress, no loss — and no other person or animal is affected through any causal chain. And yet the act itself, as described, must still clearly amount to desecration according to the sanctity view, not merely a private uncharitable thought.

The two views I want the case to separate are these. The harm-or-offence view says an act is not wrong unless it harms some actual person or animal, or seriously offends someone who actually encounters or learns of it. The sanctity view says certain objects have a set-apart status such that treating them with contempt is wrong in itself, independently of anyone's awareness, distress, or offence. The scenario should stipulate only what physically and psychologically happens, not what moral conclusion follows.

After you lay out the scenario, walk through what each view says and why. Then do three checks. Identify the single feature of the case doing the real work. Say what happens if that feature is removed. And give the strongest honest resistance a defender of the harm-or-offence view would put up — for instance, that I have built in an unfairly narrow notion of harm or offence, or that people have an interest in sacred things even when they never learn what happened — and state whether the sanctity view can meet that resistance without quietly amending its own rule.
