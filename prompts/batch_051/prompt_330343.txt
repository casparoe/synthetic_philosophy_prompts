Two friends of mine got into it over lunch after one of them described her job designing the cancellation flow for a meditation app; she's the growth-side UX researcher. I kept waiting for the debate to bottom out in some clean criterion, and it sort of didn't.

(1) The first said this is manipulation, full stop. The team A/B-tests the cancellation screen, and the winner exploits loss aversion and status quo bias: it makes you look at your 43-day streak and click through two extra screens before you can cancel. That's not persuading someone, he said, that's engineering a decision against them.

(2) The second replied that "uses psychological levers" can't be the line, because all persuasion does that. Warning someone about a bad deal is using loss aversion. Choosing when in a conversation to make a request is using timing and framing. Being likable or visibly trustworthy moves people too, and you can't switch that off. If all of that is manipulation, then the word just means successful persuasion.

(3) First came back: the difference is whether the method can survive transparency. He'd be fine telling someone "I'm bringing up the risks because I want you to feel the downside vividly." The app's design is hidden exactly because it would fail if seen—the team would never say "we added two extra screens to make canceling harder." Hidden-because-it-would-fail is the tell.

(4) Second said the transparency test is too clean. A con man can announce that he's working you and still work you, so transparency isn't sufficient for legitimacy. And most everyday influence is invisible without being manipulative: a friend changes the subject away from a topic you're not ready for, a teacher sequences examples in an order she doesn't explain. So you can't condemn the app merely for not advertising its methods.

I couldn't tell who had the better of it, and they mostly talked past each other. Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say what each is right about and what both are missing. Where you can, seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who've thought about this, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
