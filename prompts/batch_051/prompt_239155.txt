In forming beliefs about matters I cannot verify directly, I have come to follow a two-tier policy concerning testimony. For claims that are routine, checkable, or low-stakes — a colleague tells me a meeting has been moved, a neighbour reports that a road is closed — I accept what I am told unless something specific speaks against it. For claims that reach me through a chain of reports — a friend summarising a scientist's findings, a journalist relaying a leaked document she has not seen, an acquaintance recounting what a third party told them — I apply a stricter test. Before I act on, or fully accept, a chained claim, I require, for each link in the chain, positive evidence that the person at that link is competent and sincere with respect to the specific type of content they are transmitting. That the speaker is generally sensible, or has been right about other kinds of matter, does not satisfy the test; I want evidence keyed to the domain of the claim.

The practical result is that I almost never have what the rule demands for every link, and so I treat most second-hand claims — which means most of what I hear about science, history, politics, and current affairs — as something I cannot rationally believe. I find this costly but honest: it keeps me from being a conduit for errors that compound as a story passes through successive hands. A friend says the rule is self-undermining: applied consistently, it strips away almost all of my factual beliefs, including the background beliefs on which the rule's own plausibility judgements depend. I reply that this may simply be the cost of not outsourcing my judgement.

I know that philosophers writing on testimony divide, very roughly, between those who think testimonial belief needs positive evidence of the speaker's reliability and those who think testimony carries a default entitlement unless defeated. I do not want a survey of that debate. What I want is an assessment of my policy as a policy: where it is likely to be reliable and where it is likely to fail; whether the slide into near-total withholding is a reductio of the policy or only of its present, rigid form; whether there is a restated version that keeps the strictness for high-stakes or easily-distorted claims without sacrificing the benefits of ordinary testimonial trust; and whether the policy is defensible on its merits or is largely a rationalisation of a cautious temperament — and how I could tell the difference from the inside. Are several independent chains of testimony ever an adequate substitute for the positive, link-by-link evidence I am demanding, and under what conditions? Given that I cannot personally audit most links, is my actual choice between greater credulity and believing far less, and is the second stance defensible, or is it a scepticism with the same arbitrariness as the first? Please write your answer in British English.
