I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I'm putting together a session on Nozick's tracking theory for an advanced undergraduate seminar. The class has twelve students and meets for seventy-five minutes, and they have already worked through Nozick's idea that knowledge is true belief that tracks the truth, along with the familiar difficulty that this view seems to force us to abandon unrestricted epistemic closure: an ordinary proposition can be known because the belief is sensitive, while a logical consequence of it fails to count as known because the corresponding belief would not be sensitive in the relevant counterfactual situations. My students tend to treat the closure problem as a dry formal trade-off, so I want an activity that makes them feel the appeal of sensitivity first and then feel the cost of losing closure when it happens to them, rather than just being told about it. I'm imagining a small game, perhaps with cards or role assignments standing in for different doxastic bases, such as a reliable instrument, a lucky guess, a stopped clock that happens to be right, or a testimonial source that is reliable in nearby worlds but not in more distant ones. Participants would have to assemble and defend a portfolio of beliefs under a scoring rule that rewards sensitivity, gaining points for truths they believe in the actual scenario and losing points for beliefs they would hold in nearby scenarios where the proposition is false; then, partway through, the facilitator imposes a rule that portfolios must be closed under logical consequence, and the collision with the scoring rule should surface on its own. I want them to leave seeing why the sensitivity condition is initially attractive, why closure is genuinely difficult to surrender, and why someone like Nozick might keep sensitivity and accept restricted closure as a live position even if most of them ultimately reject it.
