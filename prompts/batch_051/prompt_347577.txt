I have a set of verdicts about invented languages that appear to lack pronouns, indexicals, or both, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern behind them. By an indexical I mean an expression whose reference depends systematically on the context of utterance — paradigm cases are I, you, here, now, this, that. By a pronoun I mean the grammatical category that spans personal and demonstrative pronouns, and I want to leave open whether indexicality can be eliminated merely by changing word classes. Below are my cases and verdicts.

1. Names-only for persons. A language has no first- or second-person pronouns. Each speaker uses their own proper name where I would go and the addressee's proper name where you would go, so a speaker named Mira says the equivalent of Mira is hungry where our translation would say I am hungry. My verdict: this does not eliminate indexicality. Each name is being used in two ways, ordinary referential and self- or addressee-anchored, and the self-anchored use is an indexical device wearing a proper name as a disguise.

2. Pointing instead of demonstratives. A signed language has no lexical signs for this, that, here, I, or you; it relies on physical pointing at objects, locations, chests, and interlocutors. My verdict: pointing is itself an indexical sign, so the language has not removed indexicality from the communicative system, only from its lexicon or its spoken equivalent. Whatever matters here has not been removed.

3. Absolute time, no now. A language has no temporal indexicals and no tense morphology; every assertion pins an event to an absolute date and clock time rather than to the moment of speech. My verdict: as a stored record or chronicle, this is a genuine case of getting by without temporal indexicals. But I also think a speaker who must act in the world would still need to know which of the dated claims picks out the present moment, and that is indexical knowledge. I suspect these two thoughts pull against each other.

4. The utterer rule. A language contains a word glossed as I, and its stated semantic rule is that the word denotes the utterer of the utterance in which it occurs. Someone claims this is no longer an indexical because it has been defined away as a definite description. My verdict: the description the utterer of the utterance still contains an implicit this or that, and in any case a word whose reference is fixed to the utterer is exactly what an indexical is. The definition does not eliminate anything.

5. No spatial demonstratives. A spoken language has no this, that, here, or there. Reference to objects and places always goes through proper names, absolute coordinates, or descriptions anchored to a named person, such as the chair three metres due north of Mira's body. My verdict: if the descriptions mention a person's body or position, the indexicality is inherited rather than removed; and if they use only absolute coordinates, a speaker still has to locate the coordinate relative to themselves to make the language useful, which again seems to presuppose indexical self-location.

6. The pure-record language. A ceremonial language is used only for archival inscriptions, never for conversation, questions, or commands. It contains names, dates, and non-deictic descriptions, and nothing else; a typical inscription says the equivalent of On the third day after the death of the king, the priest placed the bowl in the eastern chamber. My verdict: this genuinely lacks indexicals, and it works for its purpose; but I am reluctant to call it a full natural language, because it is structurally incapable of being used for situated action.

That is my set. I ask four things.

(a) What is the best principle, or the best small family of principles, that captures my verdicts? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause.

(b) What does the principle say about cases I have not listed — for instance, a language with first- and second-person pronouns but no third-person pronouns at all; a language whose only remaining indexicals are a single family of demonstratives; and a community of speakers who always know, by reliable non-linguistic means, what time it is and where they are, but have no linguistic way to express I, here, or now?

(c) Is there a coherent account behind all of this, or are some of my verdicts in tension? In particular, is my verdict in case 3 consistent with my verdict in case 6, and does my claim in case 1 that name-use can be an indexical in disguise trivialize the whole question? Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which, if any, I should drop.

(d) State what the surviving position implies about the original question: could a natural language genuinely lack pronouns, and could one genuinely lack indexicals, or is the most that can be achieved a change in the surface form — names, gestures, dates, coordinates — while the underlying indexical capacity remains?
