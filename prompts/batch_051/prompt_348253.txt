Ok so for a philosophy unit at school I've been trying to pin down what an "intelligence explosion" actually is, because people use the term for everything from "autocomplete got better" to "the world ends in paperclips." The criteria I came up with are: (1) there's a loop where the AI improves its own intelligence (designing its own chips or training code) instead of just being improved by humans; (2) the loop has increasing returns, so each round of improvement makes the next round easier or faster; (3) the pace of improvement accelerates enough that it's not just normal technological progress; and (4) after some point the AI is the main driver of the process, with humans just managing constraints from outside.

Now here's the case I keep going back and forth on. Suppose an AI is better than any human at designing chips and writing training algorithms. It can use its current hardware and training code to design a next-generation chip and a next-generation training run. But the chip has to be physically fabbed in a normal semiconductor plant, which takes about two years and depends on a whole supply chain—lithography machines, rare minerals, power plants, shipping—that intelligence alone can't speed up much. Each cycle gives a solid improvement, say a 30% gain in effective capability per generation, and because each new AI is smarter, it's next design is better than if the previous generation had done the same work. So there is a genuine self-improvement loop and genuine compounding. But the rate is set by fabs and supply chains, not by the AI thinking faster, and from the outside the whole thing looks like a tech company grinding through hardware generations, just with a smarter designer each time. Half my criteria say yes and half say no, which is why I'm stuck.

Go through my four criteria and say which ones the case meets, which it fails, and where my criteria are too vague to settle it. Then the thing I actually care about: is this a slow takeoff, a fast takeoff, or neither? Does my case show that the fast/slow distinction is really about whether there's a self-improvement loop, or about the doubling time, or about how much of the process is being driven by the AI versus by ordinary supply chains, or am I missing something? Also, if you had to change one of my four criteria to make the case come out clearly one way, which one would you change and why?
