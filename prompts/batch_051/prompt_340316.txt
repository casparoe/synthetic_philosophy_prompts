Population ethics reading group has gotten stuck in a loop: every week someone proposes an axiology, someone else finds a counterexample involving trillions of lives that are barely worth living, or a world with a few extra people added at no cost to anyone, and we never get anywhere. Before I read anything else, I want to step back and ask what a theory of population axiology actually has to do to count as adequate for the job — where the job is comparing possible worlds in which different people exist, and saying which is better all-things-considered. Write me the desiderata. I'm thinking of things like: getting same-number cases right, avoiding the repugnant conclusion (or explaining why we shouldn't), handling mere addition without quietly collapsing into totalism or anti-natalism, transitivity, some kind of separability across unaffected individuals, and a no-sadism-type constraint (roughly, not making it better to add extra people whose lives are worse than nothing). For each one give the case or consideration behind it, and say whether it is a non-negotiable condition of adequacy or more of a camp preference that a coherent view could drop.

Then the part I most care about: which combinations of these cannot all be satisfied at once. I keep hearing vague talk of impossibility results in the area, and I don't know how much to panic. If the full list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say exactly which joint package breaks and what the most defensible weakening is. Keep the focus on the criteria and the tensions between them rather than a full scoring of existing views; though if a desideratum is mainly there just to rule out one candidate, flag that.
