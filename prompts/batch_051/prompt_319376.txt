He swears it's the one thing he remembers from his philosophy of language unit; my line was that it can't be that simple, because saying 'it's raining' doesn't seem to do anything except describe. Anyway, he later sent me a summary he reckons is a fair account of J.L. Austin, which I think he copied from some unnamed article (or maybe his old lecture notes, he can't remember). I don't know enough to tell if its fair, which is why I'm asking.

He sent me this: 'According to Austin, sentences aren't just for describing how things are. Some, called performatives, don't report facts; they perform an action. When the registrar says "I now pronounce you married", she isn't just describing a marriage—her words actually bring one about. A performative only succeeds if the speaker is the right sort of person, the setting is appropriate, and the speaker is sincere. If the conditions aren't met, the performative is simply false, since it doesn't match reality. Austin eventually concluded that all language is like this: every utterance is a kind of act, so the descriptive–performative divide collapses. The upshot is that language builds social reality out of thin air, as long as the speaker genuinely intends what they are saying.'

On the strength of this, my friend concluded that an insincere 'I do' at a wedding says something false, and that Austin basically threw out truth and falsity as a useful distinction for language.
