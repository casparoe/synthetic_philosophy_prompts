A friend and I keep having the same argument about blackmail, and I want someone to actually locate the bad step instead of hand-waving at “coercion.”

Here is the version of the argument I wrote down after he left:

(1) Suppose I have learned something true and deeply embarrassing about you, through no wrongdoing of mine, and I am under no duty to conceal it. Telling people might be cruel, but (we can agree for the argument) it would not be impermissible.
(2) Asking you for a thousand dollars, all by itself, would also not be impermissible; nothing about a bare request for money is wrong.
(3) If two acts are each permissible on their own, then making one conditional on the other cant make the overall proposal impermissible; the package is not worse than its parts.
(4) Blackmail is exactly such a package: I ask you for a thousand dollars, and I make the asking conditional on not doing (1) — if you pay, I stay silent; if you do not pay, I tell everyone.
(5) So blackmail is permissible.
(6) But blackmail is not permissible.
(7) So at least one of (1)–(4) must be false.

Each premise sounds right to me seperately, which is why the thing will not stop bothering me. I would most like to keep (6), and after that I want to keep as much of (1) and (2) as I can, since they look like ordinary permissions everyone has. My suspicion is that the trouble is around (3) or (4), but I can also feel a version of the argument that attacks (1) or (2), so I want you to price the alternatives rather than just referee my suspicion.

Please answer as a moral philosopher who actually works on deontic logic and on the difference between threats and offers. Before you commit, it is fine to brainstorm the candidate diagnoses for a sentence or two. Then give me your verdict in this form:

- Lead with the numbered step you reject, or say outright that the argument is valid and I have to pick which premise costs the least to give up.
- Explain why that step looked innocent.
- Price the main alternatives, including what it costs to keep (3) but reject (4), and what it costs to keep (4) but reject (3).
- Keep the whole thing under 400 words, not counting any of the numbered argument you repeat.
