In my philosophy club two juniors got into a long argument and now I can't decide who was right. It started over littering. Marcus said he sometimes drops a granola bar wrapper on the trail when he runs, because one wrapper doesn't visibly change anything and the trail is already trashed. Priya said 'what if everyone did that?' Marcus said that's not an argument, it's a guilt trip: if everyone did it the trail would be wrecked, but everyone won't do it because of him, and him pocketing his wrapper doesn't causally make anyone else pocket theirs. Priya said its not a prediction, it's a consistency test — you have to be able to accept the rule 'people may litter when the impact is too small to notice' as a rule for everyone, and since Marcus obviously can't, he's just making himself an exception. Marcus said that proves to much, because tons of fine things would be terrible if everyone did them: 'what if everyone became a park ranger' or 'what if everyone did their long runs at 6am' — the world would fall apart, but his 6am run isn't wrong. Priya said those are different because one person doing them does no harm at all, whereas Marcus's wrapper does do a tiny bit of harm; it's just below the level he can see. So the real principle is something like: don't do small harms that add up, even when each one is below a threshold. Marcus said then she's stuck condemning half of what she herself does, like getting cheap chicken nuggets or driving two miles instead of walking, because each individual choice is a small harm in a bigger pile. Priya admitted she gets cheap chicken nuggets, but said that just shows she's inconsistent, not that the principle is false. Then the bell rang. Who was right about which specific points, and what are they both missing?
