Act as an analyst who studies how proposed rules and incentive schemes behave in practice, not how they are supposed to behave on paper. Work through the problem step by step, and begin by brainstorming the first several ways a self-interested person would try to exploit the scheme.

A city has been arguing about how to decide which of its problems to spend money on first. The difficulty is that it cannot simply ask citizens to compare one another's happiness or suffering directly, because nobody agrees how much one person's suffering is worth relative to another person's. A resident proposes the following scheme. Once a year, every adult receives a short list of ten circumstances, such as chronic back pain, living next to a noisy industrial plant, caring alone for a sick parent, a two-hour daily commute, or being unable to afford a dentist. Each person marks each circumstance on a personal scale from -100 to +100, according to how bad or good they believe that circumstance would be for them. The city averages these marks, one average per circumstance, and publishes them as an official Register of Hardships and Benefits. Whenever the council debates a project, its analysts estimate how many residents the project would move into or out of each listed circumstance, multiply those numbers by the official averages, and sum the results. The project with the highest total wins the money. The resident argues that this lets the city compare how much different policies help different people without ever comparing people directly: everyone votes only on circumstances, and no one person's scale is given more weight than anyone else's.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. How would it actually behave? In particular: what would residents be incentivized to do when they fill in their marks, how would organized groups try to move the official averages, what happens to people whose experience of a circumstance is far from the official average, and where does the scheme break first? Can its failures be fixed without quietly reintroducing the direct comparison of one person's well-being with another's that the scheme was meant to avoid, and what does your answer suggest about whether the scheme was ever really measuring a shared scale at all?
