When Nozick says, roughly, that the moral treatment of animals is to be worked out along utilitarian lines while the treatment of persons is governed by Kantian side constraints, I am unsure which part of that sentence is supposed to bear the normative weight. It sounds as if an animal’s pain is always eligible to be traded away for any aggregate gain, so that the only wrong-making feature left in cruelty to an animal is the failure to maximize overall utility. But Nozick himself rejects exactly that kind of unrestricted aggregative balancing in the case of persons. Is the correct reading that, for Nozick, animals are pure moral resources—like trees or rocks but with pleasures and pains that happen to count—or do they retain some kind of moral status that constrains what an owner or a passerby may do? I would like the answer to separate what his stated framework commits him to from what he left under-defined, and not to present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus among people who work on Nozick or animal ethics.
