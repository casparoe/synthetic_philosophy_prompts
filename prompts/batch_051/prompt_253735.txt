An essay I read gives the following account of Douglas Hofstadter's short satire 'A Person Paper on Purity in Language.' According to the essay, Hofstadter writes under the pseudonym William Satire and constructs an imaginary world in which language is sorted by race exactly as English is sorted by sex: white functions as the generic human category, so that the founding political document of that world declares all whites created equal, a committee is chaired by a chairwhite, and entering students are called freshwhites, while black people are singled out with the marked compound blackperson. In the satire, the imaginary language columnist who reports all this insists that these forms are perfectly neutral. The essay then states that Hofstadter's thesis is that sexist language of this kind directly produces sexist thought, and that his proposed remedy is to eliminate masculine generics from English altogether. It dismisses the piece with the observation that man in compounds like chairman is etymologically just an old word for person, and that ordinary speakers process it as gender-neutral anyway. That dismissal sounds plausible to me, but the overall summary feels suspiciously neat. How much of this account would a careful reader of the piece accept? Where exactly does it go wrong, what would a defender of the satire object to, and does the etymological dismissal survive the corrections?
