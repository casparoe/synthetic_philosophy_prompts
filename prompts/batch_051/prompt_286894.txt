At dinner last week I heard two freinds get properly worked up about a thought experiment one of them called a counterfactual mugging, and by the end I honestly couldn't say who was right. The setup they were arguing over (as I remember it):

An oracle flips a fair coin. If it lands heads, the oracle asks you for $100. If it lands tails, the oracle would have given you $1,000,000, but only if it had predicted that you would pay the $100 in the heads case. The oracle is stipulated to be perfectly reliable. You land in the heads case: you're being asked for the $100, and you know the coin came up heads. Should you pay?

Their back-and-forth went like this:

(1) Marcus said don't pay. The coin came up heads, so the tails branch is gone. Paying is just handing over $100 for nothing. "It's a mugging with a nice story attached."

(2) Dana said that's exactly the mistake. The oracle only puts the million in the tails case if you're the sort of agent who pays in the heads case. If you refuse, there's no tails case where you collect anything. If you pay, there is. You can't change the coin, but you can change which policy the coin got flipped under. The policy pay-if-asked is worth half a million minus fifty in expectation.

(3) Marcus said that's double-counting. Once you've been asked, you've updated on being in the heads case; the probability of heads isn't one half anymore, it's one. Conditional on what you now know, paying is minus $100, full stop. The million is in a branch your choices can't reach, so it isn't an outcome of your choice.

(4) Dana said yeah, but before the oracle told you the coin came up heads, you would have paid good money to precommit to paying-in-heads. Refusing now is just failing to act like the precommitted version of you, so you're leaving behind the ex ante value you'd have bought. The version of you who precommits makes the million, the version of you who updates doesn't.

(5) Marcus said this has to generalize to any con that says "in some other possible world I'd have made you rich if you'd done X." The million never actually arrives, and you can't tell the oracle apart from an ordinary trickster saying the same words. If you can't distinguish them enough to act on the difference, you shouldn't pay either one.

(6) Dana said you can distinguish them: the oracle is stipulated to be perfect. The uncertainty is over the coin, not the predictor. The lottery is over the coin just like any other lottery, except the payment happens in a branch that doesn't occur. She admitted it made her head hurt.
