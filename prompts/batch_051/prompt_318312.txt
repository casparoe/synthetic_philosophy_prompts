Consider the following claim about human values: the appearance of many is an illusion, and in fact everything people ultimately value is one thing in disguise. On this view, loyalty, fairness, curiosity, comfort, beauty, and piety look like distinct ends, but each is really an instrument or local idiom of a single underlying currency—felt satisfaction, say, or reproductive fitness or social status—and the pluralist is mistaking the denominations for the money. Taken as an empirical claim about motivation, this should be testable at least in part. Design the strongest test you can, and then say exactly where its evidence runs out.

Address each of the following:

(1) Propose two or three concretely operationalized methods that could bear on the question—for example, a forced-choice study in which subjects allocate scarce time or money across projects serving different values, a factor analysis of a broad battery of stated-value items, repeated sampling of real choices to check whether the same ordering reappears across domains, or neural or behavioral measures—and specify precisely what patterns of results would favor single-currency monism and what would favor genuine pluralism.

(2) Explain how one would distinguish a true common currency from artifacts: a general desirability or positivity bias in ratings, the fact that any finite set of correlated measures will have a first principal component, post hoc rationalization of choices, or a shared measurement method rather than a shared motive.

(3) Say what even an unambiguous result would not settle—in particular, whether the "one thing" does causal work or is merely a compact redescription, whether the finding would concern what people in fact pursue or what they ought to pursue, and whether the question "one value or many?" was well-posed before the operationalization began.
