I've been going back and forth on the bivalence thing — the claim that every declarative sentence is determinately true or false — for genuinely years, ever since I went down the heap/vagueness rabbit hole on the rationalist side of the internet. The frustrating thing is that the academic version and the forum version seem stuck in the exact same place. Everyone agrees that a borderline case in a sorites sequence (the guy with, I don't know, 47% of his scalp covered, whatever that means) is not straightforwardly true or false in the way "2+2=4" is. But the epistemicists, the supervaluationists, the many-valueders, and the presupposition-failure camp have barely moved in decades. Same edge cases, same moves. The minute someone mentions a heap, I can already write the other side's reply before they type it.

So here's what I'd like: diagnose the structure of the disagreement, don't adjudicate it. My current hypothesis is that these four camps are partly talking past each other because they're using "true" and "false" to do different jobs in their overall picture of language. The epistemicist wants to keep classical logic clean, so they bite the bullet that "bald" secretly has a sharp cutoff we're ignorant of. The supervaluationist wants to keep excluded middle while saying individual borderline predications are neither true nor false — it's only the whole disjunction (bald or not bald) that comes out true on every admissible sharpening. The many-valued person thinks the true/false binary was always a lossy compression and that truth in borderline cases should be a degree or a lattice value. The presupposition-failure person thinks the problem is earlier: borderline sentences don't manage to assert a proposition at all, so the question of truth or falsity doesn't even arise. That reads like four different subject matters wearing the same trenchcoat, but i might be underestimating how much of it is a genuine fight about one underlying thing — like whether logic is answerable to semantics or semantics answerable to logic, or whether it's ever legitimate to posit an unknowable sharp boundary to save a formalism.

So which kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it partly verbal, partly different questions under one heading, partly a disagreement about which cases are the data (the assertion patterns of ordinary speakers versus the requirements of a tidy logic)? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — e.g. about whether the rival accounts can be translated into each other modally or about what counts as an admissable precisification — that would collapse the whole thing one way or another if it got settled? Or is it just bedrock intuitions about whether bivalence is a norm of inquiry rather than an empirical claim about language?

And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like — a formal equivalence or inequivalence result, a criterion for picking which feature of ordinary assertion the formalism is answerable to, a decision that the question was badly posed and the interesting residue is elsewhere? Or should I conclude that no progress is to be expected and treat the bivalence debate as one of those permanently reloading disputes?
