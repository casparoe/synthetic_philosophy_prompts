My housemate has an argument he refuses to stop bringing up, and i can't find the exit. He's convinced there's something not just awkward but incoherent about assigning probabilities to your own decisions. I'll write it out in numbered form because he's all over the place. (1) A rational person's credence in anything should be their best estimate of how likely it is given their total evidence. (2) Your own future choices are just events in the world, and you can have excellent evidence about them: habits, track records, how you've behaved in near-identical situations. (3) So if you're rational, you should sometimes have fairly confident credences about what you're about to choose — say 0.8 that you'll stay late at the party, because you've done exactly that eight of the last ten times you swore you'd leave early. (4) But if you're actually deliberating about whether to leave early, then holding a 0.8 credence that you'll stay late either makes the choice a self-fulfilling prophecy (you'll fold your decision into your self-image as a person who stays late) or a self-undermining one (you'll feel pigeonholed and leave early just to surprise yourself). Either way, the credence isn't functioning as a neutral prediction, and deliberation is crowded out. (5) And the track record in (2) doesn't go away just because you're deliberating; ignoring it would be a different kind of irrationality, pretending you're a blank slate. (6) So every time a reflective person has usable self-knowledge, they can't genuinely deliberate, and every time they deliberate they must suppress usable self-knowledge. That seems way too strong — it would make a person with a stable character either systematically irrational or permanently unable to decide. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if i can; the conclusion in (6) is clearly too much. Talk me through which premise goes and what i give up by rejecting it.
