I've been going in circles with a friend over moral uncertainty and found an argument that seems to prove too much. Numbered out:

(1) When I'm not sure which moral theory is true, I should do the option with the highest expected choice-worthiness: for each theory I take seriously, multiply my credence in it by how choice-worthy it says each option is, then sum and pick the winner.
(2) Suppose I'm torn between utilitarianism and a deontological view on which lying is absolutely forbidden, even to prevent a massacre.
(3) On the deontological view, lying carries negative choice-worthiness so large that no amount of goods consequences could ever outweigh it.
(4) Since I assign that view nonzero credence, any option involving a lie gets a massively negative expected choice-worthiness.
(5) So I should never lie, even if a lie would save an entire city.
(6) That's absurd; a framework that turns anyone with a sliver of deontological doubt into an absolute deontologist is broken, so (1) must be false.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep something like (1) than swallow (5), but I can't see what else is doing the work.
