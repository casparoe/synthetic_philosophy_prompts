I'm a high school student, and over the past year I've been reading about the inefficacy objection, expected-value reasoning in ethics, and what individuals owe when many people's small contributions add up to a large harm. I've settled on a working set of commitments, and I'd like you to carefully audit whether they actually hang together. Please approach this as a professional philosopher. Take your time, and before you evaluate, restate each commitment in your own words and map out the relations between them. Don't flag every pair that looks superficially in tension, because I suspect some apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection and I want to know which those are.

Here are my commitments:

1. Buying factory-farmed chicken is wrong for me, because my purchase has a small but real chance of being the marginal purchase that keeps the supplier's production at its current level, and the expected animal suffering that rides on that chance is large enough to make the purchase wrong.

2. I don't think I have a duty to cut my own carbon emissions, for example by not flying or by giving up beef. The climate system is so diffuse that no individual emission makes a difference; there is no marginal trigger that my own action tips.

3. For things like sweatshop-made clothing, the right response is political and structural, not individual consumption choices. Consumers have too little leverage for individual abstention to matter much, and it is not reasonable to require someone to trace every purchase through a long supply chain.

4. In my charitable giving I am a fairly strict act-utilitarian: I give where the marginal dollar has the highest expected impact, and considerations like physical proximity, personal ties, or whether the outcome counts as "my share" don't carry much weight.

5. But I think I should vote in national elections, even though the chance that my single vote decides the outcome is essentially zero. My reason is not expected decisiveness; it's that staying home would be free-riding on the other voters whose participation makes the practice work.

6. I don't think "what if everyone did that?" is by itself a sufficient argument that an act is wrong. For a duty to exist, either my act has to make some expected difference, or the act has to be independently wrong.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which commitments, if any, pull against each other in ways I might not have noticed? Which pairs are only apparently in tension, and why? Rank the genuine tensions by how serious they are, and for each say what would have to give to restore consistency, and what I would be giving up by dropping one commitment rather than another.
