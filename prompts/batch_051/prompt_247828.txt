Question: Explain the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about pleasure. Does the fact that pleasures differ so widely in kind show that a hedonist cannot compare or aggregate them in amount? In your answer, discuss whether Mill's appeal to higher and lower pleasures is best read as a solution to the problem or as a concession of it.

Student answer: Hedonism about well-being says that the only thing good in itself is pleasure, and the only thing bad in itself is pain. The heterogeneity problem begins from the observation that the states we call pleasures are enormously varied. Consider eating a rich meal, listening to music, solving a hard puzzle, feeling relief when a danger passes, or taking satisfaction in someone else's failure. These do not feel alike. If we introspect, we cannot find one salient felt quality running through all of them; what the meal and the proof have in common is not something we can point to in experience. Since hedonism needs pleasure to be the single unit in which all well-being is measured, the absence of any common felt quality is a serious blow. It suggests that there is no such thing as pleasure, only a loose family of pleasures united by nothing deeper than the fact that we happen to use one English word for them. And if that is right, then hedonism is trying to add together items with no common measure. Comparing the pleasure of reading a poem with the pleasure of winning a game would be like comparing apples and oranges. For that reason the heterogeneity problem is best understood as a refutation of hedonism about well-being, not merely as a request for a better definition. A hedonist might reply that the common element is not a felt quality but an attitude of liking, wanting, or approving of these states. But this only pushes the problem back a step, because the attitude itself differs across cases as much as the pleasures do; liking a symphony is not the same sort of mental act as liking a joke, so no unified measure has been found. Mill's appeal to higher and lower pleasures is often used to answer this problem: he orders pleasures by the preferences of competent judges, with intellectual pleasures ranked above bodily ones. But this is really an admission of defeat. Once one concedes that pleasures differ in kind, and that some kinds count for more not because they are more intense but because of their quality, one has abandoned the quantitative picture that hedonism rests on. Mill therefore saves hedonism only by turning it into a different kind of theory, one that no longer measures well-being in units of pleasure at all.
