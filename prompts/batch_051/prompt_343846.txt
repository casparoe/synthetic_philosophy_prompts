After our philosophy of science reading group last week, two friends got into it about the old evidence problem, and I got asked to referee. I'll lay out the argument as best I remember it, pretty much move for move.

Dan started with what he called the textbook problem. On a Bayeian picture, evidence e confirms a hypothesis h only if learning e would raise the probability of h. But when e is already known before h is ever formulated, P(e) is 1, so P(h|e) = P(h), and nothing gets confirmed. Yet the odd bit of Mercury's orbit that Newtonian gravity couldn't account for was known long before Einstein's general relativity, and when GR explained it, everyone treated that as a point in favor of GR. So, Dan said, either old evidence can confirm after all, or Bayesianism is broken as a story about science. (1)

Mara shot back that the thing that was new in GR's case wasn't Mercury's orbit. What was new was the proposition that GR entails, or at least predicts, the anomaly. That proposition had probability less than 1, and it got a big boost when people worked through the derivation. So a Bayesian can model old evidence fine; you just have to remember that the evidence being learned is "the theory accounts for e," not e itself. (2)

Dan said that was a dodge. If all the action is in the entailment claim, then the observed orbit itself does no confirmatory work; it's just a fact about the theory's logical relations. But the reason the Mercury fit impressed people had to do with the actual orbit, not with the bare fact that the theory implied it. Any crank theory entails lots of things; what mattered was that the thing it entailed was a real, previously stubborn observation. (3)

Mara replied that you can recover that intuition with a counterfactual prior. Ask what probability you would have assigned to the Mercury anomaly given GR, and given its negation, before you learned the anomaly. You don't have to actually forget what you know; you just need a distribution that treats the observation as uncertain. Run the usual conditionalization on that distribution and the old evidence confirms GR in the standard way. (4)

Dan said that was worse, not better. There's no principled way to choose the counterfactual prior, and if you let people pretend they don't know what they know, you can rig it to get whatever conclusion you want. He said Mara had replaced a clean formalism with a free parameter. (5)

Neither would concede, so they looked at me, and I honestly couldn't sort out which moves were good and which weren't.
