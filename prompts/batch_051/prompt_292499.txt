Two participants in a political philosophy reading group I attend recently debated an electoral reform package that a nearby city council has under consideration. The package would lower the municipal voting age from eighteen to sixteen, extend municipal voting to non-citizen permanent residents, and make municipal voting compulsory: eligible voters who fail to return a ballot would pay a modest fine, while a returned blank ballot would satisfy the obligation. They asked me to referee. I could not produce a clean verdict, so I am bringing the exchange to you.

Mara: Non-citizen permanent residents are governed by the same ordinances, taxes, and public services as citizens. Many have lived in the city for years, send children to its public schools, and run businesses under its rules, yet have no vote for the council that sets those rules. Sixteen-year-olds likewise pay sales taxes, work, and will live longest with the city's housing and transit decisions. The principle is simple: those who are governed should have a say in choosing their governors. Compulsory voting then ensures that the electorate is representative rather than skewed toward the old, the wealthy, and the highly mobilized.

Daniel: Being affected by a law is not the same as being competent to participate in making it. A sixteen-year-old typically lacks the independence and settled judgment that voting presupposes, and a non-citizen has not made the commitment that gives democratic decision its legitimacy. And your position contains a tension: you began by treating the franchise as a right that has been unjustly withheld, but then you want to fine people for failing to exercise it. Rights can be waived.

Mara: Competence is a red herring. Many adult citizens vote on slogans or sheer partisan loyalty; if we applied your standard honestly, large parts of the existing electorate would fail it. At the margins, every age and membership line is arbitrary, so the question is which arbitrary specification does less harm. Excluding a governed person is a serious wrong, because it leaves them subject to decisions with no recourse. Including a less-informed voter merely adds one more preference among many. And compulsory voting is not a contradiction; it treats participation as a civic duty attached to the right, more like jury service than an optional indulgence.

Daniel: Then the arbitrariness charge has not been met at all. If every line is arbitrary, your line at sixteen and at permanent residency is no less arbitrary than a line excluding tourists, visitors, or fourteen-year-olds. You have not stated a principle; you have chosen a more inclusive point and then called the choice principled. And compulsion makes the line coercive: it forces the disengaged and the uninformed to produce a vote, and then counts that produced vote as democratic legitimacy.

Mara: The inclusive line has the right asymmetry. If we wrongly exclude someone who is governed, they are governed without recourse, which is a serious wrong. If we wrongly include someone, the cost is one low-information vote among many, which is a trivial distortion. The burden of proof should therefore fall on the excluder, and erring at sixteen rather than eighteen is the natural application of that idea. Your coercion charge is also overstated: the proposal requires turnout, not an opinion. A blank ballot satisfies the obligation.

Daniel: Your blank-ballot concession only relocates the problem. If blank ballots are the escape hatch, then compulsory turnout is theater: a compliant population that signs in and leaves the decisions to everyone else. That produces the appearance of participation without the engagement that democratic legitimacy is supposed to reflect. And if the point of compulsion was to force real engagement, then allowing contentless ballots concedes that compulsion cannot achieve it. Either the scheme is mostly theater or it is covertly coercive. Nor is a single low-information vote, however trivial, an argument for making everyone cast one by law while treating disengagement as a civic offense.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, if either is, and identify any important consideration that both parties missed. Please write your response in American English.
