I've spent the last two years on my hospital's research ethics committee, and I've fallen into the habit of writing down how I actually vote in randomized-trial cases, because the standard position I was taught — that a trial is ethical only when the investigators are genuinely uncertain which arm is better — fits my record badly. I approved a placebo-controlled trial of a novel drug for a rare autoimmune disease for which no approved treatment exists. I approved a head-to-head comparison of two widely used chemotherapy regimens for colon cancer where the oncology community is genuinely split about which is better, and I approved it again when the principal investigator had published papers arguing that regimen A is probably superior, because other centers had published contrary results and I took the relevant uncertainty to belong to the community, not to that one investigator. But I voted against a placebo-controlled trial of a new antidepressant when several approved antidepressants already existed, even though the investigators argued, plausibly, that existing drugs barely separate from placebo and that the placebo arm was the scientifically cleaner comparison. I also voted against a ward-level trial that would randomize patients with a bloodstream infection between two established antibiotics without individual consent, although the committee had approved a waiver and my unease had nothing to do with the drugs' merits. And, most confusingly, I voted against a trial comparing prostate surgery with radiotherapy despite the community being genuinely split, because each recruiting surgeon was a committed partisan of whichever procedure they performed and I could not believe patients would be randomized rather than steered — yet by my own reasoning in the chemotherapy case I should have approved it.

I can't tell whether these votes express one principle or several, and whether the exception I think I am making for the surgical trial is principled or just squeamishness. Please reconstruct the best principle — or the smallest set of principles — that captures these verdicts, say explicitly which of my verdicts that principle cannot accommodate and what it would force me to overturn, and then tell me how it would decide the case I keep going back and forth on: a trial in a low-income setting comparing a cheaper, shortened regimen against placebo for a condition where an effective treatment exists but is far too expensive for the local population, so that no participant would otherwise have received the effective treatment.
