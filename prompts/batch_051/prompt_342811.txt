"Paul Churchland argues that the propositional attitudes—beliefs and desires as folk psychology understands them—should be eliminated rather than reduced or preserved. First, lay out the core argument for that claim. Then explain the difference between eliminative materialism and reductive physicalism about the attitudes, and say whether the standard self-refutation objection (roughly: asserting the view requires beliefs, so the view refutes itself if true) lands differently against the eliminativist than against the reductionist. Finally, does the argument from stagnation alone justify elimination, or does it need a further premise?"

The student wrote:

"Churchland thinks folk psychology is a theory, and its central posits are beliefs and desires. He argues the theory has been stagnant for thousands of years and fails to explain mental phenomena like sleep, mental illness, learning, and the nature of intelligence. Since it is a stagnant and unsuccessful theory, its posits probably don't refer, so we should eliminate belief-talk and desire-talk in favor of a maturing neuroscience. This differs from reduction, since reductionism would identify beliefs with brain states the way heat was reduced to mean molecular kinetic energy, whereas eliminativism says belief-talk will drop out. But really these come to the same thing, because whatever neural states the brain uses when someone reasons will just turn out to be the beliefs. The self-refutation worry is that you need to believe that eliminative materialism is true in order to assert it, so the view undermines the act of proposing it. This is a worse problem for the reductionist than for the eliminativist, because the reductionist is committed to beliefs being identical to brain states, and any such identity faces the multiple realization problem. The eliminativist can dodge the objection anyway, since a theory can be useful for prediction even when false, so the fact that we need belief-talk in daily life doesn't make it true. Finally, the stagnation argument is enough on its own: two thousand years without progress is decisive evidence against any theory."
