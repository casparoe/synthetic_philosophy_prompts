Before I read any more normative work on welfare conditionality — work requirements, sanctions, benefit fraud, the whole vocabulary of the “deserving” and “undeserving” poor — I want the specification written first. Assume the project is a theory adequate for designing a defensible conditionality regime in a high-income welfare state. Give me the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement: the cases it must get right; the distinctions it cannot collapse; the empirical regularities it cannot contradict or must explicitly explain away (low take-up among eligible people, effective marginal tax rates at benefit withdrawal that can approach or exceed 100 percent, fraud as a small share of overpayments relative to administrative error, and the finding that lay deservingness judgments track control, attitude, reciprocity, identity, and need — the CARIN dimensions); and the design trade-offs it must face, including the false-positive/false-negative structure of any eligibility or behavior screen. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp: work obligations understood as reciprocal contribution versus adequacy, the less-eligibility principle (keep relief worse than the least attractive job) versus a dignified minimum, stigma as deliberate deterrent versus stigma as a take-up-killing bug, political sustainability versus normative correctness. Then flag any subset of the requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding the familiar candidates: strict workfare, a desert-based account keyed to claimant attitude, an unconditional-rights view pointing toward basic income, and a luck-egalitarian account that conditions support on brute bad luck rather than choice. A table is fine, but I care more about how the requirements are tied to the cases than about the scorecard.
