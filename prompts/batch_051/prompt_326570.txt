A colleague in our rational-choice reading group has an argument she calls the "redescription problem" for bounded rationality, and it has left several of us stuck. The conclusion feels like it proves too much, but each step looks fine when read on its own. I want you to tell me exactly which step to reject, why that step looked correct, and what I pay if I reject it.

The setup: an agent faces a sequential search task. Options arrive one at a time in an order she does not control. Each additional inspection costs something — time, effort, or the risk that previously seen options become unavailable. If she rejects an option, it is gone for good; if she accepts it, search ends. Each option's value is, in principle, representable on a single scale.

1. A satisficer in such a task is usefully characterized behaviorally as an agent with a time-indexed aspiration level a_t. When an option arrives, she accepts it if its value meets or exceeds a_t, and otherwise continues searching; she may revise a_t downward (or upward) as the search drags on.

2. Any such policy has an optimization redescription. Assign the agent a search-cost function c_t giving the cost of inspecting the next option after t rejected options, and a utility function u over outcomes whose values, together with those costs, are chosen so that the satisficer's actual stopping choices come out as well as or better than any alternative stopping rule. Once we relabel these as "utility" and "beliefs about the option stream," the rule "accept the first option whose utility meets the corresponding threshold, otherwise continue" is exactly an expected-utility-maximizing stopping policy relative to those beliefs and costs.

3. So every satisficing policy is behaviorally equivalent to an optimizing policy: same choices on every possible sequence, same stopping point, same accepted option.

4. Two policies that assign the same choice to every possible sequence of options are the same policy for the purposes of a theory of choice — or at least they differ only in notation, not in substance.

5. Therefore satisficing is not a genuine alternative to optimizing. What is called bounded rationality is just constrained optimization with an unusual utility function plus search costs.

6. But this conclusion dissolves the very phenomenon bounded rationality was introduced to explain. Real agents with limited information, computation, and time evidently do not solve the optimal-stopping calculation posited in (2); part of the point of the program is that they cannot. If (5) is right, it becomes unclear what bounded rationality is a theory of.

Where does this go wrong, and which step fails? I would most like to keep (6), and I suspect (3) and (4) are doing more work than they advertise, but I cannot tell whether the problem lies in one of them, in (2), or in the way the argument trades on two senses of "policy."
