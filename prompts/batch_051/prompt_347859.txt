A colleague of mine has a remarkably stable set of judgments about when a person should keep going with something that is going badly, and I cannot tell what single rule, if any, explains them. The judgments are about what the person should do, not merely about what people typically do.

Here are four of her verdicts.

First, a couple has already spent $70,000 renovating their house when a foundation problem is discovered. An agent tells them they can sell now for $40,000, or spend another $30,000 on repairs, after which the house would be worth $50,000 at the very most. My colleague says they should pay for the repairs and then sell.

Second, a woman has bought a $120 non-refundable ticket to a performance. On the evening, she has a bad cold and the weather has turned foul; going would mean a thoroughly miserable evening and probably a worse cold. My colleague says she should go anyway, because the money is already spent.

Third, in a dollar auction, the highest bidder pays their bid and receives a one-dollar bill, while the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing. A bidder has already bid $9.00 and is currently second to a rival's $9.50. If he drops out now, he loses $9.00 and gets nothing. If he bids $10.50 and the rival then drops out, he pays $10.50 and wins the dollar. If the rival bids $11.50 instead, he will be second again and lose $10.50. My colleague says he should bid $10.50 rather than drop out.

Fourth, a man has spent forty-five minutes waiting outside a restaurant on a friend's strong recommendation. Just before he reaches the front, he learns that a comparably good restaurant next door has open tables. My colleague says he should switch immediately and not give the forty-five minutes any weight.

Now she has been asked about a fifth case. A woman is given a $120 non-refundable ticket by a coworker who cannot use it, and the ticket cannot be transferred or resold. On the evening of the performance she is in the same situation as the woman in the second case: a bad cold and foul weather, such that going would be miserable and probably make her sicker. Should she go?

I am less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule. Reconstruct the candidate rules that fit the first four verdicts, say which of them diverge on the inherited-ticket case, and give your best prediction of what my colleague will say about it, with the reason one candidate should be preferred over the others. Then specify a further case that would most cleanly separate the leading candidates, and say which of the earlier cases is doing the most work toward ruling each candidate in or out. If you think the verdicts are better explained by two interacting rules than by one, say that too, and explain how they interact.
