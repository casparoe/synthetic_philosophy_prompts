Two of my colleagues got into a sustained argument at a recent department colloquium, and I have been unable to decide in my own mind who had the better of each exchange. The dispute concerned what is sometimes called the procreation asymmetry — the idea that the pain a possible child would experience counts very differently from the pleasure it would experience when we are deciding whether to bring it into existence. Their back-and-forth went roughly as follows.

(1) The first colleague said: Consider two prospective parents. If the child they could conceive would endure a life of unremitting pain, almost everyone agrees it would be wrong to conceive. But no one thinks a couple wrongs anyone by declining to conceive a child who would have led a happy life. The best explanation is that the absence of pain in a never-lived life is good, so we have reason to avoid creating a life of suffering, whereas the absence of pleasure in a never-lived life is not bad, so we have no corresponding reason to create a life of happiness. Pleasure and pain are simply not symmetric at the threshold of existence.

(2) The second colleague replied: There is a cheaper explanation, and it has nothing special to do with procreation. Morality already treats harming and failing to benefit differently: we have strong negative duties not to cause suffering and only comparatively weak, imperfect duties to confer benefits. Creating a child one knows will suffer is causing harm in the most direct sense; declining to create a happy child is merely failing to bestow a benefit. The procreation asymmetry is just an instance of that independent asymmetry between harm and benefit.

(3) The first colleague came back: That explanation only looks cheaper if you quietly adopt the contested baseline. In an ordinary harm case, some existing person is made worse off than they were. In creation there is no person who is made worse off — the alternative for the child is not a pain-free life but no life at all. The harm/benefit asymmetry is a principle about how we treat persons who already exist; it says nothing about the transition from nonexistence to existence until one has already decided that coming into existence can be a harm to a merely possible person. That is exactly what your explanation was supposed to supply, so the explanation is circular.

(4) The second colleague answered: Then the problem is worse for your side. Your formulation slides between two senses of "good" and "bad" that it never reconciles. When I say a child's pain would be bad, I mean bad for the child. But a child who is never conceived is no subject for whom an absence of pain could be good, or for whom an absence of pleasure could fail to be bad. You state the asymmetry in impersonal, state-of-affairs terms — that an absence of pain is good — yet its moral force comes from person-affecting intuitions. If nonexistence cannot be good or bad for anyone, the asymmetry must rest on some other basis than the one you gave.

Neither would concede, and the argument ended there. I would be grateful for a point-by-point adjudication: please rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating, and then say who is right about which specific points and what, if anything, one or both of them are missing.
