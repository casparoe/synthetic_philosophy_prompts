I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether scientists are rational during a scientific revolution — when a whole field switches from one framework to another, like phlogiston to oxygen or Newtonian physics to relativity — and I keep realizing we're arguing about criteria nobody has written down. So before I read one more paper insisting Kuhn and Popper are either totally opposed or secretly compatible, I want the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of scientific change have to get right? For each requirement, give me the case or phenomenon that forces it: for instance, the long calm stretches where a community works on puzzle-solving and mostly ignores anomalies; the fact that a whole framework can absorb almost any single awkward result by blaming an auxiliary assumption; the fact that revolutions usually turn on a few stubborn anomalies but nobody agrees in advance on how many anomalies is enough; the possibility that scientists on opposite sides of a revolution use the same terms for different things and therefore can't state thier disagreement cleanly; and the way textbooks after a revolution rewrite the field's history so the losing side looks obtuse. Tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are just historical regularities or rhetoric, flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied (because that would itself be a result), and identify which single requirement does the most to expose the weaknesses of both Kuhn and Popper. Then, in a few sentences each, score Kuhn's paradigm story and Popper's falsificationism against your list — but keep the weight on the desiderata themselves, not on refereeing the two of them.
