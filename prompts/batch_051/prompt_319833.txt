In Blaise Pascal's Pensées, in the section headed "Justice and the Reason of Effects," he writes: "Justice, might. — It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical. Justice without might is gainsaid, because there are always offenders; might without justice is condemned." I read this as the claim that justice and force are conceptually distinct but practically each needs the other: justice without force cannot be effective, and force without justice cannot be legitimate. I am compiling objections to this claim, and I already have what I take to be the standard set: (1) it blurs the distinction between a norm's validity and its enforcement; (2) it oscillates between descriptive and normative senses of "might"; (3) it assumes that justice unaided by force is powerless, although norms can alter expectations and become self-enforcing; (4) it treats the combination of justice and force as stable, when force may simply capture justice and redefine it. If these really are the standard objections, I need a further one: an objection that is not on this list, not a rewording or special case or combination of them, and that is at least as damaging as the weakest item on the list. Give one and explain how it relates to Pascal's actual text.
