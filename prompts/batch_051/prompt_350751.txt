I run a small reading group, and last month we finished The Scarlet Letter; this month I want to frame our discussion around a question I have been turning over in my own life for years. I was raised in a family that operated mostly through shame: being caught mattered more than the act itself, and the punishment was being made to feel unworthy in front of other people. My partner was raised to believe one should feel bad about what one did regardless of who finds out. We argue, in a friendly way, about which response is actually the more moral one, or whether "moral" is even the right word for either. To me the novel seems like an unusually rich test case, because Hester is publicly shamed on the scaffold and made to wear the letter, yet she grows more clear-sighted and generous rather than broken, while Dimmesdale is never exposed for most of the book and is revered as holy, yet he is slowly destroyed by private guilt. I would like an essay I can take to the group that reads the novel through this contrast between guilt and shame as responses to wrongdoing. Does Hawthorne's story take a side about which is the better moral emotion, or does it show something more complicated—for instance, that shame is survivable only when a person refuses the community's verdict, and that guilt without a witness can curdle into self-obsession? I would also like the essay to address whether the novel suggests a community can choose which of these emotions it cultivates, or whether the two are entangled in ways that make such a choice an illusion. Please treat the book as a novel rather than a moral treatise: I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, saying where the guilt/shame distinction genuinely illuminates a scene or a character and where it flattens what Hawthorne actually gives us. I am not looking for quotations or citations, and I would prefer the piece to close by saying what the novel adds to the abstract debate that argument alone does not.
