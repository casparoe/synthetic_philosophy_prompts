I just got back from a two-hour arguement with my friend about that Milton Friedman idea—the "F-twist"—that unrealistic assumptions in scientific models are fine as long as the model makes good predictions, and that making the assumptions more realistic is often beside the point. We kept going in circles because he wants to defend Friedman entirely and I want to say Friedman only survives if you add a bunch of stuff he didn't say. I ended up with what might be the seed of a short essay, and I'm not sure wether it hangs together; I'd kill for a serious pair of eyes on it before I write it up.

Here's the essay idea. I want to argue that the whole debate conflates two questions: (1) is a model's assumption false? (trivially yes for almost every model anybody uses), and (2) is the particular falsehood doing the predictive work? My claim is that a false assumption can be "inert"—the model would predict about as well if you replaced it with a more truthful version, so the distortion isn't load-bearing—or it can be "load-bearing"—the model gets things right only because the distortion lines up with some other distortion in the target system. Then I want to turn this on Friedman: his instrumentalist test (does the model predict well?) can't distinguish the two cases on its own. You have to go mess with the assumption—relax it, vary it, build the more realistic version—to find out which kind of falsehood you're dealing with. But once you admit that, you've admitted the truth of the assumption matters, at least provisionally. So pure instrumentalism is incomplete. But the realist critics who say false assumptions automatically ruin a model's explanatory value are overstating it too: an inert false assumption is basically harmless shorthand. My last move is to reread the F-twist as a "distribution requirement" instead of a blank check: unrealism is acceptable only when the burden of proof is carried by a surrounding family of models showing the distortion is idle.

Could you act as a philosopher of science who works on idealization and modeling and give me comments? Please start by brainstorming the two or three strongest ways to attack the essay, then tell me which of my moves you'd cut or reorder. Specifically: is the inert/load-bearing distinction actually doing any work beyond what people already mean by robustness? What's the most damaging Friedmanite reply I'm not seeing? And does my ending ("distribution requirement") sound original or does it just restate the usual many-models program? Be blunt; I'd rather fix the argument before I write it than feel good about it now.
