I've been trying to figure out what the law of contracts is actually committed to in the philosophy of promising, because on its face it's a bit all over the place. On the common-law picture, a bare promise — I tell you I'll give you my car next week, no strings attached — isn't enforceable. Courts mostly won't touch it unless there's consideration (something was bargained for in exchange) or unless you relied on the promise to your detriment and the court thinks letting me back out would be unjust. But once a promise crosses that line, the default remedy is expectation damages: the court tries to put you where you would've been had I performed, not merely where you were before you relied. And yet courts are famously reluctant to order specific performance for personal services — they'll make me pay for backing out of my promise to paint your house, but they won't make me actually paint it.

That pattern seems to imply something about why promises bind, and I'm not sure what. Does the institution presuppose that a promise transfers a right to performance, so that expectation damages are just the cash value of the thing you were owed? Or does it presuppose that promising matters because of reliance, which fits the estoppel exception but sits awkwardly with expectation damages? Or is the real message that promise-breaking is basically a pricing problem — that once I pay expectation damages I've done all that's owed — which would make a lot of the moralizing talk about breach ring hollow? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with, and say where the practice is underdetermined so that several different theories could license it. Then say what would have to change in the doctrine — not just the rhetoric — if you replaced the presupposed view with a trust-based view of promising, or a view on which promising binds because a person has authority over their future self. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the overall reading and single out the point you're least sure of.
