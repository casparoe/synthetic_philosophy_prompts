I'm in a seminar on refugee law and we've been arguing about a made-up case, and I'd love a careful take on it. The relevant rule is from a fictional country's statute — Lydania's 1985 Refugee Act, Section 12. It says: 'A refugee is a person outside the country of their nationality who has a credible fear of serious harm directed at them on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and who cannot obtain effective protection from the authorities of their own country.'

The case: a journalist publishes a string of articles about corruption inside a far-right party in her home country. In response, an anoymous network linked to the party doxxes her — home address, her kid's school, her phone number — and she starts receiving hundreds of threats a day, many from accounts in other countries. Twice, strangers show up at her building and have to be removed. The local police open a file but tell her they can't protect her: the posters are abroad or hidden behind VPNs, and no single threat is actionable. She flees to Lydania and claims asylum. The people threatening her aren't state agents, and it's not obvious that her government is unwilling to protect her so much as simply unable.

The drafters in 1985 were imagining secret police, prison camps, and state-run persecution. Does the text of Section 12 reach her? What about the purpose behind it, and what the drafters would have said if you'd sat them down and described the internet? I'm less interested in the practical outcome than in which approach to reading the clause should win — the plain words, the original purpose, what the drafters would have said, or what a reasonable official today would think it means — and what that tells us about what 'persecution' and 'protection' were supposed to mean in the first place.
