I keep running into a standoff when behavioral policy comes up, and I want help understanding what kind of disagreement it actually is. On one side is the familiar behavioral-economics position that loss aversion and the endowment effect are systematic mistakes: people weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain, demand much more to part with an object than they would pay to acquire it, and would often be better off if they could be induced to ignore that asymmetry. On this view the proper response is debiasing — cooling-off periods, smarter defaults, framing that encourages decisions made from final states rather than changes. On the other side is the view that there is no theory-neutral standpoint from which the status quo, or a final-state utility function, is the 'real' preference. Reluctance to part with what one has can encode genuine attachment, identity, or the cost of losing a position one has already organized a life around; if so, overriding it in the name of ex ante welfare is not correcting an error but disrespecting a person's actual values. The two sides can agree on a surprising amount of evidence — the gap shrinks with experience and repeated trading, yet persists in some high-stakes market and legal settings — and still draw opposite conclusions about whether the gap is a bug to be debiased or a feature to be respected.

The argument got sharp for me in a workshop on compensation for climate-related relocation. One participant argued that people who lose homes to managed retreat should be compensated at fair market value, since that is roughly what a willing buyer would pay; another replied that this systematically understates the loss, because being the person who has to move is precisely the harm the money is meant to cover. Both accepted the same numbers and still diverged. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is only partly empirical, and mostly a disagreement about what makes a preference legitimate in the first place. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the debate, not a verdict on who is right. Specifically: is the disagreement verbal, normative, or empirical, and how much would remain if each side had to state its welfare criterion explicitly? Which parts of 'loss aversion' and 'the endowment effect' would need to be separated before the sides could even disagree about the same thing? What kind of evidence, formal result, or conceptual distinction could actually move one side or the other — and is there a coherent version in which no experiment could settle it because the two sides are answering different questions under one name?
