I am a sixth-form student who reads philosophy in my spare time, and I keep noticing a recurring move in debates about whether an eternal afterlife could truly be blissful. The move runs like this: take any good you like — listening to music, learning new things, a friendship — and ask whether you could enjoy it endlessly. The reply is typically that, however good it is, after enough repetitions it would become stale; you would eventually have heard every conversation, so each new one would feel like a rerun, and even the deepest friendship would be exhausted once you could predict everything the other person would say. Since an eternal life contains endless repetitions of a finite stock of possible enjoyments, the conclusion is that it must eventually collapse into boredom, and therefore no unending life is worth wanting. I have also seen the usual responses: that in a perfect afterlife our natures would be changed so that we could not tire of the good, or that time would not pass there as it does now. But both answers seem to face the same difficulty: a being who cannot tire, or who does not live through one thing after another, might no longer be recognisably me. I am not asking for a reading list or bibliography. What I want to know is whether that original move has an established name, what exactly it claims, and how it should be distinguished from nearby ideas — for instance, that an endless life would lack meaning because nothing would be at stake, or that our projects only matter because we have limited time. If no single term captures it well, I would rather you say so, propose the nearest available term, and explain how you would draw the boundaries of the concept. A bare label would not satisfy me; I am after the carving of the phenomenon, not a name by itself. Please write your answer in British English.
