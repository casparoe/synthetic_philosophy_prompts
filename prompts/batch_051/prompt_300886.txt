I am a novelist drafting a book whose central crime is the deliberate destruction of a virtual home: a structure into which the victim has poured years of patient work, around which most of her friendships and a portion of her working life are now organised, and which exists inside a persistent multiplayer world. The novel's wager is that this destruction is a genuine injury, not merely a setback within a pastime. During my research, however, the head of community safety at a studio running a comparable world dismissed the premise almost without argument. A virtual world, she insisted, is at bottom a species of collaborative fiction; to resent the player who burns the house down is like resenting a novelist for killing off a beloved character. No-one thinks an author has literally harmed a reader by writing a cruel scene, however much the reader weeps, and by parity of reasoning no-one is literally harmed by what befalls an avatar or her possessions. The grief may be real, but the loss is not.

The analogy unsettles me because, as a novelist, I feel its force more keenly than I would like; yet I suspect it is doing more work than it can carry, and I cannot quite say where. I want it audited rather than merely endorsed or refuted. What precisely maps onto what between a novel and a persistent virtual world—the author, the reader, the character, the plot, the reader's emotion, the harm? Where does the analogy begin to break, and which features of fiction does it quietly import that a multiplayer world, with other agents, accumulated history, scarcity, and real economic and social entanglements, does not share? Once those disanalogies are laid out, does the conclusion that virtual harms are not real harms survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if some better analogy would do the same work more honestly, tell me what it is.
