I am a high school student, and I recently came across a pair of metaphors for evaluating explanations: cranes and skyhooks. As I understand them, a crane is an explanation that builds some complex thing up from smaller, ordinary steps, each of which can actually do the lifting; a skyhook is an explanation that stops at a designing purpose, agent, or force that is apparently doing the lifting from above. I also came across the idea of greedy reductionism, which I take to be the opposite mistake: refusing to accept any high-level description as genuinely explanatory because only the lowest-level vocabulary is real.

I have gut verdicts about several explanations, and I want to know whether one principle captures all of them, or close. My verdicts:

1. The vertebrate eye evolved through many small heritable variations, each retained because it improved vision in that environment. I find this fine. I also find "the eye was designed by natural selection" fine, as long as it is shorthand for exactly that sequence. But "mutations were guided toward producing an eye" is not fine.

2. The 2008 crash explained by many mortgage holders, banks, and funds selling in response to falling prices, which pushed prices down further. Fine. The same crash explained by saying the market needed to correct and was seeking its true value. Not fine, unless "true value" and "seeking" are unpacked into actual traders' beliefs and actions — at which point it has become a different explanation.

3. "The immune system recognizes a virus." Fine, even though recognition is mental vocabulary, because I trust it can be spelled out in terms of receptor molecules, binding, and the selective multiplication of matching cells.

4. A field guide says a seabird "abandoned the mainland and colonized the island." Fine if this is shorthand for individual birds dispersing, surviving, and breeding. Not fine if it means the species as a whole made a decision. I am honestly unsure how to tell which reading is doing the explanatory work.

5. A chess engine moved its knight because "it wants control of the center." Fine, provided the engine actually has a search procedure and an evaluation function that weights central control. But "the engine understands the position deeply" is not fine; it credits something the mechanism does not earn.

6. Worker ants care for the queen's offspring rather than their own because they "sacrifice themselves for the good of the colony." I do not accept this as a stopping point; I want it carried down to genes, kin, or some individual-level mechanism. Yet I notice the word "sacrifice" is not my real objection, since I am not confused about whether ants have selves. The missing mechanism is the objection.

7. A friend explains an angry outburst by saying "some part of me wanted him to fail." I accept this as a real explanation, even though there is no little agent inside. So my principle must not ban all talk of inner parts and agents.

The pressure: cases 3 and 7 let intentional language stand when I trust that an unstated mechanism is underneath, but case 2 has the same shape and I reject it. Meanwhile, if I demand that every high-level phrase be decomposed before I allow it, I start to sound like the greedy reductionist who cannot let "designed by natural selection" or "part of me wanted him to fail" be explanatory at all.

The principle I keep reaching for is this: a high-level or intentional-sounding description is legitimate exactly when it is a compression of a chain of lower-level causal steps that could in principle be spelled out (a crane), and illegitimate when the chain stops at a purpose, agent, or force not itself built from those steps (a skyhook). But I do not know whether this principle makes the right cut, or whether it can be stated without relying on exactly the sort of trust that separates my verdicts.

Please state the principle precisely, or offer the best candidate principle. Test it against the seven cases, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: when people say a large language model "hallucinates" facts, is that a crane-type explanation, a skyhook, or legitimate shorthand for something else — and what would you need to know about the system to decide?
