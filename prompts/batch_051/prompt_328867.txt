I'm writing a paper on libertarian paternalism, and I want to check I've not missed an obvious objection to the Thaler–Sunstein nudge programme. Assign yourself as a philosopher who works on paternalism and the ethics of influence. First brainstorm a bit, then think carefully.

Here are the standard objections I already have, as far as I know them: (1) nudges are manipulative because they bypass rational deliberation; (2) nudges are disrespectful, treating adults as less than fully autonomous; (3) the line between a nudge and a shove is unstable, so permitting nudges invites harder paternalism; (4) nudges entrench the biases of whoever designs the choice architecture; (5) individually trivial nudges amount, in aggregate, to a form of social control; and (6) many nudges fail a publicity condition, because choice architects would rather keep thier influence hidden.

I have a feeling I'm forgetting a well-known objection—one that isn't just a rewording, special case, or combination of those six. Which one is it, and what does it add that the items on my list don't?
