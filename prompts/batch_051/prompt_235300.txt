I chair the acquisitions committee of a municipal historical archive. Our mandate is to preserve the documentary record of the city, but in practice we decide case by case what to take in, and we are aware that every refusal creates a silence in the record. I am trying to reconstruct what principle, if any, our recent decisions have actually embodied, and what that principle commits us to in a case now before us.

Our recent decisions:

1. We accepted the complete payroll, injury, and shift logs of a textile mill that closed in 1962, offered by the last owner's grandson, who said he would otherwise destroy them. The mill had employed much of the city for two generations, and almost nothing about daily working conditions survived. We accepted the logs in full.

2. We accepted a set of flyers, photographs, and oral-history recordings from a tenants' union that organized against evictions in the 1980s. The committee's stated reason was that the tenants' side of those disputes was nearly absent from the archive and the material could easily have been lost.

3. We declined a prominent merchant family's offer of scrapbooks and selected correspondence, assembled by the family to document its own philanthropic record, on the condition that the collection be kept together and displayed as a unit. The committee concluded that the selection and the restriction together made it publicity material rather than a record.

4. We accepted the complete, unweeded case files of a legal aid clinic that represented tenants in eviction and housing-code cases between 1975 and 1995. Some named individuals are still alive; we accepted on the condition that identifying information about the clinic's clients be closed for thirty years.

5. We declined a police department's offer to transfer a portion of its older records chosen by the department's own public-affairs office. The committee's reason was that allowing a creating agency to choose what history receives would let it shape the historical record of itself.

6. We accepted the minutes and membership rolls of a now-defunct fraternal order that had explicitly excluded women and members of certain ethnic groups. The committee reasoned that documenting exclusion is not the same as endorsing it, and that the order would otherwise have left no trace in the archive.

Now we must decide the following case. A retired official from the city's planning department has offered us his personal copies of internal memoranda, emails, and meeting notes concerning the urban-renewal project that cleared a largely immigrant neighborhood in the 1990s. He says no other repository has them and that, if we decline, he will destroy them. The materials are his own selection — he kept only what he thought significant — and the perspective is entirely that of the planners. The neighborhood's community organization still exists, but the archive has never collected anything from it, and its own records are scattered and unorganized.

What should the committee decide if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents? Be specific about which of the past decisions is the closest analog. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, state the two or three principles that fit our record equally well, what each would require in this case, and what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Then tell me which further case — one I could bring to the committee — would best distinguish those principles.
