I keep coming back to the socialist calculation debate and I'm trying to figure out whether Hayek's side really holds up. His argument against central planning was that the knowledge needed to run an economy is spread across millions of people in little local chunks—who needs what, what can be repurposed where, which bottleneck is about to break—and that markets, mainly through prices, are what compress all that into something usable. A central board can't gather it, and Mises's version added that without actual markets in producer goods you don't get real prices to do rational calculation with at all, so you're planning in the dark. But Hayek and Mises were writing before cheap sensors, universal transaction logs, and algorithms that reprice huge catalogs every minute; Amazon runs something that looks a lot like a planned interior with market-facing edges. Suppose you had an economy where every transaction and input use is automatically recorded, and a planning board can update prices and production targets in real time with genuinely powerful computation. Does Hayek's argument against central planning survive that development, or does it mostly collapse into Lange's trial-and-error market socialism with better hardware? I'm less interested in the history and more in which actual premise is under pressure—is it the gathering problem, the computing problem, or something about incentives and discovery—and whether there's a repaired version of the Hayekian point that still has it's force.
