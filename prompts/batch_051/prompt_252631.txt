I’ve been restating Rousseau’s general will in terms of a distinction I’ve been calling first-order and second-order unfreedom after a long argument with a friend about the forced-to-be-free passage in The Social Contract. On my reading, when Rousseau says someone who refuses the general will can be compelled by the whole body, he is not claiming that the state may impose any particular conception of the good. Instead, he is distinguishing between being blocked by another private will and being blocked by an arrangement that all citizens, including oneself insofar as one is a citizen, would will. I call the first private obstruction and the second civic self-obstruction. I think this lets me explain why Rousseau insists the person is made free rather than merely subdued: the coercion removes the causal dominance of inclinations that the person would disown from the standpoint of the general will. It also lets me respond to the standard liberal objection that mandatory participation in the general will is indistinguishable from state paternalism.

I want this checked honestly. Is my distinction a real one, or am I just relabeling the difference between liberty and license? Does it beg the question by assuming there is a perspective from which the coerced person already endorses the law? What is the hardest case for it, and can the distinction survive a case where someone reasonably denies that a given law expresses the general will? I’d rather have a fatal objection than a list of caveats.
