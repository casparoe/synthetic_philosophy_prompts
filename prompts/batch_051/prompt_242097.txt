For a seminar on the typology of writing systems, I built an argument whose conclusion I find difficult to accept, because it would erase the standard distinction between deep and shallow orthographies. Each premise looks plausible on its own, but I cannot find the flaw. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?

(1) An alphabetic writing system is phonemic only if its letters stand for the language's phonemes, the contrastive sound units, rather than for words, morphemes, syllables, or subphonemic detail.

(2) But to stand for a phoneme is to specify its pronunciation: a letter for /t/ succeeds only if it tells a reader how to produce the sound.

(3) A phoneme, however, has no single pronunciation. It surfaces as different allophones depending on phonological context, and which allophone appears is fixed by rules of the language rather than by the letter itself. For example, in many American English varieties the phoneme /t/ is aspirated in "top" but flapped in "water."

(4) So a letter can do one of two things. It can represent the abstract phoneme itself, a context-free unit no one ever actually pronounces; or it can represent the context-dependent allophones, in which case the writing is no longer one letter per phoneme and has stopped being phonemic in the relevant sense.

(5) Therefore a phonemic alphabet cannot specify pronunciation: the more faithfully it encodes phonemes, the less it tells a reader about how any actual word should be said.

(6) But the familiar reason for calling Spanish or Czech spelling phonemically transparent is precisely that such systems are especially reliable guides to pronunciation.

(7) Hence the ideal of a phonemic orthography appears incoherent: a script cannot both encode phonemes and guide pronunciation, yet that is exactly what phonemic transparency is supposed to do.

If I have to choose, I would most like to hold on to (6). I am prepared to lose something else, but I want to understand the price. So: is this argument valid, and if not, which step goes wrong and what kind of mistake is it? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of phonemic orthography reject, and what alternative account of what an alphabet encodes would they need to put in its place?
