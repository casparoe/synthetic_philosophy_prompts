I am teaching a seminar on Kierkegaard's social criticism for a general audience, and I want to close with a discussion of how his mid-nineteenth-century attack on the press and the public bears on the media environment of the last fifteen years. As I plan to present his position, the core claims run something like this: the daily press manufactures an abstract public that exists only in the opinions it ascribes to others; that public is anonymous and therefore irresponsible, able to judge without any identifiable person having to stand behind the judgment; and the result is a leveling that erases qualitative distinctions among persons and positions, driven less by conviction than by envy and by a reflective age's preference for commentary over commitment. My students will have read only a short excerpt, so the interpretive work falls to me.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of what Kierkegaard would have made of the contemporary platform-mediated public sphere: algorithmically ranked feeds, pseudonymous accounts, engagement metrics, recommendation systems that amplify outrage, and the strange experience that everyone is discussing the same event while no identifiable person can be said to have said the thing being discussed. Where would he recognize the structure he had already diagnosed in the press, and where would the analogy strain or break by his own standards — for instance, because the platforms sometimes produce genuine, passionate commitment rather than the passionless leveling he described, or because the anonymity of modern users differs from the anonymity of the nineteenth-century journalist? I am less interested in the generic conclusion that he would be appalled than in a precise account of which of his categories would carry over, which would misfire, and what he would have been forced to revise. As you answer, tell me how confident you are in each major claim and single out the point in your reconstruction that you are least sure of.
