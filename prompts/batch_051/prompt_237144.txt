You are an expert in the philosophy of linguistics and in grammatical theory—equally at home in formal syntax and in functional-typological work—and you have been asked to serve as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration between two grammatical theorists who have spent years arguing past each other. The first, the natural-kind theorist, holds that familiar grammatical categories—parts of speech, case categories, and adpositions, including prepositions—are genuine crosslinguistic kinds: not merely convenient labels, but classes that carve grammar at its joints, grounded in human cognition and in invariant constraints on possible languages. The second, whom we will call the constructionist particularist, holds that these categories are family-resemblance conveniences projected by the linguist's own comparative metalanguage: word classes, case values, and relators are defined by language-internal distributions and constructions, and there is no universal inventory of "noun," "dative," or "preposition" waiting to be discovered in every language.

Draft the joint statement this good-faith collaboration should produce. Begin with a numbered list of substantive claims both parties can sign—claims that are specific enough to constrain subsequent work, not vacuous. Then present a disagreements table in which each residual point of difference is stated twice: once in wording the natural-kind theorist would own, once in wording the constructionist particularist would own, each acceptable to its own side. Next, identify the cruxes that would genuinely move at least one party. I suspect the cruxes include at least: (i) whether languages without a distinct adjective class are a refutation of a universal noun–verb–adjective trichotomy or simply different parameter settings on an underlying category system; (ii) whether the English preposition "of" and spatial prepositions like "under" fall under one kind or two; (iii) whether a single dative case used for recipients, for experiencers, and for interested-person constructions is one category or a cluster of homonyms; (iv) whether Japanese-style case particles such as ga/o/ni are cases, postpositions, or evidence that the case–adposition distinction is itself an artifact of the metalanguage; and (v) whether mixed categories such as participles show that parts of speech are gradient and family-resemblance-like, or instead that a small stock of primitive features generates both central and mixed cases. For each crux, state the concrete linguistic evidence or test both parties would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Finally, add a short section separating the disagreements that are genuine from those that dissolve once the wording is tightened.

Think carefully before writing, and spend some time brainstorming the strongest version of each side's position and the points where each would most want to resist the other's formulation; the measure of success is that each party should wince slightly at something yet still be willing to sign.
