I manage the product-policy team at a large video-sharing platform. Every week we have to decide whether to keep, demote, or remove algorithmic ranking changes, and the recurring disagreement is what to do when users' stated preferences, surveyed values, and actual engagement point in different directions. After a recent controversy—viewers told us they wanted more substantive content while the retention data said the opposite—I was pointed to the idea of indirect normativity: roughly, that we should be guided by what users would want after reflecting on their own lives rather than taking either their current statements or their immediate behavior at face value. I need that idea turned into a reusable decision procedure that an on-call policy analyst with no philosophy background can run in under an hour and that produces a written justification my VP must approve. It should specify the questions to ask in order, the evidence to weigh, rules for when self-reports override revealed behavior and vice versa, where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it hands the decision to a person because the choice is a value judgment rather than a fact. Then run it on three cases: (1) users say they want less sensational content but click it heavily; (2) a minority group demands removal of a category of content that a majority finds harmless; (3) a user states she wants to reduce her time on the platform and sleep earlier, but at 11 p.m. consistently clicks recommendations that keep her online, and our models can infer the pattern. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer our team would resist, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.
