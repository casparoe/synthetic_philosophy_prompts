I've been spending time around a group of people who are, in their ordinary purchasing, strict ethical vegans: they will not buy meat, dairy, eggs, leather, or other animal products, on the grounds that buying these things creates demand that causes more animals to be bred, confined, and killed. But several of them will eat or use animal products when the item would otherwise go to waste—food pulled from supermarket dumpsters, leftover sandwiches from a catered event, eggs laid by a neighbor's pet chickens that no one else wants, and on one occasion a deer struck by a car. They will also wear secondhand leather, though none of them would buy a new leather jacket. When I pressed one of them on whether she would eat a dog killed by a car, she said she would not and then struggled to explain why. And none of them would eat the body of a human being who had died in an accident, even though such a body satisfies the same description of something that would otherwise go to waste.

I am trying to work out what philosophical commitments this practice—sometimes called freeganism—actually presupposes. On the surface it seems to locate the wrongness of animal products entirely in purchasing, or more broadly in contributing to demand and being complicit with industries that harm animals, while treating the use of an animal's body itself as morally unproblematic. But the hesitation about the dog and the firm line at the human body suggest that something further may be in the background: a view about which bodies may be treated as resources, or about what eating a body expresses, or about the kind of person one becomes by treating a death as an opportunity. I would like you, as a professional moral philosopher, to reconstruct the implicit moral theory as precisely as you can. In particular, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that more than one theory could license it. Then assess whether the practice is internally coherent, or whether its different parts presuppose inconsistent things. Finally, tell me how the practice would have to change if the wrongness of animal products were located instead in the use of the animal's body itself, and separately how it would change if the wrongness were located in a virtue-based account of the kind of person one becomes by eating animals.

Please think the issue through carefully and entertain at least three candidate principles before settling on your reconstruction. Write your answer in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way you would write a long email. Toward the end, tell me how confident you are in the claims you have made and single out the point you are least sure about.
