I retired from a career in thermodynamics and sensory physiology and have recently been working through some philosophy of cognitive science. A younger colleague introduced me to the predictive processing framework and the free energy principle, and I have been trying to reconcile them with the Hermann von Helmholtz I knew from physics and perceptual theory. The standard account of predictive processing describes perception as Helmholtzian: the brain anticipates sensory input, treats discrepancy as error, and updates its internal model so as to minimize surprise. But the contemporary framework goes well beyond anything Helmholtz argued. It embeds perception in a Bayesian variational formalism, treats action as active inference, and elevates the minimization of free energy into a principle meant to hold for living systems quite generally. It even names its central quantity after a thermodynamic potential associated with Helmholtz's own work.

What I would like is a careful extrapolation of what Helmholtz would plausibly have made of predictive processing and the free energy principle, had they been placed before him. Which moves would he likely have recognized as a natural continuation of his doctrine that perception is constructive and inferential, and which would he likely have resisted as overextensions? How would a mathematically rigorous experimental physiologist with empiricist leanings in perception have reacted to the claim that a single free-energy minimization principle unifies mind and life? And would the borrowing of free-energy terminology have struck him as a principled identity or as a loose but useful metaphor? Please reason through this rather than offering a summary; I want to see where your verdict follows fairly robustly from Helmholtz's known commitments and where it remains a guess about his temperament.
