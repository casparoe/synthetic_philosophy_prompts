I am a retired particle physicist, and for the past few years I have been reading my way into the philosophy of physics. In three weeks I am to present a paper to a philosophy of physics reading group at my old university, and I would like help preparing for the discussion that will follow, which I expect to be courteous but pointed. My thesis is that the methods and success of effective field theory give us reason to think of physical theorising as scale-relative all the way down, and that the traditional picture of a single fundamental theory valid at every scale, from which everything else could in principle be derived, is a philosophical mistake rather than a scientific ideal. I mean something stronger than the uncontentious point that effective theories are useful instruments; I want to maintain that a system's real degrees of freedom and its real laws are internal to a chosen scale of description, and that there is no uniquely privileged description waiting at the bottom of the renormalisation group flow.

The group numbers about a dozen. Most are philosophers of science with interests in reduction and emergence, two of them have written on what renormalisation-group arguments can and cannot establish, and there is one retired high-energy theorist who thinks philosophers habitually overread calculational techniques. A few graduate students attend without fixed commitments, and there is one metaphysician who asks, of every view presented, what it says about the world itself rather than about our theories of it. I am not worried about stale objections to renormalisation as an ad hoc procedure; this group grants the physics. What concerns me is that the objections will take the form of granting the physics but resisting the metaphysics, or of accusing me of having merely re-described scientific practice in philosophical language.

My argument runs roughly as follows. Low-energy effective theories are complete and predictive for phenomena in their own domain without fixing a unique high-energy completion; the renormalisation group shows that the parameters of a theory are themselves scale-dependent, so that what a theory says about entities at one scale does not survive unchanged at another; decoupling means that many ultraviolet details are not merely unknown but irrelevant to infrared phenomena; therefore the ideal of a theory of everything as the uniquely true description of nature rests on a demand for derivational completeness that the physics itself gives us no reason to accept. What I would like from you is a question-period prep sheet rather than a restatement of my paper: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as the people in the group would phrase them, each with a reply that could be delivered in two minutes, an honest note on where that reply is weakest, and a clear indication of what I should concede at the outset rather than try to defend. Please also tell me the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and how I ought to answer it if it is put to me anyway. Since this is for me to read and annotate rather than to project or circulate, write in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, and use no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
