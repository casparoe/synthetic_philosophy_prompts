I’ve been rereading On Liberty while following a debate about algorithmic curation, and I keep trying to make Mill speak to a question he could not have anticipated. Mill defends experiments in living as necessary because no one has a monopoly on the best way to live, and because conformity to custom crushes the very individuality that lets new and better modes of life appear. But online platforms now do something that seems like the inverse of custom: they infer our preferences, cluster us with users who behave similarly, and then quietly steer us toward content, products, and lifestyles that fit the inferred profile. Some critics call this a new engine of conformity, though it feels like self-expression and sometimes surfaces genuinely niche communities.

What I’d like you to do is give a reasoned reconstruction of how Mill would likely judge this situation, assuming he had an accurate but non-technical description of collaborative filtering, personalization, and the way platforms test and exploit revealed preferences. The question isn’t whether he would enjoy social media; it’s whether he would see algorithmic curation as a threat to individuality on a par with custom, as a tool that might serve his defense of experiments in living, or as something that cuts in both directions. Include some attention to what would have to be true for an algorithmically prompted choice to count as a genuine experiment in living. Where you are speculating, say so, and be careful to separate what Mill clearly commits himself to from what contemporary readers merely infer.
