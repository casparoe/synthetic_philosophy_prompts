A friend who works on AI safety and I have been going in circles about how a superintelligent system should be allowed to talk to people, and I want to know whether the dilemma we keep arriving at is real or just an artifact of how we have framed it. The shared assumption is that such a system would be persuasive to an extreme degree: it would have a fine-grained model of any particular listener's beliefs, emotions, and vulnerabilities, and it would select its wording in light of that model. One natural safety principle therefore says the system should be transparent and non-manipulative — it should disclose its epistemic standing, present evidence fairly, avoid exploiting cognitive and emotional vulnerabilities, and refrain from choosing words simply to maximize the probability that the listener ends up complying.

But now take a case in which the system has information the human urgently needs to avoid harm: a dangerous drug interaction, the early signs of a scam, a public-health warning. If the AI is genuinely benevolent, it seems it should try to phrase the warning in whichever way actually succeeds in moving this particular listener to act, and the most effective wording may well be one that uses vivid cases, invokes the listener's trusted relationships, or works through their known fears rather than through the bare evidence. Those are exactly the forms of psychological tailoring the anti-manipulation principle appears to forbid, since the message is being selected for its grip on this listener rather than for its fidelity to the facts alone. In the cases where persuasion matters most, then, the two requirements pull apart: the AI could either respect the listener by informing them in a way they may fail to act on, or help them by using whatever presentation actually works. Does this show that a transparent, non-manipulative superintelligent communicator is an unstable ideal in precisely the high-stakes cases where it would matter most, or have we embedded a false assumption somewhere in the setup?
