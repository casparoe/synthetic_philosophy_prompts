I have been developing a small theory about delusions and would like an honest assessment. The two-factor theory holds that a delusion requires both an anomalous experience and a deficit in some downstream evaluation or inference process. I want to recast the second factor as a deficit in what I call inferential weight, and to call my resulting position the inertial belief view. On this view, the delusional proposition is sincerely accepted as a belief, but it is assigned an abnormally low inferential weight: it is reported and endorsed, yet it is highly resistant to counterevidence and only erratically imported into practical reasoning. Someone with Capgras, for example, sincerely says that a loved one has been replaced by an impostor while not calling the police, searching for the original, or grieving. My proposal is that delusions are beliefs, but pathologically inert ones, and that the two-factor theory is better read as a theory of how a belief's inferential weight is set than as a theory of whether the proposition is accepted at all. Is this a coherent and distinct position, or does it merely relabel existing views about badly integrated or compartmentalized beliefs? Where does it break? In particular, does it misclassify delusions that do extensively guide action, or ordinary high-conviction religious and political beliefs that resemble inert beliefs?
