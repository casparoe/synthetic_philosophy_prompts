I am a PhD candidate in philosophy working on population axiology, and I would like you to act as a specialist in population ethics. I am designing a graduate seminar exercise on the disagreements underlying the Repugnant Conclusion, and I need you to produce a classification instrument: a "which population axiology do you actually hold?" quiz. The quiz should sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they prefer, so no question should simply ask people whether they are total utilitarians, averagists, person-affecting theorists, or the like.

Please think carefully and start by brainstorming the underlying diagnostic axes that separate the main families of population axiology. Then write the final instrument in polished form.

The final instrument should have these features:

1. Eight to ten questions. Each question should present a determinate, numerically specified case: two populations with numbers of lives and welfare levels, or a choice that adds, prevents, or replaces lives. Answer options should be concrete verdicts about the case, not names of theories. The cases should be precise enough that the displayed answer options are the natural live options.

2. A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the named positions. The key should make clear which combinations of answers are genuinely diagnostic and which are not.

3. Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First specify the diagnostic questions on which the views come apart, such as whether mere addition always makes an outcome better, whether quantity and quality of welfare are fully commensurable, whether losses to the worst off can be offset by gains to the better off, whether there is a positive critical level below which added lives do not count, whether some levels of welfare have lexical priority over lower levels, and whether the identity of the added people matters. Then locate the named positions in that space.

4. The following positions should appear as leaves, with brief parenthetical glosses so the instrument is self-contained: total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, critical-level utilitarianism, variable-value theories, prioritarian totalism, lexical threshold or superiority views, and person-affecting or necessitarian views. If some of these cannot be separated within the length limit, say so explicitly and explain what additional case or distinction would be needed.

5. Mark unoccupied combinations of answers and briefly diagnose them: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or plausibly coherent but systematically underexplored? For example, if a pattern combines a rejection of the Repugnant Conclusion with an acceptance of all mere additions, the key should explain what family, if any, that pattern corresponds to.

6. The scoring key should include a short prose interpretation for each leaf or each final category, explaining what commitments produce that result and where the category sits with respect to the Repugnant Conclusion and the Mere Addition Paradox.

Use cases drawn from these fault lines at minimum: adding a large group of lives that are barely worth living to a population whose existing lives go unchanged; replacing a smaller high-welfare population with a much larger low-welfare population; trading a loss for the worst off against a gain for the already well off; adding one happy life versus preventing one miserable life; and a comparison in which total welfare is the same but one population has very high average welfare and the other has merely positive lives.

The intended audience is philosophy graduate students and postdocs, but the answer options should remain intelligible to someone who does not yet know the technical labels. Do not include bibliographic citations, recommended readings, or appeals to the scholarly literature; the instrument should stand entirely on its own. Please provide the finished instrument, not a draft or an outline, and write in American English.
