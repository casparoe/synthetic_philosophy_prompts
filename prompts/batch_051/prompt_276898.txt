I keep going back to the Challenger launch decision and feeling like the doing/allowing distinction should either do real work there or break in some interesting way. The night before launch, Morton Thiokol's engineers recommended against launching because the O-ring data at low temperature looked bad; NASA managers pushed back, reconvened without some of the engineers, approved the launch, and the shuttle broke apart. Skip the org-behavior recap for me (groupthink, schedule pressure, go fever, all that). I want it run through the killing/letting-die lens.

Analyze it at three levels: the final go/no-go order, the flight-readiness process in the preceding days, and the earlier design and testing decisions that locked the risky O-ring design in. At each level, say whether the relevant conduct is best modeled as a doing or an allowing; say who the player is, what the act/omission pair was, and what the distinction says each player should have done once the risk was on the table. Steelman both readings of the managers: the one where issuing the go order sets a lethal chain in motion and counts as killing, and the one where they merely failed to prevent a harm they didn't create, closer to a bystander who doesn't donate a kidney. Place the engineers too: they voiced the warning but didn't refuse to sign or blow the whistle in any way that stopped the launch. Is their conduct an allowing, and does the distinction make them complicit or clean?

Then run the lens in the other direction: what does the episode show about the doing/allowing distinction itself. My suspiscion is that this case is close to an anomaly, because the framework wants a discrete agent choosing between an act and an omission, but Challenger gives a distributed chain of sign-offs where each step likely looked to its author like an omission of caution. If that's right, can the view survive by locating the doing at the right node (the final go order? the meeting that excluded the engineers? the original design choice?), or should we admit the distinction goes quiet on diffuse institutional harms and switch to some other moral vocabulary? I want an honest verdict on whether the distinction is earning its keep here, or whether it only looks useful because we're sneaking in a blame judgment first and then back-filling the act/omission classification to match.
