Here is a claim that strikes me as being at once methodological and metaphysical: that a subject's verbal report of their own experience is best treated as evidence about the experience itself, rather than as the output of a confabulating narrative engine. As I understand heterophenomenology, it tries to have this both ways: it takes the report seriously as data while remaining neutral about whether the items the report describes exist outside the report. The obvious worry is that this neutrality comes at the price of testability; if the method refuses to move from 'she said that p' to 'p occurred in her experience', what would ever count as evidence that reports track a phenomenal reality rather than construct one?

I would like you to take that question seriously as an experimentalist. Design a programme of studies (I am not asking for citations, only for the logic of the design) that would bring evidence to bear on the contrast between two readings of a first-person report: (a) the report as the trace of, or a response to, an occurrent conscious state; and (b) the report as a post hoc fabrication that merely presents itself as one. Specify the manipulation, the populations, the timing of the report, the behavioural or neural measures you would record alongside it, and the pattern of results that would favour each reading. Then examine with equal care what a positive or negative result would fail to settle: whether a correlation between report and independent measure shows that the report issues from the experience, or only that downstream access to the processing was accurate; and whether the notion of 'a report that corresponds to an experience' is sharp enough to be operationalised without begging the question. Conclude by saying which parts of the claim are genuinely testable and which are not, and whether any experiment could dissolve the distinction rather than merely shifting it.
