Ok here's an argument I wrote out after playing around with a "two roommates and a book" case, and it's driving me nuts because each step looks right but the conclusion seems to say that two obviously desirable rules can't be combined.

Scenario. Mia and Rob are deciding among three possible outcomes: Mia reads the book, Rob reads the book, or nobody reads the book. Their preference orderings are:

Mia: nobody reads > Mia reads > Rob reads. (She thinks the book is corrupting, so no one reading is best; but if someone has to read it, she'd rather it be her, because she trusts herself to shake it off more easily than Rob.)

Rob: Mia reads > Rob reads > nobody reads. (He wants Mia to read it because he thinks she'd be funny about it; second-best he reads it himself; worst is nobody reading it.)

Now the argument:

(1) An acceptable rule for combining their preferences should respect unanimity: if they both prefer X to Y, the social ranking should put X above Y.

(2) It should also respect a minimal kind of personal freedom: each person should be decisive over at least one pair of alternatives that concerns only what they themselves do. So Mia is decisive over {Mia reads, nobody reads}, and Rob is decisive over {Rob reads, nobody reads}.

(3) Since Mia prefers nobody reads to Mia reads, her decisiveness forces the social ranking to put nobody reads above Mia reads.

(4) Since Rob prefers Rob reads to nobody reads, his decisiveness forces the social ranking to put Rob reads above nobody reads.

(5) Social rankings are transitive: if Rob reads is above nobody reads, and nobody reads is above Mia reads, then the ranking must put Rob reads above Mia reads.

(6) But unanimity also applies to the pair {Mia reads, Rob reads}, and both Mia and Rob prefer Mia reads to Rob reads. So unanimity forces the social ranking to put Mia reads above Rob reads.

(7) So the same rule is forced to rank Rob reads above Mia reads and also Mia reads above Rob reads. Contradiction.

My question: which step, if any, actually fails? I keep wanting to say (2) is sneaky, because "nobody reads" is not purely about one person's own conduct—it's also about what the other person doesn't do. But if I fix (2) so a person only decides between "I read" and "I don't read" while holding the other person's behavior fixed, that seems to require finer-grained alternatives and the argument might not even get started. Is the argument valid as written but just showing that (1) and (2) can't both hold? If so, which premise would you weaken, and how exactly?
