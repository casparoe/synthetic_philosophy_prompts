Consider the following variation on Dennett's Where Am I? scenario. A person's brain has been removed and kept alive in a vat; his living body moves through the world, and the two communicate by radio. In the familiar version the link is effectively instantaneous under normal conditions, and when it is interrupted the person discovers that his stream of consciousness remains in the vat rather than stopping with the body.

Now alter one feature. Instead of a link that is either on or off, suppose the link imposes a continuously adjustable delay in each direction, and the body has an onboard controller that can execute well-learned motor routines on its own and later send the brain a summary. Assume there is one brain and one body throughout, and the link is never severed. At zero delay the person reports that he is in his body, acting and perceiving in real time. At a few hundred milliseconds he notices a lag but still feels he is acting, with effort. At several seconds he reports that he issues an intention and then observes, too late to fine-tune, how his body carried it out. At ten minutes he gives broad instructions and later reviews a report of what happened. Throughout, the same biological brain is the only source of the body's high-level intentions.

Trace what the three main answers to the original puzzle—brain-location, living-body-location, and point-of-view-location—each say across this sweep of delays. Is there a determinate delay at which the person changes location, or does one of the answers give the same verdict at every delay? Which of these answers, if any, best survives the variation, and what does that imply about the original case in which the link simply breaks?
