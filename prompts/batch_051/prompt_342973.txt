I teach philosophy, and in preparing a unit on hedonism I have been working through the heterogeneity problem: if hedonism identifies well-being with pleasure, then we need to know what all the things called pleasures have in common, and the standard candidates each seem to fail some of my settled judgments. I have therefore written down nine cases and my own verdicts, deliberately without theory. I would like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work: find the principle, or small set of principles, that best captures these verdicts, and say frankly which verdicts it cannot capture.

The cases:

(1) A warm bath after a day of hard physical labor. Verdict: unquestionably a pleasure, and the pleasure seems located in the feel of the heat and water themselves.

(2) The wave of relief on learning that a feared medical result came back negative. Verdict: a pleasure, perhaps an intense one, but it seems to consist entirely in the removal of fear. There is no positive sensation I can isolate beyond the cessation of a bad one.

(3) The satisfaction of scratching a mosquito bite. Verdict: a pleasure, but with more hesitation than in (2), even though it too seems to be the cessation of an unpleasant sensation. I cannot say why I hesitate here and not there.

(4) A colleague's quiet enjoyment of a rival's minor professional setback. Verdict: a pleasure. It does not feel like an event in the body; it seems to be a way of regarding a fact — a positive attitude toward a proposition — and one can be in it for hours before noticing it.

(5) Solving a difficult proof after hours of dead ends. Verdict: a pleasure, sometimes intense, but it arrives whole, and I cannot locate any distinctive bodily feeling in it. It does not feel like the removal of a discomfort, as (2) and (3) do.

(6) A masochist's enjoyment of pain in a scene he has chosen. Verdict: a pleasure, though it is simultaneously pain. What the pleasure adds seems to be the wanted, savored manner in which the pain is experienced, not a further sensation laid on top of it. Any account on which pleasure is simply the opposite of pain gets this wrong.

(7) The anticipatory pleasure of looking forward to a holiday. Verdict: a pleasure, but what is felt at any particular moment is diffuse and shifting, and the object — the holiday — does not yet exist. The pleasure seems to live in the imagining rather than in any sensory quality of the imagining.

(8) Clearing an overdue administrative backlog on a Sunday afternoon. Verdict: not a pleasure. There is a mild release, but it would be false to say I enjoyed it. This verdict sits badly beside (2) and (3), since all three seem to be the bare termination of an unpleasant state, and I cannot state the relevant difference.

(9) Hours wholly absorbed in a difficult video game or a climb, with no felt pleasure while it lasts. Verdict: not a pleasure while it is occurring, though I willingly seek it and later regard the day as time well spent. I take this to be a counterexample to any simple functionalism that equates pleasure with an experience one is disposed to keep having.

My working hypothesis, which I ask you to test and repair rather than merely confirm, is a two-part view: sensory pleasures, which are experiences with positive hedonic tone (covering (1) comfortably and (3) grudgingly), and attitudinal pleasures, which consist in being pleased about a state of affairs or with one's own activity (covering (4) and (7) comfortably, and (5) only by including being pleased with one's own activity). But the hypothesis leaves (2) and (8) as a pair it cannot distinguish, leaves (6) unexplained because the pain need not itself feel tonally positive, and excludes (9) despite the fact that the person seeks and values the activity. Moreover, the two classes appear to have nothing in common except the word 'pleasure'.

Please now do the following. First, state as precisely as you can the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures all nine verdicts, making explicit which case motivates each clause. Second, apply each candidate to cases I have not settled: the muted pleasure a severely depressed person reports on hearing a familiar song but has no desire to keep listening to; the drug-induced euphoria a person does not wish ever to repeat; the feeling of being proved right after others doubted one; and the diffuse sensual contentment of falling asleep on a warm afternoon. Third, tell me which of my nine verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts would be the best to revise if the choice is between a contrived principle that saves all of them and a simpler principle that overturns one or two. I am not asking for a survey of positions; I am asking you to make the reflective-equilibrium arithmetic explicit.
