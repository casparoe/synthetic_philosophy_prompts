I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, working on the evidential status of long-run predictive theories and the difference between falsification and premature testing. I want to think through this via Malthus, because his case raises a problem I keep meeting in more modern form. In the preface to *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), Malthus distinguishes the familiar fact of natural constraint from his real object of inquiry:

> It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society.

His specific account of those means—geometrical population growth against arithmetical food increase, with misery and vice as the main checks—is now usually treated as empirically defeated. Agricultural output has repeatedly outrun population, and the demographic transition has shown that rising prosperity is associated with falling fertility rather than with mortality-driven restraint. Yet Malthusian constraints keep returning in arguments about soil degradation, water scarcity, climate change, and planetary boundaries. My question is whether that survival is intellectually legitimate. Does Malthus’s argument survive the demographic transition and the Green Revolution, or does it survive only because the repaired version is elastic enough to accommodate any outcome? Identify which premise these developments actually bear on, and state whether there is a weaker but still important Malthusian claim left standing. I want the methodological issue made explicit: can a theory be right but early when its original mechanisms were wrong, and what evidence would count against the rescued version rather than merely delaying it further? Please answer in British English.
