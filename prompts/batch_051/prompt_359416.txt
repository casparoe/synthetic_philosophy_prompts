The British philosopher John Stuart Mill died in 1873, long before the rise of digital labor platforms. In his later political economy, however—especially as he moved toward a qualified sympathy with socialism—he drew a consistent distinction between centralized, bureaucratic state socialism, which he distrusted, and cooperative ownership, in which laborers jointly own and govern the enterprises in which they work, which he regarded as the superior future form of industrial organization and as a means of moral and civic education. Suppose Mill were shown the contemporary platform-cooperative movement: efforts by gig workers to build worker-owned alternatives to the large investor-owned platforms that dispatch, monitor, evaluate, and price their labor through algorithms in ride-hailing, food delivery, and online piecework. How would Mill most plausibly respond? Would he welcome platform cooperatives as an extension of his ideal of associated labor, or would he hesitate because such ventures leave the underlying structure of precarious, fragmented work intact and oblige workers to compete as small firms against much larger rivals? Would his commitment to individual liberty lead him to defend them chiefly as voluntary social experiments, or to argue that the state should actively foster them through law, credit, or procurement? Develop the most Mill-consistent position you can, distinguishing among the strands of his late thought rather than assimilating him either to laissez-faire liberalism or to full socialism.
