I work as a software engineer on trust-and-safety systems, with no formal philosophy background, so when conceptual machinery matters I would rather have it spelled out than assumed. I have repeatedly encountered a proposal for making a large platform's content rules less parochial, and I want it analyzed as a mechanism rather than judged as a moral program.

The proposal is this. Replace the platform's current rule-making staff — a mostly Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) group — with a standing panel of about 1,000 people sampled to match the user base by country, income, education, and religious background. Every month the panel rates proposed content categories from clearly permissible to clearly impermissible; any category rated impermissible by at least 60 percent of the panel is banned platform-wide, and the rest are left to local law. Panelists serve one-year terms, their votes are public, and any user can petition to put a category on the ballot.

Walk through how this would actually behave: what gets removed, what fills the space left open, how organized groups, governments, and panelists themselves would game it, and what the sampling design, the public voting, and the 60 percent threshold each contribute to the result. Then connect it to the WEIRD-psychology worry. Most of what psychology claims about moral judgment comes from WEIRD samples. Would this panel actually capture the cross-cultural variation those samples are accused of missing, or would the selection effects and the aggregation rule swamp that variation and manufacture a false consensus? And what would that outcome tell us about the inference from cross-cultural moral disagreement to the conclusion that there are no objective moral facts?

Finally, tell me which specific empirical finding about the panel's behavior would most change your analysis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
