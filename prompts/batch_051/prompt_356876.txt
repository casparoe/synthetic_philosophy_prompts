I'm a PhD student working on how moral norms actually change, and I keep catching myself telling two kinds of story about two cases I would otherwise lump together. For the end of chattel slavery in the British Empire, I default to arguments and organized campaigning: abolitionists made the suffering vivid, public sentiment swung, and underneath it, if I'm in a materialist mood, interests were doing the work — the Williams-ish line that the slave economy had become less central and abolition was partly about cutting losses. For the end of dueling, though, I reach for honor and ridicule: anti-dueling arguments had been around for generations without doing much, and what killed the practice was that its meaning flipped, so that a duel stopped reading as dignified and started reading as ridiculous — the thing you did to protect your honor became the thing that cost you honor. That's the Appiah-style story. I'd happly call both of these moral revolutions, yet I'm giving moral argument a big causal role in the first and almost none in the second.

Is that asymmetry defensible, or is it just an inconsistency in how I narrate change? Candidate justifications come to mind: slavery had victims whose suffering could be broadcast, dueling didn't; abolition aligned with real material interests, anti-dueling mostly didn't; one practice could be made into an atrocity, the other into a joke. But those are also exactly the features that might predict which side gets to write the history afterward, so I can't tell whether they justify the asymmetry or merely explain why I have it. I'm not after the one true historiography or a reading list; I want to know whether I'm entitled to this asymmetry. And if the whole question is badly posed — say, argument and honor aren't cleanly separable as mechanisms, or asking for a justification of my explanatory habits already smuggles in something — say that plainly instead of handing me a tidied-up answer.
