I'm taking a course this term on logic and rational belief, and the other night a friend and I got stuck for an hour on the following. He says you cannot rationally have a middle-of-the-road probability in an unproven mathematical claim. His example was Goldbach's conjecture—the claim that every even number bigger than 2 is the sum of two primes. It's either true or false, period; the arithmetic facts already fix the answer even if nobody has produced the proof. So, he says, a credence like 60% is just confusion: a probability has to be spread over live possibilities, and there's no live possibility where a sentence that is actually true comes out false. Hence your credence has to be 0 or 1, and if you can't produce the proof, that's a limitation of your memory or computation, not an excuse for a wobbly number. I pushed back on the Godel-ish version of his point—'logic settles which' isn't clean once you bring in incompleteness—but even leaving that aside, his conclusion seems to assume we're perfect reasoners who already draw every consequence of what we accept. I'm not; if someone made me bet my rent money on Goldbach, 60% feels like an honest summary of where my evidence actually is, while jumping to 0 or 1 feels like pretending I have a proof. He says that's just psychology. Unfortunatley neither of us has a proof, so we're stuck on the concept.

Which of us is right? I want more than bar-argument vibes: spell out the philosophical structure of what's going on. Specifically, what should a theory of partial belief about mathematics say about this kind of case—does it vindicate him, vindicate me, or split the difference, and on what grounds? At the very end, give me a one-line verdict in the format \answer{...}, with a short phrase inside (something like \answer{partial credence can be rational}, or whatever you actually conclude).
