I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep running into the same argument in the Adam Smith scholarship, and at this point I honestly can't tell whether it's a real dispute or just two camps talking past each other. On one reading, Smith is basically an early critic of empire: in the Wealth of Nations he dismantles the mercantile system, argues that the monopoly of colonial trade misallocates capital and funnels money to a narrow merchant interest at everyone else's expense, treats the East India Company's government of Bengal as a moral and administrative disaster, and floats voluntarily giving up imperial authority over the American colonies as a perfectly sensible option. On the other reading, the same Smith is a man of his moment whose stadial theory ranks societies from hunting through shepherding and farming up to commercial society, with the clear implication that European commercial nations are the most advanced and that indigenous peoples in the Americas and elsewhere are backward; on this view his actual proposals for the American colonies include keeping them inside an imperial federation with representation in Parliament, and he defends the Navigation Acts as wise measures of national security even while rejecting their economic rationale. So both sides are appealing to the same author, they just weight different books and different passages.

People have been fighting about this for decades and nobody seems to update; if anything the same passages get cited a little more louder each year. My half-baked hypothesis is that the two camps mean genuinely different things by anti-imperialism — opposition to mercantilist trade restrictions, or support for political self-determination, or rejection of a civilizational hierarchy — and that they are also asking different questions: what Smith recommended as economic policy versus what his broader theory of social development commits him to. I'm less interested in a verdict on whether Smith was ultimately good or bad on empire than in a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement. Is this a substantive dispute about how to read the texts, a verbal one, or a bundle of distinct questions filed under 'Smith on empire'? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a sharper distinction, a more specific question, or just accepting that the corpus underdetermines the answer?
