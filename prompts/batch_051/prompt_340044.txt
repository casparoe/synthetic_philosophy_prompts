Here's an asymmetry I can't get rid of. If a couple decides not to have children, nearly everyone treats that as a private, morally neutral choice; there's no bereaved child, just a child who never showed up. But if we learned that everyone alive today would live a full life and no further children would ever be born—so the species winds down with the last generation, no plague, no war—most people would call it one of the worst things imaginable. In both cases, the only thing missing is possible people who never get to exist. So what does the second case have that the first lacks: numbers, the fact that the human story stops, values with no future minds to keep them alive? Give me the strongest principle that makes the couple's choice fine and the species' end a catastrophe, then pressure-test it against a last generation of three people, none of whom wants children, and the species ends with them. If your principle says the three-person ending isn't a catastrophe but the species-wide one is, tell me where the line is and why that line isnt arbitrary.
