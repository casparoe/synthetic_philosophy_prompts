Kavka's toxin puzzle usually works because you cannot just decide to have an intention: at midnight you have every reason to intend to drink the toxin tomorrow, but you also know that by tomorrow the money will already be in your account, so you'll have no reason to actually drink, and that undercuts the intention. The takeaway is supposed to be that intending isn't under your direct voluntary control the way raising your arm is.

Now suppose two things become available. First, a brain scanner that can reliably tell whether someone genuinely intends to do X, as opposed to merely wishing they'd do it or just saying they intend. Second, a neurostimulation protocol that can temporarily put you into a state the scanner reads as a genuine intention to do X — even when the person receiving it also knows, at that very moment, that they almost certainly wont do X when the time comes (the effect fades after a few hours).

Does Kavka's puzzle survive this development? I want to know which premise of the argument the scanner-plus-stim setup actually puts pressure on: the claim that you can't form intentions at will, the claim that a genuine intention is tied to your expectations about what you'll do, or maybe a hidden premise about what it means for a mental state to be genuine. If the puzzle is dead, say what it dies as — a practical problem, a conceptual problem, or both — and if it survives, say what form it takes now. I'm interested in the fate of the argument, not in whether the moneymaking scheme would work.
