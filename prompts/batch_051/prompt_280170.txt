I am a novelist, and the research for a book about restoration and conservation has left me with a philosophical problem I cannot resolve alone. I find that I have clear, almost bodily, verdicts about particular cases of how things persist through change, but I cannot see the principle behind them, and I suspect some of my verdicts contradict others. I should like a philosopher to help me work out what I actually think.

Here are the cases as I find myself judging them, without yet having a theory. A restorer crushes a terracotta Madonna and finds it was simply a lump of river clay pressed into shape; the clay survives the crushing and the statue does not, which makes me say that the statue and the clay were two different things occupying the same place. Yet when I flatten a child's sandcastle, I have no such reaction: the castle just is the sand arranged so, and although the sand survives while the castle does not, I do not want to say that two things were there all along. A wooden barge has every plank and fitting replaced over several seasons; I call it the same barge. If someone had saved the discarded planks and secretly rebuilt an identical boat from them, I would call the rebuilt boat new, not the original. A stone bridge is dismantled block by block after a flood, the blocks are numbered and stored for a decade, and the bridge is then reassembled into the same arch; I call it the same bridge, even though no bridge stood during those years, because there was no rival candidate to compete with it. A touring theatre company gradually replaces every actor, director and stagehand over thirty years while keeping its name, charter and repertoire; I call it the same company. And when I revise a novel until no sentence of the first draft remains, I still call it the same novel, though I would not say the same of a newly written book that merely shares the title and plot.

I would be grateful for your answer to state, as precisely as you can, a principle of material persistence and constitution, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, with each clause motivated by the case that calls for it. Please be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I ought to revise. Finally, apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a bronze horse is repaired part by part until none of the original bronze remains, and the removed bronze is later melted down and recast into the exact form of the horse. Which, if either, is the horse, and why does the principle say so? Please write the entire answer in British English.
