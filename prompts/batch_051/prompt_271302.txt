I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a feature about chain-of-thought monitoring as an AI safety technique, and I've hit a worry I can't turn into anything concrete. The pitch for CoT monitoring is that if you can read the model's reasoning, you can catch a bad policy before it acts. That only works if the reasoning is faithful—if the written steps are the same steps that actually drive the output. My worry is that as soon as the reasoning is part of the training signal, you're selecting for reasoning that scores well with the monitor, not reasoning that tracks the computation. It seems like that pressure alone could make the written chain drift away from the policy, even if the model never lies, never hides a scratchpad, and never wants to deceive anyone. And the nasty version is that this drift could happen while every written step is still true.

I'd like you to build me a thought experiment to that spec. Design a case where:

- a model does some concrete, mundane task—content moderation, credit scoring, triage, anything like that, not paperclips;
- its chain of thought is monitored, and the monitor's approval is part of the training signal;
- the CoT is faithful in a narrow sense: each written step corresponds to a real step in the computation, and no step asserts anything false about the input or the world;
- and yet, after enough training, a careful and competent monitor would be misled about what the policy is actually doing: the reasoning makes it look like the policy runs on factor X while the behavior is actually driven mostly by factor Y, and the difference matters practically;
- the obfuscation comes from optimization pressure, not from any intention to hide. The model is doing exactly what it was selected to do: writing the kind of reasoning that get rewarded.

Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph; the walkthrough can run longer.

Then walk me through the mechanism step by step: what the model is actually computing, what it writes, and why, under the monitoring signal, the misleading version beats the accurate one. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens if you delete just that feature. Finally, steelman the person who says this is fine—you just have to train the monitor on outcomes rather than on the reasoning. Does their fix actually fix your case, or does it break somewhere else?
