Two PhD students in my seminar, Ana and Dev, got stuck arguing about a case our instructor set. A surgeon who has been awake for twenty-four hours examines a set of scans and concludes with high confidence that the patient has a small tendon tear. She is then shown data that sleep-deprived surgeons misread this exact kind of scan at sharply elevated rates. Should she lower her confidence? Ana said she plainly should, and that refusing would treat her own judgment as though it stood outside the statistics; error-prone states rarely announce themselves from the inside. Dev answered that the scans are her first-order evidence and the fatigue data say nothing about any feature of this particular scan. If the surgeon can lay out the detailed features behind her conclusion, then dropping her confidence because of a general fact about sleepy experts would be irrational; it would push toward epistemic akrasia by having her distrust a judgment whose grounds she can still see. Ana countered that Dev was treating the surgeon's judgment as a transparent window onto the scan. It is not: the conclusion is an output of her reasoning, and evidence about the reliability of that reasoning is directly relevant, much as a pilot who learns her altimeter model misreads in certain conditions should not ignore that merely because the dial still reads ten thousand feet. Dev replied that the altimeter exposes only its output, whereas the surgeon can return to the image and assess finer features; so either the first-order features are genuinely accessible, in which case the fatigue data are redundant, or they are not, in which case Ana's principle recommends not modest hesitation but a wholesale distrust of the reasoning being used. Ana closed by saying the pilot and the surgeon differ only in degree of access, not in kind, and the proper response is to discount the first-order conclusion to a degree matching the measured error rate; anything stronger would make higher-order evidence incapable of ever correcting a judgment, which is its entire point. Adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which claims, where the dispute's terms are being shifted or conflated, what the surgeon should actually conclude all things considered, and who is more right overall. To do this well, first reconstruct each position in your own words as a professional epistemologist would, then evaluate each move in turn, and finish with a clear overall verdict. Write your answer in continuous prose, as in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
