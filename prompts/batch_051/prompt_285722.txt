I am a retired natural scientist, recently taken up the study of philosophy, and a conversation with an old friend has unsettled a conviction I held throughout my working life. The friend, whom I will call D, has discovered that a cheap and highly reliable genetic test can tell him whether he carries the variant responsible for a degenerative neurological condition that killed his father. The condition cannot currently be treated or prevented, and D insists that no decision in his life would change either way. His reasoning is simple. If the test is negative, he has merely learned in advance that a catastrophe will not occur, a fact that would have revealed itself eventually. If it is positive, he has purchased decades of dread at no practical benefit. Since neither result alters anything he will do, he concludes that refusing the test is the uniquely rational choice.

My own instinct, formed by a lifetime in the laboratory, is that a free piece of information can never make one worse off: at worst, one can simply decline to act on it. But I find I cannot attach this principle to the case in hand with any confidence. A fact about one's own body is not like a weather forecast for a city one will never visit. It may be impossible to set aside, and it may alter one's inner life whether one wishes it to or not. So I am left torn between a formal principle that seems impeccable and an intuition that D has committed no error at all.

I would be grateful for a careful resolution. Which of us is right, and under what precise conditions can it be rational to prefer ignorance of costless, non-actionable information? Is D covertly assigning a cost to the knowledge itself, or is there a defensible sense in which even a perfectly rational agent should sometimes refuse to know?
