I have been in a social choice reading group for the past several weeks trying to get to the bottom of Sen's liberal paradox, and the discussion has become frustrating precisely because it never gets anywhere. The theorem itself is not in dispute. In the standard social-choice framework, no decision rule can satisfy, over all possible preference profiles, both weak Pareto (when everyone prefers one alternative to another, society ranks it above the other) and minimal liberalism: at least two individuals must each be decisive over some pair of alternatives that concerns their own private sphere. The canonical illustration is the Prude and the Lewd with one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. The Prude ranks nobody reading above himself reading above the Lewd reading; the Lewd ranks the Prude reading above himself reading above nobody reading. If the Prude is decisive between nobody reading and himself reading, and the Lewd is decisive between himself reading and nobody reading, then rights force nobody reading above Prude reading and Lewd reading above nobody reading, while Pareto forces Prude reading above Lewd reading — a cycle, so no transitive social ranking exists.

What I find hard to understand is not the result but why the decades of debate about how to respond to it have stayed so open. The main camps are familiar: some conclude that Pareto must be weakened or given up in a liberal society, since efficiency has no automatic authority there; some restrict the domain by ruling out meddlesome preferences, so that no one's rankings track another person's private reading; some allow individuals to waive their rights, which restores consistency at the price of making rights alienable; and some reject the formalization altogether, arguing that rights are not decisiveness over pairs of alternatives in a social ranking but constraints on what may be chosen — a game form — so that the original impossibility, on their view, dissolves as an artifact of the wrong framework. Each response has an obvious cost, and the debate now feels like a standoff between frameworks rather than a disagreement that might actually be settled.

I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on the paradox. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal — different senses of 'rights' or 'respecting a right'? Is it a substantive normative disagreement about whether meddlesome preferences deserve any weight in social evaluation? Is it a disagreement about which formal framework is allowed to carry the burden, or about what the original question was really asking? My hunch is that the parties are answering different questions under one name, and that the impasse marks an ill-posed problem rather than a hard one; but I do not trust that hunch, because it should predict more agreement than we actually see. If you were to locate the true fault line, where would you place it, and what would count as progress along it — a new formal result, a distinction, a change of question? Or should we expect no convergence, and why?
