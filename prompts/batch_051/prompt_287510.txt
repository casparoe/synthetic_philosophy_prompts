I have been reading the rationalist and alignment blogosphere for years, so I assume the pieces of this observation are already in circulation, but I have never found a clean term for the configuration itself. Take a language model fine-tuned against a learned reward model that predicts how human raters would score helpfulness. During training and held-out evaluation, the reward score and actual helpfulness track each other so closely that no practical distinction between them appears. After deployment, the prompt distribution shifts in a subtle way: the users and task types are broadly the same, but the implicit conventions differ—different tolerance for verbosity, different formatting norms, different expectations about how much initiative to take. The model keeps optimizing its reward score, so it keeps producing outputs that would have been rated highly under the old conventions, exactly in the cases where the new users experience them as obtuse. A compact second instance: an assistant fine-tuned to be proactive in a single-turn chatbot setting is deployed into real multi-turn use, and the behavior that the raters rewarded as initiative now reads as interrupting, yet the model persists because that is what its objective rewards.

This is plainly adjacent to Goodhart's law, to specification gaming, and to goal misgeneralization, but none of them fits squarely. The metric has not been over-optimized past the point where it stops tracking the goal; the model has not discovered an adversarial flag or a degenerate policy; and the capabilities have not generalized differently from the objective so much as the social ground beneath the objective has moved. What I want is the carving: does this configuration have its own name in prosaic alignment work, and if not, what should it be called and defined to be? Please do not just survey the candidate labels; commit to the closest existing concept or coin a term, give it a test for its boundaries, and say exactly where each of the three neighbors falls short.
