I'm preparing a seminar that pairs literary cases with the philosophy of computation, and I want to use Jorge Luis Borges's 'The Library of Babel' as a way into the question of whether a finite sequence can be random. The story describes a universe in the form of an immeasurable library whose books appear to be every possible arrangement of a fixed alphabet, so nearly every volume is gibberish while a tiny, scattered minority contain intelligible text; the librarians spend their lives hunting for meaning—for a book that explains the library or redeems a lifetime—and the narrator closes by entertaining a speculative hope about the library's hidden order. I'd like a careful reading of the story through the lens of algorithmic randomness. In the technical literature, a standard move is to say that randomness is most naturally a property of infinite sequences, while a finite string can only be called random relative to a description language, a measure of compressibility, or a test; yet the story's librarians classify some finite books as nonsense and others as significant, and a few even invest particular volumes with revelatory power. Where does Borges's conceit anticipate or dramatise ideas such as incompressibility, the difficulty of telling a meaningful string from a random one, or the sense that 'random-looking' is a relational feature rather than an intrinsic property of the text? Where does the story resist the technical picture—for instance, by making the search for significance a matter of hope, authority, and interpretation rather than of algorithmic information alone? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not turn the story into a mere illustration of a theorem, and it should not distort the formal notions to fit the literary point. Please separate what is settled among specialists in algorithmic randomness from what is genuinely contested or interpretive, and avoid presenting a contested position as the consensus.
