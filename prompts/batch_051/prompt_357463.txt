I run a small reading group at my company for engineers and product managers on how language models work. The topic has recently become more than a reading group: our product team is thinking about launching a feature that shows users the model's chain-of-thought as an "explanation" of its answer, and two regular members — Sofia, who works on model evaluation, and Jonah, who works on product — have fallen into a running argument about whether any such report could have evidential value. They have asked me to referee because I organize the group, but I have found I cannot; each time one of them seems to win a point, the other exposes a genuine weakness in the reply.

Sofia's opening was that a chain-of-thought is simply more text sampled from the same model and trained on the same next-token objective as the answer. That objective rewards plausible text, not accurate reporting of whatever hidden computation produced the answer, so a model could settle on an answer for one set of reasons and then generate a chain-of-thought citing entirely different ones; absent independent checks, she said, it should be treated as a rationalization. Jonah answered that this proves too much: human beings also produce post hoc rationalizations, yet we still treat what someone says about their reasoning as evidence, often the only evidence available, and if the standard is "direct readout of the underlying computation," almost nothing in human life meets it. Sofia replied that the human analogy is the wrong one, because with a model we are not stuck with testimony: we can inspect activations and run interventions, and in the work she sees, features the model never mentions can drive the answer while the chain-of-thought is made to cite something else. Jonah countered that internal features do not come labeled as "reasons"; a feature's predicting an output is not the same as its being the model's reason for that output, so no intervention study by itself convicts the chain-of-thought of confabulation. Sofia then said the deeper problem is the direction of fit: the chain-of-thought is generated under pressure to look like a good basis for an answer that is already underway, since next-token training rewards local coherence and fine-tuning rewards explanations raters find convincing; the objective is to be accepted, not to be faithful, and that is what confabulation means. Jonah replied that she was assuming the answer is fixed before the chain-of-thought begins, but models generate left to right, so the chain-of-thought tokens causally constrain the answer tokens; if distorting the chain changes the answer, it is part of the process producing the answer, not commentary after the fact. Sofia's last substantive move was that being a causal precondition makes something a cause, not a faithful report: a symptom can be part of a disease process and still misdescribe it. Jonah's last move was that this gives the game away, because "reasons actually operative" has no clear meaning for a language model until someone says what would count as the model's real reasons — the weights, the activations, or the chain itself — and Sofia had not said. Sofia answered that the burden runs the other way: if the chain-of-thought is offered as evidence of the model's reasoning, then the person defending it needs to say what it would be faithful to, and if they cannot, its evidential status is unearned. That is roughly where they stalled.

Please go through the exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific point, where each reply overreaches or concedes too much, what both of them are missing, and whether one side is more right overall. I am not asking you to decide whether our product should surface chain-of-thought to users; I want to know, move by move, whether these arguments actually land.
