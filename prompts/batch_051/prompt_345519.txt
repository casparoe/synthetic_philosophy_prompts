In *The Querist* (1735–1737), George Berkeley asks, in Query 23:

“23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?”

Write a parody of a central bank FAQ page, about 700 words, for an imaginary national currency authority that has officially adopted Berkeley's claim as its founding doctrine. The FAQ should be a deadpan public-education document in which harried civil servants explain to a skeptical public why their money should be understood as a ticket or counter rather than as a store of intrinsic value, why its usefulness consists in stirring up industry and enabling people to participate in one another's labour, and why the absence of bullion backing should not trouble them. The humour should come from getting Berkeley's actual position right rather than from conflating it with modern fiat-money apologetics: the office is not celebrating discretionary monetary policy, but defending a credit-based circulation where money is a reckoning and transferring device. In at least one answer, address the distinction between Berkeley's ticket/counter view and the view that money is just another commodity, and in another answer explain why the public keeps relapsing into talk of intrinsic value despite official guidance. The piece should be readable by someone who has never read Berkeley, while remaining accurate enough that a scholar of eighteenth-century monetary thought would recognise Berkeley's view rather than a straw man.
