A year ago I was elected to the district council of a city where local politics has long been conducted through something between public service and clientelism, and I now face a complaint under the council's new code of conduct. The code, rewritten with advice from an international anti-corruption programme whose template was drawn from Western European public-integrity codes, provides: "A councillor shall not accept any gift, hospitality or other benefit, whether for themselves or for another, if a reasonable member of the public could conclude that the benefit was given to influence the councillor's conduct in office or to create a sense of obligation."

The practice in question is one in which I, like most councillors of the majority party, have taken part. Councillors secure discretionary benefits for individual households: a transfer to a particular housing estate, a place on a municipal training scheme, the expediting of a licence that is ostensibly subject to a queue. The councillor telephones the relevant official and the matter is settled. There is no written agreement and no explicit bargain, but a clear norm of reciprocity governs the practice. A household that has been helped is expected, when the election comes, to attend the councillor's rallies, to allow the party's posters to be displayed on its walls, and to vote for the councillor. A household that fails to reciprocate is treated as ungrateful and finds that its later requests are not answered. I accepted these expectations as part of the moral economy of my district; I have never taken cash or goods from constituents, and I have never made the exchange explicit.

The complaint against me is not that I secured benefits for constituents, but that I accepted a benefit in return: the organised electoral support of the households I helped. My instinct has been to answer that votes are not property, that I solicited nothing in so many words, and that support given freely in return for good service is ordinary democratic gratitude rather than corruption. I no longer trust that instinct, because the drafters plainly had lunches, tickets, envelopes of cash and holidays in mind, not the quiet architecture of an exchange in which loyalty follows assistance as a matter of course.

I am not asking for a defence or a practical strategy. I am asking whether the clause, honestly read, extends to my conduct. Please work through what the plain words, the evident purpose, what the drafters would likely have said if consulted, and what a reasonable member of the public would make of the practice each yield, and say where these approaches pull apart. In the course of that, consider whether the underlying difficulty is that a rule drafted to police the acceptance of benefits treats as corruption a relationship this political culture regards as moral—help and obligation between a representative and the people who put their trust in them—and what a code of conduct would need to look like if it were to govern that relationship without simply pronouncing an entire way of conducting local politics illegitimate. I would prefer the response in continuous prose, as one would write in an email or letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
