I am a graduate student in philosophy of science, and a disagreement between my two officemates has started to follow me around because I am supposed to present a short paper in our Bayesian confirmation seminar next month, and their argument has made me worry that I do not know what confirmation actually measures. After a session on the problem of old evidence, Jonah and Lena continued the debate over coffee; I was present, and I have not been able to sort out who is right.

Jonah began by saying that the Bayesian framework seems to entail something badly counterintuitive. If a piece of evidence E is already part of one's background knowledge, then one's probability for E is 1, and consequently P(H|E) simply equals P(H); by the usual criterion P(H|E) > P(H), there can be no confirmation. Yet the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was documented long before general relativity was formulated, and it was widely treated as among the strongest evidence for the theory. So either Bayesians must deny that such old data provide evidence, or their notion of confirmation is incomplete. Lena replied that Jonah was conflating explanation with confirmation. A theory's power to explain already-accepted facts may be a theoretical virtue, but Bayesian confirmation is a measure of change in credence; where P(E) is already 1, no such change can occur, and the sense that the data support the theory is, she said, explanatory satisfaction rather than confirmation. Jonah answered that this shows the static-credence picture is the wrong tool. What matters, he said, is not the agent's present credence function, in which E is already absorbed, but the credence one would have had before learning E; if H would have been more probable after conditionalizing on E under that counterfactual prior, then E confirms H, and that is what scientists are implicitly doing when they cite old observations for a new theory. Lena said this merely relocates the problem: there is no unique way to construct the counterfactual prior that forgets E, and different reconstructions assign different degrees of support, so Jonah has replaced a formalism that delivers an unintuitive answer with one that delivers no determinate answer unless one rigs the prior to match the desired result. Jonah finally said that even if the counterfactual prior is underdetermined, Lena's alternative is worse, because it leaves no epistemic role for the way old evidence can accumulate into a serious case for a theory; on her view, he said, a genuine scientific achievement becomes a psychological afterglow.
