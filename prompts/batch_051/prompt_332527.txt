Since I retired from a career in the natural sciences and began reading political philosophy, I have settled into an epistemic rule that governs how I react to direct democracy. Whenever a California ballot proposition or a national referendum is decided, I treat the result as carrying almost no evidential weight about the merits of the measure, regardless of the margin. A two-to-one yes vote does not incline me toward thinking the policy was wise, nor does a narrow loss incline me against it. My reasoning is that the electorate is a self-selected sample; that most voters, including me, cannot trace the downstream consequences of a complex statute; and that the framing of the question and the money behind the campaigns can manufacture a majority for a measure few would endorse after seeing it implemented. For my own beliefs about policy, I therefore substitute the judgment of the legislative staff, auditors, or agency experts who would have to administer the thing, unless I have independent evidence about its effects.

A former colleague who retired to Switzerland objects that the Swiss have made decisions by referendum for generations, often on highly technical questions, and their record seems at least as good as their parliament's. She thinks I am confusing a decision procedure with an information source. I feel the force of that, but I also suspect there is a selection effect: Swiss referendum practice may have built up institutions and habits of scrutiny that filter out the worst cases, so the Swiss record may not transfer to other settings.

Is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it a technician's prejudice dressed up as skepticism? Under what conditions, if any, should the size and distribution of a vote be allowed to move my beliefs about a measure's merits, and how would a more defensible version of the rule be stated?
