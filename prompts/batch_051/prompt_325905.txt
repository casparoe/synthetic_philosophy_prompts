For the past several years I have been running a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory, and one discussion reliably stalls in the same place. When we reach the Allais paradox, the room splits into two camps. One camp holds that expected utility theory remains the correct normative standard, and that Allais-type preferences—where changing a common branch flips a choice—are mistakes to be corrected by reflection; they argue that the common branch cancels out, and that anyone who sees this and still holds the preference has not fully thought it through. The other camp, drawing on rank-dependent and prospect-style models, replies that the common branch is not inert, because the certainty or near-certainty of an outcome plays a distinctive role in rational evaluation that the independence axiom rules out from the start; on their view, it is the axiom, not the preference, that should give way.

My working hypothesis is that this is neither an empirical disagreement about how often people choose in Allais-type cases nor a formal disagreement about what the independence axiom entails. I suspect the two sides differ over the direction of fit between axioms and intuitive preferences: one treats the axioms as the standard to which preferences are answerable, the other treats stable, reflectively endorsed preferences as the data to which axioms are answerable. But I am not confident in that diagnosis. The impasse might instead be a verbal disagreement about the word rational, or a hidden disagreement about whether there is a single notion of preference under risk to theorize about at all.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this debate that engages what each side actually says, rather than a survey of non-expected-utility theories or a judgment about which side is right. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress—a theorem, an experimental design, a new distinction, a reframing of the question—or make the case that the impasse should be expected to persist, and why.
