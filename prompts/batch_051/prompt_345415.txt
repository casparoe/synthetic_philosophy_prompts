I'm a PhD student working on the theory of writing systems, and I've been turning over the following problem. The standard argument for phonemic alphabets runs roughly like this: a script's job is to record speech, so the ideal script assigns one distinct grapheme to each phoneme and nothing to anything else, and deviations from phonemic transparency are inefficiencies left over from history. The phonemic ideal was built on the assumption that writing is best understood as a notation for sound and that readers learn and process a script by recovering sound from it. Recent computational practice seems to treat writing rather differently. Modern language models are typically trained on text segmented into subword units produced by frequency-driven algorithms such as byte-pair encoding; these units often cut across phoneme and morpheme boundaries, yet the models perform well without anything like a phonemic analysis of the writing system. Does the phonemic-ideal argument survive this development, or does it need repair? Identify which premise of the standard argument is actually under pressure, say what a repaired version would have to give up, and explain what, if anything, the original argument still shows about alphabets. Please answer in British English.
