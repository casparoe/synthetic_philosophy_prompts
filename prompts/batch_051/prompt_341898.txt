I'm a second-year PhD student in psychology, and my advisor runs a pretty strict pre-registration lab: before I'm allowed to collect data I have to publicly file my hypothesis, design, and analysis plan, and he says a result only "really counts" if it was predicted in advance. The other week I found a strong pattern in some old survey data the lab had lying around — everyone agrees it's probably real — but my advisor says we can only write it up as "exploratory," and the first journal we tried said the same thing. Meanwhile I'm taking a philosophy of science course for fun, and we just did Hempel's raven paradox: on Hempel's picture, "all ravens are black" is equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," so a white shoe confirms it, and more generally confirmation is a kind of timeless logical relation between evidence and hypothesis — it doesnt matter when you thought of the hypothesis or whether you went looking for the evidence.

To me the pre-registration norm looks like it presupposes the opposite view: the very same data confirm more — or maybe only confirm at all — when the hypothesis was registered first, and a finding that would be publishable if pre-registered becomes "merely exploratory" if it wasnt. But I'm not sure exactly what theory of confirmation the practice is committed to, as opposed to what it merely fits well with. So: what does strict pre-registration presuppose, in the "what must the world be like for this practice to make sense" sense? Does it presuppose that predictions confirm more than accommodations, that exploratory findings confirm nothing, that researcher degrees of freedom destroy evidential value, or something else? Is the practice coherent, and can it actually be reconciled with the Hempelian line, or are they in genuine tension? And if a psychology department really adopted Hempel's view, what concrete changes to how it handles pre-registration, exploratory analyses, and post hoc findings would follow?
