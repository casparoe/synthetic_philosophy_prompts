I am preparing for a seminar that moves between modal logic and the foundations of probability, and a mismatch between the two keeps nagging at me. In modal settings, I have picked up the habit of reading possibility existentially: a proposition is possible just in case there is at least one accessible world in the model at which it holds. In probabilistic settings, I have also learned not to read probability zero as impossibility, since under a continuous distribution each exact outcome receives probability zero and yet some exact outcome will occur. I want to hold both habits at once, but they seem to collide.

When I try to combine them, I picture a probability model as a set of possible worlds together with a measure that assigns each world some weight. If modal possibility is just existence in that set of worlds, then every world in the set looks like something the model counts as possible. But then it seems the measure should give each of those worlds at least some positive weight, which would force every possible outcome to have positive probability. If instead a world can sit in the model while carrying zero weight, then possibility seems to have floated free of the measure entirely, and I can no longer see what the possible-worlds picture contributes to the probability model. Where exactly does the analogy between possible worlds and sample points break, and which of my assumptions should I give up?
