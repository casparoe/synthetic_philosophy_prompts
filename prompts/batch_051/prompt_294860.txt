I am a third-year undergraduate taking a module on distributive justice, and after last week's seminar on Nozick's entitlement theory two of my coursemates, Hannah and Daniel, got into an argument about the Wilt Chamberlain example. Our lecturer had reconstructed the example as follows: begin from any distribution one regards as just; then allow a much-admired basketball player to sign contracts under which each willing spectator pays him a small additional sum, with the result that he becomes vastly wealthier than everyone else. Nozick's conclusion is that if the starting distribution was just and the transfers were voluntary, the resulting distribution is just as well, and that any principle which demands restoring the original pattern requires constant interference with people's choices. I could not decide how much of this argument survives scrutiny, and neither party would yield, so I would welcome a move-by-move adjudication.

The exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Hannah said that the argument has no purchase on actual redistributive politics, because no existing distribution is the just baseline the argument needs: actual holdings descend from conquest, enclosure, theft and discrimination. So the conclusion that the unequal result is just never gets off the ground.

(2) Daniel replied that this misses the structure of the argument. Nozick is not deriving the justice of any actual distribution; he is asking the egalitarian or the desert theorist to stipulate their own favoured starting point and then watch it unravel through unremarkable exchanges. The target is the internal coherence of any patterned principle, not history.

(3) Hannah answered that the stipulation is doing all the work. If one grants at the outset that people have unrestricted rights to transfer whatever they legitimately hold for whatever others will freely pay, then of course any fixed pattern will be upset; the contested question is precisely whether people have such rights. Describing the transfers as voluntary presumes a full-blown property entitlement, so the argument cannot get purchase against someone who holds that what one may do with one's holdings is partly settled by the pattern itself.

(4) Daniel said this dismissed the example too quickly. The transfers in question—gifts, ticket purchases, agreed fees for performance—are exactly the sorts of acts most moral views regard as permissible unless special reasons rule them out. To preserve a pattern one would have to prohibit or continually tax countless ordinary exchanges, and that ongoing interference is a real moral cost rather than a question-begging description.

(5) Hannah responded that Nozick's own historical entitlement theory faces a parallel problem. It must verify that every link in a holding's chain of title is untainted, and since Nozick admits the need for rectification of past injustice but provides no account of it, his view is no less committed to pervasive correction of holdings. Pointing out that a principle is costly to apply therefore does not distinguish entitlement theory from the patterned principles it opposes.

Please present your adjudication as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Each point should assess the corresponding move above as sound, partly sound, or mistaken, with a brief justification; your fifth point should also indicate which of the two is closer to being right overall.
