I've spent the past year recording a friend's verdicts about small everyday failures — lateness, flaking, forgotten errands, promises half-kept — and she is unusually consistent about them, which makes me want to reverse-engineer the rule she is actually applying before I bring her a new case. Her record so far:

(a) A colleague says she'll send the agenda before Monday's meeting, forgets, and sends it twenty minutes beforehand; the recipient had already made her own notes and says it made no difference. My friend's verdict: not a moral failure, just sloppy.
(b) A man promises to drive his neighbor to a Thursday-morning medical appointment, and on the strength of that she turns down a ride from her sister. Wednesday night he cancels with a text saying work came up; she has to book an expensive ride-share and nearly misses the appointment. My friend: clearly wrong, and not a small wrong.
(c) Two old friends arrange to meet for coffee on a Saturday while one is in town. That morning the one who was supposed to meet her texts that she can't make it. The visitor, who had set aside the morning, shrugs it off and goes to a museum, and later says it was fine. My friend: mildly wrong — you shouldn't say yes if you might flake, but it's small.
(d) A person promises to water a neighbor's plants for six days, waters them on four of the six, and the plants are droopy when the neighbor returns; they recover, but the neighbor is hurt that she didn't simply say she was overcommitted. My friend: genuinely wrong; she took responsibility and then half-did it.
(e) At the end of a phone call a friend says, "I'll call you this weekend," and doesn't. The other person hadn't particularly expected the call and wasn't disappointed. My friend: not wrong; that's social noise, not a promise.
(f) A woman promises her sick friend that she'll pick up a prescription on the way home, forgets, and the sick friend has to go out to get it herself. My friend: clearly wrong.

The new case is this. Sam promised his brother, weeks ahead and in so many words, that he would be at his Saturday-evening birthday dinner, and told him he had already blocked off the evening. By Thursday the brother, newly absorbed in a relationship, told their mother he would be relieved if the dinner were called off; he did not cancel it, and he said none of this to Sam. On Saturday afternoon Sam, worn out from the week, texts that he can't make it and wishes him a good evening. The brother replies at once: "No worries at all — really, it's fine," and later tells their mother he was relieved. What would my friend say about Sam — did he do anything wrong, and how wrong, and how confident would you be in that prediction? I'd also like the reasoning behind it: what rule or rules fit her entire record, which earlier case is the closest analogue, and if more than one rule fits, what further case would cleanly separate them. My own suspicion is that she ultimately cares about what the promisee actually leaned on and whether anyone was let down, but I can't rule out a fidelity-to-one's-word view that the record also happens to fit.
