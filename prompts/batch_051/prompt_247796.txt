Here's the tension. On one side, I'm attracted to the view that there are what I'll call free-floating rationales: normative reasons that are not reasons for any particular agent. Example: there is a reason that the asteroid not hit Earth, even in a scenario where it's undeflectable and no one can do anything about it. Or: there is a reason for there to be less cruelty in the universe, full stop, before we ask who, if anyone, could bring that about. I like this view because it unifies things. Agent-relative reasons — my reason to save this drowning child, your reason to keep your promise — start to look like free-floating reasons plus facts about who is positioned to respond to them. It also gives a non-metaphorical sense to comparisons like 'one of these two worlds is simply better than the other', not just better-for-the-people-in-it or better-by-someone's-lights. And it makes sense of why I can regret something that was impossible for anyone to prevent: I'm not just sad about it, something actually counted against the world being that way.

On the other side I have a very firm intuition that reasons are essentially for someone — that a reason is the sort of thing that can weigh in deliberation and guide a reasoner. A 'reason' that no one could ever act on, respond to, or even register feels like a lever connected to nothing. My intuition is that 'there is a reason the asteroid not hit' is either elliptical for what some possible agent — an ideal observer, God, whatever — would have reason to do if they existed, or it's just a fancy way of saying the asteroid hitting would be bad. And badness is not a reason; it's an evaluative property that generates reasons only when there's an agent who can do something about it. On this picture, free-floating rationales are either hidden-agent claims or category mistakes.

I genuinely dont want someone to tell me which side 'wins.' I want a negotiated verdict. Present the strongest version of both positions, then walk me through it: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked and dropped? What would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned instead? And crucially, is there a reading of 'reason' on which both survive — say, free-floating normative pressures that aren't themselves reasons but ground reasons when a reasoner shows up — and would that be a real compatibility or just relabeling one side to avoid losing?
