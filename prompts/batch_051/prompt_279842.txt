I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on John Stuart Mill’s *On Liberty*, with sixteen students and one 90-minute session. I would like to run a classroom activity built around a specific passage from Chapter III, “Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Wellbeing”:

“But the evil is, that individual spontaneity is hardly recognized by the common modes of thinking as having any intrinsic worth, or deserving any regard on its own account.”

The session should make students feel the force of what Mill is diagnosing here: that everyday practical judgement treats spontaneity and originality as dispensable or merely decorative, rather than as something with independent value, so that the case against conformity tends to be lost before argument even begins. I do not want a lecture or a textual commentary exercise, and the passage should not be distributed until afterwards. I want a game, role-play, or structured deliberation in which students first find themselves thinking as “the common modes of thinking” do, and then have to confront what that way of thinking leaves out. Please give me full rules, roles, materials, a timed sequence for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps the moments in the activity back to Mill’s distinction between experiments in living, conformity to custom, and the idea that individuality is a positive ingredient of human happiness. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which aspect of Mill’s view the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Write the answer in British English.
