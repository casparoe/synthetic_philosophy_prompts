A colleague of mine chairs a research ethics committee that decides whether study participants gave valid consent to enroll. She tends to issue brief rulings rather than long opinions, and I have collected the following six. She ruled consent invalid when an instructor recruiting for his own study told a student that if she refused to participate she would not pass his course. She ruled consent valid when a researcher offered twenty-five dollars for an hour-long interview and an undergraduate with no particular financial need enrolled for the money. She ruled consent invalid when a researcher described an injection as painless and free of side effects even though, as the committee later determined, it carried a small but real risk of temporary nerve damage. She ruled consent valid when a researcher falsely claimed his study was run by a prestigious university, where the participant later said the institutional affiliation had made no difference to her decision. She ruled consent valid when a participant enrolled because his supervisor had made clear that employees who declined would be seen as uncooperative, and the researcher neither knew of nor encouraged that pressure. She ruled consent invalid when staff enrolled a man with advanced dementia after a short conversation in which he seemed agreeable but did not understand what the study involved.

Next week she takes up a new case. A researcher running an early-stage drug trial is paying nine thousand dollars for a three-week residential study. The side effects are significant but were disclosed accurately. The volunteer in question is behind on rent and facing imminent eviction, and she told the committee that she felt she had no real choice but to enroll. The committee accepts that she understood the risks, was not deceived, and was not threatened by the researcher.

What do you expect my colleague to rule? I am less interested in the local regulations than in the principle she is implicitly running. Set out the candidate principles that fit all six of her prior rulings, explain how each would decide the new case, identify the closest prior ruling and whether it is genuinely controlling, and give your best prediction together with a further case whose outcome would settle which principle she actually holds.
