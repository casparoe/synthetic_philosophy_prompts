I spent much of last night arguing with a friend about whether Galileo's way of reconciling Copernican astronomy with Scripture is best read as an early version of the idea that science and religion operate in wholly separate domains, and the argument left me wanting extra care on exactly that point. Coincidentally, I have to grade the following exam answer for a course on the Scientific Revolution, and I would like your help.

Question: Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century critics of Copernicus held that the new astronomy contradicts passages of Scripture, such as the sun standing still at Joshua's command. Explain the main argumentative moves by which Galileo, in his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, tries to show that Copernican astronomy and Scripture are compatible. Reconstruct at least three distinct moves, and then identify one respect in which Galileo's position remains open to a theological objection even on its own terms.

Student answer: Galileo's Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina argues that the Church's scriptural objections to Copernicanism rest on a mistake about the purposes of Scripture and of science. His central move is the principle of accommodation: Scripture speaks according to the understanding and ordinary speech of the people for whom it was written, so passages like Joshua's command to the sun are not intended as astronomical claims. He supports this with the remark he attributes to Cardinal Baronio, that the Bible teaches the way to go to heaven rather than how the heavens go. Galileo also invents the doctrine of the two books: God is the author of both Scripture and the book of nature, and since God cannot contradict himself, the two books cannot genuinely conflict. From this he concludes that, when a passage of Scripture appears to conflict with Copernican astronomy, the passage should be reinterpreted, because Scripture has no authority over natural questions. In this way Galileo establishes a non-overlapping magisteria position three centuries before Stephen Jay Gould: Scripture governs faith and morals while science governs nature, so there is no conflict left for the Church to worry about. His position is therefore difficult for any theologian to reject without denying that God authored nature.
