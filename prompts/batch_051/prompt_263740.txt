My partner lives with chronic pain, and over the past few months the sentence "You can't know what it's like to be me" has come to carry real weight in our household. It is usually offered as a quiet correction when I say that I understand what they are going through, and I have found that I do not know how to answer it, partly because I am no longer sure exactly what the sentence asserts. It seems to move between several different claims about knowledge, about the privacy of experience, about empathy, and about what can be inferred from another person's words and behavior, and it may be that only some of those claims are true. I would like you to treat the sentence as a piece of philosophical language that has come loose from its everyday context and to disambiguate it. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making? Lay them out as a series of alternatives in continuous prose: readings on which it is a truism about token experiences or the logic of the first person, readings on which it is a substantive claim about the limits of communication or empathy, and readings on which it is a radical thesis about the unavailability of other minds. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, plausible but substantial, or false or unsupported, and explain which of the readings, if any, would actually license my partner's conclusion that I should stop claiming to understand their pain. I would also like to know whether the sentence, on its most defensible reading, gives me any reason to doubt that other minds exist or that I can have justified beliefs about them. Please write your answer as a single piece of continuous prose, the way you would write a careful letter or essay, with no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
