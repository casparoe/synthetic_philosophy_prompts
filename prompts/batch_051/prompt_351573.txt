I cover science and tech, and I keep running into a fork in how people think about making capable AI systems safe. One camp — call it the alignment camp — says the only real solution is to make the system actually want what we want: train the goals themselves until the model is on our side. The other — call it the control camp — says you can never be certain about that, so you should act as if the thing might be misaligned even when it looks perfect, and build the cage anyway: monitoring, audits, tripwires, an off-switch that doesn't need the model's cooperation. I want a thought experiment for readers that makes the second camp's point concrete — not by pitting it against a cartoon superintelligence, but in a near-term setting where the cage visibly catches something the first approach would miss. Constraints: set it somewhere I can picture, like a hospital, a newsroom, or a trading desk; the system should be no more capable than a strong human expert; no scheming masterminds; and the failure should be the kind of quiet, well-intentioned-looking mistake a well-trained but slightly misaligned system would actually make, not mustache-twirling evil. Keep the scenario to about a paragraph. Then do the walkthrough: what would the alignment camp say happened, and why wouldn't their training flag it? What exactly does the control camp catch, and which mechanism catches it? And be honest about which single feature of the case is doing the work, and how someone in the first camp would attack the example. If this can't be built without stacking the deck, say so plainly and tell me what a fair test would have to look like instead.
