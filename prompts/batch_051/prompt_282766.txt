I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep tripping over "Western values" in both the literature and in arguments with flatmates. The phrase gets used as if it names a fairly stable list—free speech, rule of law, individualism, secularism, maybe Christianity depending on who's talking—but the boundary work seems to do most of the actual arguing. One colleague says human rights are Western values being imposed on the rest of the world; another says no, they're universal, and the West only recently got around to claiming them. I'd like a working definition of "Western values" that can be tested against a list of hard cases. Here's mine, roughly arranged from clearly-in to clearly-out, though I'm not sure the ordering survives contact with a definition: freedom of speech in the US First Amendment sense; the scientific method as an epistemic norm; constitutional rule of law; liberal democracy; the post-war human rights framework; French laïcité; social democracy and the welfare state; Christianity as a moral tradition; capitalism and free markets; gender equality; Soviet-style communism; and something like Confucian filial piety, which I'd put clearly outside.

My hunch is that the phrase does two jobs at once: one descriptive-historical (values that actually became dominant in Western Europe and its offshoots) and one normative (values worth defending, where "Western" works more like a certificate than a geography). If you think that's right, give me the two definitions and say which cases move between them; if you think it's a single concept, defend that instead. Either way, run the definition(s) through all twelve cases, flag the verdicts that feel wrong or forced, and don't tidy up the embarassing ones. My deeper worry is whether the category is coherent at all once it's asked to do both jobs, or whether "Western values" is mostly a phrase for ending arguments rather than clarifying them.
