I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a near-future book about a gerontologist, Vera, who has become the reluctant public face of an anti-life-extension movement. The transhumanist characters call her a deathist, but she doesn't think she's that: she's fine with most ordinary medicine, she just thinks there's a line somewhere and the culture has stopped seeing it. I want to make sure her position isn't just vibes before I write the big debate scene, so I've been collecting her gut verdicts on cases. Here's the verdicts as I've got them:

- A 9-year-old with bacterial meningitis gets antibiotics and recovers completely. Fine.
- A 72-year-old with a broken hip gets surgery and rehab, and is back gardening that summer. Fine.
- A 55-year-old with high LDL and a family history of heart attacks goes on a statin. Fine.
- An 80-year-old with fainting spells gets a pacemaker. Fine.
- An 85-year-old with wet macular degeneration gets eye injections to save her sight. Fine.
- A healthy 50-year-old starts taking an off-label drug that slows several recognized hallmarks of aging, hoping to live to 110. Not fine.
- A 45-year-old biohacker does monthly plasma exchanges with young donors to slow aging. Not fine, and she finds it a bit vampiric.
- A 30-year-old with a dominant mutation for early-onset Alzheimer's takes a drug that delays onset by decades. Fine.
- A 62-year-old with early-stage Alzheimer's is prescribed a drug that slows progression. Fine, but she's quieter about this one.
- A healthy 70-year-old joins a trial of a senolytic drug predicted to add eight healthy years. Not fine, even though theres no disease yet.
- A 70-year-old with terminal pancreatic cancer declines chemo that would buy maybe six months. Fine; she'd probably do the same.
- A 70-year-old with terminal pancreatic cancer takes the chemo. Also fine, somehow.

I keep trying to turn her view into one principle, something like: treat what's broken, don't try to move the finish line. But the statin and the pacemaker already treat aging processes under other names, and the early-Alzheimer's case feels close to the healthy 50-year-old's anti-aging drug. The case I go back and forth on is a 66-year-old with positive Alzheimer's biomarkers but no symptoms, who wants the same delay drug the 30-year-old gets. It looks like the 62-year-old, but it also looks like a healthy person taking an anti-aging drug.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most of Vera's verdicts? State it precisely, say which of her verdicts it can't keep, and tell me how it comes down on the 66-year-old case and on a 75-year-old with no complaints who wants senolytics anyway.
