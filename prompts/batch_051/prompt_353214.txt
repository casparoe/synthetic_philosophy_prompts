I spent most of Saturday arguing with my friend Dana, who underwrites life insurance. She's convinced that for any particular person or thing there's a right reference class for its probability, and she acts like she can always point to it, so I started writing down her rulings. For a coin toss by my nephew, she says the class is fair-coin tosses, not tosses by that nephew on Tuesdays. For my 58-year-old uncle who smokes a pack a day and has high blood pressure, she says use 58-year-old male smokers with hypertension, not 58-year-old males, and definitely not 58-year-old males whose first names start with R. For a specific suspension bridge, she uses bridges of that design, age, and traffic level, not all bridges, not bridges last inspected on a Monday. For a used car she'll go down to make, model, and year but refuses to add color, says it makes no difference. For a parole applicant she uses offense type and age, not whether the guy's last name has seven letters. My read of the pattern was: throw out the classes that don't help prediction and go as fine as the data let you. She said that wasn't it and kept talking about "real causes," but she couldn't state her rule to my satisfaction. I told her she's just bluffing her way through the reference class problem.

Now her company has a new underwriting model that predicts mortality a lot better than their standard age/sex/smoking tables. It gets much of that edge from credit score and how often the customer orders late-night food delivery. There's no causal story for why those two things would make someone die sooner; the modelers just keep saying the validation numbers are great. The case on Dana's desk is a 52-year-old woman, non-smoker, normal blood pressure, low credit score, lots of late-night delivery orders. Dana has to decide wether to price her in the broad class or the model's fine-grained class. If she's going to be consistent with her own track record, what should she do? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog and does it actually settle it? What rule is she running—my go-as-fine-as-the-data-allow rule, her causal-type rule, or some third thing? Where do those rules disagree on this case, and what would a further case need to look like to tell them apart?
