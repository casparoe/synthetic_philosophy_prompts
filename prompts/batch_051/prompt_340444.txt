My supervisor works on reasons and is always saying a situation can contain a free-floating rationale: a reason that is already there before anyone in the situation has noticed it. I've been collecting her verdicts, and so far they look like this:

- A lone hiker reaches a bridge with a warning sign in a language she can't read; the sign says the bridge is out. She says the hiker has a reason to stop.
- A guy has an unread text in his pocket warning that his flight is boarding and his passport is still at security. She says he has a reason to turn around even before he reads it.
- A camper is about to eat wild mushrooms that are poisonous, but he has no training to identify them; a guidebook with clear pictures is sitting open next to him and he hasn't looked. She says he has a reason not to eat them.
- Two identical lottery tickets, with no further difference between them: no reason to pick one rather than the other.
- Watering a plastic plant to make it grow: no reason to do that.

Next week she's giving a talk about a case that's new to her. A guide must choose at noon between two mountain passes: one is clear and one is blocked by a fresh rockslide. The only person who could of seen the slide died before reporting it, cloud cover rules out air reconnaissance, and by noon there is no route by which anyone could determine which pass is clear. The left pass is in fact the clear one. Does the guide have a reason to take the left pass rather than the right?

I don't just want a yes/no. I want the candidate rules that fit her track record, what each one predicts about the passes, your best guess about her verdict, and a further case that would cleanly separate the rules. Please write the response in these sections: Candidate rules (about 120 words), What each rule predicts here (about 100 words), Best prediction (about 120 words), Tell-apart case (about 80 words).
