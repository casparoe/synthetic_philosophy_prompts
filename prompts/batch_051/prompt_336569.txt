Consider the Coen brothers' film O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000), which opens with a title card acknowledging that it is based on the Odyssey and then transposes that epic's materials into Depression-era Mississippi: an escaped convict named Ulysses Everett McGill struggling home to a wife on the verge of remarrying; a blind seer encountered on a railroad handcar; a predatory one-eyed Bible salesman; three singing women by a river who part the hero from his companions; and a climactic flood that overturns every plan. I would like a reading of the film through a standing dispute about myth. One side holds that behind the many variants of a myth lies something with authority — a canonical or original version, an ur-myth, or at least a stable narrative armature — so that a retelling is best understood as an adaptation of that prototype, more or less faithful to it. The other side holds that a myth simply is its variants: that in a living oral tradition there is no original, that every performance is a recomposition, and that what we now call the Odyssey is only one retelling that later prestige fixed into a standard text. Where does this film stand in that dispute, and does its own practice count as evidence for either position? On one reading, its easily recognizable Homeric episodes, dressed in comic local substitutions, seem to presuppose a durable core that survives transposition intact. On another reading, the film flaunts its distance and its medium-specific substitutions so heavily that the Odyssey begins to look like one node in a network of retellings rather than the source. Work through at least two or three specific scenes or correspondences, give an honest account of what they show, and then say clearly where the film complicates or resists your eventual verdict instead of merely illustrating it. Conclude by saying whether the film adds anything to the theoretical dispute that arguing about canonical versions in the abstract would miss. Please answer in American English.
