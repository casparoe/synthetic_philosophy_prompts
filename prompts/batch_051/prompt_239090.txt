I'm a junior in high school and I sit on my school's science research review committee. We have been asked to approve or reject a student project, and the case has split us, because it turns on something the authors of our research policy clearly never anticipated: whether a computational simulation can be the kind of thing the rules protect.

Our district's independent research policy reads, in Section 9 ("Vertebrate Animal Research"): "No student project may subject any live vertebrate animal to pain, distress, or death, except momentary pain or distress incidental to approved observation. All animal use must be supervised by a qualified adult advisor and must, when feasible, be replaced by non-animal methods such as computer simulation."

The proposal that broke us is, on its face, exactly the kind of replacement Section 9 encourages. The student has coded a simplified mouse-brain simulation — a few hundred thousand spiking model neurons, run on her laptop — and she wants to train it to avoid choices that trigger a simulated electric-shock signal, then compare its learned avoidance with published data from real mice. She argues the rule cannot possibly apply: there is no live vertebrate animal, no body, no tissue, and no pain in any ordinary sense. Another committee member replies that if the simulation is computationally similar enough to the circuitry that, in a biological mouse, underwrites pain, then approving the project uses the letter of the rule to defeat its purpose, which is to keep students from causing suffering.

We are stuck between two readings. Under the text of the rule, is the proposed simulation outside Section 9 altogether? Under the purpose of the rule, should it be treated as covered because the project studies and administers something functionally like pain? If the two readings diverge, which should govern a student review committee, and should it matter that the drafters, if asked today, would probably say they intended computer simulation as the safe alternative and never imagined it could fall within the rule? Beneath all of that, I think this is a question about what the rule was ever protecting: the biological animal, the capacity to suffer, or something else — and, more sharply, how we should decide which computations matter morally, if any.

Please respond as an expert in research ethics and the philosophy of mind, with real familiarity with debates about artificial sentience and the boundaries of sentience. Think through the issue carefully before answering: start by laying out the readings that the text, the purpose, the drafters' likely answer if asked, and a reasonable student's understanding each support, then say where each reading leads and why the disagreements run deeper than the wording. You can assume no one on the committee wants citations or a reading list; we want the reasoning itself. End with a recommendation for the committee, even if it is provisional.
