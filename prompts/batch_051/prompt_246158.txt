Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about Anselm's ontological argument, and he ended on an objection I still cannot shake. He reconstructed Anselm's reasoning like this. (1) God is by definition a being than which nothing greater can be conceived. (2) A being that exists in reality is greater than a being that exists only in the understanding. (3) If God existed only in the understanding, then something greater than God could be conceived: namely, God existing in reality. (4) That would contradict (1), so God must exist in reality. Each step seemed acceptable to me as he walked me through it, and I could not locate the mistake. Then he pressed the parallel. (5) By the same form of reasoning, define the perfect island as the island than which no greater island can be conceived. (6) If the perfect island existed only in the understanding, then an island greater than it could be conceived: namely, that island existing in reality. (7) So the perfect island exists in reality. (8) But that conclusion is absurd; no description of an island can make one exist. (9) Since the two arguments share their form and the second is plainly unacceptable, Anselm's argument must be unsound in just the way the island argument is. I want to say the analogy breaks down somewhere, but whenever I tried to say where, I sounded as though I was inventing an exception to protect the theological conclusion rather than finding a real difference between the cases. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to give something up, I would rather give up the island than premises (1) and (2), but I would like to know whether that preference can be made principled rather than merely convenient.
