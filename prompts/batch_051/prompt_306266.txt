I’m still chewing over a long argument I had with a friend yesterday about anthropic reasoning. We were trying to settle whether the self-sampling assumption or the self-indication assumption is the right way to handle observer selection effects, and he kept returning to Bostrom’s Dungeon thought experiment as though it settled the matter in favor of SSA. The passage he cites is from Nick Bostrom, *Anthropic Bias: Observation Selection Effects in Science and Philosophy*, chapter 4:

“The world consists of a dungeon that has one hundred cells. In each cell there is one prisoner. Ninety of the cells are painted blue on the outside and the other ten are painted red. Each prisoner is asked to guess whether he is in a blue or a red cell. (Everybody knows all this.) You find yourself in one of the cells. What color should you think it is?—Answer: Blue, with 90% probability.”

I want you to argue for the position that this passage, taken by itself, does not adjudicate between SSA and SIA: a careful defender of SIA can also give 0.9 credence to being in a blue cell. Please make the strongest possible case for that underdetermination position. In particular, show exactly how the SIA calculation would go if I treat my own existence as evidence about the number of observers rather than as a random draw among pre-existing observers; then answer the most serious objection someone like my friend would raise, namely that SIA’s use of my existence seems to ignore the information that ninety cells are blue and ten are red. Act as an expert in formal epistemology and anthropic reasoning. Think through the argument carefully before you write, and then produce your response in American English, in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
