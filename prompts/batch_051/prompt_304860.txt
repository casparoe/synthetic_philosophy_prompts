In Friedman's 1953 essay The Methodology of Positive Economics there's that passage where he basically says the realism of a theory's assumptions doesn't matter much for judging it, and in fact the most important hypotheses tend to have wildly unrealistic assumptions. Our professor called it the F-twist and now I'm wondering whether the argument survives the rise of cheap simulation and agent-based modeling. Back then you often needed a simple, tractable model to get any predictions at all, so unrealistic assumptions were kind of a forced tradeoff. But if you can now simulate millions of heterogenous agents with more realistic decision rules, doesn't Friedman's line start to look like an excuse for not doing the hard modeling work? Or does he still have a point because any simulation also has to make false simplifying assumptions somewhere? I guess what I'm asking is: which specific premise in Friedman's argument does cheap simulation actually attack, and can the argument be repaired once that premise is weakened?
