Here's one I've been chewing on for my language-and-identity seminar, aimed at the old chestnut of when Latin becomes Italian. Counterfactual: from around 800 until 1790, nobody in Italy commits the spoken vernacular to writing. Notaries, chanceries, preachers, poets — they all keep writing in the best Latin they can manage, while the spoken language drifts exactly the way it actually did (case endings falling away, vowels shifting, the usual). So none of the tenth-century notarial scraps that people sometimes call the first written Italian, no Dante or Boccaccio or Petrarch writing in the vernacular. Assume the rest of history otherwise runs as it did: printing shows up in the fifteenth century, the peninsula stays politically fragmented until the nineteenth, the same cities are rich and prestigious. The only difference is that the vernacular never gets written down. Then, in 1790, a generation of educated Italians — in Milan, Naples, Venice, and elsewhere — decides for the first time that it wants to write the language people actually speak and give it a name.

Predict, with reasoning, what happens over the next hundred years. I want the chain of reasoning more then a story. Specifically: (i) when these people sit down to write 'the spoken language,' do they understand themselves as writing several different languages, or one language with regional variants, and what decides that? (ii) Which variety (if any) becomes the basis for a written standard, and through what mechanism — a court, a publisher, a city, one poem getting copied everywhere? (iii) In this world, is it more or less natural than in ours to say that Italian is just Latin continued by another name, and does the question 'when did Latin become Italian?' land differently? (iv) What does this do to the later standard-language/dialect split, and to the idea that a language needs a name, a grammar, and a literary canon to count as one at all?

Be explicit about which parts of the prediction are robust and which are guesses, and say what would have to be different for each link to fail. Then, for calibration, tell me how confident you are in the overall picture and which single claim you're least sure of.
