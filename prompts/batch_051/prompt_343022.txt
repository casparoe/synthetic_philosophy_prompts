You are a philosopher of social science who works on the measurement of well-being, and you are being asked to assess a belief-formation policy rather than to produce empirical findings. Think carefully, and before you answer, distinguish the main senses of happiness that might be in play.

My policy is this. When I need to judge whether people in one country are genuinely better off than people in another, I treat the World Happiness Report's annual ranking as if it settled that question directly. A country's place in the ranking, or its movement up or down the ranking, determines what I believe about its people's happiness unless I have some specific reason to distrust that country's data. I know the ranking is built largely on answers to a life-evaluation question rather than on moment-to-moment reports of emotion, and that the report fits those answers together with factors such as GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, and perceived corruption. But I have found that trying to be more skeptical than this leaves me paralysed, so I keep using the ranking as my default.

Is this shortcut defensible? Under what conditions, if any, is life evaluation a good enough proxy for happiness to license my policy? Does whether it is defensible change if I also use the ranking to answer the practical question of whether a government should pursue policies aimed at moving its country toward the top?
