Ever since a close friend began working in longevity science and started insisting that an indefinitely extended life would be worth wanting, I have been trying to settle whether a finite mind could even make use of an endless life. I keep coming back to this argument, which feels nearly airtight, and I want to know exactly where it goes wrong, if it does.

(1) A mind's experiences are wholly fixed by its brain's total physical state: two physically identical brain states would be identical in how they feel from the inside.
(2) A human brain is a bounded physical system, and a bounded system at a fixed energy can occupy only finitely many distinct physical states.
(3) Therefore, a human mind has only finitely many possible kinds of total experience.
(4) An unending human life would contain an infinite sequence of experiences.
(5) Any infinite sequence drawn from a finite stock of kinds must contain at least one kind infinitely many times.
(6) So an unending life must contain at least one kind of experience endlessly repeated, and no unending life could consist of endlessly novel kinds.

Conclusion: the ideal of endless novelty is therefore not a coherent object of desire; at most, an extended life could offer rearrangements and recurrences of kinds of experience already had.

I suspect the trouble is in premise (2), and perhaps also in the slide from recurrence of a kind to the life's being repetitious in the pejorative sense, but I cannot make either objection precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the most defensible version of that step? If the argument can be repaired by restating it, does the conclusion then follow?
