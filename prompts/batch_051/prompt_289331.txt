I am trying to work out what I actually believe about the principle of bivalence — the thesis that every statement is either true or false — and I would be grateful to have my reactions to a series of cases examined as a set. Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. "France is a monarchy" is simply false.

2. "The present King of France is bald" is neither true nor false. The sentence does not strike me as putting forward a falsehood about the world; rather, it misfires, because there is no present King of France for it to be about.

3. "It is not the case that the present King of France is bald" is true. Here the negation, as I hear it, denies the whole of (2), and that denial seems right.

4. "The present King of France is not bald" is neither true nor false. This is a different way of negating (2), and it seems to inherit the same gap.

5. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow", uttered at a time when no present fact or law settles whether there will be one, is neither true nor false.

6. "Either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be one" is true — even though, on my verdict in (5), neither disjunct is true or false.

7. "A man with no hair on his head is bald" is true.

8. "A man with 60,000 hairs on his head is bald" is false.

9. "A man with 4,000 hairs on his head is bald" is neither true nor false; that count seems to sit in the borderline region.

10. "The number five is green" is false, not gappy; and "The number five is not green" is true.

I cannot tell whether these reactions add up to a coherent view. Three points of tension worry me amongst the set. First, (2) and (10) both apply a predicate to something that seems to lie outside the predicate's proper range, yet I treat one as gappy and the other as plainly false. Second, (3) and (4) are two ways of negating the same sentence, but I give them different verdicts. Third, (5) and (6) jointly imply that a disjunction may be true although neither disjunct is true or false, which sits uneasily with ordinary truth tables.

What I would like is this. Please state the strongest candidate account — or a small set of candidate accounts — of when a statement is true, false, or neither that captures as many of these ten verdicts as possible. For each candidate, give the principle precisely, indicate which of my cases motivates each clause of the principle, apply it to all ten cases, and apply it to some cases I have not listed, including at least: "Either a man with 4,000 hairs on his head is bald or he is not bald"; "If there is a present King of France, then the present King of France is bald"; and the liar sentence "This sentence is false". If no candidate delivers all ten verdicts without becoming ad hoc, say so honestly, identify which of my verdicts must be abandoned, and explain why those are the expendable ones. Please write your answer in American English.
