I direct the model-review unit of a national public-health and environmental agency. My reviewers approve or reject the predictive models the agency uses to set exposure limits and emissions targets, and they handle several dozen submissions a year; the decisions are binding and often litigated, and the reviewers have strong technical backgrounds but no training in the philosophy of science. Please write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the debate over Milton Friedman's so-called F-twist — the claim that a model's assumptions need not be realistic as long as the model predicts well — that my reviewers can follow for each submitted model: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with an approval or a rejection, and the points where the unrealism of an assumption should matter despite good predictive fit. Then run the procedure on two worked submissions: a pharmacokinetic model that treats the body as a single well-stirred compartment, and an emissions model that omits a known ocean-carbon feedback but reproduces historical observations well. Include at least one case in which the procedure returns a verdict you expect me to dislike, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle and where a human must sign off because the issue is one of scientific prudence or values. Also indicate which argument or consideration would most likely lead you to revise the procedure or a worked verdict, and write the whole thing as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter or email.
