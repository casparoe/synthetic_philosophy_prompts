I'm currently taking a course on well-being, and we recently spent a week on the paradox of hedonism. I tried to turn the paradox into a full argument, and the result now makes me suspicious because the conclusion is much stronger than anything we discussed. Here is the argument as I've been setting it out:

(1) Psychological claim: directly and single-mindedly aiming at your own happiness tends to make you less happy than you would be if you aimed at other things for their own sake.

(2) Hedonism: happiness is the only thing that is ultimately valuable; everything else is valuable only insofar as it contributes to happiness.

(3) If hedonism is true, then a rational person should treat happiness as their only ultimate aim, and should regard all other goods—friendship, work, understanding—as merely instrumental to happiness.

(4) But by (1), treating those other goods as merely instrumental to happiness is self-defeating: it makes the happiness they are supposed to produce less likely.

(5) Therefore, the rational hedonist is required to adopt a policy of treating certain things as ends in themselves while believing that they are not; the theory tells its adherents to live as though it were false.

(6) A theory that can only be put into practice by being disbelieved, or by maintaining a standing pretense about one's own ends, cannot be a correct account of how to live.

(7) So hedonism is either false or not liveable; since an account of well-being should be liveable, hedonism must be rejected.

I find each step plausible on its own, but the argument seems to prove too much: it turns a wrinkle about how to pursue happiness into a refutation of hedonism. I want a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of options: identify the exact step or inference that fails, and say what hedonism would have to concede to escape the argument unscathed. If I had to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (6), but I want to know what I am paying for keeping it.
