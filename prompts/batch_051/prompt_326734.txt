You are a philosopher of science with expertise in the methodology and limits of physics, and you are being asked to adjudicate a dispute. Please think carefully and reconstruct the strongest version of each side's position before you rule.

Two friends of mine—a theoretical physicist, Mara, and a philosopher of physics, Jonas—got into an argument I could not settle. Here is their exchange, as close to verbatim as I remember it.

Mara: "Physics has a direction. Newtonian mechanics became a limiting case of relativity, and classical electromagnetism became a limiting case of quantum electrodynamics. There is no reason to think the sequence must stop. A final theory would be one with no free parameters and no unexplained structure left over, and we would know when we had it: every measured value would come out as a prediction, not a fit."

Jonas: "That story can only be told backward. Each theory you call a stepping stone was once treated as the full account of nature and later turned out to be an effective approximation valid only in a limited regime. The Standard Model is already known to be an effective field theory. Any candidate final theory has exactly the same status: it is empirically adequate at the energies we can reach and possibly wrong beyond them. So even if we held the final theory in our hands, we could never know it was final."

Mara: "You are confusing finality with certainty. We never require exhaustive testing to accept a theory; we require the theory to be so constrained that altering any piece of it makes it incoherent. A theory with no adjustable parameters that still gets every measured value right is not just another empirical fit. And of course you can always construct contrived rivals—but being contrived is a reason to dismiss them. That is how theory choice works everywhere in science."

Jonas: "Finality is not an ordinary theory choice, like preferring one dark-matter model over another. To say a theory is final is to say there is no possible circumstance under which it will be replaced—a claim about the entire future of inquiry. But our evidence is finite and local. You can always add an unobservable sector that makes your 'final' theory a low-energy approximation of something deeper while leaving every current prediction unchanged. You have not escaped underdetermination; you have just declared it irrelevant."

Mara: "Then your standard proves too much. It would show we cannot know any theory, not just that we cannot know the final one—and you do not believe that. We know general structural impossibilities, like the impossibility of perpetual motion machines, without canvassing every mechanism. A judgment of finality would be that kind of claim: warranted on theoretical grounds, not by exhaustive measurement."

Jonas: "The disanalogy is exactly the point. Perpetual motion is ruled out by a conservation law whose structural role we can demonstrate inside the theory. There is no structural feature of an equation that tells you no deeper layer exists beneath it."

Adjudicate their argument point by point. At each stage of the exchange, say who is right about what, where each overreaches, and what both are missing about what it would take to know that a theory is final. Do not simply declare an overall winner; evaluate each move as actually made.
