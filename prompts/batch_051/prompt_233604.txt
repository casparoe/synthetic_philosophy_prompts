I have been arguing with a friend who works in wetland restoration, and we keep arriving at the same impasse. Consider two cases. A mining company drains a small, unremarkable peat bog and, as a condition of its permit, creates a new wetland nearby, carefully replicating the hydrology, vegetation, and soil biology of the old one; within a decade a wetland scientist studying it without knowledge of its history cannot reliably distinguish it from the original. The regulators nonetheless treat the original as a separate, unrecovered loss, and my friend maintains that the created wetland, however faithful, is only a substitute. Now consider a city that condemns a nondescript steel bridge from the 1960s, demolishes it, and builds a replacement with the same span, capacity, and appearance. No one treats the old bridge as an additional, unrecovered loss once the new one is carrying traffic; the replacement simply is the bridge, fully and without remainder. What explains the difference? In both cases, as stipulated, the end state is functionally and structurally interchangeable with what was there before; what differs is that the bog came about without human design, while the bridge is a human artifact.

The easy answers run out quickly. The original bog cannot be dismissed as irreplaceable in practice, because the case stipulates that the created one is indistinguishable in its present properties; it cannot be a matter of lost sentient creatures, because the new wetland is stipulated to support the same kinds and numbers of creatures; and it cannot be a matter of public attachment, because almost no one knew the bog existed. A kidney transplant makes the contrast sharper: the healthy transplanted organ is not called a counterfeit for lacking the biography of the failed one, because we think an organ's value is exhausted by what it does. But then why does the bog's value apparently not reduce to what it does? We seem to assign the natural object a value that adheres to its history and origin, while assigning the artifact a value that resides entirely in its present properties and functions.

I want the strongest principled defense of this asymmetry, and a candid verdict on whether it survives scrutiny. Does the bog possess a kind of value — intrinsic value, value in its independence from human purposes, or something relational and historical — that the replacement cannot share? Or should someone who mourns the old bog while accepting the new bridge admit that she is merely attached to origins? Test whatever principle you offer against the hardest version of the case: the replacement is stipulated truly to be indistinguishable in every present respect, differing only in its history of coming into being. If the principle survives, say exactly what the replacement lacks. If it does not, say whether the asymmetry should be kept as a useful commitment or given up as an inconsistency.
