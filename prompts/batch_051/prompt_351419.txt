I serve on the appeals committee of a charitable fund that pays for the treatment of serious diseases in patients who cannot otherwise afford care. The deed establishing the fund defines disease this way: "For the purposes of this fund, a disease is a pathological process of a part, organ, or system of the body, arising from any cause other than direct physical injury, that is manifested by a characteristic set of signs or symptoms." A case has come before us that the drafters, writing in 1984, plainly did not anticipate. A child who received whole-genome sequencing at birth is found to carry a variant known to cause a progressive neurodegenerative condition; the onset of signs and symptoms in early adulthood approaches statistical certainty, but the child is now asymptomatic and will remain so for more than a decade. The treating physician argues that a disease process is already under way at the molecular level and that treatment begun now would materially slow the later damage. The fund's administrator argues that the definition, read in its ordinary sense, covers only a person in whom signs or symptoms have appeared. Work through the case under four approaches—the words of the definition, the purpose the fund serves, what the drafters would have said if asked about a presymptomatic genetic diagnosis, and what the phrase would convey to a reasonable contemporary reader—and then say which approach should govern and why. I care less about the administrative outcome than about what the disagreement exposes: does this case show that a naturalistic, value-free definition of disease cannot do the work asked of it without smuggling in a normative judgment about what counts as a pathological process or a genuine symptom, or can the dispute be settled on biological facts alone? Set out your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the four approaches, and then your ruling on which should govern and what it shows about the naturalism/normativism dispute over the concept of disease.
