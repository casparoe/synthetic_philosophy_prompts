I work for a mid-sized city's climate action team, and our comms people keep coming to us with individual-behavior campaigns — "pledge to take the train once a week," "go meatless on Mondays," "turn your thermostat down," that kind of thing — and every time we end up arguing the same two hours about whether we're lying to people. The worry is the inefficacy objection: any single resident's one less car trip or one less burger is such a tiny part of the total emissions problem that it seems to make no measurable difference, so pressing people to do it might be ethically slimy or at least intellectually dishonest. Some of the team says we should only run sytemic campaigns (zoning, transit funding, utility policy) and drop individual pledges entirely; others say if everyone thought that way nothing would ever change, and that the small-chance-of-tipping-something reasoning rescues the individual ask. We have this fight roughly once a month, and our director is sick of it.

I want you to write us a decision procedure we can actually run on any proposed campaign to decide whether the inefficacy objection kills it or not. It'll be used by non-philosophers, usually in under 45 minutes before a comms deadline, and it has to be defensible if a reporter or a hostile councilmember asks why we ran it. The procedure should be a step-by-step checklist: what question to ask first, what counts as a good enough answer, where we stop with a "no, don't run it," and where we have to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a fact question. It should deal with the moves people actually make around this — expected value of a tiny chance of crossing some threshold, "what if everybody did that" universalization-style reasoning, whether a campaign targets real individual emissions or is just laundering the city's failure to do structural stuff, and cases where the actual value is norm-shifting or signalling rather than the literal tonnes of carbon. Be honest about which of these are genuine considerations and which are rationalizations, and don't pretend the procedure is more objective than it is.

Then run it on three proposals we're currently looking at: (1) a "don't fly to your vacation this summer" pledge aimed at residents, (2) a get-out-the-vote push in a council district where the outcome is effectively certain, and (3) a "meatless Mondays" campaign run jointly with the public schools. For at least one of them I expect — and want you to say plainly — that the procedure gives an answer we probably won't like, because we're alreadyattached to the campaign.
