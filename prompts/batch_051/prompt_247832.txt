I direct a team that writes annotation guidelines for a large vision dataset, and I keep running into a pattern I cannot name. Our first task asked raters to sort images into "sharp" and "blurred." Agreement was excellent at the two extremes and poor in the middle, so we added a third category, "partially blurred." The original disagreements mostly vanished, but new ones appeared at once: raters now disagreed about the boundary between "sharp" and "partially blurred," and the contested images were not merely difficult examples of either class—the new cut-off itself seemed to have a fringe of cases that were neither clearly on one side nor clearly on the other. When we added a fourth category, the same thing happened again at the new borders. Tightening the written definitions relocates the dispute rather than shrinking it, so I do not think this is ordinary inter-rater noise. I know philosophers discuss the sorites paradox for vague terms like "heap" and "bald," but that is usually a forced march through small accumulated differences in a single property. What I am observing is different: every distinction we introduce creates a boundary, and the boundary itself resists sharp application, no matter how many distinctions we add. Does this pattern have an established name? Is it a distinct phenomenon from the sorites paradox, and is it a genuine feature of vague categories or just an artifact of imposing discrete labels on continuous variation? I am not asking for a bare label; I want an exact account of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its limits are.
