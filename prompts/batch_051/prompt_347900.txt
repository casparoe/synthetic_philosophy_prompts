I keep noticing a kind of decision problem in discussions about superintelligent predictors, and I dont know if it has a name. The basic version: an Omega-like predictor flips a coin. If tails, it asks you for $100. If heads, it pays you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would have paid in the tails branch. After you're told it landed tails, you know the $10,000 is gone; paying now seems to buy you nothing. Yet before the flip, you'd want to commit to paying. Fine.

Then there's a second version I keep half-remembering, maybe from a forum argument, maybe I dreamt it. The predictor tells you: I wrote something in this envelope. If you refuse to pay me $100, I open it. It will either say "I predicted you'd pay, so here is a $10,000 check" or "I predicted you would not pay, so here is a letter that costs you $10,000 in reputation damage." If you pay, the envelope stays sealed and nothing happens. Here the payment doesnt causally affect the envelope contents, but the predictor chose the contents based on its prediction of your payment. It feels similar to the first but nastier, because in the first the heads payout is a genuine counterfactual branch, while in the second the payout or punishment is sitting in the same branch, sealed, contingent only on your refusal to pay.

I want to know whether this second case is a known variant in decision theory, and more importantly whether the rational action differs from the first. Is the envelope version just a restatement of the first, or does the hidden-content structure change whether its rational to pay? I'm not just after a name; I want the exact carve: what distinguishes these two cases, what boundary cases sit between them, and whether it matters that in the second case the predictor's threat is already implemented rather than merely counterfactual. Please give a definite position: pay, don't pay, or pay in one and not the other, with reasons, not a list of options.
