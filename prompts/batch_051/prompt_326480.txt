I serve on an eight-person committee that reviews internal proposals and produces a ranked priority list for funding. It is well known that members vote tactically, and I have been arguing that we should test alternative procedures, including approval ballots, to make tactical voting less tempting. A more senior member shut the discussion down with the line "There's no such thing as strategy-proof voting," and everyone treated that as decisive. I believe this is a loose paraphrase of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, but I do not know exactly what that theorem says or what it rules out. I would like you to lay out the different precise claims that one-liner could be making, from the near-tautological to the strong and likely false. For each reading, tell me whether it is a defensible theorem, a platitude, an overgeneralization, or simply wrong. In particular, I need to know which readings would actually justify the conclusion that redesigning our voting procedure is pointless, and which scope conditions matter for that verdict: the number of alternatives, whether the method uses ordinal rankings or something like approval ballots, the role of restricted preference domains, and the difference between worst-case manipulability and manipulation that is likely or profitable in practice. I want the readings presented as a menu I can take back to the committee, so I can tell precisely where the theorem stops and the rhetoric begins.
