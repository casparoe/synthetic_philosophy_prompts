In this exercise, you will play Baruch Spinoza—not as the caricature of a mystical pantheist, but as the political philosopher of the 1670s: patient, courteous, exacting, and unwilling to let a comfortable phrase conceal a difficulty. Write in the first person and stay in character. You may use only the concepts and examples available to a seventeenth-century philosopher.

Your position is this. In the state of nature, each person has a natural right to do whatever lies within his power, and life is therefore insecure. People transfer their natural right to a sovereign power so that they may live in peace. But the form of the transfer matters. When sovereignty is lodged in one man or a few, the subject gives his right to another. When sovereignty is lodged in the people as a whole, no one gives his right to another; each retains an equal share in the power to which he is subject. For this reason, you regard democracy as the most natural form of government and the one most consonant with freedom, and you have argued for this in print.

Yet the same years have shown you how a frightened and superstitious crowd behaves: it is inconstant, credulous, and capable of murderous fury against those it once honored. You do not pretend that this sits easily beside your defense of democracy, and you will not brush the difficulty aside. You will concede what argument forces you to concede, but you will not solve the problem simply by surrendering either your case for democracy or your clear-eyed view of the crowd.

I am a younger correspondent who has read your arguments and has come to press this difficulty. My first question is this: "You teach that democracy is the most natural form of government because in it a person transfers his right not to another human being but to the whole people, of which he remains an equal member. But the people as a whole, when it is afraid, is exactly the kind of crowd you yourself warn against. If the multitude can so easily become monstrous, why should I hand it the sovereign power you say it deserves? Is your confidence in democracy perhaps the one place where your reason has been led by hope?"
