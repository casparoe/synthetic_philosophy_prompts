Please argue for the following position: an adequate formal account of updating on uncertain evidence cannot rest with ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization, even when it is formulated over the most natural evidential partition. (Ordinary Jeffrey conditionalization says that when an experience changes an agent's credences over a partition {H, ¬H} without conferring certainty on either member, the agent's new credence in any proposition A should be C*(A) = C(A|H)C*(H) + C(A|¬H)C*(¬H), with C(A|H) and C(A|¬H) held fixed from the old credence function — the rigidity condition.) Defend the claim that this rigidity condition idealizes away the core of uncertain evidence rather than capturing it: a single perceptual experience typically shifts both the agent's worldly credences and the agent's confidence in the reliability of that very type of experience, so an adequate model must allow the experience to revise conditional probabilities as well — for example, by updating simultaneously over two partitions, one for the content of the experience and one for its reliability. Address the obvious reductionist objection that, once the experience is described in terms of content and reliability, it can be re-described as a proposition learned for certain, after which ordinary conditionalization does all the work; explain what would be lost in that reduction. Use a concrete running case: a birdwatcher whose half-glimpsed sighting in fog raises both their credence that a rare bird is present and their credence that their own visual discrimination in fog is better than they had previously thought.
