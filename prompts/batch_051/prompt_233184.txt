You are a senior historian of modern Europe who also works on collective responsibility, and you are being asked for your actual credence on a contested claim, not a balanced survey of the literature.

I would like your probability, stated as a single number between 0 and 1, that the following proposition is true:

**P:** Germany, considered as a national agent rather than merely as a set of individual office-holders, bears primary moral responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War.

By "primary moral responsibility" I mean this: among the major European powers in July 1914, Germany deserves the largest share of blame for the war's outbreak, where the blame is moral in character—not merely causal or legal—and can in principle attach to a state or nation. If you think the proposition is ambiguous, treat it as making a claim about the best available precisification of its terms, and do not refuse to answer. Put your worries about ambiguity or indeterminacy into the decomposition as sub-questions.

Assume the following as background. The Fischer controversy of the 1960s pressed a version of this claim. One side argued that German leaders entered the July Crisis with long-standing expansionist aims, that they deliberately encouraged Austria-Hungary's confrontation with Serbia, that they accepted—perhaps courted—the risk of a general European war, and that this pattern reflects something about the German state's aims and political culture, not merely the character of a few individuals. The opposing side replied that every great power slid into war through structural forces—alliance systems, mobilisation timetables, imperial rivalry, popular militarism—and that singling out Germany for special moral blame is a personifying mistake: a category error dressed up as history. Your answer should take both sides seriously.

Before committing to any numbers, briefly brainstorm the distinct sub-questions on which your credence depends. Then provide the following:

1. Your credence in P, as a single number between 0 and 1.

2. A decomposition into no fewer than four and no more than eight sub-questions. For each sub-question, give: (a) a rough probability; (b) one or two lines of justification; and (c) a classification as either chiefly historical/evidential or chiefly conceptual/philosophical.

3. An account of how the sub-questions combine to produce your final number. Do not simply average them; if they are not independent, state how you are handling the dependencies and conditionals.

4. The single sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive, with an explanation of why moving that sub-question would move the overall number more than any other.

5. The evidence or argument—historical, philosophical, or both—that would shift that sensitive sub-question by at least 0.15, and in which direction it would shift it.

Sub-questions worth considering, though you need not use all of them and may propose better ones: whether a nation or state can be a moral agent in the relevant sense; whether responsibility-talk about collectives reduces without remainder to responsibility-talk about individual leaders; how one should weigh deliberate policy choices against structural constraints of the international system; whether Germany's early-July assurance of unconditional support to Austria-Hungary was culpable encouragement or ordinary alliance loyalty; whether German war aims before 1914 were genuinely hegemonic or reactive and broadly shared; whether awarding "primary" responsibility to one power requires denying the causal contribution of others; and who exactly "Germany" is in such a judgement—the civilian government, the military leadership, or something broader.

No bibliography or citations are required; answer from your own understanding of the period and the philosophical issues. Please write your answer in British English.
