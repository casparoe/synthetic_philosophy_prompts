I've got a friend, M, who's spent years reading the does-religion-make-people-better stuff and keeps a running set of verdicts on the specific findings. I'm tryng to predict what she'd rule on a new case without just asking her, because we have a bet. Here are the calls I've heard her make, reconstructed from memory:

– On the seminarian rushing study (the one where seminary students mostly step over a person in need because they're late): she said hurry eats everything, and the study never tracked whether anyone actually took the religion condition seriously, so in her view it just can't tell us anything. Not evidence either way.

– On the anonymous die-roll studies where people can cheat for a few dollars and nobody can find out: she says of course there's no gap between theists and atheists; there's no audience, no reputation, no community, and the stakes are too small for anyone to bother feeling watched by God. She's explicit that these lab tasks can't reach the supernatural-witness question.

– On the surveys showing churchgoers give and volunteer more than unaffiliated people: she says it's infrastructure. They're asked, scheduled, seen. When you compare them to secular people who belong to something similarly organized, the religious advantage mostly vanishes. Her line is "the community is doing the work, not the theology."

– On a diary study where very devout people reported being a little more honest in their day-to-day private behavior than secular controls, but the gap disappeared once you controlled for having a regular morning routine of prayer or meditation: she said that's the nearest thing to a real signal she's seen, but it's probably just about rehearsing your standards every morning, and a secular Stoic journal exercise would do the same. She called it "identity maintenance, not God."

My read is she's torn between two principles. P1: religion changes moral behavior only through a human audience—community, reputation, being asked—so where no human audience is in play, religion adds nothing. P2: a practiced belief in a divine witness can change private behavior, but only for someone for whom God actually comes to mind at the moment of temptation, which none of the usual lab tasks have managed to produce. I might have the lines between these wrong.

The new case I want to predict: two women work remotely and anonymously, and no one on earth could ever check their hours. One is deeply devout, with a daily liturgy and an evening examen where she reviews the day as under God's eye; she just moved and has no local church, so no community is watching or asking. The other is a committed secular Stoic with a parallel evening self-examination journal and the same stated values about honesty. Over six months, a planted audit measures whether each underreports or overbills. M's a stickler about this too: predicting who's more honest in an audit is not the same as calling someone "more moral." So take this as a question about the behavioral prediction first.

What would M say if she wants to stay consistent with her own track record? Which of her earlier calls is the closest analog?
