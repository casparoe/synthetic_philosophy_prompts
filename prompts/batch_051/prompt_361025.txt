I'm a PhD student in Chinese philosophy, and I keep running into an asymmetry in how people read Mencius and Xunzi on human nature, and I honestly can't tell if it's defensible or just pro-Mencius bias.

Think as an expert on pre-Qin Confucianism. Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles before you commit to one.

Take Mencius's child-at-the-well passage. He says, roughly, that anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well will feel a spontaneous alarm and compassion, not because they want the parents' gratitude or a good reputation, but just because they have a heart that cannot bear another's suffering. He takes this as evidence that human nature contains a sprout of benevolence. Most people I know treat this as a compelling, intuition-friendly inference.

Now take a passage from Xunzi's Xing'e, chapter 23, often translated Human Nature Is Bad. He says, roughly: everyone who wants to become good does so because their nature is bad. The thin want thickness, the ugly want beauty, the poor want wealth. If they already had a thing within themselves, they would not reach outside for it. So the very fact that we desire goodness shows that goodness is not already there.

Here's the asymmetry. The same people who find Mencius's inference from a spontaneous feeling to an innate moral sprout compelling will usually say that Xunzi's inference from the desire for goodness to the absence of goodness is a bad analogy or a non-sequitur. But I don't see a structural difference: both go from some feature of our current psychology (a feeling of compassion / a desire for X) to a claim about what human nature has or lacks. Mencius infers the presence of something from a feeling; Xunzi infers the absence of something from a desire. If anything, Xunzi's move looks more modest to me, because wanting what you lack is a familiar pattern.

Tell me: is there a principle that justifies treating these two arguments differently? If you defend the asymmetry, state the principle exactly and test it against a nearby case where the same principle forces you to accept an inference that seems bad. If you reject the asymmetry, say what should be done: take both seriously, reject both, or something else.
