Here's something I've never been able to square. In a normal trial, a witness swears not just to avoid falsehoods but to give the whole truth—the oath makes completeness part of the promise. Yet almost everything about how the examination actually works pushes the other way. Perjury is only prosecuted for statements that are affirmatively false. A witness who gives a technically true answer that's carefully built to leave the jury with the wrong impression—like being asked whether she has a Swiss bank account and answering with something true about the company's account instead—hasn't committed perjury, and in U.S. federal courts that's an explicit rule, not a loophole: a literally true answer can't support a perjury conviction even when the witness intended to mislead. Meanwhile the witness isn't there to volunteer information; lawyers decide what gets asked, and a lawyer who lets a witness ramble is generally making a mistake. So the same trial treats lying as a serious crime against the proceeding and treats misleading as pretty much the cost of doing business. What view of the moral difference between lying and misleading does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's coherent in light of the oath, and tell me what would have to change in courtroom procedure if we decided the lying/misleading distinction carried a lot less moral weight than the system currently acts as though it does.
