I teach philosophy of religion, and I've been going to a C of E parish for the past term because the music is good, and I keep tripping over the same thing. The liturgy addresses Jesus in one breath as the eternal God who made everything and is owed worship, and in the next it speaks of this same person being hungry, weeping, not knowing things, and dead on a cross. It calls his mother the Mother of God, while the vicar insists the divine nature didn't have a mother and the Trinity didn't die. Last Sunday the Gospel reading had him disclaiming knowledge of the day and hour; a few minutes earlier the congregation had called him true God from true God. The whole arrangement seems to run on some distinction between person and nature, but nobody I ask can tell me what a 'person' is meant to be such that one person can have two natures, one infinite and one finite, without collapsing into two people or one mixed nature. I can't work out wether the practice genuinely needs a worked-out metaphysics or just a few rules of thumb.

Reconstruct, as precisely as you can, the theory of personhood and nature that this practice actually presupposes. Then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say where it's philosophically underdetermined, in that several rival theories would license the same worship; and tell me what would have to change in the liturgy or the devotion if the underlying view were replaced by (a) the view that the human Jesus is a distinct person from the divine Son, or (b) the view that in Christ there is really only one nature, the human side being in some way absorbed. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
