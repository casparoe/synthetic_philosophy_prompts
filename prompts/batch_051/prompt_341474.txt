I serve on the strategy committee of a small foundation. We regularly assess unique events—a bill passing in a particular session, a technology becoming viable within five years, a partner organization surviving a leadership transition—and we must usually decide on a deadline that rules out waiting for better base rates. My personal rule is never to assign such an event a point-valued probability or feed one into an expected-value calculation unless I can identify a reference class with a usable base rate. Otherwise I remain at the level of ordinal or interval judgments ("more likely than not," "unlikely but not negligible"), on broadly Keynesian grounds: the evidence in these cases supports non-numerical comparisons of strength rather than a precise degree of belief; a number would counterfeit measurement; and the weight of evidence—how much relevant information bears on the question—is distinct from the probability itself, so I can act decisively on a well-grounded qualitative judgment even when no precise figure can be extracted. A colleague says this is unfalsifiable cover: my forecasts are vague enough to survive any outcome, and I never have to face a calibration score or a decision-theoretic audit. Is my rule defensible as it stands? When would assigning even a rough subjective probability make my judgments better rather than merely more precise-looking? Would you answer differently for a better-resourced organization that could plausibly construct the missing reference classes? If the rule is only partly defensible, restate it so that it keeps the Keynesian cautions without becoming an excuse for avoiding comparison, updating, and accountability.
