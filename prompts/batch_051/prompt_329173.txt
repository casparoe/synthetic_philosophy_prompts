Here's a puzzle I can't stop thinking about. Most mainstream languages treat a number as interchangeable whenever the value agrees — 5 is 5, full stop — but a list is a thing that persists even as its contents change, and two lists with identical contents can still be different objects; mutate one through an alias and every other alias sees the change. What theory of identity over time does that design presuppose? I don't mean the implementation — I mean, does it commit you to a picture where an object is a bare 'it' that stays the same thing while its properties change, so 'which object it is' and 'what it currently contains' are two different facts? Does the design quietly rule out the view that a thing just is its current contents? And is this split between identity and value the real reason mutation is so hard to think about, or does the difficulty come from somewhere else? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics, say what the practice strictly needs versus what it merely fits, and tell me what a language would have to give up if all sameness were sameness-of-content and updates made new versions instead of mutating in place. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, don't restate the question.
