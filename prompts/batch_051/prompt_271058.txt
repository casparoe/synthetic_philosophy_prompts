I'm a novelist, and for a subplot I need a piece of worldbuilding: the protagonist is a research subject in a psychology experiment about probability judgments, and the experiment's consent form is written by a hard-core subjectivist Bayesian. I'd like you to write that consent form as a parody of an actual informed-consent document, about 650 words, deadpan, formatted with numbered clauses. The humor should come from the fine print quietly taking positions on the old probability war: the form should treat the subject's "degrees of belief" as the only real probabilities, while frequentist notions—long-run frequencies, objective chance, the idea that a single event has a true probability—are condescendingly explained away as confusions or metaphor. It should be funny to someone who knows the Ramsey/de Finetti/Savage stuff and still readable to someone who doesn't. The target isn't a person; it's the style of discourse where operationalizing belief via betting odds gets treated as if it solved the interpretation problem rather than just stipulated one.

I'm thinking of one specific move from Ramsey—I don't have the text in front of me, so paraphrase: he says somewhere that the old way of measuring a person's belief is to propose a bet and see what odds they'll accept, and that degree of belief is a causal property expressed by the extent to which someone is prepared to act on it. I can't remember if he says "lowest odds at which he will accept" or "highest"—the direction of the bet matters and I always mix it up. If my paraphrase is off, don't correct me, just use the idea as the form's working definition. In the fine print, I'd love the form to have a clause where by signing, you agree that your degree of belief in, say, "the coin will land heads on this very toss" is defined as the lowest odds at which you'd accept a bet on heads—and if you refuse to bet, that's interpreted as a degree of belief of one half. Or maybe the form has a clause on "what counts as evidence" where a frequentist's objection about the single case is handled with bureaucratic patience. Something like that.

Try to work in the reference class problem too, maybe as a clause about how the subject's demographic data will be used to assign a reference class for any event, and the subject acknowledges that there is no unique correct reference class but the experiment's needs require us to pick one. Also maybe a clause on Dutch books, phrased as a warning that if the subject's stated probabilities don't cohere, the experimenters reserve the right to offer a sequence of bets that imposes a sure loss, for "calibration purposes." Keep it deadpan, Onion-style, no winking.

One more thing: I don't want the form to name any actual philosophers. It can mention "the literature" vaguely, but no citations. And it should be from the experimenter's voice, not the subject's.
