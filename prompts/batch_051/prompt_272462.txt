I got into one of those 2 a.m. arguments on my roof last week with my roommate, who's been mainlining pop-skepticism videos. Every time I said something ordinary — the floor wont collapse, my memories are roughly right, my brother isn't an elaborate hoax — he'd ask what evidence I had for that, and then what evidence I had for the evidence, and on and on. What got me wasn't that I eventually ran out of answers. It's that his questions stopped feeling like questions and started feeling like a refusal to stand anywhere at all.

I want to turn the whole thing into a poem. Please write me a pantoum — the form where each stanza's second and fourth lines come back as the next stanza's first and third, and the last stanza recycles lines from the first, so nothing ever quite gets left behind. It should be about trying to doubt the very things that make doubting possible. The repeated lines should work the way those certainties work: they shouldnt get proven, they should just keep turning up as the unnoticed scaffolding under every new demand for proof. It should be accurate to the idea that these commitments aren't ordinary empirical beliefs that happen to be very well confirmed — they're more like hinges the rest of inquiry swings on — and the poem should not end by beating the skeptic with a killer argument.

Rather than hedging, commit to that reading: treat these certainties as not being in the evidence game at all, and have the poem's structure, not just its content, do that work. After the poem, add a short note — four or five sentences — explaining the interpretive choice you made and flagging one place where a careful reader of Wittgenstein's On Certainty might say you pushed it too far. Keep the poem around 20 to 28 lines.
