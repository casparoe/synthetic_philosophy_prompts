Ordinary thought about bringing people into existence contains a sharp asymmetry. We are ready to say that someone who conceives a child knowing it will live a life dominated by pain has done wrong, and that the child is wronged by being made to live that life. But we are not ready to say that someone who declines to conceive a child who would have had a happy life has done anything deficient; the child who is never conceived is not thought to be deprived of any good. Is this asymmetry justified, or is it only a bias we do not apply to ourselves?

Schopenhauer offers what looks like a grounding for it in On the Sufferings of the World (from Studies in Pessimism): "I know of no greater absurdity than that propounded by most systems of philosophy in declaring evil to be negative in its character. Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt. Leibnitz is particularly concerned to defend this absurdity; and he seeks to strengthen his position by using a palpable and paltry sophism. It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end."

Does this passage actually justify the asymmetry, or does it merely restate it in metaphysical language? If happiness is only the removal of a felt want, then a life never begun has no want to remove and so misses no good, while a life begun in pain adds something positively bad. Test this against nearby cases—ending someone's pain versus giving them a pleasure they never wanted; prolonging a contented life versus shortening a painful one; knowingly creating a miserable child versus knowingly failing to create a happy one. State precisely what would have to be true about pleasure and pain for the asymmetry to be justified, and say whether it should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
