I am a novelist at work on a book about a woman who, at the outset, has no desire to reconcile with her estranged father, and who by the end has come to regard reconciliation as something she has reason to pursue. My working assumption is that the philosophical distinction between internal and external reasons maps neatly onto this trajectory: an internal reason is one that arises from a person’s existing desires, projects, and commitments, while an external reason is one that comes to her from outside—from family expectation, moral demand, or social role—and asks her to alter those commitments. On that understanding, the novel’s turning point would be the scene in which the protagonist internalizes what had until then been an external reason. Could you clarify where in such an arc an external reason properly becomes an internal one? I want to stage that moment of internalization accurately, so that a philosophically literate reader finds the character’s development credible rather than conceptually muddled.
