I serve as chair of my university's committee on the ethical review of computational subjects. We are considering a proposal from a computational neuroscience group that plans to train tens of thousands of reinforcement-learning agents whose reward architecture includes an aversive channel: simulated bodily damage produces a negative signal that drives learning, and some agents are also equipped with meta-cognitive modules that represent and avoid their own aversive states. The investigators want to compare these variants to study the computational role of pain-like processing. They argue our committee lacks jurisdiction because no biological organism is involved and the agents are not subjects in any institutionally recognized sense. One member replies that if suffering can be realized computationally, its substrate is irrelevant, so uncertainty about moral status should itself trigger review; another replies that sentience, not mere aversive computation or avoidance behavior, grounds moral status, and the proposal offers no evidence of sentience. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend whether the committee should treat this research as within its mandate, and whether to approve it, approve it with conditions, or refer it to a separate review, while engaging seriously with the strongest consideration on the side you reject.
