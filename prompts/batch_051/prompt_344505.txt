In a political theory seminar on the uses of history, one student defended a fairly assertive policy toward a rising power by appealing to what he called the lessons of history. When pressed, he invoked George Santayana's maxim, "Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it," and treated it as if it settled the matter: to set aside the historical pattern is to invite disaster. Around this sentence he clustered several different historical analogies — Munich, meaning that conciliation toward a revisionist power emboldens aggression; Vietnam, meaning that incremental military involvement becomes a quagmire; and the Thucydides trap, meaning that a rising power and an established power have a structural tendency toward war.

I want to understand what that sentence is actually claiming before it is attached to any particular analogy. Lay out the distinct precise theses the maxim could be expressing, from the banal to the radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. In particular, separate claims about historical recurrence, claims about the psychology of forgetting, claims about the predictive value of historical study, and normative claims about negligence or blame. Then identify which of these readings is doing the real work when the sentence is used to underwrite a specific analogy such as Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap, and say whether the maxim adds any justificatory force to such analogies or merely absorbs their rhetorical force. I would prefer a menu of readings with verdicts rather than one interpretation defended at length.
