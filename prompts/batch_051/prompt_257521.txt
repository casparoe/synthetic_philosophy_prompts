Bit of background: I'm in this casual reading group that's been working through Thucydides, and last week we paired the Mytilene debate and the Sicilian expedition with Plato's Apology. The discussion turned into a proper row between two of the regulars, and they roped me in to referee. I honestly couldn't call it, so I'm writing up the back-and-forth as well as I remebered it.

(1) Leah argued that the three episodes expose a structural flaw in Athenian democracy, not just bad luck. The assembly first voted to kill the whole adult male population of Mytilene and enslave the women and children, and only reversed itself the next day because the mood happened to shift; that's not deliberation, it's impulse with a lucky second vote. The same assembly approved the Sicilian expedition on wildly inflated promises and then meddled with its own generals from home. And the court that convicted Socrates was, in effect, the demos in a narrower room, so even when Athens did follow legal procedure, it was still majority fear deciding which speech was too dangerous.

(2) Dan said hold on: Mytilene actually shows the virtue of the system, because a public assembly could correct a horrifying first vote within a day — try getting a king or an entrenched senate to reverse that fast. Sicily, he said, was imperialism and bad military planning, not democratic procedure; oligarchies and monarchies have wrecked plenty of expeditions. As for Socrates, he said we have to remember the timing — 399, after the oligarchic coup of the Thirty and the civil war, with Critias and other Athenian aristocrats in his circle. A frightened community lashing out at a perceived enemy of the restored democracy isn't evidence that democratic institutions are systematically broken; it's evidence of a crisis.

(3) Leah replied that the speed of the Mytilene reversal cuts both ways: if a decree to massacre a city can be passed and rescinded inside a day, the first vote was institutionalised mood, and the rescue was a near-run thing that depended on a second trireme overtaking the first. On Sicily, she said the assembly didn't just authorise the expedition — it voted on its size and command, and when Nicias tried to frighten everyone into rejecting it by demanding an absurdly large force, the demos took the bait and made the expedition bigger. That, she claimed, is a failure mode peculiar to popular deliberation: a crowd can outbid itself into a disaster nobody individually would have proposed.

(4) Dan said that at least the decisions were made in public and had to be justified with reasons, and the same people reaped the losses; he didn't see why an oligarchy's smaller decision circle is better at checking emotion, since elites in a closed room have their own pathologies and no need to reverse anything. And he disputed that the court was just the assembly in another room: jurors were selected differently, cases were bounded by the indictment, and there was a counter-penalty procedure; the trial was orderly in a way mob violence is not. Leah then said that's exactly her worry — orderly fear is worse than disorderly fear, because it feels like justice to the people doing it, and a democracy with no standing protection for dissenting speech cannot host the very criticism it needs. Dan called that a goalpost move: she'd started with 'the demos doesn't deliberate' and now seemed to be saying 'orderly majorities are worse than tyrants', which he thought was much stronger and much harder to defend.

So, if you had to referee: rate each of the four moves (and Leah's last reply, if you want to separate it) from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right about which specific point, and then say what both of them are missing. And could you write the whole thing in British English please?
