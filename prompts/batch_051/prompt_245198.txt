I am a doctoral student in philosophy of physics, and I keep returning to a tension I would like you to help me think through, not by adjudicating it but by laying out what each possible resolution would cost and what would justify it.

On one side stands relationism, and I find its systematic virtues hard to resist. It refuses to posit any furniture of the world beyond material bodies and the spatial and temporal relations in which they stand; it therefore does not have to tell me what a point of space is over and above a place in a network of distances; and it honors a principle I find antecedently plausible, namely that two worlds that are observationally identical and differ only in how they are embedded in an unobservable container should be counted as the same world. General relativity seems to push in this direction as well: the hole argument and the theory's diffeomorphism invariance suggest that labeling spacetime points independently of the metric is surplus structure, and that what is physically real is the pattern of fields and distances rather than a substantival manifold lying beneath them. If I let theory choose, I would be a relationist.

But I cannot shake a firm intuition, and it is Newton's old one. Consider a bucket of water alone in an otherwise empty universe, set spinning and then left to rotate with the water at rest relative to the bucket's walls. The surface of the water is concave, and it seems to me that this is a genuine physical fact about that world, not a fact about how the world is described. Yet the most natural relationist relata, the water and the bucket, are mutually at rest, so the concavity cannot be read off their relative motion. The same point arises with two spheres joined by a cord in an otherwise empty universe: if the whole assembly rotates, the distance between the spheres never changes, and still the cord is under tension. The difference between spinning and not spinning seems, intuitively, to be a difference in the world itself, not merely in our choice of coordinates or in some distant material reference frame. I am aware that the relationist can fall back on relations to the fixed stars or to the rest of the universe, but in an otherwise empty universe there are none, and I do not see why the water's behavior should depend on bodies that are not there. If relationism is right, it owes me an account of what makes the water climb; if the intuition is right, relationism is at least incomplete.

I do not want a verdict. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading, perhaps structural or modal, on which the relationist's austerity and the reality of rotation can both be preserved. Please write this as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter.
