I am preparing a teaching exercise on metaphysical monism, and I want you to act as an expert on Spinoza’s metaphysics. In Part I of Spinoza’s Ethics, Definition III reads:

“By substance, I mean that which is in itself, and is conceived through itself: in other words, that of which a conception can be formed independently of any other conception.”

Definition VI reads:

“By God, I mean a being absolutely infinite—that is, a substance consisting in infinite attributes, of which each expresses eternal and infinite essentiality.”

These two definitions seem to pull in opposite directions. The first says a substance is conceptually self-contained; the second says God—the one substance—consists in infinitely many attributes, each of which expresses an infinite essence. A pluralist reading would say that if each attribute is a distinct essence and can be conceived through itself, then Spinoza has granted a plurality of substances after all. A monist reading would say the two definitions are compatible because attributes are not separate beings but only how the intellect perceives the essence of one substance.

I want you to do two things. First, state the strongest version of the pluralist objection that these two passages seem to create, and the strongest Spinozist reply that keeps one substance. Second, give a verdict on whether Spinoza’s monism is genuinely preserved here, or whether he has smuggled plurality back into substance. To do that, you will need to distinguish at least three positions: attributes as really distinct entities, attributes as distinctions of reason only, and attributes as something else. In particular, explain what Spinoza could mean by “infinite attributes” if there is only one substance, and whether the move from “infinite attributes” to “one substance” is legitimate or just terminology.

Before you write your final answer, work through what the word “infinite” adds here: why would Spinoza choose “absolutely infinite” rather than “infinite in its kind”? Also ask yourself whether any interpretation that saves monism makes Definition III collapse into triviality. Your final answer should be 600–800 words. At the end, add a short methodological note: identify the single argument or consideration that, if it could be shown, would most likely change your verdict, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
