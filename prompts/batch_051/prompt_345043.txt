Question: State Kant's Formula of Universal Law in your own words, then use it to test the maxim 'when I need money, I will make a false promise to repay it.' Identify the two ways a maxim can fail the test and say which way, if either, this maxim fails.

Student answer: Kant's Formula of Universal Law says we should act only on maxims we could, at the same time, will to hold as universal laws. The test is basically a rigorous version of 'what if everyone did that?', and it looks for two kinds of failure. Contradiction in conception means the maxim can't even be thought coherently once universalized. Contradiction in willing means the maxim is thinkable, but a rational will couldn't will it. For the false-promise case, the maxim is 'whenever I need money, I will make a false promise to get it.' Universalized, everyone would make false promises whenever they needed money. That isn't a strict logical contradiction—I can still picture a world full of lying promise-makers—but I couldn't will it: promising as an institution would collapse, nobody would trust a promise anymore, and then my own false promise wouldn't even get me the money. So the maxim fails the universalizability test, but at the stage of contradiction in willing rather than contradiction in conception.
