I have been arguing with a colleague about whether it ever makes literal sense to say that a company itself—not its executives, shareholders, or employees—is morally blameworthy. Our shared example is a pharmaceutical firm that suppressed adverse trial data for a drug across several teams and two changes of management; no single person saw the whole picture, yet the organization, through its reporting structure and incentive system, reliably produced the suppression. My colleague treats corporate blame as at best a useful shorthand or a category mistake: blame requires a subject that can intend and feel guilt, and a corporation has neither. I am at around 0.4 that corporations can be blameworthy in a non-metaphorical sense; my colleague is much lower, and we want to locate exactly where the gap lives.

I would like your own credence, stated as a number between 0 and 1, that moral blame can attach to a corporate entity itself—in a sense that is neither shorthand for blaming individuals nor mere metaphor—rather than only derivatively to its members. I do not want “it depends on one’s theory of blame”; I want a number and the derivation. Please decompose it into at least these sub-questions: (a) whether blame requires a unified subject with phenomenal consciousness; (b) whether a corporation can have genuine beliefs and intentions in virtue of its decision procedure—consider the discursive dilemma, in which a three-member body can accept premise 1 by majority, accept premise 2 by majority, and reject the conclusion by majority, because different members combine the premises differently; (c) whether irreducibly collective attitudes of that kind are the sort of thing blame could attach to; and (d) whether reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation can fittingly be directed at a collective rather than at people.

Please structure your answer in four titled sections, in this order:

1. Bottom line — one sentence giving your credence and the sense of “blameworthy” you are estimating.
2. Term estimates — for each of (a) through (d), a probability and one sentence of justification (roughly 8 to 12 sentences total).
3. Combination — one paragraph showing how the terms combine into your final number, noting any dependencies among them.
4. Most sensitive term — one paragraph identifying which sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence, empirical finding, or argument would move it most, and roughly by how much.
