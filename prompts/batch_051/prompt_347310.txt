Over the last couple of years I've been kicking around an idea about creativity and I want someone to take it apart honestly, not just tell me it's interesting. I call it the Curatorial Theory of Creativity, CTC for short. The core claim: creativity isn't in producing new candidates, it's in the act of selecting among them. More precisely, an artifact is creative iff (1) some generative process produced it along with a bunch of alternatives it could have produced instead, and (2) some selector—a person, a trained critic, an algorithm—picked that artifact out for attention or use in a way that was responsive to the features of the candidates rather than automatic. On this view the generator supplies only novelty, and creativity enters at the selection step. So a diffusion model plus a human curator who sifts through hundreds of outputs can be jointly creative, while the same model spitting out its first image is not; and a painter doing oil sketches until one "looks right" is running the same two-part machine. I know this bangs up against Boden-style distinctions between exploratory and transformational creativity, and probably against anything that locates creativity in the process rather than the choosing. Give me the three strongest objections to CTC, tell me which existing view it is closest too, and say whether any of the objections is fatal rather then just uncomfortable. Also give me a counterexample I should be scared of: either something everyone calls creative that has no real selection step, or something nobody calls creative that satisfies both conditions.
