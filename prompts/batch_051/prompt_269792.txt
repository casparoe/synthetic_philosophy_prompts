Question: "Max Black asks us to imagine a universe that contains nothing but two cast-iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, composition, temperature and so on, floating two miles apart in otherwise empty space. Is such a world a counterexample to the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII)? Be clear about which version of PII is at stake, and about whether the answer depends on what kinds of properties we're allowed to appeal too (qualitative versus impure)."

Student's answer: "Black's world is a counterexample to PII, which is often just called Leibniz's Law. PII says that any two things that share all their properties are really the same thing. The two spheres share all their intrinsic properties — size, shape, mass, composition — so PII would force them to be identical. But plainly they aren't identical: there are two of them, and we can count them. So PII fails, at least in that world, and since Leibniz held it as a necessary truth, that's a serious problem for his view. A defender might reply that if we allow impure properties, like being identical with sphere A, then trivially each sphere has a property the other lacks, and PII comes out true — but that's empty, so the real question is whether the spheres differ in any qualitative property. They do. The obvious relational candidate, being two miles from the other sphere, is shared by both, so it won't distinguish them. But one sphere has the property of being two miles to the left of the other, and the other has the property of being two miles to the right of the other. Those are different qualitative relational properties, so the spheres are discernible after all, and Black's example doesn't refute the qualitative version of PII. One last point: the converse principle, the Indiscernibility of Identicals, is unaffected by all this, since it just says a thing has the same properties as itself."
