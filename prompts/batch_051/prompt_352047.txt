I have been trying to get clearer about what it means to respect someone in attitude rather than merely in outward action, and I have arrived at a distinction I would like checked. I call the two parts 'bare recognition' and 'shaped recognition'.

By bare recognition, I mean the baseline acknowledgement that another person is a centre of experience whose perspective can make a claim on me, quite independently of whether I like them, admire them, or think their views are correct. By shaped recognition, I mean the further attention I give to the particular person: their history, commitments, and self-understanding, and the way I adjust my responses in light of those specifics.

I think bare recognition is what it means to treat someone as a person at all, and because of that it is not something that comes in degrees; you either admit someone into that status or you do not. Shaped recognition, by contrast, is gradable. We naturally give more of it to friends than to strangers, and some of it is earned or lost through what someone does.

I also hold a stronger, more psychological claim that I am less sure about: that many ordinary complaints about disrespect, such as being ignored, interrupted, spoken over, or treated as though one were not in the room, are really complaints about a failure of bare recognition, even when the hurt is felt very personally. The offence is not that someone failed to know me but that they failed to let me count at all.

Is this distinction already drawn under other names, and if so, how does my version differ? Is it right to treat bare recognition as non-gradable, or is even that something that can be given partially? And where does the distinction break down: are there cases that fall badly between the two categories, or that show my reading of ordinary complaints is too neat?
