I have been turning over a parallel between the role of coordinate frames in physics and the role of indexicals in language, and I want help deciding whether it is a deep structural identity or a suggestive accident.

In physics we routinely say that a coordinate frame is a representational device rather than part of the world: any genuine physical content should be restatable in invariant, frame-free form, and yet frames are indispensable for hooking the theory up to particular observations — before a quantity is a number, someone has to fix an origin, an orientation, and a time. Words like 'I,' 'here,' and 'now' appear to me to play the same kind of role in speech and thought. They do not pick out their referent by description; the context of use supplies it, and their entire contribution seems to be anchoring a proposition to a particular speaker, place, or moment. But there is a familiar argument that this anchoring cannot be paraphrased away, at least for action and self-locating knowledge: someone can know every third-person fact about the shopper who is spilling sugar through the supermarket and still fail to be moved until they realize that they themselves are that shopper. If that is right, a first-person thought has a content no third-person description of the world can express.

So the tension I want dissected is this: if coordinates are, in principle, eliminable through invariants while indexicals are, in principle, essential, then the analogy seems to collapse exactly at its strongest point. But I am not sure the contrast is that clean, because physics still requires someone to take a measurement from inside the world, and the choice of a frame may be the physicist's own 'here' and 'now.' I would ask three things. First, map the analogy precisely: which feature of coordinates corresponds to which feature of indexicals, and where does the mapping come apart? Second, state what the parallel, if taken seriously, would predict — and identify at least one such prediction that seems false. Third, say whether anything on the physics side actually bears on the original question of whether a language could in principle do without pronouns or indexicals, or whether the two issues merely look alike from a distance.
