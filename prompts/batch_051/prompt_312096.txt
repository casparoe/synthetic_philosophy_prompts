I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of science seminar on scientific change. My professor has spent the term issuing verdicts on historical and invented episodes, and she refuses to state her general view outright; she wants us to infer it from the pattern. So far she has said:

(1) Single anomaly. When a well-confirmed theory failed a delicate experimental prediction, she held that the rational response was to check the measurement, attempt replication, and protect the theory from a single negative result, not to abandon it at once.

(2) Mere accommodation. When a theory's defenders met a persistent anomaly by adding an auxiliary hypothesis that accounted for the anomaly but implied nothing independently testable, she called the theory's state "losing momentum" and said confidence should decline if that was all the theory could manage—while insisting the theory had not been refuted by the anomaly.

(3) Immature rival. About a case in which a new theory explained a long-standing anomaly of an entrenched theory but could not yet reproduce most of the entrenched theory's standard results, she said it was rational for individual scientists to stay with the old theory and rational for others to defect to the new one; the old theory was not refuted, and no decision rule fixed the moment at which the field should flip.

(4) Protracted decline and a usable rival. About a research programme that spent twenty years handling anomalies by post hoc qualifications while a rival grew into a workable alternative—solving those anomalies, reproducing much of the old programme's standard results, and making new empirical predictions—she said the community's slow shift to the rival was rational, perhaps overdue, and that no single falsifying experiment caused it; the comparative trajectories of the two programmes did.

(5) Disputed standards. In a controversy where the two sides could not even agree about what would count as a decisive test, she said there was no neutral algorithm that determined which side was rational at the time, but that this did not make all choices equally good; afterwards one could still judge the programmes by the work they actually produced.

Next week's case is this. A long-dominant research programme has made no confirmed novel prediction in twenty-five years and now handles its anomalies mainly by tacking on after-the-fact qualifications. A young rival programme has just made and confirmed two surprising predictions, but it cannot yet reproduce the old programme's textbook results, and most practitioners do not yet know how to use its formalism. In class we have been asked two questions: (a) Is a graduate student irrational to choose a dissertation project inside the old programme? (b) Is a funding agency irrational to keep most of its money in the old programme?

Predict what the professor will say to each question. In doing so, work through the two or three views that fit her track record so far—a Kuhnian reading, a Lakatosian reading, perhaps a Popperian or Feyerabendian one—and identify which earlier verdict pulls toward which reading and which verdicts would have to be explained away. Then give your best guess about the position she is actually running, and why. Finally, specify the additional fact about next week's case (or one briefly described further case) that would most cleanly decide between the remaining readings, and say which way it would cut.
