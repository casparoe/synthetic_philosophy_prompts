I keep returning to Lois Lowry's The Giver because the obvious philosophical reading is almost too neat. The novel is usually read as a warning about a community that flattens emotion, assigns twelve-year-olds to lifelong occupations, and makes the old and the unfit disappear through a ceremony it calls release. I want to read it through one specific idea: the child's right to an open future, the view that children hold a portion of their autonomy in trust for the adults they will become, so that adults wrong them when they permanently close off most worthwhile futures before the child can choose among them. On its face the community is a textbook violation of that right, and a reading that merely uses the novel to illustrate the principle isn't worth much. Press in both directions. What does the novel show is gained, not only lost, when a society deliberately narrows children's futures, and is the place beyond the community that Jonas flees toward genuinely an open future or a different kind of necessity one is assigned by the story itself? Where does a defender of the open-future view have to strain to keep the novel from counting against it, and which scenes should a careful reader point to? Finally, say what the novel lets us see about the right to an open future that an argument in its favor cannot. Write this out as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
