I’m a PhD student working through Bentham’s unpublished manuscript *Offences Against One’s Self: Paederasty* (written c. 1785), and I’m trying to get a complete inventory of his independent arguments for the legal toleration of consensual same-sex sexual acts. So far I have five distinct strands:

1. The antipathy argument: existing condemnations track disgust and ascetic prejudice rather than the principle of utility.
2. The harmlessness argument: consensual same-sex acts have no non-consenting victim and produce no direct mischief to others.
3. The demographic argument’s failure: population is not a direct function of the number of non-procreative sex acts, since it is constrained by subsistence and social circumstances.
4. The inconsistency argument: if the objection is simply that the acts do not produce children, this would equally condemn many practices that the law and common morality already tolerate.
5. The costs-of-enforcement argument: criminalization creates terror, encourages blackmail and extortion, and imposes punishment disproportionate to any demonstrated harm.

The passage currently occupying my attention is Bentham’s reply to the population objection, where he denies that a decline in births would follow from tolerating non-procreative sex, because population size is regulated by the means of subsistence rather than by the mere frequency of coital acts. That passage seems to me to fall under strand (3), so I am not treating it as a separate item.

I’m looking for a sixth argument that is genuinely different in structure from all five—not a restatement, special case, or combination of them. It can be one you find elsewhere in Bentham’s writings or one you think is constructible from his principles, but if it is the latter, say so explicitly. If you think the task as posed is confused—for example, that these five strands collapse into one another, or that the manuscript contains no further independent argument—please say that plainly instead of manufacturing one.
