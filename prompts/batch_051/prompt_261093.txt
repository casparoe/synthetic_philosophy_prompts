I spent most of my career as an earth scientist before I retired, so I'm used to hypotheses whose real test won't happen for longer than a human lifetime — erosion rates, aquifer recharge, that sort of thing. Lately I've been reading philosophy of science for fun, and the old Malthus debate keeps bugging me. The standard line is that Malthus predicted population grows geometrically while food grows only arithmetically, so we should have seen catastrophic famine by now, but instead we got the Green Revolution and more people fed than ever, so the theory is simply falsified. I don't buy that. It seems to confuse "wrong" with "early." The underlying point — that exponential growth against finite resources eventually hits a hard limit — hasn't been repealed; the limit just got pushed outward and relocated, to groundwater, topsoil, phosphorus, the atmosphere's capacity to absorb carbon, and so on.

I'd like you to argue for the position that Malthus's central claim is better described as right but early than as falsified. In particular, defend the view that a long-run scarcity prediction can remain scientifically respectable without being unfalsifiable, even when its time horizon keeps getting postponed by technology. The objection I keep running into is that this is just moving the goalposts, so please face that head on. And try to say what woud actually count as disconfirming evidence for such a prediction, becuase if nothing could count, it isn't "right but early," it's just empty. Write it in American English, please, and don't worry about citing anybody.
