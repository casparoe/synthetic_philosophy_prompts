Give a working definition of propaganda — precise enough to decide the following cases — and test it against each of them. By "test it" I mean: apply your definition to every case, say where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict at all, and then either revise the definition or defend the verdict it yields. Here are the cases, with my own confidence flagged in brackets.

Cases where I am confident the item is propaganda:
- A short government-produced film urging young people to enlist by showing only moments of courage and camaraderie, and none of injury, fear, or doubt.
- A leaflet distributed by a political faction that pairs a photograph of a rival candidate with headlines about crime and public disorder.

Cases where I am confident the item is not propaganda:
- A parent humming a lullaby to an infant at bedtime.
- A student's counterpoint exercise composed only to satisfy a technical assignment and never intended for an audience.

Cases where I am genuinely uncertain or think the classification is genuinely borderline:
- A carefully researched historical novel about an actual slave revolt that makes no direct argument, but whose protagonists are consistently drawn from one side of the conflict.
- A corporation's advertisement showing a same-sex couple buying life insurance, aired in a market where that image is politically contested.
- A documentary about a famine that presents accurate statistics but uses close-up shots and a swelling score in its appeal for donations.
- A national anthem sung at a school assembly.
- An abstract mural commissioned by a city government, accompanied by an artist's statement about collective memory.
- A novel written by an author who openly says she wants to advance racial justice, and that is widely reviewed as serious literature rather than as a tract.
- A short lyric poem with no political subject, admired by its readers as pure art.

Now bring the definition to bear on the following claim, which I am paraphrasing rather than quoting: W. E. B. Du Bois held that every artwork is propaganda, because every artwork is made under social conditions, selects what it will make salient, and works on its audience's sense of what matters; on his view, the serious question is not whether an artwork promotes something, but what it promotes and whether that serves liberation or its opposite. Say whether your definition makes this claim literally true, trivially true, false, or true only by stretching "propaganda" until it loses its contrast with the non-propagandistic. Then identify the single verdict in your whole response that you find least secure, and explain why.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled — classifications nearly everyone who uses the term would accept — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on art and propaganda. Do not present a contested theoretical choice as the consensus.

Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence: your proposed one-sentence definition. Then give the rest of the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
