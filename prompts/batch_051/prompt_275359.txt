I'm a machine learning engineer who builds models that generate narrative histories of science and technology from large corpora. A historian I work with keeps telling me our outputs are "Whig history": the practice of writing the past as a steady, inevitable progress toward present knowledge and arrangements, with earlier figures and ideas assessed chiefly by how far they anticipated, enabled, or obstructed where we ended up. He and others evidently regard this as a serious intellectual error with a well-developed critique behind it, but he cannot state the objection in terms I can use, and I cannot tell whether his complaint is just the failure modes I already know by other names — lookahead bias, data leakage, an objective function overfitted to the present — or something my whole way of thinking is built not to notice.

I am fluent in the working vocabulary of machine learning: training and test distributions, objective functions, optimization, teacher forcing, sequence models, lookahead bias, leakage, hindsight, selection effects in data curation, generalization, reward hacking, Goodhart's law. Please do two things. First, restate (a) the Whig view of history-writing, treated as an intellectual position about how the past should be narrated, and (b) the standard critique of that position, in that vocabulary, as faithfully as possible without assuming the two domains are isomorphic. Second, give me the residue report: which parts of the position and its critique translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then, on the basis of the residue, tell me whether my working suspicion is right — that the anti-Whig objection is essentially the leakage/lookahead problem and I therefore already understand it — or whether my vocabulary systematically conceals what the historians are actually objecting to, and if so, what that concealed thing is.
