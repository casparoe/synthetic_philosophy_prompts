I've been working through a view from the Mīmāṃsā school of Indian philosophy: the claim that the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya), and that a text doesn't need an author's intention behind it to be a source of knowledge, because a cognition is presumed valid until a specific defeater is shown (svataḥ prāmāṇya). I'm not committed to the theological package — I don't care whether the Sanskrit corpus is literally uncreated — but I've found myself defending a cleaned-up version of the underlying epistemology: for a certain class of transmitted texts and testimonial practices, the default should be trust, and the skeptic has to identify a particular error source rather than demanding that the believer produce the author, the chain of transmission, or independent confirmation before trusting anything. I suspect this matters beyond Mīmāṃsā — for legal precedent, oral tradition, or the way most of us accept scientific testimony without checking the primary sources — and I suspect the speaker-intention objection is the deepest one, but I don't want to anchor you there.

What I want is a map of what would count against this view, including what it is committed to that a default-skepticism epistemology is not. Don't stop at the obvious historical objection that the Vedas show signs of human composition over time; say how much that transfers to the cleaned-up version. Give me the philosophical defeaters: cases where a body of apparently valid testimony could be globally corrupt without any specifiable local error, arguments that understanding a sentence at all requires positing a speaker with intentions, problems about conflicting injunctions or conflicting interpretations that intrinsic validity can't adjudicate, and the circularity of identifying which texts get the default trust in the first place. For each, say whether it is fatal, merely awkward, or something a defender would dismiss, and say which of these a committed Mīmāṃsaka would themselves concede counts against them. Then be honest about wether the view can be qualified enough to absorb all of this, and if it can, whether that is a feature or a sign it has stopped taking risks. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about your own assessment — which consideration, if it went differently, would flip the verdict, and where your reasoning is most fragile.
