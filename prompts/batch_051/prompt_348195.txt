I want to settle a question about the line between blackmail and legitimate hard bargaining. Consider the following four criteria for an act's being blackmail; an act counts as blackmail to the degree that all four are satisfied.

1. Conditional structure: A informs B that A will do X unless B does Y, where Y is a transfer of value from B to A, paradigmatically money.
2. Liberty: X is an act A is legally at liberty to perform — for example, revealing true, lawfully acquired information that B would prefer to keep private.
3. Harm without entitlement: X would foreseeably harm B, and A has no independent legal or contractual right to Y.
4. Sale of silence: A makes the conditional threat in order to obtain Y rather than in order to do X; what A is effectively selling is B's exemption from X.

Now judge this case against those criteria.

An independent security researcher discovers a serious, currently unpatched vulnerability in a widely used software product. She discovered it through lawful testing and follows a standard coordinated-disclosure policy under which she publishes technical details 90 days after first notifying the vendor, to give the vendor time to fix the flaw. The vendor runs a public bug-bounty programme with published reward levels, and the researcher has been paid through such programmes before, but she has no standing contractual claim to any payment for this particular finding.

She emails the vendor, identifies the affected component in general terms, and writes: "I have a detailed report and a proposed patch. If you retain me as a paid consultant at my usual fee, you get the report, the patch, and the full 90-day embargo while you fix it. Otherwise I will publish on the 90-day clock." The vendor replies that the finding is overrated and refuses. The researcher repeats the offer once, then falls silent while the 90-day clock runs.

Assume that publication is lawful, that publication would genuinely harm the vendor — by reputation and by drawing attackers to an unfixed flaw — and that the consulting fee, although within the range the vendor has paid other outside researchers, is not owed to her under any contract.

Does this case qualify as blackmail under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, and mark any criterion the case satisfies only partially or only under one reading. Then say whether the residual uncertainty shows that the criteria themselves need changing — for instance, by adding a condition about the legitimacy of the demand, the proportionality of the threatened act, or the value served by the disclosure — or whether the case is merely borderline in a way the four criteria already predict.
