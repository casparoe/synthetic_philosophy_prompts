I am a philosophy professor, and I have gradually become aware that I follow, and pass on to my doctoral students, a fairly rigid policy about the division of labor between principles and cases. The policy is this: when a general principle I find attractive conflicts with a firm intuition about a concrete case, I treat the intuition about the case as the datum and place the entire burden of revision on the principle. I complicate the principle, add ceteris paribus clauses, limit its domain, or discard it. I have repeatedly done this even when the principle was strikingly simple, unified, and transitive, and when the recalcitrant case was highly contrived — a distant transplant scenario, a stipulated piece of outlandish technology, an intuition that emerged only after a vivid vignette. The asymmetry is the striking part: I do not in practice let a principle's fit with many cases lead me to distrust a single conflicting case intuition, but I readily let a single conflicting case intuition lead me to distrust a principle. I enforce this in my own work, in supervising dissertations, and in my anonymous referee reports, and a colleague has suggested to me that it is less a method than a temperament — a bias toward the vivid, the concrete, and the surveyable dressed up as a philosophy of evidence.

I want to know whether this policy is defensible as an epistemic habit. Under what conditions is it reliable to treat case intuitions as fixed points, and under what conditions does that systematically mislead — for example, when the intuition has been shaped by framing, by cultural salience, or by a case lying far outside the domain the principle was meant to govern? Could the policy be restated so as to preserve whatever is good in it while giving the theoretical virtues of simplicity, transitivity, and logical strength their due? And is there a non-question-begging way to decide how much weight these two kinds of evidence should receive, or is that weighting itself something that must ultimately be settled by philosophical temperament rather than by argument?
