OK so I'm in a seminar on animal minds and moral status, and for my term paper I've been trying to get clear on the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously. I think I've reconstructed it, but now it seems to proof too much, and I can't figure out which step is the bad one. Here it is:

(1) If I'm unsure whether some creature can suffer, I should give its possible suffering weight in proportion to my credence that it's sentient — an expected-value rule for moral uncertainty.

(2) I can't assign zero credence to insect sentience. The evidence in bees and fruit flies (nociceptors, learning, stress responses) is contested but not nothing, so a rational person has to leave at least some nonzero probability.

(3) Insects are absurdly numerous — on the order of 10^18 alive at any time — so ordinary human practices like agriculture, pest control, and land use affect a genuinely enormous number of them.

(4) By (1) and (3), even if my credence in insect sentience is only one in a thousand, the expected number of sentient insects at stake is enormous: at 10^18 insects, p = .001 already gives an expected quadrillion sentient insects before we even talk about how bad their lives are.

(5) And we can't defuse this by saying one insect matters much less than a human. Even with a one-billionth discount per insect, 10^18 insects would be a billion human-equivalents of welfare at stake if they were definitely sentient; with p = .001 that's still a million human-equivalents. So the expected-value rule seems to say insect welfare swamps almost every human-focused cause I could devote resources to.

(6) But that conclusion feels intolerable. I cannot actually bring myself to think a malaria bednet charity is less important than, like, figuring out how to make crop harvesting kill fewer thrips.

(7) Worse, the same structure generalizes. Once any nonzero chance of sentience generates obligations, I also have to ask whether I can assign literally zero credence to sentient nematodes, zooplankton, bacteria — and I'm not sure on what grounds I would. There are roughly 10^30 bacteria on Earth. A one-in-a-trillion chance would give an expected 10^18 sentient bacteria; hand-washing or taking antibiotics would then look like a moral catastrophe.

So which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (2) and (3) — they feel like empirical facts — and I'd like to keep something like (1), because I don't know how else to decide under uncertainty. But I also can't swallow (6). Where exactly does the argument go wrong? And what's the price of whatever fix you offer? Please keep the answer under 400 words (hard cap), since I have to fit it in the discussion post.
