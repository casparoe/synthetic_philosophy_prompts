In my ethics seminar on blame, we have been working through short cases about standing to blame: not whether the person who is blamed is blameworthy, but whether the blamer is in a position to blame them. Our professor has not yet stated her rule; she simply rules on each case. Assume throughout that the party being blamed is clearly blameworthy. Her verdicts so far are:

- A man who threw the first punch in a bar fight blames the other man involved in the fight for the blow that knocked the victim unconscious. Verdict: no standing.
- A man who has been lying to his partner about an affair blames a truck driver for sideswiping a cyclist. Verdict: has standing.
- The cyclist from that accident blames the truck driver. Verdict: has standing.
- A stranger with no connection to either party reads about the accident and publicly blames the truck driver. Verdict: has standing.

Next week’s case is this: a woman who committed perjury in a civil suit eight years ago, has since admitted it and apologized publicly, and has no connection to the following events, now publicly blames a pharmaceutical executive for committing perjury in an unrelated FDA investigation.

Predict the professor’s verdict. In your answer, do the following: identify at least two distinct candidate rules about standing that are each consistent with her record so far; state what each candidate rule predicts about the perjury case; give your best prediction of her verdict and defend it; and propose a further case that would settle which rule she is really using, explaining what her verdict in that further case would reveal.
