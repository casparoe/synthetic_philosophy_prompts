I have been working through the philosophical literature on the tautology objection to natural selection: the worry that if fitness is defined as actual reproductive success, then natural selection says only that those who reproduce most reproduce most, and the theory loses its explanatory content. The standard reply is to interpret fitness not as actual success but as a propensity — a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce that an organism or genotype has in an environment, and that can be present even when the outcome happens to go against it. I would like to know whether this reply is empirically substantive or merely a definitional maneuver. How could one test the claim that fitness is a propensity rather than mere actual reproductive success? Design the best experiment or observational study you can, say what result would favor the propensity reading and what result would favor the actual-success reading, and then lay out precisely what such a test would and would not settle. In particular, address whether any empirical test could show that the propensity is a real causal property rather than a compact summary of long-run frequencies, and whether the tautology objection is the sort of claim an experiment can target at all or is instead a point about the logic of the theory. Finally, say how confident you are in the proposed design and in your reading of the two positions, and single out the point you are least sure of.
