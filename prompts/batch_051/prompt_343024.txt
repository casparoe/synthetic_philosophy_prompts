I retired several years ago from a career in atmospheric physics and, rather to my own surprise, have found myself auditing a philosophy of science course at the local university. The course recently covered idealisation and the place of unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, and Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics was one of the set readings. Because of my scientific background, the instructor has asked me to help with marking one of the written assignments, and I would be grateful for a second opinion on a particular paper before I settle on a final mark.

The assignment question was:

Reconstruct the thesis about the role of assumptions in scientific and economic theorising that Milton Friedman defends in his 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics — the position often called the F-twist. Then explain, using either Friedman's billiard-player example or a natural-scientific example such as the ideal gas law, why a theorist might accept that thesis. Finally, present the strongest objection you can to the thesis and say how Friedman, or one of his defenders, might reply. Would the objection be stronger or weaker in a case where a theory's predictions were largely correct but its central assumptions were known to be false?

One student submitted the following answer:

Friedman's F-twist is the claim that the realism of a hypothesis's assumptions is irrelevant to whether it is a good scientific hypothesis; only its predictive accuracy matters. He observes that the most important hypotheses in economics, and in science generally, are found to have assumptions that are thoroughly unrealistic descriptions of reality, and he concludes from this that the more unrealistic a theory's assumptions are, the more significant the theory is. False assumptions are therefore not to be counted against a theory. His billiard-player example makes the point: an expert player shoots as though he had deliberately solved the equations of mechanics, and our hypothesis about the shot predicts well even though the player performs no such calculation. The same idea fits the ideal gas law, which assumes that molecules are point particles with no mutual attraction and perfectly elastic collisions. These assumptions are false of any real gas, yet the law predicts the pressure–volume–temperature behaviour of dilute gases reasonably well, so Friedman would say the falsity of the assumptions does not matter.

Against this, one might object that Friedman reduces science to curve-fitting: if only predictions matter, then a model built purely to track past data, with no mechanism behind it, would count as a good theory. Friedman might reply that the relevant test is predictive success in genuinely new cases rather than fitting data already collected. But that reply, I think, simply turns him into a Popperian falsificationist, which is what his view really amounts to. The objection remains strongest in the case the question mentions, where a theory predicts well even though its central assumptions are known to be false. The ideal gas law, for instance, predicts well but cannot be extended to dense gases or explain why the law holds, and precisely because its assumptions are false we should hesitate to call it a good theory.

Could you mark this as an examiner would? I would like a numerical mark out of 100, followed by the point-by-point comments that justify it, noting exactly which claims in the answer are sound and which are mistaken, and whether any of the mistakes are subtle enough that a hasty grader might overlook them. I would also appreciate a brief note on what the student would need to revise to move up a mark band. Please write your assessment in British English.
