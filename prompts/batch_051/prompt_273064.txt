I'm a student in a philosophy of language seminar, and two members of my study group got into a long argument about generics after class. I kept out of it, because I genuinely wasn't sure how to apportion what they were saying. Below is my reconstruction of the exchange from notes. I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of the argument as it actually happened, not a general essay on generics.

A: "Take 'ducks lay eggs.' It's true, but most ducks don't lay eggs: males and juveniles don't. So a generic cannot be a loose majority quantification, something like 'ducks, generally, lay eggs.' The truth has to concern the kind itself. Egg-laying is part of the duck life history, the reproductive character of the species."

B: "That's too quick. Speakers don't evaluate 'ducks lay eggs' over all ducks; they first restrict the domain to the relevant ducks—mature females—and then say of that class that it normally or generally lays eggs. So the truth conditions can stay broadly statistical: 'normally' or 'in general' over a contextually or predicate-selected domain. The restriction does the work; nothing in the semantics has to mention the kind."

A: "But the restriction is exactly what needs explaining, and it isn't coming from context the way ordinary quantifier restriction does. Compare 'ducks lay eggs' with 'ducks are female.' Both properties are restricted to females in roughly the same way, so a view that counts prevalence should place them on a par; but the first generic is true and the second is false. The difference is that egg-laying is characteristic of ducks—bound up with how the kind reproduces—while being female is a distributional fact about half the kind."

B: "I can absorb that. The predicate 'lays eggs,' together with world knowledge, selects a domain of female ducks; 'is female' does not select a domain that excludes the males, so it fails. That's still a domain-restricted statistical semantics. Noticing that this selection pattern is nonrandom is fine, but calling it 'characteristic of the kind' doesn't yet give truth conditions; it names the phenomenon I'm trying to explain."

A: "Then consider 'mosquitoes carry malaria.' No domain restriction yields a majority or even a large minority: only a tiny minority of mosquitoes ever carry malaria, even within the species, sex, and life stage that can. If you lower the threshold far enough to make that generic come out true, the quantifier becomes vacuous and licenses false generics. What unites ducks, egg-laying, and malaria-carrying mosquitoes is that the property is salient and characteristic of the kind's causal or explanatory profile, not statistical prevalence."

B: "The threshold isn't arbitrary; it's interest-relative. Malaria transmission matters to us enormously, so a low but stable prevalence is enough to make 'mosquitoes carry malaria' a usable 'in general' generalization. Dangerous or practically important predicates get lenient thresholds, and that's disciplined by our explanatory and practical interests. Your 'characteristic' is really that pragmatic filter, not a primitive semantic ingredient."

A: "Then your semantic core has been drained of content. All the explanatory work is in world knowledge, domain selection, and interests, and the bare truth conditions say only that the property holds normally in some suitable domain—which no longer distinguishes true from false generics. You've moved the problem into pragmatics without saying why the leftover 'normally' should still count as the literal meaning of a generic."

B: "Ordinary quantifiers work that way. 'Everyone arrived on time' doesn't quantify over everyone in existence; context restricts it, and we don't respond by inventing primitive kind-notions in the semantics of 'everyone.' I can say the same for generics: the core is a normality/generality operator over a contextually supplied domain, with restrictions and thresholds set by context and interests. You still owe truth conditions for 'characteristic of the kind'; so far, it's a redescription of the data."

It more or less ended there, with each insisting the other had pushed the explanatory burden across a boundary without earning it. I want the dispute broken into its claims: Does A's first move actually refute a majority-based semantics, or only a naive one? Does B's domain-restriction reply genuinely handle the 'ducks lay eggs'/'ducks are female' asymmetry without smuggling in kind-characteristicness? Is the interest-relative threshold for 'mosquitoes carry malaria' principled, or does it make the account too forgiving? And is the residual disagreement a substantive semantic dispute or just a bookkeeping choice about the semantics/pragmatics boundary—and what would settle that? Please distinguish, throughout, what is settled among people who study generics from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. I don't need names or citations; I need the argumentative structure.
