Write a short philosophical dialogue between two equally sophisticated characters: an optimist and a pessimist about moral testimony. They meet after a workshop and try to locate, precisely, where they part company. The pessimist, charitably stated, holds that there is a standing reason to avoid letting another person's say-so settle one's moral beliefs, even when that person is reliable: when we morally defer, we may end up with a correct belief, but we lack the relevant kind of understanding or self-directed grasp of the moral truth, and that lack matters. The optimist, charitably stated, holds that morality is not importantly different from other subjects of inquiry: testimony can transmit moral knowledge just as it transmits empirical knowledge, the common reluctance to defer is either an unprincipled double standard or an inflated estimate of one's own moral insight, and a blanket refusal to take moral testimony would leave us unable to learn from moral education, from other cultures, or from people who have thought about an issue longer than we have.

The dialogue should:

- Begin with the characters separating three questions that often get run together: whether moral testimony can yield moral knowledge; whether it can yield moral understanding; and whether there is a distinct normative defect in deferring even when the resulting moral belief is true and justified.
- Have the pessimist press the asymmetry charge directly—why should the ethics seminar be different from the doctor's office—and have the optimist answer it rather than deflect it.
- Have the optimist turn the tables with the charge that the pessimist's stance is a form of epistemic arrogance or parochialism, and have the pessimist answer that charge rather than wave it away.
- Require the pessimist to distinguish being shown how to work through a moral question from merely adopting someone else's verdict, and require the optimist to press on where that distinction begins and ends—for instance, by asking when, if ever, it stops being permissible for a child to believe a parent on a moral matter.
- Include at least one substantive concession from each side, where a character grants a real point and revises their position in response.
- End not with a resolution but with the disagreement narrowed to its deepest remaining issue.

Do not have the characters name or quote real philosophers, and do not have them cite any works; all arguments should be carried in the characters' own words.

After the dialogue, add a brief italicized editor's note. The note should separate the characterizations and framings that would be standard or uncontroversial in this debate from the moves that are genuinely contested or optional readings of it, and it should flag wherever the dialogue treats a contested diagnosis as if it were central to one side's case. Keep the note short, and do not include citations.
