I am a novelist, not a machine-learning researcher, and I am drafting a book whose technical hinge is a distinction I want to get right rather than gesture at. The protagonist is an engineer who must defend a claim to a skeptical regulator: that her company's model has been trained to be genuinely legible, meaning the natural-language explanations it gives for its decisions track the actual determinants of those decisions, rather than merely trained after the fact to tell a plausible story about decisions produced by an unchanged, opaque process. The novel turns on whether this difference is real enough to be tested, or whether it is only a comforting way of talking. I would like you to design, as concretely as possible, the best empirical test of the claim that a model's explanations can reflect its genuine reasons rather than rationalize its outputs. Specify the contrast the experiment must build in to defeat the surface-fluency confound, for instance by comparing a model trained with a legibility objective from the start against a frozen model fitted with a separately trained explainer, or by intervening on the features an explanation names and observing whether behavior changes as the explanation predicts. State what pattern of results would count as evidence of genuine legibility, what pattern would count as evidence of post-hoc rationalization, and exactly where a positive result would still leave the claim unproven, including whether passing such a test shows that the explanation is causally responsible for the decision or only correlated with its causes at the level of description the test happens to measure. Keep the residue sharp: I need to know what must remain unsettled so that the novel's auditor can be intelligent in her skepticism rather than merely obstructionist.
