In discussions of whether Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics should be taken seriously as an early attempt at specifying safe machine behavior, I find that the laws are almost always assessed as an indivisible bundle. I would like them assessed separately, as constraints ordered by priority. Interpret the first as a constraint against causing harm to human beings, including harm that comes from failing to act; the second as a lower-priority requirement to follow human orders except where doing so would violate the harm constraint; and the third as a still lower-priority permission for the machine to preserve its own existence except where that would conflict with either of the higher constraints.

Please score each law out of ten on two dimensions—implementability as a formal constraint on behavior, and moral adequacy as a component of a governing ethical code—and then rank the three laws by how much independent value each would contribute to a workable alignment proposal if considered in isolation. Finally, say which single law, if any, a contemporary alignment researcher should carry forward, and which of the three does the most unnoticed work in the overall scheme. I want an assessment that is candid about the ways the laws fail while giving credit to whatever they genuinely get right; they are widely mocked, so the useful task is to separate the merely literary elements from the parts that remain instructive.
