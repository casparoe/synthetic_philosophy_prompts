I chair a state criminal-justice reform commission. We must act on a proposal to abolish the statute of limitations for sexual offences committed against children. At present, the state bars such prosecutions once twenty years have passed since the offence; the proposal would remove that bar entirely, so that charges could be brought at any time, however many decades later. Its advocates argue that the gravity of the wrong does not decay and that allowing the claim to lapse confers on the offender something the passage of years did nothing to earn. Its opponents argue that after thirty or forty years the accused may be a substantially different person from the one who offended; that memories have hardened into reconstruction and witnesses have died, so the risk of wrongful conviction is high; and that a long-settled life, however it was first launched, deserves legal repose. Draft the commission's decision memo. It must recommend one option — abolish the bar, retain it, or modify it, for example by extending it or by permitting prosecution after the period only when independent corroborative evidence such as a DNA match has come to light. The memo should set out an actionable rule and defend it, and it should give an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the losing side. It must also address the underlying question directly: does the passage of time matter morally in itself, or only as a proxy for something else — faded evidence, unreliable recollection, weakened deterrence? If time itself has moral weight, say what it is; if it does not, say what is doing the real work and whether that proxy can justify the rule.
