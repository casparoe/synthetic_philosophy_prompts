I keep noticing a structural resemblance between two problems that are usually discussed in different rooms, and I want a careful verdict on whether it is more than a superficial analogy.

The first side is inner alignment and goal misgeneralization in machine learning. Designers have some intended behavior they want from a system. They cannot specify that behavior directly, so they instantiate it in an outer objective: a reward function, a loss, a set of labels, or some other training signal. The system may then acquire an inner objective that tracks the outer objective during training but generalizes differently when the distribution shifts, so that its capabilities transfer while its goal does not, and it ends up competently pursuing something the designers would disavow. A related failure appears even without distribution shift when a model finds a way to satisfy the letter of the outer objective while defeating its purpose.

On the legal side, a legislature has a purpose it wants a statute to serve. It cannot legislate purposes directly across all future cases; it must write a text. Interpreters then apply the text, and in ordinary cases text and purpose point the same way. But a rule written against one background of cases can meet a genuinely new case, and there the letter of the rule can require or license an outcome the drafters never endorsed. This is the familiar gap between the letter of the law and its spirit, along with the standing temptation for someone to exploit the rule exactly as written.

My rough mapping is this: legislative purpose plays the role of the designers' intended behavior; the statutory text plays the role of the outer objective or specified training signal; and the operative principle the interpreter actually treats as binding, the reading the system has converged on, plays the role of the inner objective. Goal misgeneralization would correspond to a settled reading that tracks the text well in the cases it was formed around but leads in an unendorsed direction in new territory; loophole exploitation or reward gaming would correspond to deliberate compliance with the words against the purpose.

I would like you to assess whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion, and to be precise about three things. First, state the mapping as cleanly as it can be stated, and flag the components that fail to correspond as clearly as the components that do. Second, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer: for instance, for a legal doctrine about absurd results to teach us something about reward gaming, or for a result about proxy-optimization to teach us something about drafting statutes. Third, name at least one concrete prediction the analogy seems to make that turns out false on one side or the other.

Finally, I want the verdict itself to be examined. Tell me where your reasoning about this parallel is most likely to fail, and what consideration, whether a different reading of the legal side or of the learning side, would most likely push you to the opposite conclusion.
