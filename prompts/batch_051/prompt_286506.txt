In the philosophy of science discussion group at my school we have been deadlocked for several weeks over a question I thought would be straightforward: whether the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning really capture Hume's problem of induction. Both sides accept the mathematics. One side argues that the theorems amount only to the formal claim that no learning algorithm dominates every other across all possible data-generating processes, and that they are therefore silent about whether any particular inductive inference is justified. The other side argues that this is exactly a rigorous restatement of Hume's point: without some prior assumption of uniformity, no generalisation from observed cases to unobserved ones can be guaranteed. The same examples keep being rehearsed, and nobody has moved. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a ruling on who is right. Is this a verbal disagreement, where the two sides are using 'induction' in different senses? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work? Are they disagreeing about what would count as solving, or dissolving, Hume's problem in the first place? Or is the question itself ill-posed? Please commit to one diagnosis and defend it, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis.
