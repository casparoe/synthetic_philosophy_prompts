Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole about skepticism and it's honestly been loosing me some sleep. It started with Descartes' evil demon — if all my experience could be produced in me by a being that isn't showing me reality, how do I know I'm not being fooled? Then it got deeper: any standard I use to check my beliefs would itself need to be checked, and so on forever. I mentioned this to two friends a few nights ago and they ended up in a full argument, which I'll reconstruct as best I can. I stayed out of it, they asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't — so they told me to ask someone who knows this stuff.

(1) Mina opened by saying the evil demon is really just the vivid tip of an older problem. To tell whether your standards of evidence are reliable, you'd need some further standard, and then a standard for that one; so you either go in circles, fall into an infinite regress, or stop somewhere arbitrary. Any stopping point is as arbitrary as any other, so the demon can't be ruled out without begging the question.

(2) Dev replied that this is exactly the kind of doubt Hume said we can't actually sustain — the moment you go have dinner with friends or play a game, the spell breaks and you're back to trusting your senses and memory. He also said the dilemma is a false one, because we don't have to settle on a criterion before we start: we can start from clear particular cases of knowledge and work backward to principles.

(3) Mina came back: "You've given me a psychological fact, not a reason. 'I can't help it' is what any dogmatist says about the epistemic practices they were raised in. If you'd never accept the cannot-help-it defense from someone who can't shake their holy book, why accept it here? Feeling calm after lunch doesn't answer a live skeptical problem."

(4) Dev said there's a real difference. Trusting perception and memory as such isn't one contestable framework among others — it's a precondition for evaluating any framework at all. A rival system still has to be assessed using somebody's perceptual and inferential equipment, so the analogy to tribal dogma is misleading.

(5) Mina answered that this just circles back: calling perception a "precondition of evaluation" is itself a claim made inside our scheme, and the demon scenario is exactly a world where our faculties aren't preconditions of anything except systematic error. You can't rule that out by saying it would make evaluation inconvenient.

(6) Dev said he doesn't need to rule it out with certainty, only to show it's less reasonable than the ordinary explanation. The skeptic's trick, he said, is treating "not conclusively eliminated" as "just as credible as my actual view." Unlike a dogmatist, he added, we constantly revise in response to perception and memory — that's fallibilism, not an arbitrary stopping point.

(7) Mina said that fallibilism is just a restatement of the very practice under suspicion, not an answer to the suspicion. And that's where they stalled.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall? It would also help if you said what the strongest point on each side was, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
