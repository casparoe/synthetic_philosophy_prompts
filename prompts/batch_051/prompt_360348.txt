I've kept a running list of my friend Dana's moral verdicts, because she's consistent in a way I can't quite name. Here are the five I'm most sure about:

- A guy who walks past a toddler drowning in a shallow pond because he doesn't want to ruin his shoes: wrong, obviously wrong.
- A guy who could sell his house, downsize, and give a couple hundred grand to a global health charity, but doesn't because he likes his garden: not wrong, just not especially generous.
- A woman who declines to donate bone marrow to a stranger in another state because the procedure scares her: not wrong.
- A man who, during a heat wave, won't take ten minutes to bring water to an elderly neighbor two doors down: wrong.
- A woman on vacation who doesn't give five thousand bucks to an earthquake relief fund, because she's saving for a car: not wrong.

Now the new case. Our coworker Sam declines a fifty-dollar donation to a well-regarded charity that treats intestinal worms in kids in another country. Fifty bucks is genuinely nothing to Sam—she spends that on lunch in a week. Nobody she knows is involved.

What would Dana say about Sam, or at least what do her prior verdicts commit her to saying? I can see a few rules that fit all five earlier calls but cut differently here: a small-cost rule, a proximity rule, maybe a rule that treats money differently from showing up in person. Which of those rules is she actually running? Give me your best guess at her verdict on Sam, and name the next case that would settle which rule it is.
