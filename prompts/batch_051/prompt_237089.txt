I teach an undergraduate seminar on value theory, and we are reading Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart alongside philosophical debates about whether culture is an intrinsic good — that is, whether languages, traditions, artistic forms, and ways of life matter for their own sake, over and above the good they do for the people who live within them. One position says a culture's disappearance is a loss only to its members, except insofar as it deprives outsiders of instrumental benefits such as knowledge, adaptive diversity, or aesthetic material. The rival position says something in the world has gone out of existence even if no identifiable individual is made worse off, and that outsiders can have reason to mourn it. The novel is standardly read as a rebuke to colonial contempt for Igbo culture, and it is also unsparing about that culture's rigidities, violence, and exclusions. I want a reading of the novel through this debate, written for someone who knows the book but has not thought about it philosophically.

What, in the novel's terms, is lost as Umuofia changes under missionary and colonial pressure — an intrinsic good, an instrumental good, or something that breaks that distinction? Does Achebe suggest the loss is world-sized, so that even the District Commissioner has reason to care about it, or is it a loss only from the inside, located in the lives of Okonkwo, Nwoye, Ikemefuna, and their community? And where does the novel resist the reading you are trying to give — where does it insist that a culture's value cannot be cleanly separated from the people who live it and the uses to which they put it? The reading should be faithful in both directions: use the philosophy to sharpen the novel, and let the novel put pressure back on the philosophy.
