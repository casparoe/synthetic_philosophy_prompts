The paper is about pain asymbolia and the badness of pain. The passage I'm building the paper around is from Nikola Grahek's *Feeling Pain and Being in Pain*. I don't have the book in front of me right now, so I'll paraphrase from memory. Early in the book, when he is motivating his view by describing asymbolic patients, Grahek says in effect that these patients feel a pain but don't mind it: the sensory component is present, they can identify the location and intensity of the sensation, it clearly hurts, but the usual unpleasantness and urge to escape are gone. He treats their own descriptions—roughly, that it hurts but it doesn't bother them—as evidence of a clean dissociation between the sensory side of pain and the affective-motivational side.

My draft argues that Grahek slides too quickly from the absence of normal pain *behavior* or expressed distress to the absence of the affective-motivational *component* of the pain experience itself. Pain asymbolia might instead involve impaired access to, or a strange evaluative stance toward, the unpleasantness of pain, rather than its absence from the experience. If so, Grahek's case does not by itself establish the two-component dissociation he wants. I suspect the strongest reply to me would say that I'm asking for too much from a clinical case: the dissociation claim is an inference to the best explanation of the patient's preserved ability to identify the pain as painful while genuinely not being bothered by it, not a direct observation of an experience with the affect cleanly removed.
