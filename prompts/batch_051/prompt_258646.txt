I run a small reading group on the philosophy of private law, and I want you to help me work through a case before I present it. Please give me the actual reasoning, not a forecast of how a particular court would rule.

A consumer company, RestWell, runs a sleep-tracking app. Its terms of service include the following clause, which users accept upon signing up:

"Any dispute, claim, or controversy arising out of or relating to this Agreement, the Service, or any aspect of the relationship between you and the Company, however characterized, shall be resolved exclusively by final and binding arbitration before a single neutral arbitrator. You and the Company each waive the right to a trial by jury and the right to bring or participate in any class, collective, or representative action."

When the clause was drafted, RestWell did only one thing: it collected users' sleep data and gave those users charts and recommendations. The company has since built a second, separate line of business. It sells a "fatigue risk score" derived from subscribers' sleep histories to employers and landlords, who use it to screen applicants. A subscriber applied for a job; the employer bought her score without her knowledge, treated it as evidence that she would attend poorly, and rejected her. She has evidence that the score misread her data and that the model penalizes night-shift workers systematically. She wants to sue RestWell for defamation and for violating consumer-reporting statutes, and she wants an injunction forbidding the sale of the scores altogether.

I am not asking you to predict the holding under any particular jurisdiction's arbitration statute. I want the question argued from first principles. Does this dispute "arise out of or relate to" the Service or the relationship between her and RestWell, as those terms function in the clause? Does it matter that she is pursuing a public remedy—an injunction stopping the whole scoring program—and that individual arbitration would be a private, one-off proceeding with no class mechanism and very limited publicity? Work through the four interpretive approaches—the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would likely have said had they been asked in advance, and what a reasonable user would have understood—and say exactly where they diverge. Then take a position: should this dispute be sent to private arbitration, or is it a case in which enforcing the clause would privatize an adjudication that the public has an interest in keeping public? I care less about the outcome than about the principle you would defend.
