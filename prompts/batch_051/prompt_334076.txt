I am a philosophy professor teaching a graduate seminar in the philosophy of physics. Next week's two-hour session is on Noether's theorem, for students who are comfortable with elementary calculus but have not seen Lagrangian mechanics. I want them to leave with one idea: a conservation law can be explained by a continuous symmetry of the action, and the direction of explanation runs from symmetry to conserved quantity rather than from observed regularity to an ad hoc principle. I do not need the theorem restated to me; I need a teaching plan.

In the plan, give me the following.

1. The sequence of component ideas, in the order they must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit. The components should include at least: a symmetry of a state versus a symmetry of a law; the action and the idea that a trajectory is a stationary point of an action; a one-parameter family of continuous transformations; and the conserved quantity associated with such a family. For each component, say what earlier idea it rests on and what later step depends on it.

2. One concrete running example that can carry the whole session, and a justification for why it is the right one. Consider whether the primary example should be spatial translation for momentum conservation or time translation for energy conservation; say which you would make primary and why, and state what the example must contain and what it must not contain if the intuition is to survive without the full formalism.

3. A misconception map for this exact audience. I expect at least these: assimilating symmetry to visual or mirror symmetry; confusing a symmetry of a single trajectory with a symmetry of a law of motion; overgeneralizing from continuous symmetries to every invariance at all; and treating Noether's theorem as a merely formal bookkeeping fact rather than an explanation. For each, identify the point in the sequence where it will arise and how to head it off, rather than correcting it after the fact.

4. A final check for understanding that would reveal whether the students have grasped the explanatory direction, as distinct from merely being able to state the theorem.

5. What I should explicitly refuse to cover or flag as beyond the session, and how I should answer a student who asks about a conservation law that appears to have no obvious symmetry.

The aim is not that they can derive the theorem but that they understand why it makes conservation laws intelligible rather than miraculous.
