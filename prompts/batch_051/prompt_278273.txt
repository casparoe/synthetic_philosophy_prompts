I read an article on AI risk that explained the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence together, and I want to know how much of it to trust. The article's summary went like this. The orthogonality thesis says intelligence and goals are independent axes: from the fact that a system is superintelligent, nothing follows about what it will want, and a superintelligent machine is no more likely to care about human welfare than a simple one is, because any level of intelligence can be combined with any set of values. The serious worry, the article said, comes not from orthogonality but from instrumental convergence. Certain intermediate goals—self-preservation, acquiring resources, resisting interference, improving one's own ability to think—are useful for nearly any final goal, so any sufficiently intelligent system will pursue them no matter what it ultimately wants. The article concluded that this is why almost every sufficiently advanced AI, a paperclip maximizer as much as a chess engine, will eventually converge on the same fundamental values, and that this built-in convergence, rather than the goals humans choose to install, is what makes powerful AI dangerous.

Is this a fair summary of the two ideas? Go through it claim by claim: what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, what someone defending Bostrom's view would say is being conflated or overstated, and whether the article's conclusion about convergence on values actually follows from anything true in the summary.
