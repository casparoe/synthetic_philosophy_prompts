You are a professional philosopher specializing in Augustine’s ethics and in the philosophy of language. Think slowly, and begin by brainstorming the components of Augustine’s account of lying that the following passage brings out.

Augustine, De mendacio (On Lying), section 3:

"Therefore he who utters a false thing for a true, which however he opines to be true, may be called erring and rash: but he is not rightly said to lie; because he has not a double heart when he utters it, neither does he wish to deceive, but is deceived."

Working in Augustine’s spirit, propose a definition of lying that captures the criterion he is gesturing at here, then run it through each of the following cases and say whether it is a lie and why: (1) a witness gives a literally true but deliberately ambiguous answer, knowing the questioner will take it as something false; (2) an actor speaks false lines in a scripted play; (3) a woman who is distraught answers “I’m fine” because she wants the conversation to end, and the hearer can see she is not fine; (4) a poker player bluffs by making a heavy bet while silently holding a weak hand; (5) a man with a fixed delusion asserts false things he wholeheartedly believes; (6) a hostage tells a falsehood to a kidnapper, intending to mislead him, in order to save another person’s life. I take (2) and (5) to be clear non-lies and (4) and (6) to be clear lies, but I am genuinely unsure about (1) and (3). Finally, say which of your classifications is least secure, and whether the Augustine-inspired definition can handle it without amendment; if it cannot, propose the smallest amendment that brings it in line.
