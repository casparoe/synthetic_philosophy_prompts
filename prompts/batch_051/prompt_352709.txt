I recently got into an argument with a friend about aggregating very small harms. The case on the table was the familiar "torture or dust specks" puzzle: compare a single person undergoing a genuinely awful experience, such as an hour of severe pain, with an enormous number of people—billions or more, as many as you need—each undergoing something only barely unpleasant, like a stray eyelash in the eye for a few seconds. My friend kept insisting that "every little bit counts" to justify the conclusion that, if the number is large enough, the dust-specks side eventually becomes the worse outcome, since each of those tiny harms is real and somebody actually undergoes it. I suspect that sentence is doing more work than he realizes, and I want to know precisely what work it could be doing.

Please take that one sentence—"every little bit counts"—as it functions in this debate, and lay out the distinct precise claims it might be expressing. For each reading, say what it commits one to at the level of value theory: for example, though do not stop at these, whether harms enter a common total across persons, whether there is some threshold below which a harm fails to count at all, whether the claim is about reasons or about outcomes, and whether "counts" is compatible with the view that some bads are lexically worse than any amount of others. Then say for each reading whether it is trivial, defensible but substantive, or false, with a brief explanation. Finally, identify which reading or readings would actually be needed to support my friend's conclusion, and assess whether the proverb offers any independent reason to accept those readings or merely restates the conclusion in more sympathetic language. I am asking for a disambiguation with verdicts, not for a single correct interpretation; I want the menu of options laid out clearly enough that I can bring it back to the argument. Please write your answer in American English.
