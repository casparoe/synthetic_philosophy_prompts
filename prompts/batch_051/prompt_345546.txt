I keep coming back to a passage in Nietzsche’s *On the Use and Abuse of History* (translated by Adrian Collins) while thinking about a local dispute over how a community commemorates its founding. The passage is this:

“I hate everything that merely instructs me without increasing or directly quickening my activity.” These words of Goethe, like a sincere ceterum censeo, may well stand at the head of my thoughts on the worth and the worthlessness of history. I will show in them why instruction that does not “quicken,” knowledge that slackens the rein of activity, why in fact history, in Goethe's phrase, must be seriously “hated,” as a costly and superfluous luxury of the understanding: for we are still in want of the necessaries of life, and the superfluous is an enemy to the necessary. We do need history, but quite differently from the jaded idlers in the garden of knowledge, however grandly they may look down on our rude and unpicturesque requirements. In other words, we need it for life and action, not as a convenient way to avoid life and action, or to excuse a selfish life and a cowardly or base action.

The passage seems to entail a whole policy about collective memory, and when I tried to formalize the entailment I got an argument whose conclusion I do not want to accept:

(1) For Nietzsche, history is justified only insofar as it serves life and action; knowledge that slackens activity is a costly superfluity and an enemy of the necessary.

(2) A community’s collective memory typically serves life and action directly: it supplies the identity that makes coordinated civic activity possible, gives point to public rituals, and provides a usable orientation toward the shared past.

(3) The scholarly historical record, when it conflicts with collective memory, often does not quicken activity; it disrupts identity, complicates commemoration, and produces the kind of merely instructive knowledge Nietzsche says should be hated.

(4) Therefore, by Nietzsche’s own criterion, when collective memory and the historical record conflict, the memory should be preserved and publicly transmitted, while the record’s correction should give way.

(5) This holds, it seems, regardless of whether the memory is accurate.

(6) But that conclusion is unacceptable: it makes historical truth purely instrumental and appears to require historians to suppress well-supported findings whenever a false memory is more useful to a community’s vitality.

I want to know where this argument goes wrong, if it does. I would most like to hold on to (1) and (2): I find the passage’s standard compelling, and I do think collective memory can be life-serving in a way that the scholarly record sometimes is not. But I cannot accept (6), and I do not see how to avoid it without either denying (2) or making the banal claim that true history always serves life. Which premise or inference is the weak one, and what does rejecting it commit me to? More broadly: in a conflict between a community’s memory and the record, what, on your diagnosis, should give way, and under what conditions?
