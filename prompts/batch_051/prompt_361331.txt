Some learner errors are isolated slips; others are theory-like. A theory-like misconception is systematic rather than incidental: it recurs across different problems, unifies a range of superficially distinct mistakes, and lets the learner make predictions about novel cases. I am compiling a list of theory-like misconceptions across the curriculum, and I would like you to contribute one more. The ones I already have are:

1. In physics, the impetus view of motion: a thrown object is held to receive an internal force from the thrower, and that force is gradually used up, which is why the object slows and eventually falls.
2. In the study of heat, the substance view: heat is conceived as an invisible fluid that flows from hot bodies to cold bodies and can be stored in materials.
3. In electricity, the consumption view of current: electric current is conceived as leaving one terminal of the battery, being partly used up by the bulbs, so that less current returns to the other terminal.
4. In biology, Lamarckian inheritance: traits acquired through an organism's use or disuse of an organ, or through its striving to adapt, are believed to be passed on to offspring.
5. In biology, species essentialism: each species is believed to have a fixed inner essence shared by all its members, so variation and intermediate forms are treated as anomalous.

Looking at my list, I notice that these examples cluster into two structural kinds. The first three explain errors by positing a hidden substance or quantity that is transferred, stored, or consumed. The last two explain errors by positing a hidden fixed essence or a goal-directed tendency. I want to know whether there is a theory-like misconception whose explanatory structure is of a fundamentally different kind.

Give me one more theory-like misconception that does not have either of those two structures. It should be drawn from a domain not already covered, or at least from a clearly different area of reasoning. State the misconception precisely, explain which learner errors it unifies, give at least one novel prediction it would make about how learners will respond to an unfamiliar case, and say why it should count as theory-like rather than as an isolated error or a mere gap in knowledge. What counts as new: not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of the items on my list. Finally, briefly assess how strong your example is as a theory-like misconception compared with the five above.
