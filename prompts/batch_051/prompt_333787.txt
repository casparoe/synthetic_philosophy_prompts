I have a colleague who does clinical ethics consultation, and over the years I have kept track of how she rules on advance directives. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it, is this. A man with motor neurone disease signed a directive refusing invasive ventilation once he could no longer breathe on his own; when the time came he was fully alert and communicating by eye movements, and he asked to be ventilated, and she said he should be ventilated, because a now-competent person can revoke an earlier refusal. A woman with early Alzheimer's signed a detailed directive refusing CPR, intubation and intensive care once she reached the stage of not recognising her daughters, while expressly accepting antibiotics and comfort care; she reached that stage, was placid, and then had a cardiac arrest, and my colleague said the refusal should be honoured. A man with frontotemporal dementia signed a directive stating that once he no longer recognised his wife he refused antibiotics even for readily treatable infections, because he did not want his demented life prolonged; later he no longer recognised his wife, seemed content, and developed pneumonia that oral antibiotics would probably have cleared with little discomfort, and she still said the antibiotics should be withheld. She is sceptical of the idea that the later, demented self is a different person whose present contentment should override earlier wishes, and she treats revocability by a now-competent patient as the main limit on an advance directive's authority.

The case I now want to put to her runs in the opposite direction. A woman with early Alzheimer's signs, and renews each year for three years, a directive stating that if she ever develops dementia she wants everything done: CPR, intensive care, ventilation, surgery, whatever can prolong her life, because she has known people with dementia and regards such a life as still worth living and is afraid of being given up on; she also adds that no one should treat her later contentment as evidence that she has changed her mind. Years later she has advanced Alzheimer's: she recognises no one, is mostly content, cooperates with daily care, and shows no sign of pain. She develops a bowel obstruction from a tumour. Surgery would relieve the obstruction and might meaningfully extend her life, but it would probably mean postoperative intensive care, delirium, and a recovery that would disorient and frighten her. Her children invoke the directive and demand the operation; the surgical and geriatric teams think symptom management and allowing a natural death would be kinder. The question is not what anyone thinks in the abstract but what she, given the track record above, would say should be done. Please be definite: commit to a prediction, lead with it in your first sentence, and then give your reasoning. Set out the principle she seems to be running, identify the other principle or principles that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, say which of those principles your prediction rests on, and name the further case that would most sharply tell the competing principles apart. No preamble and no restating of the question.
