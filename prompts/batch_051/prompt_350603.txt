Two people in my cohort, Lena and Ravi, got into an argument at a department happy hour about the Great Divergence that ended up being more about the logic of historical questions than about history, and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. I'll reconstruct the exchange as best I can.

Lena: "The question everyone asks — 'why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain and not in China?' — isn't a real question. 'China didn't industrialize first' isn't an event, it's a non-event, so it can't have a cause. And the two relata aren't even the same kind of thing: Britain is a small island state and China is a huge empire. The question just invites you to pick one of thousands of differences and retroactively call it the reason."

Ravi: "That's too quick. All explanation is contrastive anyway. 'Why did the patient die?' is short for 'why did she die rather than survive?', and 'survive' is a non-event too. Naming the contrast explicitly — Britain, not China — isn't a sin; it just makes the contrast class visible."

Lena: "But in the medical case there's a normal baseline: given her condition and the treatment, the alternative is well defined. Around 1750, Britain and China differ on basically everything, with no shared normal path. So the difference-maker isn't discoverable — it has to be imported from whatever answer you already wanted."

Ravi: "Okay, but then you've shown the question needs specification, not that it's ill posed. You can ask a narrower version: why did the Yangzi delta, which was highly commercialized, not develop steam-powered factory production when Lancashire did? That has a shared baseline, and historians have offered real candidate difference-makers — coal location, access to overseas resources, things like that."

Lena: "But narrowing it like that changes the question. 'Yangzi delta versus Lancashire' isn't 'Britain versus China.' The original question only has its grip as a grand comparative question, and at that scale it's a Rorschach blot. Saying some smaller question is well posed doesn't rescue the grand one."

Ravi: "That's too strict. 'Why did Rome fall?' is also underspecified and nobody thinks it has a single cause anymore, but it organized a century of serious work. Big contrastive questions can be frames for research rather than queries with determinate answers, and a frame can be productive without being well posed."

Lena: "Productive how? If a frame can't be wrong, it can't select between competing stories, so calling it productive is just calling it rhetorically useful."

They went around in circles after that.
