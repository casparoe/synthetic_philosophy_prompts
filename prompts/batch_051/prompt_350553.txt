I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I follow a fixed rule in my reporting. When a researcher who is established in their field tells me something within their professional competence, I treat the claim as one I may report as fact, unless I already have a specific reason to doubt that source or that claim. I do not attempt to reconstruct the underlying evidence, reanalyze the data, or form my own independent judgment of the researcher's reliability beyond the usual institutional markers: peer review, academic appointment, and a record of prior published work. I know this posture has led me, on occasion, to report claims that later turned out to be wrong, and part of my justification is that publication exposes claims to correction by other researchers. Yet I also know I could not function on deadline if I had to earn each expert's reliability from my own independent evidence.

A philosopher friend tells me that I have simply adopted anti-reductionism about testimony in a journalistic setting: I treat being told something as a default source of justification, one that requires no positive evidence of the speaker's reliability. She adds that the institutional markers I lean on are themselves largely testimonial, so my policy may be circular unless some such default entitlement can be defended. I resist this because I do not extend the default to everyone — not to anonymous sources, not to obvious partisans, not to strangers on social media — and I do not regard myself as a blanket optimist about others' honesty.

Is my policy rationally defensible, and is it best understood as a restricted form of anti-reductionism about testimony? What is the strongest version of my rule, what are its precise boundaries and failure conditions, and can I keep the benefits of deferring to expertise without collapsing into mere credentialism or a general license to believe whatever one is told?
