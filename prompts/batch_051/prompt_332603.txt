I am a novelist, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a small posthumous exhibition of my late friend's sculpture. She spent the last thirty years of her career working in clay and bronze: a piece would be built up in wet clay over an armature, cut apart to make a mold, cast in bronze, and then the clay would be wedged back into the bin and reused, so that a work she could still alter with her thumb in 1992 now exists as a bronze she never once touched. The room will be mixed — her family, working sculptors who understand the casting process better than I do, and a few people who will recognize the old problem of the statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it: the lump can be squashed and remain the very same lump, while the statue would be destroyed, so the two coincide without being identical.

I want the speech, about six or seven minutes long, to use that point as its quiet structural spine rather than as decoration. No jargon, no named philosophers, no footnotes; it has to land for the family and the working sculptors as much as for the people who know the puzzle. The philosophical point should do real work in what I am allowed to claim. I do not want to tell the room that my friend lives on in the bronzes, as if some part of her had been poured into the metal; I also do not want to reduce what survives to so many pounds of alloy. The statue-and-lump distinction seems to me to mark a third possibility — that what persists can depend on form rather than on the underlying matter, without anything ghostly added to the world. Write the speech as affection, not as a seminar.

After the speech itself, add a brief note, not meant for delivery, saying how confident you are in your handling of the statue-and-lump distinction and singling out the one point you are least sure of, especially wherever the speech stretches the analogy.
