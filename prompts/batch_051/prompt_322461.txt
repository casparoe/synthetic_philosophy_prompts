I'm giving a workshop talk next month at an AI lab that does a lot of practical reward modeling and RLHF-based value learning, and I want to be prepared for the question period. My thesis is that the way inverse reinforcement learning is standardly used to infer human preferences from observed behavior faces an underdetermination problem that goes beyond the familiar point that many reward functions fit the same data: once you grant that the behavioral data pin down only an equivalence class of reward functions, every standard way of selecting one member of that class, such as a simplicity regularizer, a sparsity bias, a maximum-entropy prior, or a hand-coded feature set, amounts to a normative choice about what the human should be modeled as caring about rather than an empirical discovery of what they actually do care about. The learned reward function is therefore better described as a stipulation, and the engineer's or lab's values enter under the description of inference. I plan to make this vivid with a small gridworld in which two reward functions that disagree qualitatively, for instance about whether the agent is seeking food or avoiding an aversive stimulus, generate exactly the same trajectories, and then to show that each standard fix quietly breaks the tie on grounds that are not in the data.

The audience is mostly ML researchers who build reward models day to day and are sympathetic to alignment concerns, plus two economists who keep reaching for revealed-preference arguments and a statistician who works on inverse problems and regularization. I know the objections I would get from philosophers of science; this room will press on different points. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
