I cover behavioral genetics for a general-interest science magazine, and I am planning a long feature on the gap between what heritability coefficients actually measure and what confident readers take them to license. The audience is policy-literate but not statistically trained: people who can recite that heritability is a population statistic and not a verdict about any particular person, but who have rarely seen that slogan forced to do real work on the claims that circulate about education, public health, criminal justice, and racial or socioeconomic group differences. I want to avoid the usual explainer that simply lists misconceptions, and instead write something with a thesis: some of the inferences people draw from heritability results would genuinely reorder a policy conversation if they were properly understood, while others are much less important than they seem, and a few are outright invalid.

Suppose I have room for three substantive sections. Please rank the following candidate takeaways and defend the ordering. The candidates are: (a) a high heritability estimate does not mean environmental interventions are futile, and it is fully compatible with large average effects of environmental change; (b) heritability estimates are local to a specific population and environment, so they cannot be exported unchanged to new conditions and set no ceiling on how much a distribution can shift; (c) the fact that variation within a population is partly genetic does not license any inference about the causes of average differences between populations; (d) heritability is a claim about variance in a population, not about the cause of any individual's outcome; (e) the textbook distinction between narrow-sense heritability, which tracks additive genetic variance and predicts response to selection, and broad-sense heritability, which also counts non-additive effects, matters more than most public discussion concedes; (f) a trait can be substantially heritable while its population distribution is highly responsive to social and institutional arrangements; and (g) a high heritability estimate for an outcome that shows a socioeconomic gradient implies that the gradient itself is largely genetic in origin. For each candidate, say whether it survives scrutiny, what practice or research program it would most change if internalized, and why it occupies that place in the ranking. I am less interested in the list than in the weighting: which criteria should dominate when the candidates pull in different directions, and how you would trade off immediate policy consequences against blocking downstream confusions.
