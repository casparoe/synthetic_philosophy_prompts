I am an undergraduate in an environmental ethics course, and we have been working through the question of whether the suffering of wild animals should figure in decisions about conservation and land use. I find myself caught between a line of argument I cannot see how to reject and an intuition I cannot bring myself to abandon, and I would like help deciding which should give way.

The argument runs as follows. If the welfare of nonhuman animals matters morally, then it matters in the wild, not only on farms or in laboratories. Wild animals suffer enormously: predation, parasitism, disease, starvation, exposure, and above all the early death of the vast majority of offspring before they reach maturity. On a straightforward aggregative consequentialist view, the value of an ecosystem for the animals in it is fixed by the summed welfare of the lives it contains. If most of those lives are on balance bad—and there is a serious empirical case that they are, since so many die young and death by starvation or predation is plausibly very bad—then reducing the number of such lives is an improvement. But that is just what habitat destruction does: it prevents future generations of wild animals from coming into existence. So the argument seems to license, and perhaps even require, moral approval of habitat destruction in at least some cases. On this view, protecting a thriving wetland or forest may not be good for the animals in it; it may be maintaining a system that produces enormous amounts of suffering. The argument has real virtues: it is impartial, it refuses to let the word natural do moral work, and it applies to wild animals the same welfare test that many of us already apply to farmed animals.

My intuition runs the other way. Draining a wetland or clearing a forest strikes me as a grave wrong, and I do not think this is only because the empirical claim about net-negative wild lives is uncertain. Even if I accepted the numbers, I would still find the conclusion monstrous. There seems to me to be something wrong either in the move from each of these lives is, on balance, bad to it is good that there will be fewer of them, or in treating the value of a living system as nothing over and above the summed welfare of its present and future members.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—what would have to be shown about where it comes from or what it tracks for me to dismiss it—and what it would take for the theory, or this application of it, to be the thing that goes. Is there a reading on which both survive? For example, one might accept the aggregative reasoning about individual welfare while denying that it settles the moral question about habitat, or one might locate the wrongness of habitat destruction in something other than effects on individual welfare, such as the loss of a species, a wrong to future generations, or the destruction of a kind of value not reducible to the welfare of individual animals. Please present the strongest version of each side, including the best version of my intuition, and then give a negotiated verdict: which should yield, under what conditions, and whether both survive is more than a verbal compromise.

Finally, as you answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
