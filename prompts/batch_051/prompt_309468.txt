I'm writing a seminar paper on comparative church-state arrangements, and the question stopped being abstract for me after a German friend showed me his payslip and pointed to the church-tax line. The four cases I keep returning to are England, Germany, France, and the United States, because each one violates a different part of the picture I grew up with. England has an established church whose bishops sit by law in the national legislature, yet it is broadly tolerant in practice. Germany has no established church, but the state collects a membership surcharge from registered members of certain religious bodies and withholds it from their wages like income tax. France calls laïcité its central constitutional value, bans conspicuous religious symbols in state schools, and yet the state owns and pays for the upkeep of many church buildings that predate the 1905 separation law. The United States forbids an establishment of religion, but grants churches broad tax exemptions and has opened its legislative sessions with prayer for most of its history. I no longer know whether "secular" names one thing or four.

What I want from you is not a country-by-country scorecard; I will do that myself. I want the desiderata that a theory of church-state relations must meet in order to count as an adequate account of all four cases at once — adequate for both descriptive comparison and normative evaluation. Give me the list of requirements, with the specific case or consideration behind each: the phenomena the theory must explain, the distinctions it must respect (establishment versus mere cooperation, a voluntary membership tax versus a subsidy from general revenue, state recognition versus coercion, symbolic endorsement versus material support), and the normative results it must deliver. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are preferences of particular camps (strict separationist, liberal neutralist, republican laïcité, institutional pluralist, and so on). If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable when applied to these four cases — for example, if the French and American arrangements pull toward strict detachment while the English and German ones pull toward regulated partnership — say so explicitly; that is the part I most want to see.

At the very end, after your analysis, write a single line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence stating the most important conclusion.
