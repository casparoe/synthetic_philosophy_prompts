I cover psychology and philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and one night a week I teach a small upper-level seminar through a university extension program for working reporters. It's called "Why We Do What We Do: Egoism, Altruism, and the View From Inside." I'm drafting the final, and I need one essay question that can't be answered by parroting the two standard defintions. My students can all recite them: psychological egoism is the descriptive thesis that every action aims ultimately at the agent's own welfare, and ethical egoism is the normative thesis that one ought always to act for one's own welfare. But the minute I ask them to put the two in the same room, they flounder.

The crux I want the question to force them into is this. On one hand, if psychological egoism were true, ethical egoism looks trivially true—there's nothing left to recommend, since nobody could do otherwise. On the other hand, if psychological egoism were true, ethical egoism also looks pointless or impossible as a normative theory, because a norm that mirrors an inescapable fact isn't action-guiding. I also want the question to make them confront the way the usual defense of psychological egoism—re-describing every apparently altruistic motive, like giving to charity because it makes you feel good, as "really" self-interested—is the very thing that makes the descriptive claim vacuous, and how that infects the whole exchange. And I'd like the model answer to use something like the Butler-style point that desiring the satisfaction of your desire is not the same as desiring your own welfare as the aim of the action, so a person can want a friend to succeed as a final end while also taking pleasure in it.

Please write the question itself (you can stage it as a short exchange between two students), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. Make the scheme seperate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. You're an experienced ethics professor—think carefully, maybe brainstorm the candidate answers first before settling on wording. No citations or reading lists; everything should be answerable from the material covered in a seminar like this.
