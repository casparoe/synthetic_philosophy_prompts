My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. He kept treating the story as a classic utilitarian thought experiment: one child kept in wretched conditions, whose suffering secures the genuine flourishing of an entire city, and he wanted to know what level of aggregate happiness would finally make such a bargain defensible. I pushed back from a suffering-focused direction: the child's misery, I kept saying, is not just another entry in the ledger, and no amount of others' joy can license deliberately inflicting it. By the end we had wandered into negative utilitarianism — roughly, the view that preventing suffering takes priority over producing happiness — and I realized I wanted a careful reading of the story through exactly that lens rather than another abstract argument about trade-offs.

I am preparing the story for a discussion group, and I would like you to give me a critical reading of it through the lens of suffering-focused ethics. Does Le Guin's parable actually endorse something like a negative-utilitarian verdict — that the city's bargain is wrong no matter how the sums come out — or is it making a narrower point about self-deception, scapegoating, and the way a community manages its knowledge of what it does? The story is striking partly because it refuses to describe the child's suffering in much detail; that refusal seems to treat some suffering as unassimilable to any calculation. But the tale also ends with people merely walking away, which does not help the child; a philosopher of a strict suffering-focused view might object that a principled exit is just moral self-cleansing, and that if the city cannot exist without the child's misery, the consistent verdict is not to walk away but to do whatever actually ends the misery. Does the story notice that difficulty, and does it have an answer to it? Take a definite position on what the story is doing philosophically and whether its implied ethic holds up; do not just lay out possible readings, but defend the one you think is correct, and flag where your reading leans on a specific version of negative utilitarianism.
