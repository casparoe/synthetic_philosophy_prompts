You are a professional philosopher working in social epistemology, with a side interest in the ethics of self-formation and individuality. Think through this question carefully; before you answer, mentally sketch the two or three strongest objections to the policy described below, and let those objections temper whatever you endorse. I have a standing rule for weighing other people's claims about how to live. Before I take seriously anyone's defense of a particular form of life — a conventional career-and-family life, a religious or monastic vocation, an artistic or political life, polyamory, strict traditionalism, and so on — I ask what alternatives they have actually lived from the inside. If someone has known only the option they now defend, I discount their judgment heavily, no matter how thoughtful they are. My reasoning is that an insider cannot separate what is genuinely good in a form of life from what merely feels inevitable, familiar, or required. As a result, I give great weight to defectors, converts, ex-members, and serial experimenters, and very little weight to lifelong loyalists.

A friend objects that this is not a Millian commitment to experiments in living but a bias in favor of the restless and the recently converted. Defectors, she argues, are selected for unhappiness, and their retrospective stories are no more trustworthy than an insider's rationalizations; a person who stayed and a person who left are both biased witnesses, just in opposite directions.

Is my rule defensible, or does it systematically privilege one kind of biased testimony over another while dressing that preference up as an epistemic principle? If it has a defensible core, state exactly what that core is.

Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Your items should do the following, in order: (1) give the strongest case that the policy improves the evidential quality of testimony about how to live; (2) give the strongest case against it; (3) identify a condition under which a defector's or experimenter's testimony genuinely carries more evidential weight than a loyalist's; (4) identify a condition under which it carries no more weight, or less; and (5) state the corrected rule I should adopt in place of the original.
