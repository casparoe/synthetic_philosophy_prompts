I’m a retired physical chemist—spent three decades on reaction dynamics—and I’ve recently gotten into medieval philosophy. I keep coming back to Anselm’s Proslogion and Gaunilo’s reply In Behalf of the Fool, not because I’m especially religious but because the argument has a strangely clean structure. I want to test a set of criteria I’ve abstracted from Anselm’s answer to Gaunilo. Here’s my criteria for a concept being eligible for the ontological argument: (1) the object isn’t a member of a kind with a built-in maximum, so greater than isn’t capped by the kind itself; (2) its great-making features don’t have a specifiable finite upper limit; (3) its existence doesn’t depend on contingent facts about the world. Now take Gaunilo’s lost island. His point, as I understand it, is that if Anselm’s reasoning proves the greatest thinkable being exists, it should also prove the greatest possible island exists, because existing is greater than not existing. I don’t want to quote him from memory, so treat that as a loose paraphrase of the island move. So is the lost island a borderline case for my criteria, or merely a clear miss? I’m genuinely unsure whether it passes criterion (2)—you can always add more beaches, better harbors, whatever—and whether criterion (1) is doing real work or just restating the conclusion. Go through the criteria one by one. Then tell me if the island case exposes a flaw in the criteria themselves, and whether the distinction between capped and uncapped kinds is robust or just a trick of which properties I happen to be measuring. Also, I keep mixing up greater than in the logical sense and better than in the everyday sense, so if that’s relevant, sort me out. I definately don’t want to get into the theological side; I just want to know whether the logic survives the island.
