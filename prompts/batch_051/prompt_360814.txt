You're a political philosopher with a working knowledge of classical Chinese political thought. Take a few minutes to steelman both sides before you commit.

I've been reading Han Feizi, and I find the Legalist picture genuinely attractive: you can't run a state by hoping for virtuous officials, because virtue is private and the state is staffed mostly by ordinary, self-interested people. So you govern through public, fixed, mechanical incentives—clear rewards for results, clear punishments for failure—and you design the system so it works even if everyone is just looking out for themselves. It has exactly the virtues I want in a theory: it's simple, it scales, it doesn't depend on scarce moral character, and it only uses things a ruler can actually observe and check. I'm drawn to that.

And yet I can't shake the intuition that a political order run entirely as an incentive machine would gradually teach its officials to game the metrics, fake compliance, and corrode whatever intrinsic concern for the public they started with—and then the rules fail precisely in the corners where nobody is monitoring or where the metric is too coarse to capture what actually matters. On that intuition, cultivating virtue or ritual isn't a luxury add-on; it's load-bearing, because it supplies the motives that explicit incentives can't specify ahead of time.

I'm not asking who wins as a matter of history. I want to know what it would take for each side to give. Could the intuition be debunked—written off as a romantic picture of character that a well-designed incentive system already answers—and what would that system need to include? Conversely, what would it take to amend or abandon the Legalist theory: does the intuition point to a repair (incentives plus something like norm cultivation), or does it show the whole program was flawed from the start? Is there a reading of "rule by incentives" on which both survive? I'd like a negotiated verdict, not just a scoreboard.
