I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I'm drafting a short paper for a workshop on finite minds and the limits of possible experience, under the working title "Exhaustibility Without a Closed Repertoire." The project grew out of a conversation with a colleague about whether a digital duplicate of a finite mind would eventually become bored or would instead go on recombining old elements indefinitely; I came away suspecting that the boredom worry depends on smuggling in a picture of experience as a closed list of repeatable types. I'd be grateful for comments on the argument below before I develop it further.

The paper reconstructs a familiar line of thought, often associated with Bernard Williams's discussion of the tedium of immortality: a person whose mind is finite has only a bounded repertoire of cares, projects, and qualitative responses, and an endless life would traverse that repertoire until each item had been activated too many times and no experience any longer engaged the will. I want to press on the step from the finiteness of the mind to the finiteness of an inventory of experience-types. A finite base of capacities, sensitivities, and memories can plausibly generate indefinitely many distinct episodes under combinatory operations, rather as a finite lexicon and syntax generate indefinitely many sentences. If that is right, the tedium argument cannot rest on finiteness alone; it needs the stronger claim that the space of experiences that could count as significant for this subject is closed. The paper's positive suggestion is that the real difficulty lies elsewhere: not in producing novelty, but in revising the standards by which novelty is appraised as significant. That yields a dilemma. If the standards of significance are held fixed, then even unbounded novelty may come to feel like variations on themes that have stopped mattering. If the standards are allowed to transform without limit, then it becomes unclear whether we are still describing one life rather than a succession of lives connected by memory. I suspect the anti-immortality side wins only by holding character fixed, while the pro-immortality side wins only by letting identity dissolve.

I would especially welcome comments on three points: whether the distinction between type-finiteness and generative capacity does real work or only relabels the problem; whether the concluding dilemma is a fair way to frame the debate, or whether there is a stable middle position the paper has overlooked; and which premise in the reconstructed tedium argument a hostile reader is most likely to deny.
