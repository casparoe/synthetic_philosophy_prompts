I've been trying to work out what the annual World Happiness Report is actually assuming. It ranks countries by asking samples of residents to place their lives on a ladder from 0 to 10 and then averaging the answers, yet most of the report discusses underlying factors—income, health, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceived corruption—and politicians routinely cite a small move up or down the list as proof that a policy is working or failing. What view of happiness does this practice presuppose? Must it assume that happiness is a single thing people can accurately report on a common scale, that one person's number can be meaningfully averaged with another's, and that a national average is a sensible target for a government to try to raise? Or can the practice fit with several different theories of well-being while the ranking measures something narrower than happiness? Spell out the view built into the practice, separate the assumptions the practice truly needs from the ones it merely fits well with, and explain what commitments would have to be added for a government to treat the ranking as a target worth pursuing. Please answer in American English.
