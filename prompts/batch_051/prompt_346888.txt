I have been working through a passage in Jeremy Bentham's unpublished essay usually titled *Offences Against One's Self*, and I want to test the argument I have extracted from it. Since the text is not in front of me, I will paraphrase the passage rather than quote it. Bentham there says something like this: he proposes to examine the acts in question with the same instrument he applies to any offence, namely the mischief it produces. He distinguishes the *primary mischief*, which falls directly on the parties to the act, from the *secondary mischief*, which spreads to the public through alarm and danger. He then submits that where a sexual act is voluntary and the parties expect and receive pleasure from it, there is no primary mischief; and as for the secondary mischief, if the public's alarm is itself caused chiefly by the penal law and by the prejudice the law embodies, that alarm cannot be treated as an independent evil that justifies the very law from which it arises.

From this passage I have reconstructed the following argument, and now I find its conclusion too strong, even though I cannot locate the fault.

(1) A penal law is justified only if it prevents some real mischief, where a real mischief is a pain experienced by some assignable person or persons.  
(2) The mischief of a sexual act is either primary, borne by the parties to the act themselves, or secondary, consisting in the alarm and danger that spread to the public at large.  
(3) If the parties engage in the act voluntarily and derive pleasure from it, no primary mischief occurs—provided there is no force, fraud, or abuse of a dependent relation, in which cases the wrong is already punishable under separate heads of law.  
(4) Therefore, for any voluntary and mutually pleasurable sexual act, the only possible legitimate ground for punishment would be its secondary mischief.  
(5) But the secondary mischief of such acts is in large part the alarm produced by the penal law itself and by the social prejudice the law teaches. To justify the law by appealing to that alarm would be to let the law manufacture its own evidence.  
(6) Hence no voluntary, mutually pleasurable sexual act should be legally punished.

The conclusion surely covers the acts Bentham was defending, but it also seems to commit me to far more: consensual sexual relations between adult siblings who meet as adults with no power imbalance, polygamous unions among fully consenting adults, and any number of other practices that a given society regards as abhorrent. I do not want to license all of those, but I also do not want to abandon the utilitarian requirement that punishment answer to assignable pain, nor the charge that circular alarm is a bad reason for a penal law. I would most like to keep the argument's application to same-sex acts while blocking the wider application. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Is there a step or a distinction I am missing, and what does it cost to reject that step?
