here's a policy i've been using for a while, and i want to know whether its defensible or just a way of dodging hard questions. whenever two positions in a philosophical debate both have arguments that feel genuinely compelling, and the debate has lasted a long time, i don't pick a side. i conclude instead that the question is probably ill-posed — that some shared concept ('the world as a whole', 'free will', 'heap') is being pushed past the conditions where it makes sense — and i treat the debate as dissolved rather than settled.

i've applied it to: whether the physical world is finite or infinite in space and time; whether time itself had a beginning; whether anything has an absolutely simple part; whether free will and determinism are compatible; and sorites-style cases about when a heap stops being a heap.

my friend says this is just kant's antinomies with the machinery left out. kant's thought was that when reason tries to think the world as a completed whole, it can produce arguments that look compelling for both a thesis and its opposite, and the right move is to reject the assumption the two sides share, not to accept either side. my friend says kant's four antinomies come with a specific diagnosis (roughly, transcendental idealism) and that my policy just applies that diagnosis to every standoff without earning it.

so i want to know: is this policy defensible as a general epistemological rule? under what conditions does a standoff between two compelling arguments license the conclusion 'the question is ill-posed' rather than 'one of these arguments has a hidden flaw'? could i state a version of the policy that keeps the good part without overapplying it? and do kant's own four antinomies count for or against the policy — do they work because of special features of those four questions, or are they just the first four entries in a pattern i should keep applying?
