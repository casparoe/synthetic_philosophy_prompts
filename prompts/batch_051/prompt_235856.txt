I keep getting stuck on this bit of David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, book 3 part 2 section 5, "Of the obligation of promises":

All morality depends upon our sentiments; and when any action, or quality of the mind, pleases us after a certain manner, we say it is virtuous; and when the neglect, or non-performance of it, displeases us after a like manner, we say that we lie under an obligation to perform it. A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise. But 'tis certain we can naturally no more change our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens; nor by a single act of our will, that is, by a promise, render any action agreeable or disagreeable, moral or immoral; which, without that act, wou'd have produc'd contrary impressions, or have been endow'd with different qualities. It wou'd be absurd, therefore, to will any new obligation, that is, any new sentiment of pain or pleasure; nor is it possible, that men cou'd naturally fall into so gross an absurdity.

I want you to unpack the inference that's doing the real work here. Hume sounds like he's treating an obligation as literally identical to a sentiment, so that creating an obligation would mean creating a bit of mental furniture out of thin air by an act of will. But that seems too quick, and I cant tell whether he means (a) the sentiment is the obligation, (b) the sentiment is just what tracks or constitutes our sense of the obligation, or (c) something weaker. Walk me through the strongest version of Hume's argument you can give, then push back on it yourself: what exactly would an opponent have to say about the metaphysics of obligation (or about whether the will can alter sentiments indirectly) to break the inference? Assume I've read the section, so dont just restate the two propositions he sets out at the start. I want the dialectic, including the best objection you can find and how you'd answer it on Hume's behalf.
