We've been doing a unit on personal identity in my high school philosophy elective, and one view we covered is origin essentialism: the idea that you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones you actually came from, so if your parents had waited a month you never would have existed at all. My friend, who has identical twin cousins, thinks she found a counterexample to this. She laid it out like this:

(1) If origin essentialism is true, the particular sperm and egg you came from is what makes you the specific person you are — change the origin and you get a different person, or nobody.
(2) Identical twins come from one sperm and one egg: the zygote splits, so both twins trace back to the exact same sperm-and-egg origin.
(3) So, by origin essentialism, both twins have the very same person-making origin.
(4) If two people can share the same person-making origin, then the origin doesn't actually distinguish them, so it can't be what makes each twin the individual he or she is.
(5) But that's exactly what origin essentialism is supposed to do, say what makes you the individual you are.
(6) So origin essentialism can't handle identical twins, so it's false — or at least it can't be the whole story.

I feel like one of these steps is pulling more weight than it should, but I cant figure out which one. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails and why? If any step is ambiguous, say what the most charitable reading of it would be and whether the argument still goes through under that reading. And if the argument is actually fine as a refutation, just tell me that too.
