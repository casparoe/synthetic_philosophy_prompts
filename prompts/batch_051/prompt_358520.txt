Ok so I've been thinking about the problem of induction—why we think the sun will come up tomorrow just because it always has—and I came across this no-free-lunch theorem thing. I have zero background in this, I just watched some explainer, so I might be butchering the theorem, but it got me to an argument that's been bugging me and I want someone to tell me where it goes wrong.

(1) The no-free-lunch theorem (as I understand it) says: take any two prediction rules, and for every possible sequence of the next, say, 1000 events, count how many events each rule gets right; then average each rule's count over all possible sequences. The averages come out exactly tied, no matter which two rules you picked. A rule like "always predict the worst possible thing" ties with the fanciest pattern-spotting rule.
(2) If two rules are tied on average over the whole space of possible sequences, then there's no fact of the matter about which one is better. "Better" has to mean something, and if every possibility, weighted equally, says they're tied, then saying one is better is just a feeling.
(3) Induction—roughly, the rule that patterns which have held up in the past will keep holding up—is just one prediction rule among others.
(4) So induction is no better than its opposite, or than flipping a coin.
(5) So there's no more rational reason to use induction to predict the future than to guess. Induction can't be justified.

I go back and forth on this. Step 2 feels like it's doing a lot more work than its earned—maybe it slides from "tied under this one weird averaging scheme" to "tied, period"—but I can't make the slide explicit, and maybe it's actually fine. Is the argument valid? If not, which is the first step that fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say?
