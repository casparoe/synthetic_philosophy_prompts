So I've been thinking about Hofstadter's tangled hierarchy thing—Escher's Drawing Hands, where each hand draws the other, and his claim that these loops are stable only if there's some inviolate level outside the loop that breaks the circularity. I want to run Marbury v. Madison through it and see where the analogy earns its keep. The quick facts as I understand them: Marbury was one of Adams's midnight judges, Madison as Secretary of State wouldn't deliver his commission, Marbury sued in the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus relying on a section of the Judiciary Act of 1789, and Marshall's Court held that section unconstitutional because it gave the Court original jurisdiction beyond what Article III allows. The self-referential part is that the Court used its reading of the Constitution to strike down a statute about the Court's own powers—and in doing so it more or less created the practice of judicial review, which is the very practice that later makes its readings of the Constitution authoritative. So the Constitution grounds the Court, and the Court authoritatively interprets the Constitution; each one seems to draw the other. What I want from you: (1) reconstruct the loop properly—who or what is each hand here, and why isnt this just a boring case of one thing depending on another. (2) Say what the inviolate level is supposed to be. Is it the text of Article III, popular acceptance, the other branches choosing not to retaliate, or is there just no clean one? (3) Where does the theory misdescribe the law—is judicial review a logical loop at all, or is it more like a social convention that one court announced and the others tolerated? (4) What does the episode do to the theory—does Marbury show a legal system can contain a tangled hierarchy without an obvious inviolate level and stay stable, or is the stability itself evidence that an inviolate level exists somewhere outside the loop? I'd rather you push on the fit than just restate the case or the theory.
