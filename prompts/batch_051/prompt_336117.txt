A structural parallel has been nagging at me, and I cannot tell whether it is a real identity or just a pattern match. In software, event sourcing, often paired with CQRS, replaces a mutable current state with an append-only log of events: the current state is never stored as such but is computed on demand as a projection obtained by replaying that log, and different read models can project different, equally legitimate views from the same history. There is no single object in memory that is the state; there are many states-for-a-purpose. Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness seems to me to make the same move about the mind: instead of a Cartesian theater in which a finished representation is presented to a central observer, the brain continuously edits many parallel interpretations of incoming signals, and the moment of consciousness one reports is an artifact of probing that stream at a particular time and place. No draft is canonical; the experienced present is reconstructed rather than resident.

So the proposed mapping is this: no central theater, no privileged representation, and the now as a projection rebuilt from a continuously rewritten history, while the event log would correspond to whatever raw neural history all drafts share. Is this a deep structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion? In particular, for the analogy to bear theoretical weight, the brain would seem to need something like the event log: an authoritative, append-only, replayable record from which every draft could in principle be rebuilt deterministically. My suspicion is that this is exactly where the analogy fails, because Dennett's drafts are subject to later editorial revision that overwrites earlier content without leaving a preserved original, whereas event sourcing guarantees immutability and rebuildability. Does that disanalogy kill the comparison, or is it the illuminating part of it?
