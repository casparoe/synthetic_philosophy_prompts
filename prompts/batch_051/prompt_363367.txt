I cover the life sciences and philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and a colleague has asked me to run a three-week unit on the species problem in her upper-level undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar. The students have already worked through the classic statements of the biological, phylogenetic, and ecological species concepts, plus the pluralist response, so a list-and-define question will not discriminate among them. I need a single take-home essay question, to be written in about 90 minutes and 800–1,000 words, that separates students who see why the species debate has been so intractable from students who have only memorized the menu of concepts.

Please build the question around this scenario rather than an open-ended survey: a ring of interbreeding populations circles a mountain range, the two ends meet but do not interbreed, and a small asexually reproducing lineage is nested inside one of the terminal populations. The question should require students to state how the biological, phylogenetic, and ecological species concepts would each parse this case, with real attention to how the biological concept handles the asexual lineage, and then to argue whether the resulting disagreements give us reason to think species are not real, or only that different concepts track different features, stages, or processes in a messy evolutionary world. It should also force them to confront the hard follow-up: if a pluralist grants that all three counts are legitimate, what keeps that pluralism from collapsing into the claim that species boundaries are merely conventional?

Please write (a) the exam question itself, (b) a model answer of roughly 800 words, and (c) a marking scheme with five must-hit moves, each worth 5 marks (total 25), with a one-line note on how students typically get that move wrong. Begin directly with the exam question itself, with no preface or restatement of my request, and then give the model answer followed by the marking scheme.
