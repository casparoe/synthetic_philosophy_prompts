I've been going around in circles on this idea about decision-making, the thought that deliberation crowds out prediction. The way I understand it: an outside observer who knows you can assign a probability to what you'll do — "he'll probably take the safe job, 70 percent" — and that's a perfectly fine prediction. But if you, the person actually choosing, try to take that same outside view on yourself, something breaks. Saying "ok, 70 percent I take the safe job" isn't a neutral read on your own future; it's already a move inside the deliberation. It half-commits you, it changes what you know about yourself, and then you'd have to factor the fact that you just assigned 70 percent into a new probability, which would shift it, and so on. The thought is that while a choice is genuinely live for you, you can't rationally hold a credence about your own choice — the question "what will I do?" is answered by deciding, not by estimating, and the predictive stance just isn't available from the inside.

Could you write me a pantoum that enacts this instead of just describing it? Use the form to do the work: let one recurring thread be the predictive, outside stance and the other the deliberative, first-person stance, and let the pantoum's machinery — the second and fourth lines of each quatrain becoming the first and third of the next, with the last quatrain looping back so the opening lines return as its second and fourth — show the regress. Each prediction about the choice becomes part of the evidence the next prediction has to account for, so the speaker never lands on a stable credence, and the closing return of the opening lines should make it felt why the outside view can't be had from the inside. It doesn't need to name any philosophers or use jargon, but someone who works on this stuff should recognize the puzzle, not just the mood. And write it in American English.
