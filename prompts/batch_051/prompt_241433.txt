Two friends of mine spent most of last Sunday evening on an argument I still cannot settle, and I would like someone to adjudicate it move by move. The subject was whether a direct brain interface could ever produce a genuinely new colour — a reddish-green, say — rather than just a blend or distortion of colours we already know. I wrote the argument down as four moves once I got home.

(1) The first friend said the whole idea is confused. Colour vision is built on opponent channels, red against green and blue against yellow, and a 'reddish-green' would require one channel to signal two opposing values at once. That is impossible in the wiring, not merely hard to imagine, so no implant can deliver it any more than a switch can be both on and off. (2) The second replied that this mistakes an early encoding stage for a law about experience. Opponent cells are a compression trick applied to cone signals; a cortical implant would not have to respect that stage. If it drove the populations that code reddishness and greenishness at the same time, or combined their outputs downstream in a ratio natural light never produces, the brain could land in a state it has never been in before. The fact that we cannot visualise it is exactly what you would expect from a genuinely novel state, and no more counts against it than the fact that we cannot imagine an unfamiliar sort of pain. (3) The first came back that even if a subject reported such a colour, you could never verify it. They might be putting a suggested label on a muddy brown, a flicker, or an ordinary after-image. He mentioned the old stabilised-image experiments that claimed forbidden colours and said they were exactly the sort of thing that falls apart once you worry about suggestion and replication; without independent access to someone's qualia, the claim that a machine produced a new colour is unfalsifiable. (4) The second replied that this is a problem for all colour science, not a special weakness of the implant idea. We already have to rely on structured reports — matching, similarity, and hue-ordering tasks — and those are precisely the tests that could show a state is not any known colour but resembles both red and green in a way no mixture does. That is not unfalsifiable; it is merely hard. And he ended by saying that nothing in neuroscience fixes the number of possible qualitative states that the visual brain can occupy, so the burden is on the other side to explain why the channels evolution happened to build should mark the boundary of the possible.

I honestly could not tell who was winning, which is unlike both of them. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, then say who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, say what experimental or theoretical result would most change your verdict on the underlying question, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
