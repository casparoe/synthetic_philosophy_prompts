In 1987, a settlor created the Rookery Islands Trust by a deed whose operative clause reads:

"The Trustees shall hold and apply the income of the Trust exclusively for the protection and restoration of the native fauna and flora of the Rookery Islands as they existed at the date of this deed, and shall not use any part of the Trust fund to introduce or to promote the spread of any species not native to the Rookery Islands."

The Rookery giant tortoise, a native herbivore that had been the archipelago's dominant grazer, was declared extinct in 2003, almost certainly because introduced black rats consumed its eggs. Preserved museum tissue now holds much of its genome. A consortium submits a two-part funding proposal. First, it would reconstruct a tortoise population by editing the genome of a closely related extant tortoise to carry Rookery tortoise alleles recovered from the preserved tissue, then release the edited animals on the islands. Second, because the rats would still prey on tortoise eggs, it would release a CRISPR-based gene drive designed to spread an infertility allele through the black-rat population and eliminate that population from the archipelago.

The drafter in 1987 did not anticipate gene drives or de-extinction. State precisely what the quoted clause permits, forbids, or leaves unsettled in this case. Then analyze the clause under four interpretive approaches: the ordinary meaning of its words; the settlor's likely purposes and the mischief the clause was meant to prevent; what the drafter would have said if asked about this proposal; and what a reasonable reader of the deed would take it to mean. Where the approaches diverge, say which should govern and why.
