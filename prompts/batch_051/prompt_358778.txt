I keep going back and forth on whether induction has any rational grounding, and a friend gave me this argument, which he says shows that inductive bias can't ever be justified:

(1) Any algorithm that generalizes from past data to unseen cases only does so because it has some inductive bias — a built-in preference for smooth curves over spiky ones, simpler hypotheses over complex ones, whatever.

(2) The no-free-lunch theorem says that, averaged over all possible sequences of data, every learning algorithm does exactly the same: no inductive bias outperforms any other on average.

(3) If all inductive biases are equally good on average, then no inductive bias is privileged over any other.

(4) But choosing an inductive method just is choosing an inductive bias.

(5) So no inductive method is more rationally justified than any other.

(6) Therefore induction isn't rationally grounded at all.

I want the conclusion to be false, but I cant find the bad step. Step 2 feels too strong to me because it averages over all possible worlds, and I have no reason to care about the average world — but then I worry that rejecting the uniform average is just an ad hoc way of privileging the kind of world I happen to be in. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails exactly, and does replacing step 2 with a weaker claim about our actual world rescue or sink the conclusion?
