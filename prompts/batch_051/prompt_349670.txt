Please act as a moral philosopher who works in the style of reflective equilibrium — testing principles against considered judgments and being willing to revise either side. Take your time, and before you answer, sketch two or three candidate principles and stress-test them against my verdicts; don't just label, argue. Here is my problem: I am trying to work out whether there is a principled way to separate moral biases from legitimate evaluative preferences, and I have collected my own gut reactions on a batch of cases. I react with far more indignation to a video of a dog being beaten than to a video of a pig being beaten in similar conditions, and I think that is a bias — cuteness and companion-status should not track moral weight. Yet I would leap into a river to save a child drowning in front of me while giving only modestly to charities that prevent distant child deaths, and I don't think that asymmetry is a bias; I take it to be a defensible feature of situated agency rather than a failure of impartiality. I more readily excuse a friend who lies to cover for another friend than a colleague who lies to pad a résumé, and I count that as a legitimate valuing of loyalty, not a bias. I routinely favor my own children's education and well-being over the equal needs of strangers' children, and I don't think parental partiality is a bias — I think it is licensed. When I allocate a fixed donation budget, though, I prefer to help fellow citizens over equally poor foreigners, and there I lean toward thinking the preference is a bias in disguise. I am more outraged by an individual who deliberately hurts a stray cat for amusement than by a corporation whose negligence slowly kills hundreds of farmed animals, and I regard that asymmetry as a bias produced by the vividness of a single victim. I judge a doctor who switches off a ventilator more harshly than one who withholds a ventilator, even with identical intentions and outcomes, and I call that differential reaction a bias because I cannot defend it. I am more prepared to excuse lethal violence by insurgents against a genocidal regime than identical violence by state forces suppressing dissent, and I want to say that is not merely my political sympathy but a real difference in the justice of the cause. And I consider breaking a promise to a ten-year-old worse than breaking the same promise to an adult colleague, which I take to track real differences in vulnerability and trust rather than mere sentiment.

So my question: is there one principle that sorts these cases the way I have sorted them? If not, which of my verdicts does the best candidate force me to overturn, and why? Please state the principle precisely, say which cases drive each clause, and be explicit about where my sorting has to give. And what does that principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — I discount the interests of people who will exist two centuries from now relative to those alive today when I think about climate policy, and I cannot tell whether that is a legitimate temporal partiality or a bias.
