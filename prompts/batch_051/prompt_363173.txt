I retired from an organic chemistry lab a few years back, and I've been reading philosophy since — slowly, and not always in the right order. Something keeps nagging me about how everyone around me talks about free time, and I'd like someone with more training to sort it out. My old university justifies sabbaticals to donors as "recharging" faculty so they produce more research. My town's school board kept recess because studies show children focus better afterwards. My former employer subsidizes gym memberships because fit employees cost less to insure. Even in my own life: friends ask what I'm "up to now," and they seem releived when I mention woodworking, because it has a point — steady hands, good for aging, maybe small gifts for the grandchildren. Nobody ever says the woodworking is just worth doing.

But the same people would be uneasy if you said you read to your child mainly to build vocabulary, or that a marriage is good because married men live longer. Those things are supposed to be done for their own sake. So there's an inconsistency: some activities we allow to be ends in themselves, while play, rest, and leisure get justified almost exclusively as means to productivity, health, or profit. I gather there's a tradition — Huizinga, Pieper, a few others — that treats play and leisure as ends in themselves rather than as recovery, and I want to know what our actual institutional practice commits us to. Does our practice really presuppose that work or productivity is the only intrinsic value and that leisure is at best restorative? Or is the instrumental talk just rhetoric that runs alongside behavior that isn't really committed to that view? Could we keep the same schools, companies, and retirement norms if we genuinely held that play is an end in itself, and what concretely would have to change?

Please organize your answer under four headings: "The implicit theory" (roughly 300 words: reconstruct the view of value these practices presuppose, distinguishing what is strictly presupposed from what merely fits); "Testing the commitment" (roughly 350 words: identify where the practice is inconsistent or underdetermined — when do we already treat activities as ends in themselves, and are there multiple theories that could explain the same behavior?); "What would change" (roughly 300 words: redesign one or two of the practices, like sabbaticals, recess, or retirement, on the assumption that leisure and play are ends in themselves, and be specific); and "Your verdict" (roughly 150 words: is the instrumental stance defensible or a mistake, and why).
