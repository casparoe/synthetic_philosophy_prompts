As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep encountering the confident claim that the brain is literally a computer. The difficulty is that there is an almost trivial version of this claim and an interesting version. In the trivial version, any physical system rich enough to have many discriminable states can be mapped onto the steps of some algorithm by an observer who chooses the mapping shrewdly; stated that way, the slogan says almost nothing about the brain. The interesting version says that neural activity computes in an intrinsic or explanatory sense: the best account of what the brain is doing is computational, and the computations are not merely projected onto the tissue by a convenient observer.

Write a piece that treats the interesting version as testable at least in principle. Design the strongest concrete test you can of the claim that a brain, or some more tractable neural system, literally computes in that intrinsic, explanatory sense. Specify what you would study, what you would measure or intervene on, and what pattern of results would push toward or against the claim. Be clear about what such a test could never settle even if it went perfectly — for instance, whether a residual gap reflects an experimental limitation or a deeper problem with treating computation as a natural property that a physical system has independently of any description of it. End the piece with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states the operationalization you would recommend and the main residue that no test of this kind can resolve.
