I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have worked my way into an argument while plotting a novel that I cannot dissolve on my own. The novel concerns the fall of a Lucifer-figure: a created angel of clear intellect and undivided love of the good, with no body, no inherited weakness, and no prior corruption, who nevertheless does the first evil thing and is justly held culpable for it. In the story, nothing outside this being tempts it at first; the refusal of the good seems to arise from inside an originally good will. I want this to be possible without making God the author of what follows and without making the angel a victim of chance.

Here is the argument, set out in steps.

(1) A first sin is a first turning away from the good by an agent who, before that act, has no defect of nature, no bad desire, and no habit of sin to explain the turning.

(2) If the first turning is genuinely the angel's own act rather than an accident that happens to the angel, then something in the angel as it was before the act must explain why it turned.

(3) Before the first sin, what could explain the angel's turning is exhausted by three candidates: the angel's intellect, its character, and its will.

(4) If the intellect explains the turning, the angel either followed a real good, which would not be sin, or followed something it took to be good but was not. But a created intellect with clear and unclouded knowledge cannot make the second kind of error, and following a real good cannot be a turning away from the good.

(5) If the angel's character explains the turning, then a wholly good character produced a defective choice. That would mean the character was already defective before the choice, which pushes the first defect back one step and contradicts the premise that this is the first sin.

(6) If the will explains the turning, the will either acted for a reason, in which case the explanation collapses into intellect and returns to (4), or it did not act for a reason, in which case the act was arbitrary. An arbitrary event is not a free, responsible choice.

(7) The remaining possibilities are that the turning was produced by an external cause or by nothing. An external cause of the first evil in creation would ultimately trace back to the creator, which is the conclusion I am trying to avoid. Nothing at all is not an explanation but chance, and chance is not culpability.

(8) Therefore, whichever candidate explains the first evil choice, the choice is either not evil, not free, not the angel's own, or not without a prior defect. So a first sin by a wholly good rational creature seems impossible.

(9) But the story requires that the event be all of the following: genuinely first, genuinely free, genuinely evil, genuinely imputable to the angel, and not traceable to God as its author.

I cannot keep all of these at once. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument contains a mistake, I want to know exactly where it is and why that step looked forced when it was not. If it does not, I want to know which of the claims in (9) must be weakened and what the least damaging weakened version would be. I would most like to keep the event free and imputable to the angel, and to keep God's non-authorship of evil; I am more willing to reconsider what original goodness, firstness, or having an explanation must mean.
