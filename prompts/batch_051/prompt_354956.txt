I'm in a small reading group on the ethics of war, and we've spent months with the same split. One of us is basically a UN Charter restrictivist (call him R); the other is a conditional interventionist who buys the Responsibility to Protect and thinks a blocked Security Council can't be the last word (call her C). They've finally agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration, and I've been landed with drafting the document they would both actually sign.

Both of them know Kosovo, Iraq and Libya in some detail, and neither is a pacifist; both accept that military force can in principle be legitimate. But R thinks the 1999 Kosovo intervention — NATO acting without Security Council authorisation — was a moral and institutional mistake even if it stopped something real, because it weakened the norms that constrain great powers, and he'd say Iraq and Libya are what you get when you relax that rule. C thinks Kosovo was right on the merits and that Security Council legality is at most a procedural good that has to give way in extremis; she defends the 2011 Libya authorisation but thinks the mission was botched the moment it slid into regime change; she thinks Iraq was wrong because the evidence and planning were reckless, not because armed intervention across borders is inherently suspect.

I'd like the document their collaboration would produce, not a mushy compromise. Structure it like this:

- a numbered list of claims both can sign — about the Charter, about what mass atrocity does to a state's claim to non-interference, about the need for high epistemic standards before acting, about what's gone wrong in practice;
- then a disagreements table, with each row giving the precise point of difference, phrased so that each side would own the wording of its own position and would accept the wording of the other's as fair;
- then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether legitimate authority is a stand-alone just-war condition or only an instrument for reducing error; another is whether a bad precedent — say, Russia later citing Kosovo — can count against the justice of the original intervention at the time; a third is how much confidence we need about impending mass atrocity before acting, and where Iraq sits relative to Kosovo and Libya on that; a fourth is whether regime change can ever be a legitimate aim rather than an unintended side-effect of protecting civilians;
- finally, for each crux, a concrete case or kind of evidence both of them agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — for instance, what would R expect a close study of the counterfactual Kosovo-without-intervention to show, and what would C expect.

Write it in British English. Start with the document itself — no "Here's a draft", no throat-clearing, and no commentary at the end; just the first heading or numbered claim through to the last prediction. And please make the agreed claims substantive rather than anodine; if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why.
