I have recently assumed the editorship of a mathematics journal, and the position now forces on me a decision I had previously been happy to leave to someone else. A manuscript under review proves its main theorem by an exhaustive computer search through hundreds of millions of cases. The search program is elaborate and has not been independently verified; the authors argue that it produces a compact certificate which a much simpler program could check, so that accepting the proof would require trusting only the certificate-checker and not the search. I find I have no fixed standard for this, and I want to build one before the next such paper arrives rather than after.

Write the desiderata that an adequate account of mathematical proof -- or, failing that, an adequate editorial standard for the acceptance of computer-assisted proofs -- must meet. I want the criteria themselves, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, not a survey of the positions. A workable account must not reject by fiat the accepted computer-assisted proofs of the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, and it must not reduce proof to the bare fact that a machine reported success. For each desideratum, mark whether it is a non-negotiable constraint on the notion of proof or a preference of one tradition of practice, such as the demand that a single mathematician be able to survey the entire argument.

Use at least the following cases, including the manuscript now on my desk, to fix the requirements and test them: (a) the manuscript above, with its short human-readable argument, unreadable computational trace, and compact certificate; (b) the same theorem established by a computation reproduced inside a proof assistant, so that the computer-dependent steps are checked by a second program whose correctness rests on a small, independently inspected core; (c) a probabilistic verifier that certifies the theorem with error probability below 2^-100 but supplies no line-by-line derivation a human could inspect; (d) a one-off computation on a large, heterogeneous cluster, never reproduced, which passes the authors' internal consistency checks; and (e) a lengthy traditional proof that the community accepted and used for years before a fatal gap was found.

For each desideratum, say what would be lost by dropping it, and where a requirement turns out to be negotiable, say which distinction or principle it was a proxy for and whether that distinction can be kept without it. I especially want to know which subsets of the requirements, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied once realistic rates of human and machine error are admitted, since that is the result most likely to change my editorial policy. Finish by naming the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise plausible standards, and explain why.
