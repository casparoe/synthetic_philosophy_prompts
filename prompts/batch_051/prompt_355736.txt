I am a PhD student in political theory, and I keep noticing a structural analogy between arguments for sortition and the logic of random sampling in statistics, and I cannot tell whether it is deep or superficial.

The lottocratic argument runs roughly as follows: a legislature selected by lot will be a microcosm of the citizenry, so its composition, and therefore its decisions, will track the considered judgments of the population as a whole. The randomization is what does the work. It prevents any interest, profession, or faction from being systematically overrepresented, without requiring us to trust the character or expertise of the particular people selected. This seems to me to have the same structure as the standard argument for survey sampling. In sampling theory, random selection is what makes a sample representative: as long as every member of the population has a known positive chance of being selected, a random sample gives an unbiased estimate of the corresponding population quantity; by the law of large numbers the sample mean converges on the population mean as the sample grows; and none of this requires modeling the population beforehand. In both cases, representativeness is a property of the random mechanism across possible draws, not a property of the particular people drawn.

But I suspect the analogy may break exactly where it seems strongest. A survey sample is a measuring instrument: the estimand is a fixed, pre-existing quantity defined independently of the sampling process, the sampled units are measured but do not act back on the population, and the statistician does not hand the sample control over the phenomenon it is supposed to represent. A legislature is not an instrument but a decision procedure. The public's considered judgment may not exist as a fixed quantity waiting to be estimated; it may be formed through deliberation, agenda setting, and institutional rules. And selection into a chamber changes the selected: they acquire information, face pressure, lobbyists, and party discipline, and may cease to be representative in the relevant sense. If the thing to be estimated is partly constituted by the process that samples it, the sampling-theoretic guarantees do not obviously transfer.

So I would like you to adjudicate the analogy. Is it a genuine identity of structure — a lottocratic chamber as an unbiased estimator of the public's considered judgment — or a loose analogy carried by the word representative? Lay out the mapping in detail, say exactly what would have to be true on both sides for results from sampling theory to transfer to the political case, and give at least one concrete prediction the analogy makes that fails for real political assemblies. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
