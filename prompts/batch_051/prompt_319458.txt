In my Hellenistic ethics class we've spent the last few weeks on the Stoic theory of the passions, and I'm realizing the view I keep drifting toward is a wierd hybrid that might not hang together. I have a paper due soon, so I want a consistency check before I commit to writing it. Here's roughly where I am.

(1) I find the Stoic claim that the passions are judgments pretty convincing: anger is the judgment that I've been wronged and that retaliation is appropriate, grief is the judgment that something bad in itself has happened, and so on—fix the judgment and you fix the feeling.
(2) I think apatheia, freedom from the passions, is a genuine ideal and not emotional flatness. The sage still has the good feelings the Stoics allow—joy, wishing, caution—and can love people warmly, so I don't see why serenity should make someone cold.
(3) But I also think love requires being vulnerable to loss. A parent who didn't grieve after their child died would be defective, not just wired differently, and I'm not sure I'd want to be the sage in that situation: the wrecked parent seems to have loved better.
(4) I think anger at grave injustice—abuse of a child, a massacre—is not just useful but a fitting response, and someone who never gets angry about those things is missing a moral sensibility even if they still fight the injustice.
(5) I'm also convinced by the Stoic argument that no one errs willingly: people do what seems good to them given their beliefs, so wrongdoers are mistaken rather than evil, which should push us toward pity rather than hate.
(6) Yet I can't shake the sense that some wrongs leave a moral residue, and that forgiving immediately, treating the offender the same as before, would be servile rather than virtuous.

So my question is whether this package hangs together, and if not, which commitments are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and what the least costly revision would be.
