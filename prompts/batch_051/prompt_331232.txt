I'm a journalist working on a piece about the idea that some moral norms don't end because people get argued out of them or because their material interests shift — they end because the practice stops looking honorable and starts looking shameful. I've been using four criteria to decide when that's the right account: (1) the behavior faded before new laws or material penalties could explain the change; (2) the behavior was re-described as shameful or ridiculous, not merely as wrong or harmful; (3) the people who gave it up mostly talked about disgrace and mockery rather than legal risk or money; and (4) no new argument against it appeared right before the fade — the old ones had been laying around ignored for generations. Now take the standard story of the end of dueling among Anglo-American elites in the nineteenth century. People had been calling it murderous folly for ages, so (4) looks safe. But dueling had also been illegal and lethal the whole time, so (1) is murky: the costs were old, the practice stuck around anyway, and when it finally collapsed, legal crackdowns and ridicule were tangled together. (2) and (3) are only partly true. So is dueling a borderline case for the honor account, or do my criteria blur things that should be kept apart? Go through the four criteria one by one, and as the fifth point tell me whether I need better criteria or just a better case. Give me exactly five numbered points — one per criterion, then the verdict — each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In the fifth point, also say how confident you are overall and which of the five points you're least sure of.
