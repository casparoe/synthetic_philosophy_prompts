A philosopher friend has been insisting to me that Sen's liberal paradox is deep, and that I should care about it even though I think natively in the language of distributed systems — replicas, quorums, linearizability, causal consistency, version vectors, multi-master replication, conflict resolution, access control, and impossibility results like CAP and FLP. As I understand the paradox, Sen showed that no social preference aggregation can simultaneously satisfy three conditions: (U) unrestricted domain — any profile of complete, transitive individual preference orderings is admissible input; (P) weak Pareto — if every individual strictly prefers x to y, society strictly prefers x to y; and (L) minimal liberalism — at least two individuals are each decisive over some pair of alternatives, meaning their strict preference on that private pair is binding for society no matter what anyone else wants. In the standard illustration, a = the prude reads Lady Chatterley's Lover, b = the lewd reads it, and c = neither reads it; the prude ranks c > a > b and the lewd ranks a > b > c; the prude is decisive over {a, c} and the lewd over {b, c}; Pareto forces a > b socially, while the two decisiveness claims force c > a and b > c, so social preference cycles.

I'd like you to act as someone genuinely fluent in both social choice theory and distributed systems, and treat me as a colleague who knows the latter cold but has never studied the former. Translate Sen's theorem, as faithfully as you can, into my vocabulary: what is an individual and a preference profile, what is social preference, what plays the role of the committed order, and what exactly does the prude/lewd cycle become — an unserializable history, a reconciliation conflict, a causal cycle, or a violation of acyclicity in some dependency graph? Then give me the residue report: which of the three conditions translate cleanly, which you had to fudge, and which have no real counterpart in distributed systems. In particular, I want your considered view on whether (L) should be read as availability — each node keeps serving its own "partition" of decisions — or as something closer to access control over who may propose writes to which key; and whether the fact that my native vocabulary wants to model rights as access control, rather than as a guarantee of the coordination protocol, shows that my vocabulary is missing something, or that Sen's own decision-theoretic formalization of rights-as-decisiveness is what should be jettisoned. Be explicit about any place where you are smuggling distributed-systems assumptions into the social-choice problem rather than simply translating it. Before settling on your mapping, brainstorm two or three candidate translations, say briefly why you reject the others, and then carry the chosen one through carefully to the residue report.
