In most undertakings, we treat conscious effort as the proper instrument of success. A student who resolves to try harder at calculus, a weightlifter who renews her concentration before a lift, an orator who prepares his points more deliberately—each is acting in a way we ordinarily count as praiseworthy and likely to improve the result. We would find it strange to advise the student to stop trying to understand derivatives, or the lifter to stop monitoring her form. Yet with other activities we give the opposite advice. We tell the pianist to stop thinking about her fingers, the insomniac to stop trying to fall asleep, the conversationalist to stop trying to be charming, the meditator to stop trying to attain calm. The same effort that elsewhere seems like the engine of competence here appears as an obstruction.

The Zhuangzi sharpens the puzzle. In one passage, if I read it aright, an archer is said to shoot with full skill when there is no prize, to grow anxious when a small token is at stake, and to go nearly blind to the target when gold hangs on the shot. The archer is unchanged, the bow is the same, the distance is the same; only the pressure to try varies with the stakes. That suggests effort does not merely fail in some domains—it actively harms, and the harm grows exactly when the reason to try grows.

I want to know whether this asymmetry between effort-helping and effort-harming activities can be justified, or whether our ordinary practice of praising effort in one context and discouraging it in the other is an unexamined inconsistency. In particular: should wu-wei be understood as the absence of trying, as a disguised form of trying, or as something else? Propose a criterion that separates activities where greater conscious effort improves performance from those where it degrades performance, test it against the cases above and against the Zhuangzi’s archer, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. If your criterion yields a counterintuitive verdict about one of the cases—say, that the insomniac should try harder—defend it.
