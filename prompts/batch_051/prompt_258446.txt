I'm a philosophy professor, and I have a policy I fall back on when people around me propose changing or abolishing some long-standing university arrangement—a course requirement, the thesis defense, the way we run qualifying exams. The policy is: I won't seriously engage the proposal until the proposer can state, in their own words and to my satisfaction, what the existing practice is actually for. If they can't articulate any plausible function, I treat that as a reason to keep the thing, not merely as evidence that the proposer is underprepared. Last term a group of students argued we should drop the logic requirement for the major because most of them will never use formal logic; when I asked them to say what the requirement is for, they couldn't do better than "it's traditional," and I counted that in favor of keeping it. A colleague tells me this is just Chesterton's fence with self-importance, maybe with some Burkean faith in accumulated institutional wisdom thrown in; she says I'm dressing up status quo bias as epistemic humility. She's not entirely wrong, but somtimes I think she's missing the point. Some of these practices are older than the people now defending them, and the rationale for them might be distributed across the people and generations that built and adjusted them, so the fact that no one in the room can state the function is exactly what you'd expect whether or not the practice earns its keep. So: is my rule a defensible epistemic policy, a bias, or some mixture? I want you to write an essay that reaches a definite verdict—don't end on a non-committal note. Assign yourself the role of someone who actually works on the epistemology of institutions and social conservatism, and think carefully. Start by brainstorming two or three concrete situations where my rule would make me epistemically worse off, then consider whether a restated version of the rule could keep the benefits without those failures. As you go, please seperate what is genuinely settled about this from what is actively contested among people who know the area, and don't present a contested reading of Chesterton or Burke as the obvious one.
