I'm in an early modern philosophy class and we've just been on Émilie du Châtelet's ideas about happiness. The way our professor laid it out, she is not in the tame-your-passions camp: she thinks happiness isn't about minimizing pain or getting your passions to quiet down so reason can steer. Instead you need lively passions, a certain openness to self-illusion, and a real attachment to study, which she treats as the most dependable source of pleasure because it's always available and doesn't depend as much on other people. So there's this strange mix: she wants you to actively cultivate your own happiness, but she also seems to think you shouldn't be too rational about it.

I keep wondering what she would do with the modern quantified-wellbeing world: mood logging apps, meditation streaks, gratitude reminders, positive psychology studies that treat happiness as a variable you can measure and optimize. If we dropped her into the present with her basic commitments intact, would she treat all that as a natural extension of her project—she also thought you should deliberately arrange your life for happiness—or would she think its exactly the kind of dispassionate self-surveillance that kills off the passions and illusions she said were necessary? And if the positive-psychology crowd is right that social connection and small daily habits matter, would that force her to revise any part of her view, like her line that study is the steadiest path to happiness?

I'm not expecting a single obviously right answer, and if my setup is too anachronistic in a way that distorts her view—or if you think she wouldn't have had a determinate stance on this at all—push back on that first. Otherwise give me your best reconstruction of what she'd say, and tell me how confident you are in it and where you think you're stretching her. Please write in American English.
