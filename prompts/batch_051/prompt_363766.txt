Two people in my epistemology reading group got into a long argument after our session on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and neither of them managed to persuade the rest of us. The dispute started over a third member, Daniel, who now says he cannot assign more than middling credence to the claim that the world is older than five minutes, because any evidence one could point to—fossils, written records, overlapping memories—is exactly what a world that sprang into existence five minutes ago with apparent age would contain anyway, so the evidence cannot, he thinks, settle the matter. Miriam said Daniel's problem is not that he is weighing evidence badly but that he is trying to doubt something that is not an ordinary empirical claim at all. On her view, the stability of the past belongs to the framework within which fossils and records count as evidence in the first place; once a person suspends that framework, he cannot go on citing the very practices of evidence-gathering that the framework supports, and his "doubt" no longer has any consequences that distinguish it from confusion. She added that Moore's gesture of holding up a hand to prove an external world makes the same point from the other direction: Moore did not acquire another piece of knowledge there, because the proposition at issue plays a different role from the claims that get established by evidence within the game of ordinary knowledge. Jonah pushed back hard. He said the five-minute hypothesis, in a Russell-style form, is perfectly meaningful—after all, we can state exactly what it says—and a meaningful empirical hypothesis is settled by evidence and inference, not by being declared out of bounds. The reason the five-minute hypothesis loses, he argued, is not that it is unthinkable but that it is a very bad abductive explanation: it posits an enormous, unparsimonious, ad hoc fabrication, while the long-past hypothesis explains the same data with far fewer unsupported assumptions. When Miriam replied that any inference to the best explanation already presupposes the stability of the past, Jonah said that merely shows evidence is theory-laden and underdetermined, which is true throughout science, and nobody concludes from that that rival theories cannot be rationally ranked. Miriam then shifted ground: the past, unlike a scientific theory, is not up for grabs because it is a hinge on which particular inquiries turn; if Daniel really doubted it, he should also doubt whether his words mean what he takes them to mean, at which point his own doubt loses its sense. Jonah's last move was that "hinge" is doing too much work: either hinges are empirical commitments, in which case they can be false and revisable—people once treated the fixity of the Earth as a fixed background, and gave it up—or they are non-empirical rules, in which case they are not true-or-false descriptions of the world at all, and calling them certain is no longer an epistemic verdict but something closer to a decision. Miriam answered that the distinction between a hinge and a merely entrenched empirical belief is a distinction of role, not of origin, and that the role can be read off from how a commitment actually functions in inquiry: as a norm of description, or as a description of the world. The group split on whether that answer was adequate.
