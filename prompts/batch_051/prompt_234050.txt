I am trying to work out what I actually think about moral responsibility and the ability to do otherwise, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. I have strong views about each case below, but I cannot see how they fit together. Do not worry about matching official labels; I want the principle itself.

1. Jones is deliberating about whether to shoot Smith. Black, a neuroscientist, has secretly implanted a device that will activate only if Jones is about to conclude his deliberation with a decision not to shoot. If activated, the device would directly cause Jones to decide to shoot. In fact, Jones concludes on his own that he should shoot, the device never activates, and he shoots. I blame Jones fully. I also think he could not have done otherwise—the device would have prevented any decision not to shoot—but this does not reduce his blame in my eyes at all.

2. Same set-up, except that Black's device activates before Jones has begun to deliberate and directly produces the decision to shoot. Jones never forms the intention on his own. I do not blame Jones for the shooting, or at most a very small amount.

3. Jones deliberates and decides not to shoot. As he turns to leave, Black's device bypasses his decision and directly causes him to shoot. I do not blame Jones for the shooting at all. The act did not come from his decision.

4. Same as the first case, except the device is triggered by a small neural event that precedes any conscious choosing. If that precursor event occurs, the device takes over and causes the decision to shoot. In the actual sequence the precursor does not occur, and Jones decides on his own. I still blame Jones fully, even though the only alternative to what actually happened was a different firing of a subpersonal neural precursor rather than a different decision or action.

5. A person signs a false financial report because her employer threatens to have her fired and blacklisted if she refuses. I hold her responsible for signing, though less responsible than if she had signed freely for money. The pressure matters, but the choice is still hers.

6. Ruth was raised from birth in a violent sect that taught her that defectors deserve death. Her upbringing made it psychologically impossible for her to refuse orders to kill a defector. As an adult, she deliberates—within the only frame of mind she is capable of—and kills a defector. I blame her much less than I blame Jones in case 1, and perhaps not at all. Yet she seems to me just as unable to do otherwise as Jones was.

7. Imagine that a completed neuroscience shows that every human decision is determined by prior brain states together with inputs, with no indeterministic gaps. In an ordinary case with no intervener, threat, or indoctrination, a person deliberates, decides to harm someone, and does it. I go back and forth on whether to blame him. On some days I think I should, because everything that mattered went through his own deliberation. On other days I suspect this is just a prejudice I am carrying over from case 1.

I would also like to know what the principle says about a case I genuinely cannot settle. Nadia, a fully informed adult, voluntarily undergoes a procedure that is guaranteed to make her want to embezzle money from the charity she runs. The procedure works; she later embezzles and, at the time, wholeheartedly endorses what she is doing. Is she fully blameworthy like Jones, less blameworthy like Ruth, or somewhere else?

Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I abandon? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, derive its verdicts on the determinism case and the Nadia case, and be honest about any of my listed verdicts it cannot accommodate.
