ok so I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background who fell into the cause prioritization rabbit hole through a few Effective Altruism forum posts, and I'd like a sanity check on whether the views I've ended up with actually hang together. here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the right way to rank causes is the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework: score each one, multiply, and put your effort where the product is biggest.

(2) I think neglectedness is purely instrumental. a crowded cause isn't bad because popularity is bad, it's bad because with fewer people on it the marginal hour or dollar does more. in a perfect model it would already be baked into tractability.

(3) but I still keep neglectedness as a separate score when I actually rank things, because how many people are already on a cause is also evidence about how tractable it really is — if everyone smart abandoned a problem, I've probably underestimated how hard it is, and if something's totally neglected there might be low-hanging fruit.

(4) I think a big enough importance swamps low tractability. I'd take a tiny, basically made-up probability of stopping an x-risk over a near-certain win on a moderate global health problem, and the fact that the probability is basically made up doesn't bother me much.

(5) but when it comes to ordinary near-term causes, I get super conservative and basically only count effects I can measure — bed nets, cash transfers, that kind of thing — and treat speculative downstream effects as zero.

I can feel (2) and (3) pulling against each other but I can't tell weather that one is real or just a labeling thing, and (4) and (5) feel like I'm using two different rules depending on how far away the damage is. go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, order them by how much trouble they cause, what the cheapest fix is for each, and which single change would clean up the most.
