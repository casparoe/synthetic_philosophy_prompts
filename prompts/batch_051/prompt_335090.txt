I'm a novelist, and I've been living inside Plutarch's parallel lives while writing a novel about a biographer who's loosing faith in the "facts" side of his genre. I want a character to defend the following position as hard as he can: that the formal device of setting a Greek life next to a Roman life and treating both as instances of the same virtue is already a moral argument—it commits Plutarch to the idea that virtue is universal and translatable across cultures, rather than being the property of a particular polis or tradition. And this sits badly with the way Plutarch keeps praising each man for embodying his own city's customs. Please make the strongest possible argument for that reading of the genre: the parallel form is doing moral work, and biography that teaches by example has that unresolved tension built into it.
