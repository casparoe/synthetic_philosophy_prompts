Construct a thought experiment to the following specification, in the domain of sweatshops and mutually beneficial exploitation.

The case must pull apart two accounts of exploitation, which should be defined as follows.

(i) The betterment-plus-consent account. On this view, an employer exploits a worker only if the exchange leaves the worker worse off than she would have been under her best available alternative, or if her consent is obtained by force or fraud. A voluntary exchange that leaves both parties better off than their no-exchange alternatives is therefore not exploitative, however small the worker's share of the gains may be.

(ii) The surplus-share account. On this view, exploitation consists in one party's taking advantage of unequal bargaining power to capture an unfairly large share of the surplus that their cooperation creates, even when the other party still gains from the exchange. On this account the same transaction can be both mutually beneficial and exploitative.

Construct a realistic case in a contemporary supply chain — garment assembly, electronics, or agricultural packing, for example — in which these two accounts deliver opposite verdicts: the betterment-plus-consent account must say that the employer does not exploit the worker, and the surplus-share account must say that the employer does exploit the worker. The point of the case is to test whether 'mutually beneficial exploitation' is a coherent category or a conflation of two different questions, so the verdicts must be generated by the two accounts rather than stipulated.

Constraints. The worker's best available alternative must be genuinely poor but survivable — for instance, a smallholding that feeds a family but leaves no margin for school fees or medical care — rather than a life-threatening emergency, so that the benefit of the job is real but modest. The employer must not be a literal monopsonist: let there be at least two other employers in the region, none of whom collude with one another. The bargaining asymmetry should instead come from a less dramatic feature, such as the worker's skills being specific to one product, prohibitive moving costs, or a slack labour market. The scenario should include enough about the worker's productivity and the firm's revenue that a reader can see that a surplus exists and roughly how it is divided. There must be no violence, debt bondage, deception, or threat beyond ordinary economic pressure.

After presenting the scenario, do all of the following.

1. Walk through the case once with each account, showing step by step that it satisfies the relevant test and yields the specified verdict.
2. Identify the single feature of the case that does the real work in separating the two verdicts, and explain how removing or changing that feature would collapse the case into a standard disagreement about desperate poverty.
3. Give the strongest response a defender of the betterment-plus-consent account could offer — for example, that the case describes a regrettable but non-exploitative bargain, or that the surplus-share account would classify much ordinary commercial life as exploitation — and assess whether the response succeeds.
4. Finally, separate two questions the case might be taken to settle but does not: whether the worker is wronged by the transaction, and whether outside parties such as a state, a buyer, or a boycott campaign would be justified in interfering with it. Explain briefly where the two accounts will diverge, even though each has a clear answer to the first question.
