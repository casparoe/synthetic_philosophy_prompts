I've been teaching an undergraduate unit on trolley problems, and a student offered a line of argument that I keep failing to refute. I understand the doctrine of double effect as the view that there is a moral difference between intended harm and merely foreseen harm: an act that would be wrong if the harm were intended as an end or as a means can be permissible if the harm is a foreseen but unintended side effect and is proportionate to the good achieved. This is supposed to explain why it is permissible to turn the trolley away from five and onto one, while it is not permissible to push a large person off a bridge to stop the trolley; in the bridge case the person's being struck is intended as the means of stopping the trolley, whereas in the standard switch case the one's death is treated as a side effect of routing the trolley down the side track.

The student's argument runs like this. (1) Whether I intend an outcome is settled by what I choose as my means, not by what I explicitly hope or regret. (2) In the standard switch case, the means I choose is the trolley's traveling down the side track. (3) But in the circumstances, the trolley's traveling down the side track is constituted by, or is inseparable from, its running over the one person. (4) So in choosing that means, I intend the one's being run over. (5) If I intend the one's being run over, then I intend a lethal harm to an innocent person as part of my means, which the doctrine forbids. (6) Therefore the doctrine, consistently applied, does not permit the standard switch case. The student then generalizes: any case in which the good result is achieved through a causal route that includes a known death will be one in which the death is internal to the chosen means, so the doctrine will forbid nearly every case of knowingly causing collateral death, including many acts of rescue, warfare, and self-defense. That conclusion seems far more restrictive than what defenders of the doctrine themselves accept. I would very much like to keep the switch/bridge distinction, but I cannot see which premise to deny without making the notion of intention arbitrary or gerrymandered. Which step of the argument should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the doctrine of double effect?
