I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please define any technical terms you introduce. I have been trying to get clear about when a single person is morally responsible for contributing to a collectively caused harm — the sort of case in which no individual act makes a difference on its own: one flight, one burger, one vote. The thought I keep returning to is that if my act would not have changed the outcome, then I am off the hook; but I do not find that fully convincing, and I want better criteria.

The working criteria I currently use are these:

(a) There is a specifiable causal route from the act to some portion of the harm, even if the portion is tiny.
(b) The act is performed within a larger shared practice whose aggregate effect is the harm, and the person is aware of that.
(c) The act raises the expected size or likelihood of the harm by an amount that is not negligible.
(d) The person had a reasonable alternative that would have avoided the contribution at little cost, and no comparably important reason justified the act.

I want to test these criteria against the following case, drawn from my own field. An engineer on the backend team of a large video streaming platform changes an HTTP keep-alive timeout from 60 seconds to 55 seconds. The purpose is to free idle memory sooner; the change slightly improves a performance metric. A side effect is that some clients now close connections earlier and must re-establish them more often. Any single additional connection setup consumes an amount of CPU and network energy too small to register on any individual server or any single user session; it sits below the noise floor of the company's observability tooling. The platform handles billions of requests per day, and the team's rough quantitative model estimates that the change will add on the order of 40,000 kWh per year to the platform's electricity consumption, and with it the platform's associated emissions, concentrated in data centers. The engineer has reviewed the estimate, finds it credible, and ships the change anyway. She could have left the timeout unchanged at no cost to her project, although her team's performance dashboard would have looked marginally worse. If she had done nothing, the platform's total energy use would have been practically indistinguishable.

Does her shipping the change qualify as a morally responsible contribution to a collective harm under these four criteria? Please go criterion by criterion, identify where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case suggests that any criterion should be revised, removed, or sharpened.
