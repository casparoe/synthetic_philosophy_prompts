I teach a literature and philosophy course, and next week we are pairing Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice with Aristotle's account of money and exchange in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics. I would like a close reading that uses Aristotle as a lens without reducing the play to an illustration of his theory. In particular, read the play through two ideas. The first is the Politics's contrast between natural household acquisition, which serves use and sufficiency, and unnatural wealth-getting, chrematistics, which treats money as an end; connected to this is the claim that usury is the most unnatural form of gain because it makes money yield money instead of serving exchange. The second is the Nicomachean Ethics's proposal that money is a conventional measure that renders unlike goods commensurable enough for reciprocal exchange. Where does this framework illuminate the play's distinctions, for instance among Antonio's interest-free loan to Bassanio, Antonio's mercantile profit from overseas ventures, and Shylock's lending at interest, or in the way a monetary debt is converted into a pound of flesh by the bond? Where does the play resist the framework, for instance by treating Antonio's trade as honorable in a way Aristotle's categories cannot easily ground, or by having the flesh-bond expose a limitation in the idea of money as a neutral measure of value that the Nicomachean Ethics underplays? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: take Aristotle seriously as a theorist of exchange, and take the play seriously as a drama about debt, risk, and belonging. End by saying what the play adds to the philosophical problem of how money relates to value, need, and obligation that the theory leaves out.
