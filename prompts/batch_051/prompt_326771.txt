I'm a retired physicist, and I've spent the last couple of years trying to work my way through philosophy of mind. I keep coming back to the causal exclusion argument, and I'm stuck. The argument seems to me to have all the virtues I used to want in a theory: it's simple, it respects the causal closure of physics (which I basicly take to be non-negotiable after a lifetime of doing science), and it has a clean decision procedure for what does causal work. If a mental event M is distinct from the physical event P that realizes it, and P is already a sufficient cause of the physical effect E, then M would have to cause E as well, which looks like systematic overdetermination — overdetermination everywhere would be a cosmic coincidence, not something science should accept — or M doesn't cause E at all. So the view ends up being: either mental events just are physical events, or they're epiphenomenal. That's a tidy, honest picture and I'm drawn to it.

But I can't get the intuition to shut up. When I decide to stand up and make coffee, it seems to me — not as a bit of folk psychology but as directly as I experience anything — that the deciding is what does the causing. If you told me my decision was epiphenomenal, just along for the ride while the brain does the work, I'd understand every word and I still wouldn't believe it about my own case. I also can't give up the thought that deliberation matters: if my weighing up whether to have a second cup made no difference to whether I actually do, then that whole activity would be a kind of causally inert show, and that looks like a reductio, or at least a very expensive bullet.

So here's the question. Is this a case where the intuition is a product of how agency feels from the inside and should be debunked, or is it tracking something the exclusion argument leaves out — maybe mental and physical events aren't distinct in the way the argument needs, or the argument is using the wrong notion of 'cause'? A friend of mine says the whole problem shifts if you go in for an interventionist conception of causation, where higher-level variables can be causally relevant even when the lower-level story is complete, so the exclusion argument just doesn't go through for mental causation. I can't tell whether that's a real resolution or just redefining 'cause' to save the appearances. Give me the strongest version of both sides, and tell me which I should give up or amend, or whether there's a reading on which they are actually compatible. Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase saying what the verdict is (e.g. the intuition should go, the theory should be amended, they're compatible).
