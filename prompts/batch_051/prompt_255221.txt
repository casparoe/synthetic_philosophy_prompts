I keep returning to Hobbes's position in Six Lessons to the Professors of the Mathematics, where he argues, as I read him, that geometry is demonstrable because the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves, and that civil philosophy has the same status because we make the commonwealth. That maker's-knowledge criterion has a strong pull on me: it promises a clean line between what can be known demonstratively and what can only be conjectured, and it explains the peculiar certainty of geometry without appealing to a realm of Platonic forms. But I also have a firm intuition that natural philosophy gives us genuine knowledge of things we did not make—a magnet's attraction of iron, water's freezing point, the phases of the moon—even though we are not the authors of those phenomena. I cannot dismiss that intuition as mere habit. I want you to present both sides at their strongest and then give a reasoned verdict: should I abandon the maker's-knowledge criterion, amend it to cover natural philosophy without collapsing the distinction, or reinterpret my intuition about natural philosophy as knowledge of something other than what I took it to be? What would justify each move, and which move is best?
