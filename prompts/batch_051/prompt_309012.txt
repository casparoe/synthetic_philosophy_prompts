I am taking a course on the philosophy of economics, and we recently discussed Milton Friedman's so-called F-twist: the thesis that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant to its assessment, and that hypotheses should be judged solely by whether they yield sufficiently accurate predictions. In discussion afterward, a classmate defended a model we had been studying — one whose assumptions about individual behavior everyone in the room conceded were empirically false — with an analogy. Models, they said, are like maps. Every map distorts; the London Underground map bears little resemblance to the actual geography of the lines, yet it is more useful for navigating the Tube than a perfectly faithful map would be, precisely because it discards nearly everything. By the same token, they argued, unrealistic assumptions in an economic model are not a defect but a feature: the model, like the simplified map, works because it throws away detail that would only confuse its user. The only test, on their view, is whether the map gets you where you are trying to go — that is, whether the model predicts well enough for its intended purpose.

The analogy is appealing, but I suspect it is carrying the argument rather than merely illustrating it. Please audit the models-as-maps analogy in the context of Friedman's F-twist. Begin with an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the territory, the map, the scale, the distortions, the route, the destination, and the traveler. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and be especially careful about what the analogy quietly imports from the source. Maps have a settled purpose and a user whose destination is known before the map is drawn; a map's distortions are representational simplifications, whereas a model's unrealistic assumptions can be substantive false claims about what causes what; and a map's success is checked against the same territory it was drawn from, which is not obviously true of a model's predictions. Finally, say whether the analogy can support the strong conclusion my classmate wanted — that no amount of unrealisticness of any kind counts against a model so long as its predictions are accurate — or whether it supports only a weaker claim about a limited class of idealizations. If the analogy fails at the load-bearing points, say whether some better analogy would do the job, or whether the F-twist would be more honestly defended without analogy altogether.
