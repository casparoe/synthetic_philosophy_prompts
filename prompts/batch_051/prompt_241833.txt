I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and its limits, with twenty students and one eighty-minute session per week. I want to open the unit on Herbert Simon with a game rather than a lecture, so that students feel the core idea before they hear the vocabulary. The idea is this: once looking at more options is costly and the distribution of what is available is unknown, computing the true optimal stopping point is out of reach, and committing in advance to an aspiration threshold — take the first option that clears this bar — is not a lazy fallback but a reasonable strategy for a finite mind under pressure. I would like a game in which each student repeatedly decides when to stop drawing from a hidden set of options, with some rounds revealing the full distribution beforehand and others withholding it, and with a small cost or time penalty attached to each additional draw. To make the satisficing structure visible, I would like one variant in which participants must write down their stopping threshold before seeing any options, and their score then depends on how their realized option compares to those of others while still respecting their commitment.

Please specify the exact rules, the materials (cards, envelopes, timers, response slips, and so on), and a non-monetary payoff scheme with real stakes for the students, such as a running leaderboard or a small classroom privilege. Include an eighty-minute timeline and a debrief script that maps particular moments in the game onto search costs, aspiration levels, satisficing versus optimizing, and the difference between choices made with and without knowledge of the distribution. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, students treating the exercise as pure luck, or finding ways to game the leaderboard — and how to handle each failure. End with an honest note on what the card game cannot capture about real satisficing, such as how real-world aspirations become sticky and emotionally loaded, and what I should add in discussion to fill the gap.
