I'm a high school student in a seminar that mixes physics with philosophy of science, and over the semester I've settled into a policy about thought experiments. I will treat a thought experiment as a reason to believe a claim only if I can see how the lesson of the scenario could, in principle, be recovered by a real experiment or a clean formal derivation. If a scenario only supplies a vivid picture or a strong intuition — say, what someone would feel inside a windowless elevator, or what Maxwell's demon could sort without expending energy — I treat it as a device for making an argument memorable, not as an argument that should carry weight on its own.

Last week we discussed Einstein's elevator. My classmates took the fact that an observer cannot tell uniform acceleration from a gravitational field while inside the elevator as a reason to think the two situations are physically equivalent. I objected that the scenario shows only that they are indistinguishable from the inside, and that indistinguishability in a pictured example is not yet a physical principle. My teacher replied that I was setting the bar too high, and that some thought experiments earn their keep by reorganizing our concepts before a real experiment becomes available. That has made me worry that my rule might be a bias against theoretical reasoning dressed up as caution.

Is my policy defensible? Under what conditions do scientific thought experiments actually yield knowledge rather than merely vivid intuitions, and under what conditions should they be demoted to rhetoric? Could my rule be restated so that it keeps the protection without discarding the legitimate work thought experiments do? Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
