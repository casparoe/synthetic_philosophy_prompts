Consider an Ubuntu-inspired relational account of personhood, on which a human being does not automatically count as a person. Rather, one becomes a person through other people, and personhood can be achieved, sustained, diminished, or lost depending on the state of one's relationships. For this exercise, let the account rest on three criteria:

1. Recognition: the individual is recognized by some community as one of its own, with a name, a history, and a place in a network of care and obligation.
2. Participation: the individual actively takes part in mutual relationships, responding to others, being shaped by them over time, and bearing small obligations toward them, rather than being merely a passive object of others' concern.
3. Self-understanding: the individual conceives of itself as one person among others, so that its identity is partly constituted by its relationships.

Now consider the following case. A retired schoolteacher in her mid-seventies, after the death of her partner, withdraws to a remote house and lives there almost entirely alone for thirty years. She is not ill, deluded, or hostile; her groceries are delivered, her telephone goes unanswered, and the letters of former students and neighbors accumulate unread. She keeps a garden, reads, and writes a long diary in the form of an ongoing letter to her dead partner, in which she recounts her days, argues with her, and consults her about decisions. The townspeople still speak of her, remember her name, and regard her as one of their own, but this recognition is now entirely one-sided. When a nephew eventually visits, she receives him politely and remembers him, but she treats the visit simply as an interruption.

Does she still qualify as a person under the three criteria, and to what degree? Work through the criteria one by one, and where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say so explicitly. Then consider whether the case exposes a defect in the criteria as stated: for example, whether they need to distinguish the capacity for relationship from its current exercise, or whether a remembered or internally sustained relationship can do the same relational work as a living one.
