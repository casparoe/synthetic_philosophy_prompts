I've been teaching a short course on Hellenistic ethics, and after a class on the ideal of apatheia, freedom from disturbance, a student reconstructed an argument that has been nagging at me ever since. Each step looked unassailable while she was laying it out, but the conclusion seems to prove too much, and I cannot decide where the reasoning goes wrong, or even whether it goes wrong after all. I want a precise diagnosis rather than the usual objections.

The argument, numbered as she gave it:

(1) For any particular episode of distress — a night of anxiety, a bout of grief, a flare of anger at an insult — the possible life exactly like yours except without that episode is, other things equal, better for you. In each instance, distress is a harm.

(2) Much of the distress involved in caring about fragile things — a person who may die or leave, a career that may collapse, a public standing that may be destroyed — is useless distress: the feeling itself does nothing to steady the fragile thing. Each such episode is therefore a cost you incur simply for caring, not a price paid for any change in the world.

(3) If each such episode is a cost, and if there is a policy that removes a whole class of those costs while sacrificing nothing else, then you should adopt that policy.

(4) A policy of not caring about fragile things except where caring is useful purely as an instrument to some further end would remove the whole class of costs while sacrificing nothing else, since the fragile things themselves would remain as they are and only your relation to them would change.

(5) Therefore you should adopt that policy: love only to the extent that love is instrumentally useful, avoid committing yourself to projects whose failure you could not take lightly, and treat reputation and politics as indifferent except for their effects.

(6) But that conclusion cannot be right. Paying for tranquility by giving up exactly the attachments that seem to make a life worth living looks less like wisdom than like a quiet kind of self-abandonment; a life shaped by (5) would be poorer, not richer, than one that risks real losses.

(7) So at least one of the earlier steps must be false, or the inference from them must fail.

Where does the argument go wrong? If you think it does go wrong, name the exact step or inference, explain why it looked safe, and spell out what rejecting it costs elsewhere. If you think it is actually valid, say which conclusion I would have to accept and which of the premises I would then have to surrender. For what it is worth, I would be most reluctant to give up (1) and (6). Please answer in no more than 650 words.
