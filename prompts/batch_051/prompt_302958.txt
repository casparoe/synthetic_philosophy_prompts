I've been rereading the first edition of Darwin's Origin of Species (gutenberg, because I'm cheap), and I got stuck on a sentence in Chapter 3, "Struggle for Existence," that I'd never really noticed:

"The face of Nature may be compared to a yielding surface, with ten thousand sharp wedges packed close together and driven inwards by incessant blows, sometimes one wedge being struck, and then another with greater force."

What grabbed me is that this reads like a pretheoretic version of the whole "evolution is a stupid optimizer" discussion from the rationalist blogosphere. The wedges don't know where the soft spots are, nobody's representing the whole surface, and the system just hammers whatever happens to be there. So I tried to turn the intuition into an argument, and now I cant tell whether it's sound or whether I've smuggled something in.

(1) If a process searches a space by applying a fixed local update rule—no lookahead, no global model of the space, just the local signal of "how well am I doing right here"—then it's a stupid optimizer.
(2) Natural selection is that kind of process: in Darwin's simile, the surface yields to whichever wedges happen to take the hardest local blows, with no one and nothing surveying the whole surface.
(3) So natural selection is a stupid optimizer.
(4) Gradient descent as it's actually used in ML is also that kind of process: it steps in the direction of the local gradient of the loss function, with no lookahead and no model of the loss landscape.
(5) So gradient descent is a stupid optimizer in the same sense.
(6) Therefore the standard contrast—natural selection is the blind dumb optimizer while gradient descent is a clever principled one—collapses.

I half-suspect (1) is doing something sneaky, maybe sliding from "optimizer" in the engineering sense to "optimizer" in the evolutionary sense, and I also suspect (2) over-reads Darwin, since the wedge sentence is about competition among individuals rather than selection across generations. But maybe the argument is just valid and the conclusion is unwelcome. So: is the argument valid, and if not, where exactly does it break? Take each step, and if a step is false, give the strongest true reading of it and say whether the argument still goes through. And if it does go through, what should I actually conclude about the natural-selection/gradient-descent analogy—does this show gradient descent has nothing useful to learn from natural selection, or only that the two share one property? Please respond in American English.
