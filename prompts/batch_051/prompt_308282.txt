Okay, so I'm taking a course on action theory and we spent a week on Buridan's ass, and I've got a home-made idea I want checked before I put it in a paper. I've been calling it the "tie-license." The thought is that a perfectly symmetrical choice isnt a problem about first-order reasons at all; it's a problem about second-order permissions. An agent facing two identical options doesn't need a reason to prefer one of them — the symmetry rules that out by definition — they need a standing policy that says "when first-order preferences are tied, arbitrary selection is permissible." The ass doesn't starve because it lacks a reason to choose; it starves because it conflates "no reason to prefer this one" with "no permission to act," and so it lets a first-order tie veto a second-order policy it otherwise holds. On my view tie-breaking is a competence or a policy, not a property the options could have. I think this disolves the puzzle, but I'm worried I've just relabeled the old picking-versus-choosing distinction and added the word "license" to it. Stress-test this honestly: is it actually doing any work, or does it just move the bump one level up, since the tie-license itself is presumably one policy among several and the ass could freeze over which of those to adopt? Give me the two or three strongest objections and tell me which, if any, is fatal rather than just awkward.
