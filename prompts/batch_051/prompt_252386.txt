I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've picked up a habit from the Fitch knowability literature that I can't tell is a genuine epistemic rule or just a verificationist tic. You know the move: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is known — because a truth of the form "p and nobody knows p" can't itself be known without contradiction. So here's the policy I've caught myself using. Whenever someone says something like "there are truths we'll never know" or "some facts are in priciple undiscoverable," I don't treat it as a claim about how the world is. I treat it as a failed speech act. I ask them to give me a single example. When they can't — or when the example they give is something they've just stated, which seems to undercut the point — I discount the original claim entirely. I've done this with historical questions too: someone says "we'll never know why the Bronze Age collapse happened," and I immediately go to "name one fact about it that's unknowable rather than just currently unknown," and if they can't, I feel licensed to dismiss the whole thing. But I suspect I'm conflating "can't be known" with "can't be pointed to without knowing it," and maybe smuggling in a verificationist principle through the back door. Is this policy defensible? If there are genuinely unknowable truths, how would I distinguish them from truths nobody happens to know yet — and does the Fitch argument actually rule out anything here, or just show that particular conjunctions are unknowable? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble, no restating the question.
