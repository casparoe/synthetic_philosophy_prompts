Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason, defended in The Methods of Ethics (1874), is the thesis that rational egoism and utilitarianism each seem to rest on compelling rational intuitions, yet can issue incompatible injunctions, and that practical reason contains no deeper principle with which to adjudicate between them. The dilemma is standardly understood to presuppose two rival standpoints: the agent's own good and the good of all persons considered impartially, with the boundary between one person and another treated as deep and fundamental.

Now suppose that reductionism about personal identity is correct. This later development in the metaphysics of persons, associated most prominently with Derek Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984), holds that there are no separately existing selves. A person is constituted by overlapping relations of psychological and physical continuity among person-stages, and whether a given chain of such stages amounts to one life or several is, at the margins, partly a conventional or empty question. If this is right, my concern for my own future self is already a concern that reaches across a boundary: it is directed at stages to which I am connected in certain ways and to certain degrees, rather than at a single enduring subject. But impartial concern for other people is likewise a concern directed at stages to which I am connected in different ways, or to a lesser degree.

Does Sidgwick's dualism survive this development? Please address, at minimum, the following. First, does reductionism dissolve rational egoism by showing that the very idea of an agent's own good, taken as a unified object of concern, depends on a fiction of a separately existing self? Second, does it instead dissolve the sharp contrast between prudence and benevolence, so that utilitarianism loses a genuine rival? Third, does the conflict instead reappear at the level of person-stages, as a tension between the claims of one's present stage and the distributed claims of all stages, one's own included? Identify exactly which premise of Sidgwick's argument is under pressure, and give a clear verdict on whether the dualism is dead, in need of repair, or substantially unaffected.
