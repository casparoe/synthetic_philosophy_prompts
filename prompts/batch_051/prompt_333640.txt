I've come to accept a make-believe account of our emotional responses to fiction: when we are moved by a character we know does not exist, the state we are in is not an ordinary emotion directed at that nonexistent person, but a kind of attenuated, quasi- or make-believe emotion, perhaps consisting in imagined emotion or in emotion-like states whose objects are the thought contents rather than the world. In his essay Of Tragedy, Hume notices that spectators seek out and enjoy representations that arouse terror, pity, and sorrow, even though these same passions are disagreeable when their objects are real; that observation seems to me to rule out any view that takes fictional emotion to be ordinary emotion triggered by a false belief. Tell me what would count against this make-believe account. I want a map of its exposure, not just the standard objection that people physically startle or cry. Which actual and possible cases, which features of ordinary emotion such as physiology, appraisal, action tendency, phenomenology, and memory, and which findings from neighboring areas such as attention or imaginative resistance would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which would its defenders dismiss as describing a different phenomenon? What does the view predict that rival accounts such as thought theory or a suspension-of-disbelief account do not? If the position ends up being hard to falsify because defenders can always reclassify recalcitrant responses as make-believe too, say whether that insulation is a problem or a feature.
