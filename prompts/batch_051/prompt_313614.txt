Take the St Petersburg game and change one thing. A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if the first heads is on the nth flip, you don't get 2^n. You recieve (−1)^(n−1) × 2^n / n dollars: you win that amount when n is odd and pay it when n is even. The amounts still blow up, but the sign alternates and the flip number dampens them. Run the expected-value calculation term by term in the natural order: what number comes out? Is that number actually the fair price, or does summing in a different order change it? Do the usual fixes for St Petersburg—bounded utility, a finite bankroll, ignoring tiny probabilities—still give a determinate value here, or does the alternating sign make a new kind of problem for expected utility theory? And would you pay a dollar to play it? I want to know what the standard analysis says once the signs start flipping.
