I gather that what I have been struggling with is sometimes called the boundary problem — the question of who belongs to the demos, the body of people entitled to take part in a state's collective decisions. I have been working through it on my own, and I have talked myself into a conclusion that feels too strong, which makes me suspect I have made a mistake somewhere. I would like to know whether the argument below is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails first and why.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) For a state's decisions to be binding on a person in a way that is legitimate, that person must have had a real opportunity to take part in making them.

(2) A decision about where to draw the boundary of the electorate is binding on everyone who stands under the state's authority, including people the boundary leaves out, such as resident noncitizens, older minors, and the residents of an annexed territory. The decision determines whether they will have any share in the state's collective choices or only be subject to them.

(3) Therefore, by (1), the people whom the boundary leaves out ought to have a real opportunity to take part in the boundary-setting decision itself.

(4) But they cannot take part in that decision while they stand outside the electorate, because the decision about who is to be included is made by those who are already included.

(5) Every actual boundary-setting decision therefore binds some people whose exclusion gives them no opportunity to take part in it, and so every such decision is illegitimate with respect to those people.

(6) If the very act that constitutes an electorate is illegitimate, the later decisions of that electorate cannot repair or inherit their legitimacy from it. A body cannot draw its authority from an act that was illegitimate in constituting it.

(7) Therefore no actual electorate, except one that already includes every person subject to its decisions, has a democratically legitimate boundary.

That conclusion seems too strong, because it appears to rule out nearly every real democracy as it actually exists. But I cannot locate the error. Is the argument valid? If it is, what exactly does it establish? If it is not, please identify the specific step or steps that fail, explain how the relevant term or principle is being used ambiguously or too broadly at that point, and say whether the underlying worry about boundary-setting can be restated in a way that avoids the error. Please answer in American English.
