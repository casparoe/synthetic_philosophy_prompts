There's a classic argument that self-deception is impossible: to deceive yourself you'd have to know the truth and also get yourself to believe the opposite, which would mean both knowing and not knowing the same thing at the same time. If you really know it, the comforting belief can't be genuinly held; if the comforting belief is genuinely held, you're not deceived, you're just wrong. Now suppose real self-deception looks less like that and more like this: someone suspects bad news, avoids the test results, asks only the questions that invite reassuring answers, holds denials to a much lower bar than they would for anything else, and after months is sincerely convinced things are fine. There's no single moment in that story where they both know and don't know. Does the impossibility argument survive this, or does it only kill a straw version of the thing? I want to know which specific premise the messy process actually challenges, and whether there's a repaired version of self-deception that keeps what the original argument was worried about while dropping the part that made it look impossible.
