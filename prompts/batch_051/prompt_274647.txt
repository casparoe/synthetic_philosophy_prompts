Consider a small invented domain: a heritage tabletop dexterity game called Flickstep. Flickstep is played on a stepped wooden board with three raised shelves and a trough at the foot. A player flicks a brass disc so that it slides up the steps and comes to rest on a shelf; the lowest shelf scores one point, the middle shelf two, and the highest shelf three. A disc that falls into the trough scores nothing, and a disc that strikes an opponent's disc sends the struck disc back to the starting line. Four rounds are played, and the highest total wins. I will call this the received rules.

I have considered several later games, variants, and copies that are related to Flickstep in different ways. I want you to find the principle, if there is one, behind my verdicts about which of them are "the same game" as Flickstep.

Here are my verdicts.

(a) Faithful copy. A cabinetmaker in a different town builds a board of the same dimensions from the same published plans, using a different wood, and the players there follow the received rules exactly. Verdict: obviously the same game. Anyone who called it merely Flickstep-like would be drawing a distinction without a difference.

(b) Digital port. A phone game implements the received rules exactly, with virtual discs and a swipe in place of a flick. I do call it Flickstep, and I would tell a friend to try "Flickstep on the phone", though I would not say I had handled a Flickstep board. Verdict: the same game in a different medium.

(c) Scoring tweak. A widely adopted club variant drops the top shelf's value from three points to two, on the ground that always aiming for the top shelf makes play boring; everything else follows the received rules. Verdict: still Flickstep. If a rulebook called this variant Flickstep, I would not object.

(d) Repurposed board. A group of Flickstep players keeps the board and discs but changes the object of play: players now race to knock their own disc off the far end, and the shelves function only as obstacles. Verdict: not Flickstep at all, even though it uses Flickstep equipment and was devised by Flickstep players.

(e) Independent twin. A game from another country, with no historical contact with Flickstep, turns out to have the same board layout and the same received rules. Verdict: not Flickstep. It is a distinct game that happens to be rule-identical; an indistinguishable rules printout would not make it the same game.

(f) Drifted descendant. One lineage has revised the rules every year for a century. The current game has a different number of rounds, a smaller disc, changed scoring values, and different striking rules; but it is still played on a stepped board and still consists in flicking a disc so that it comes to rest on a shelf for points. The lineage has never stopped calling it Flickstep. Verdict: still Flickstep, the same continuous practice, even though a stranger shown only the current rulebook might not recognise it.

(g) Copycat rebranding. A company markets a new tabletop game under the name Flickstep that keeps only the broad idea of flicking counters onto raised zones for points, on a different board, with added cards. Within a few years most regular players use "Flickstep" for this newer game and call the original "Old Flickstep". Verdict: I now use "Flickstep" for the newer game too; the term has slipped, and the old game has become Old Flickstep.

Two pairs of verdicts bother me. (d) and (f) both involve continuous descent from the original game, yet I insist that one is Flickstep and the other is not. (e) and (g) seem to point in opposite directions about whether historical connection matters at all.

Please give me the best principle you can find, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, that captures these verdicts. For each principle, say which of my verdicts it captures and which it forces me to abandon, and why. Then apply it to these further cases:

(h) A shop sells a "Flickstep kit" containing a standard board, discs, and the received rules, along with a deck of cards implementing three historical variant rules. Is the kit a Flickstep set?

(i) Two Flickstep lineages have drifted far enough that their current rules cannot be played against one another, though each has been called Flickstep for generations. Are both Flickstep?

(j) A set of rules for an exact copy of the received game is published under the name Ledge-run, with no mention of Flickstep. Is Ledge-run Flickstep under another name?

If there is a simple principle that handles eight of my verdicts at the price of overturning one or two, I would rather have that than a baroque principle that saves every verdict by adding a clause for each case. State what you take the term "same game" in this domain to be tracking at bottom: historical continuity, a structural or functional core shared with the original, current usage, or some weighted combination. Please write the answer in American English.
