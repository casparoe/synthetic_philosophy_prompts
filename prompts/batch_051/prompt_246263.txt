A friend of mine thinks carefully about personal identity and has, over years of conversation, converged on a stable set of verdicts about particular cases. She holds that a person who undergoes deep general anesthesia and wakes up is straightforwardly the same person as before; that a person whose whole body below the neck is replaced over a year with functionally equivalent prosthetics survives the process; that a person who steps into a destructive teletransporter, which scans the brain, destroys the body, and builds an indistinguishable duplicate elsewhere, does not survive — the duplicate is someone new; and that when the machine builds the duplicate without destroying the original, the original survives and the duplicate is a distinct person from the moment it is created.

The new case she has so far refused to pronounce on is this: over several years, each of a patient's neurons is replaced one at a time with a synthetic device that reproduces exactly that neuron's input–output behavior, each device being tested and integrated before the next neuron is replaced. The patient is awake for much of the process and reports no interruption in her sense of being herself; at the end her memory, personality, and skills are intact, but no biological neurons remain.

Predict what she should say about the new case if she is being consistent, offering your best guess with reasons. Identify the principle that best fits her earlier verdicts, any other plausible principle that fits those verdicts equally well and what it would predict here, and the further case that would cleanly decide between the two.
