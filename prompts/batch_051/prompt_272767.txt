I spent most of my career in statistical thermodynamics, and only since retiring have I worked through the philosophical literature on reduction and emergence. I have arrived at a distinction I have begun calling the **surplus criterion**, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it is coherent, whether it is already someone else's view under another name, and where it breaks.

Here is the idea. Fix a base description B — say, the microphysical description of a system, or a lower-level chemical description — and a higher-level description H (temperature and pressure, an enzyme's active-site description, a cell type, a mental state). By a *prediction* I mean a conditional claim, licensed by the description, about observable behavior under specified interventions, within a stated tolerance and time horizon. Let P(B) be the set of predictions that can be extracted from B given some finite computational budget — simulation time, memory, rounds of coarse-graining, and the like. Let P(H) be the set of predictions that H licenses. Then:

- H is *redundant* with respect to B at a given budget when P(H) is contained in P(B): everything H lets you say, B lets you say too, for no more computation than one is willing to pay.
- H has a *surplus* when P(H) contains predictions that P(B) at that budget does not — predictions that, so far as the base description goes, would have to be gotten by running the system rather than by reasoning with the higher-level description.
- A property described by H is *emergent*, in my sense, to the extent that its surplus is *budget-stable*: the surplus does not disappear as the computational budget applied to the base description increases. I call it weak emergence when the surplus closes only at budgets that grow so fast with system size that the closure is practically meaningless, and strong emergence when no finite budget closes it.

The central claim is that this restates, in a form a scientist can use, what the strong/weak emergence debate is really tracking: not whether higher-level talk is convenient, but whether the higher-level description licenses predictions that the lower-level description fails to license no matter how much computation is thrown at it. Redundancy is reduction in good order. Budget-stable surplus is what ineliminability amounts to.

Two examples. The thermodynamic properties of an ideal gas are redundant: temperature and pressure predictions are recovered from kinetic theory with modest computation. A turbulent flow described by the Navier–Stokes equations sits less clearly: the higher-level description may license predictions about large-scale structures that a molecular description does not license at any feasible budget, though I suspect the surplus would close at some enormous but finite budget — weak emergence. A mental state would be strongly emergent only if no finite computation over the underlying physics could recover the predictions that a mental-state description licenses, for instance about what someone will say next.

I am aware this sounds as though it may collapse into epistemic emergence, since everything turns on what counts as a budget and what counts as licensed. I am also aware that without a principled measure of computational budget, "weak" and "strong" may not be well-defined, and that "no finite budget closes it" may be unverifiable. Those are exactly the weaknesses I want pressure-tested.

Please assess the view in prose: reconstruct the criterion in its strongest form, say what it gets right, name the nearest existing position in the emergence literature (in a phrase or two, without turning this into a reading list), and then give the three strongest objections, indicating whether any is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. - "nearest_existing_position": a short string naming the closest position or distinction you know of.
- "strongest_objections": an array of three objects, each with fields "objection" (a string) and "severity" (one of "fatal", "serious", "moderate", "minor").
- "fatal_or_fixable": a string stating which objection, if any, is fatal, and whether the criterion can be repaired or should be abandoned.
