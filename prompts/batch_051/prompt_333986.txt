I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we keep going around in circles about whether the brain is a computer. One guy in my section keeps insisting that the brain is literally a computer, not a metaphor, but when I push him on what that's supposed to mean he falls back on neurons summing inputs and firing, which honestly just sounds like the brain is describable in computational terms, not that it actually is one. I want to know whether there's any empirical test that could seperate his view from the much weaker claim that the brain is merely simulable by a computer. After all, almost any physical system you idealize enough can be simulated by a Turing machine, so "you can model it computationally" can't be the load-bearing claim.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take the claim "the brain literally performs computations over representations" and design the strongest test or package of observations you can for it. You could try to operationalize computation as the manipulation of representational vehicles in a way that's sensitive only to their formal properties rather than their physical ones, or look for decodable, recombineable symbols and rule-governed transitions, or something else entirely—but make it concrete. What would you measure or intervene on, what result would favor literal computationalism, what result would favor the "merely simulable" reading, and what residual part of the debate would no test of this kind settle (like whether the computational description is intrinsic to the system or something we project onto it, or whether any of this matters for explaining consciousness)? Basically I'm trying to figure out how much of this fight is empirical and how much is just a disagreement about what "computer" ought to mean in the first place.
