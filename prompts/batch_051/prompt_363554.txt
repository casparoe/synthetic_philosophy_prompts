I teach a course on medieval philosophy, and in our discussion of Boethius's attempt to reconcile divine knowledge with human freedom in the Consolation of Philosophy, a student reconstructed the following argument, which he offered as the problem Boethius really has to solve. I would like to go through it line by line in the next seminar, and I want a precise diagnosis before I put it on the board, since I half-suspect a trick but do not want to mark it up hastily. The argument, as he wrote it:

(1) If God foreknows that Socrates will sit tomorrow, then it is true that Socrates will sit tomorrow.

(2) If it is true that Socrates will sit tomorrow, then it is necessary that Socrates will sit tomorrow; for otherwise a true proposition about the future could turn out false.

(3) Therefore, if God foreknows that Socrates will sit tomorrow, it is necessary that Socrates will sit tomorrow.

(4) If it is necessary that Socrates will sit, then Socrates cannot do otherwise than sit.

(5) If a person cannot do otherwise than what she in fact does, then she does not do it freely.

(6) Therefore, if God foreknows that Socrates will sit, Socrates does not sit freely.

(7) God foreknows every future human action.

(8) Therefore, no future human action is free.

Does this argument succeed in establishing its conclusion? If not, exactly which numbered step fails and what is the precise fault in it? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state whether the argument succeeds. The remaining four should give the precise reasons, keyed to the numbered steps, drawing on Boethius's distinction between simple necessity and conditional necessity and on his account of God's knowledge as an eternal, all-at-once vision rather than a temporally prior foresight, wherever these genuinely locate the fault.
