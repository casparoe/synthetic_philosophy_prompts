A sociologist makes the following claim: peoples have national characters — not merely shared institutions, values, or self-conceptions, but durable, transmitted, group-level differences in ordinary behavioral dispositions, such as how readily members of the group approach strangers, tolerate disorder, queue, interrupt, or extend trust. Her colleague replies that this is a cognitive illusion: apparent national differences in everyday behavior are fully explained by situation and incentive — differences in law, wealth, density, climate, language, and the selectivity of whom one meets — plus a standing human tendency to perceive character where there is only context and stage-setting.

Treat this as a question about what evidence could in principle settle the matter, rather than a debate settled by citing authorities. You may design a research program with realistic instruments: surveys, naturalistic observation, cross-border or migration comparisons, longitudinal measurement, behavioral games, administrative records, or anything else that could actually be run. Do not rely on citations; build the tests yourself from the ground up.

In your answer, first propose the two or three most diagnostic studies you can, and explain precisely how each one separates a shared disposition from situation and incentive. Give the skeptic the strongest version of the case: each study should be designed to rule out at least three confounds — behavior produced by institutions, sorting or migration of already-different people into nations, and the possibility that the observer's stereotype does the work of noticing a pattern. Second, say what pattern of results, including realistic effect sizes and directions of difference, would favor each side, and why. Third, address the interpretation question directly: if your results came back positive, would that vindicate the phrase "national character," or only the weaker claim that national populations differ on average on certain measured behaviors? State what further evidence would be needed to move from one to the other, and whether any of it could actually be obtained. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your proposed tests and their interpretation, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure about.
