I'm helping teach an undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and last week I found myself fixated on the color phi phenomenon after running a classical demonstration on myself and a classmate. A dot flashes in one position with one color, say red, and then a second dot flashes nearby with a different color, say green. We both reported seeing a single dot travel smoothly from the first position to the second and change from red to green roughly halfway along the path, and we both felt no awareness whatsoever of revision or filling-in. The puzzle is that the early part of the trajectory and the mid-path color change are experienced as if they had already been "seen" before the second dot appeared, yet only the second dot supplies the information that specifies the second half of the trajectory and the color it should become. The brain has evidently used later information to determine what the earlier phase of the experience was like.

Philosophers usually mark out the space with two hypotheses and a sceptic's option. On the Orwellian account, you really do first experience the red dot as red, and the later green information then revises or overwrites your memory of that first experience, so your final report is the moving, color-changing dot. On the Stalinesque account, the green information is woven in before anything reaches consciousness at all: you never have a standalone red-dot experience, and the moving, color-changing dot is the only conscious content there ever was. A third family of views denies that the question has a determinate answer, because it presumes a definite moment at which a content "becomes conscious"; on this view that moment is an artifact of a mistaken picture of the mind, not something to be discovered. I want a teaching instrument that sorts students into whichever of these structural pictures their own case judgments commit them to, without ever asking them to pick a theory by name.

Here is the task. Act as a philosopher of mind who also writes unusually clear teaching materials. Begin by brainstorming concrete cases and candidate distinctions, then think carefully about which combinations of answers are coherent before attaching any labels to them. Then produce either a decision tree or a "which view do you hold?" quiz with a scoring key, whichever you judge better at separating the positions for first-time students. Include at least these leaves: Orwellian memory revision, Stalinesque pre-conscious integration, no-fact-of-the-matter (multiple-drafts-style), and a fourth substantive position of your own choosing that earns its place (candidates might include a methodologically quietist view or a "there is a fact but only neuroscience can find it" view). Keep the instrument compact: a scored quiz should have six to eight questions, and a decision tree should have no more than six branching questions, in either case landing in four to six leaves. The questions should be about concrete cases — the color phi itself, flash-lag (where a briefly flashed item appears to lag behind a moving item it is aligned with), apparent-motion demonstrations, backward masking (where a later stimulus degrades or erases reportability of an earlier one), and the like — and should be answerable from the student's own judgments about what seems right, without knowing the labels. Do not include any question of the form "which of these theories do you accept?" Build the instrument from the logical space first: questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the named positions located inside that space, with any combination of answers that is logically open but unoccupied by any real position listed and diagnosed in one sentence (is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?). Flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate rather than pretending a question splits them.

Return the instrument as a JSON object with the following fields:

- 'format': either "tree" or "quiz".
- 'preamble': a short paragraph the student reads before answering.
- 'questions': a list of objects, each with 'id', 'text', and 'options'; each option has 'label', 'response' (the wording the student assents to), and, depending on the format, 'leads_to' (the id of the next node, for a tree) or 'points' (an object mapping leaf name to score, for a quiz).
- 'leaves': a list of objects, each with 'name', 'one_sentence_summary', and 'diagnostic_answer_pattern'.
- 'scoring_key': for a quiz, the rule that maps total points to a leaf; for a tree, an empty object.
- 'unoccupied_combinations': a list of objects with 'answer_pattern' and 'one_sentence_diagnosis'.
- 'flagged_inseparables': a list of objects with 'position_a', 'position_b', and 'why_this_instrument_cannot_split_them'.
- 'expected_result': one short paragraph on which leaf you expect undergraduates to land in most often, and why.

Give no bibliography, reading list, or citations: the instrument should stand on the cases and distinctions alone.
