I am a PhD student in decision theory, working on the foundations of the Kelly criterion and long-run growth. I have been trying to give a principled account of why almost no one bets full Kelly, and why fractional Kelly persists even though expected log-growth maximization alone seems not to explain it. I have developed a criterion I call *geometric quantile dominance* (GQD). Here is the idea.

For an i.i.d. favorable bet and a fixed allocation f, let W_n be wealth after n trials, and let g_n = (1/n) log(W_n / W_0) be the realized per-period log growth. Standard Kelly selects the allocation maximizing E[g_n], which for a fixed f is the same at every horizon. I want to say that a rational bettor should not maximize a single scalar at all, but should compare the whole distribution of g_n over every finite horizon. Definition: allocation f *horizon-dominates* allocation h just in case, for every finite n and every quantile q in (0,1), the q-th quantile of g_n under f is at least as large as the q-th quantile of g_n under h. An allocation is *admissible* just in case no other allocation horizon-dominates it. My claim is that this captures what fractional Kelly is doing: full Kelly does not dominate fractional Kelly, because at moderate horizons its low quantiles of g_n are worse (larger drawdowns), while its central and high quantiles are better. Conversely, allocations that are plainly worse than Kelly are dominated. The criterion is thus a unanimity rule over all quantiles and all finite horizons, and it yields a set of admissible allocations from which fractional Kelly cannot be excluded.

I know this sits close to stochastic dominance, and I suspect that is either the main weakness or the whole point. I would like an honest assessment. Specifically: what is the strongest version of my criterion? Is admissibility here nearly vacuous, and if so, what is left of the idea? Does GQD actually recommend fractional Kelly, or does it merely fail to rule it out? And is it a rival to expected utility, or just a redescription of quantile-based risk measures? Give me the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
