Was reading about algorithmic randomness lately and I can't decide if the parallel I noticed is a real structure or just me pattern-matching. The standard definition of a random infinite sequence is that no computable betting strategy can win an unbounded amount off it; the incompressibility version says prefixes of the sequence have no significantly shorter descriptions than themselves. Now consider the efficient market hypothesis: a market is efficient when no trading strategy can beat the market on a risk-adjusted basis after costs. Both say "random iff no process can systematically exploit it," and both cash out exploitability as a finite bankroll that turns into more and more money. And both have the same wrinkle, I think: on the randomness side the notion only makes sense for infinite sequences, since any finite string is a prefix of some computable sequence and of some random one, so a finite sample never settles it. On the finance side, a finite price history can always be data-mined into some rule that would have won in-sample. So, deep structural identity or shallow analogy? If I tried to formalize the finance claim as "price sequences are random relative to all practically computable trading rules," where exactly would it break — and would the usual limits-of-arbitrage stuff (noise trader risk, capital constraints) be the thing that breaks it, or does it correspond to some piece of the randomness formalism I'm missing?
