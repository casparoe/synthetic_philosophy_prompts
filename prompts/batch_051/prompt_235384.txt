Question:
Do we owe more to our fellow citizens than to people in other countries? In your answer, discuss at least two ways of defending the claim, identify the strongest objection to each, and say which defense you find most plausible.

Student answer:
I'll defend the common-sense view that compatriots have special claims on one another that outsiders lack. The first way of grounding this is associative: fellow nationals share a language, a political culture, and a common history, and that shared identity itself generates special duties, roughly the way membership in a family generates duties. The main objection to this associative account is that national membership is unchosen — you're born into it, and morality shouldn't bind you through accidents of birth. I think this objection misses the mark, because we also don't choose our families, yet no one doubts that parents owe their own children more than they owe strangers. So the fact that nationality is unchosen cannot by itself show that it grounds no special duties.

The second grounding is reciprocity. The state provides public goods, a legal order, and social cooperation, and I benefit from all of these. Having accepted those benefits, I owe a special return to the fellow participants in the scheme, but not to outsiders, because outsiders are not participating in the particular scheme that benefits me. The strongest objection to this view is that I also benefit from the labor of foreign workers and from a global economy in which many outsiders participate, and if mere benefit-receipt generated special duties, I would owe them in exactly the same way. The only difference between the two cases is that my relation to fellow citizens is backed by a coercive state. But that just swaps the ground from reciprocity to coercion, and it's not clear why being coerced together should create duties rather than impose costs that themselves need to be justified. Given all this, I'm inclined to stick with the associative account as the least bad option, since the voluntarist worry can be defused by the family analogy.
