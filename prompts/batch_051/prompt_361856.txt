I'm teaching a unit on how rational people are supposed to resolve disagreement, and I keep getting stuck on the old Bayesian convergence story: if two people start with different priors but keep updating on the same stream of observations, under fairly weak conditions their posteriors converge and the priors wash out. The whole argument seems to hang on "the same stream of observations." But that premise looks less and less like the world. Most of the observations a person actually gets now—which studies they see, which economic statistics get shown, which anecdotes about a policy go viral in their feed—are chosen by a personalized recommender that is itself learning from their past engagement, which is in part a function of their priors. So the data you get depends on the beliefs you started with, and on the beliefs of the people the platform thinks you resemble.

I want to know whether the washing-out-of-priors argument survives this change, needs repair, or just dies. Specifically: which premise of the convergence argument is actually under pressure—is it the shared-observation assumption, the requirement that everyone knows the likelihood or selection mechanism, the exchangeability setup, something else? Can you restore a weaker version, say convergence to different limits, convergence within algorithmically-created clusters, or convergence in what people could learn if they audited the feed? Or should we stop expecting convergence and start expecting something like polarization as a rational response to genuinely different evidence streams? Be explicit about where your verdict is fragile—what result or consideration would make you say either "actually the theorem already handles this fine" or "no repaired version is worth keeping."

Also, please write in American English.
