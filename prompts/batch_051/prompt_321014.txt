In the debates I keep returning to about whether a company—BP after the Gulf spill, say, or Volkswagen after the emissions scandal—can itself be blamed, as distinct from blaming particular executives, engineers, or shareholders, one analogy is doing a lot of the work: a corporation is like a person. Its defenders use it to conclude that a firm can intend, act, be guilty, and deserve punishment as a single agent; its critics dismiss it as a legal fiction or as shorthand for a network of individuals. I would like that analogy audited rather than merely gestured at. Please give the explicit correspondence: what is supposed to play the role of belief, desire, intention, memory, conscience, and the capacity for remorse or for being the apt target of resentment? Where does the mapping hold, where does it break, and which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion—that the corporation itself is a fit object of blame—rather than being incidental? Which features of persons does the analogy quietly import, such as phenomenal experience, emotional vulnerability, or a body that can actually suffer punishment, and is any of them load-bearing? Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a better analogy is available. Answer in British English.
