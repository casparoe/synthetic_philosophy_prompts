Two people in my rationalist reading group got stuck last night on common knowledge of rationality and its one of those arguments where I can see both sides but can't adjudicate it myself. The dispute was whether 'common knowledge' is a real distinction or just a fancy way of saying everyone knows. Mara said the iterated part matters: you can have a situation where everyone knows the plan, but each person isn't sure the others know that they know, and rational coordination fails — the two-generals / e-mail-game thing. Dev said that in any actual finite situation you only ever have finitely many levels of 'I know that you know,' so the infinite definition is a modeling convenience, not a state anything is actually in. Mara came back that the limit still does work, because the equilibrium set changes discontinuously: with any finite number of ack'd messages there's still no attack, but with actual common knowledge there is, so the idealization marks a real behavioral gap. Dev said that only shows the model isn't robust; in real life the gap is just uncertainty about whether a message got through, and calling it 'lack of common knowledge' just relabels that uncertainty. Mara then brought up Aumann: two Bayesians with a common prior can't agree to disagree if their posteriors are common knowledge, and she claimed that wouldn't go through for mere mutual knowledge. Dev replied that Aumann shows the distinction does negative work: real people agree to disagree all the time, so the interesting explanation is that common priors or the rationality assumptions fail, not that they're missing some iterated epistemic state nobody can actually be in. Mara said fine as a descriptive point, but public announcements are how you make common knowledge, and they change what a group can coordinate on, so it's not just a diagnostic for failed assumptions. Dev said there are no truly public events anyway — any announcement has background doubt about whether everyone heard and interpreted it the same way — so 'public' is just another idealization.

Then say what the whole dispute misses: who's right about what overall, and what distinction or result would need to be added to settle it. I vaguely suspect something about 'almost common knowledge' or approximate common knowledge would complicate both of them, but I can't remember the details.
