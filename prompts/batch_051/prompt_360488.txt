A recent project at work had me implementing tamper-evident storage: checksums flag when a record has been altered, and error-correcting codes reconstruct the original from redundant data. The work has made me think differently about the ethical idea of incorruptibility. There, too, corruption seems to be a kind of motivated alteration—of one's commitments rather than one's data—under pressure, flattery, or self-deception, and a person of integrity is often described as uncorruptible. An analogy suggests itself: conscience as a checksum that detects, after the fact, that a commitment has been altered; remorse and self-examination as an error-correction routine that tries to restore the original from redundant records such as promises made, journals kept, or the testimony of friends. I want to know whether this is a real structural parallel or only a pun on the word integrity. If it is real, what should transfer from data integrity to moral integrity—for instance, that a tamper-evident system is worthless when the attacker can rewrite the log, or that reliable correction requires redundancy wide enough to outvote the corruption? And where precisely does the mapping break: does the software metaphor treat the original self far too rigidly, given that changing one's mind in response to reasons is sometimes what integrity demands rather than what it forbids?
