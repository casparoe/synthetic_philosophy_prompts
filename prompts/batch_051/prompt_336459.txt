I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about character traits, going by gut verdicts on cases rather than by theory. My working hunch is that a virtue only counts as a virtue if it shows up across the situations that call for it, and that a pattern of behaviour tied to a narrow trigger or a passing mood is something weaker than a character trait. But several of my verdicts pull against that hunch, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, holds them together.

Priya is walking a signed contract across town with three minutes to spare when a man drops a pile of folders right in front of her; she steps around him and keeps going. The same week, with an afternoon free, she spends forty-five minutes helping a lost tourist find an obscure repair shop. I don't count the stepping-over against her compassion at all. But Marcus, over three years in a genuinely awful job, became irritable and stopped helping junior collegues — before and after that job he was the most helpful person on any team he joined — and I do count that stretch against him, not just against the job. It feels like it reveals a limit to how deep his considerateness went, not like a situation anyone would cave under. Elena is reliably warm and attentive with clients in sales meetings, and reliably cold and sharp with her spouse; I don't think she's a warm person — the warmth is tied to the sales role, and I wouldn't call it a character trait. Sam gives to every charity that mails him or rattles a can under his nose, but never once seeks out a cause on his own, and when the appeals stop he gives nothing; I can't call him generous, though I can't call him stingy either. And I genuinely don't know what to say about a person who is scrupulously honest with her friends but lies routinely to strangers on the phone.

I know there's a line of social psychology — situationism, or what ever it's called — that says these stable traits mostly don't exist and that we're all more like a bundle of situational responses than like the people I keep describing. That doesn't settle anything for me, because I still want to know what my own verdicts add up to, or which of them I should revise. Can you give me a principle that captures the pattern in these cases, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it? I'd then like to know what the principle predicts about the honest-with-friends case and about one further case of your choosing, and I'd like an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Finally: be explicit about what's actually settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this; if a part is contested, don't present one side as the consensus.
