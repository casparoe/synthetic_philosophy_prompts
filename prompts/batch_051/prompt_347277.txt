Been rereading the Copycat chapters in Hofstadter and the Fluid Analogies Research Group's *Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies* (the mid-90s book), and I'm stuck on a passage that lays out conceptual slippage. The idea, as I remember it, is that when the program faces a letter-string analogy like abc to abd and ijk to something, it doesn't lock in a single fixed rule; instead it builds a whole pressure network where the last letter can slip into the next letter, and under some pressures the idea of a letter can slip into the idea of a group of identical letters, or the successor relation can wrap around at the end of the alphabet, with z's successor treated as a. Hofstadter frames it as the mind needing to decide which concepts are allowed to bend, and how far, before the analogy snaps.

Here are my gut answers on a bunch of cases, and they don't obviously cohere:

- abc -> abd; ijk -> ? I say ijl. No slippage needed.
- abc -> abd; xyz -> ? I say xya. Here I'm happy to wrap the alphabet around and treat z's successor as a.
- abc -> abd; kji -> ? I say kjj, but only barely; there's a real pull toward lji, because kji runs backward and the position that got changed in abc feels like the run's end, which in kji is the front.
- abc -> abd; mmnn -> ? I say mmoo, treating the final nn as a block and bumping the whole block one notch.
- abc -> abd; aabbcc -> ? I say aabbcd, bumping just the rightmost c, not the whole cc block — which feels inconsistent with what I just did for mmnn.
- abc -> abd; cba -> ? I say cbb, bumping the rightmost a, and I don't feel any pull to flip the direction.

I'd like to know whether there's a single principle of how far slippage goes that gets all of these, or close. If not, which verdicts should I dump? And what does your account predict for abc -> abd; mrrjjj -> ? I keep going back and forth between mrrjjk and mrrkkk, which I suppose is exactly the same letter-vs-group tension.
