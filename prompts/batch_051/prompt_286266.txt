I have been reading some recent discussions about the long-term future, and I keep noticing a pattern for which I have no precise term. What people call a slippery slope seems to name only the shape of the sequence, not the conceptual puzzle inside it. The pattern runs as follows. A society holds some commitment as central: say, that the suffering of the severely cognitively impaired matters as much as anyone else's, or that certain things, such as criminal guilt or political representation, must never be turned into quantities to be traded or optimised. Over many generations this commitment is relaxed in a series of small steps. Each step is defended in the terms of the previous generation: it removes an inefficiency, harmonises two rules that conflict, or widens an existing exception only slightly. At the time it is taken, nobody is openly repudiating the original commitment; the change looks like a repair, not a rejection. After many such steps, the society ends up somewhere the original generation would have condemned as monstrous, not merely unfamiliar.

Here is the part I cannot place. Compare any two adjacent generations, and the later one seems at least as defensible as the earlier, often more so. But compare the last generation with the first, and by the first generation's own standard the last is clearly worse, worse in the very currency of the original commitment rather than merely different. And there is no single step at which a mistake, if there is one, can be located. Is there an established concept for this configuration? If the nearest concepts in the philosophical literature capture it only imperfectly, please say exactly where each of them falls short, demarcate the phenomenon as precisely as you can, and, if no established term fits, propose one with a definition and boundary cases that separate it from an ordinary slippery slope and from the familiar paradox of the heap. Please ensure your answer is written in British English.
