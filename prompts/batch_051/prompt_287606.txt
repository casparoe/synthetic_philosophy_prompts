I'm presenting a paper next term at a joint philosophy and cognitive science seminar, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet. The paper defends a version of Davidson's claim that metaphor has no metaphorical meaning of its own — that nothing is communicated beyond what the words literally mean. My version states it like this: when someone uses a metaphor, the utterance does not encode a figurative message for the hearer to recover; rather, the literal meaning of the words, together with the situation and shared background, causes the hearer to notice likenesses, and that noticing is a psychological effect of the utterance rather than content the speaker has asserted. My departure from Davidson is that I accept the central finding of conceptual metaphor theory — that much metaphorical thinking is organised by standing mappings, for example running arguments through the frame of physical combat — but I insist those mappings belong to the background that shapes what gets noticed, not to the semantics of the utterance and not to what the speaker has said. The paper's core argument is that everything the conceptual metaphor theorist attributes to a metaphorical message can be explained by literal content plus background-conditioned noticing, and that parsimony therefore favours my account. I expect the hard objections to target that last move, not the familiar anti-Davidson arguments.

The audience is why I am nervous. It will be mostly cognitive linguists and psycholinguists who take conceptual metaphor theory more or less for granted, two philosophers of language inclined towards contextualism, an NLP researcher who builds figurative-language and paraphrase models, and a chair who suspects the whole dispute is verbal. They know the stock examples and will each think I am too deflationary or too concessive in a different direction. I want to know what each of them will actually ask, and what to say back.

Please write the prep sheet in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.

1. The backbone (about 150 words): restate the thesis and identify the one move the entire defence stands or falls on, so I can return to it when an answer is going badly.

2. The objections I will actually get (about 1,500 words): ten of them, ordered by expected damage — the product of how likely each is to be asked and how likely it is to wound — rather than by likelihood alone. For each: name the kind of audience member who asks it; phrase the question as they would, in their own idiom; give a two-minute reply; and add a sentence on where that reply is soft. At least three of the ten should target my departure from Davidson rather than Davidson's original view. End the list with the question I am hoping nobody asks.

3. Concessions to make up front (about 200 words): the points I should grant in the opening minutes so the room does not spend the Q&A correcting a caricature, with a note on which of them I can grant without weakening the paper.

4. Lines to hold (about 150 words): the two or three commitments I should not soften even when it makes me look obstinate, with one sentence apiece on how to defend each compactly.

5. The structural weakness (about 150 words): where the position is genuinely least secure, stated as plainly as I would need to say it to myself, with a short prepared response if a sharp audience member presses it from the right angle.
