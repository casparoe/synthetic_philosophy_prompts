Ever since I rewatched Arrival (the 2016 one) a couple weeks ago I've been trying to put my finger on what the movie is actually saying about the future. My sister, who saw it for the first time, insisted that Louise can't meaningfully choose to have Hannah, because she already knows the child will die young and that Ian will leave her over it; her 'choice' is just acting out a script she's already read. I wanted to argue that her knowledge doesn't force her to do anything, that the knowledge is downstream of the choice, not the other way round, but I couldnt make the distinction stick. I'm putting together a module where I want to pair the film with the old sea-battle problem about future contingents—the puzzle that if it was already true yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, the battle seems unavoidable, and if it was already false, the peace seems unavoidable, so either way the future appears already settled before it happens. I'd like you to read the film through that lens for me.

What I'd want is an actual reading, not just the free-will-versus-determinism summary students usually give. Is the film committed to bivalence for future-tense propositions—that 'Hannah will die' and 'Hannah will not die' are each already true or false before Hannah exists—and if so, does the film run together that semantic point with determinism, or with the darker fatalism my sister was pushing? Walk me through the scenes where future 'memories' arrive out of chronological order, especially the ones where Louise acts on something she knows hasn't happened yet, and say what those scenes contribute to the argument. Also: does the film distinguish knowing the future from causing it, and does that distinction actually rescue her choice?

Finally, I'd like you to be honest about where the film resists the sea-battle framework. I have a hunch the non-linear editing is doing philosophical work the logic puzzle can't, because it makes the future feel already present before the viewer understands what they're seeing, and that this is the film's real answer rather than anything said in dialogue. If the film muddles the issue at points, say so. And the one thing I keep going back and forth on: Aristotle's worry was partly about whether both branches of a future contingency are already one true and one false, but Arrival gives us a character who literally remembers a future that is (in the film) already determinate. Is that a case Aristotle's puzzle just refuses to countenance, or does it actually sharpen the original worry?
