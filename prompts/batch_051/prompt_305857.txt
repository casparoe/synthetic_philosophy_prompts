I spent thirty years in molecular biology and have lately got into philosophy of science, and I keep tripping over the phrase "the genetic code." I know the history: we've called it that since the early sixties, and I used the word my whole career without flinching. But now I'm in a reading group and people keep saying that DNA contains information, that the codon table is literally a code, that a cell "reads" the sequence and "translates" it — that the genome is a blueprint or a recipe. My old physicist instincts want to say: no, it's just chemistry. Base pairing, polymerases, tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases with particular binding preferences. Nothing in the cell is assigning meanings to codons; there's no sender, no receiver, no convention. And yet I can't quite let go of the informational intuition, because the same codon specifies the same amino acid across wildly different organisms, and a point mutation acts very like an error in a text.

So here's what I want to get clear on. Take the claim "DNA literally carries information" — not as a metaphor. Is there any observation or experiment that would bear on whether it's true, rather than just a useful shorthand? I'd like a concrete test, the result that would favour each reading, and an honest account of what even the best test would leave unsettled. For instance, would it make a difference if we found or engineered an organism whose codon assignment differed at one position, the way some ciliates and mitochondria have reassigned stop codons? Or is that just more chemistry? And is there a real difference between showing that DNA has high mutual information with protein sequence and showing that it encodes something in the semantic sense — that some outcome is "about" the sequence? I go back and forth on whether "information" here is a factual claim at all, or whether I'm smuggling in a metaphor and then asking science to confirm it.
