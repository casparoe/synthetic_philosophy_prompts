I'm taking a comparative memory course this semester — we've been reading about Vergangenheitsbewältigung, the German project of working through the Nazi past — and something keeps bugging me. In Germany, denying the Holocaust is a crime, displaying a swastika gets you arrested, and there are no public statues of Nazi leaders left standing. In the American South, you can still buy a Confederate flag at a gas station, there are hundreds of monuments to Confederate generals outside courthouses, and plenty of people describe the Civil War as being about states' rights rather than slavery. Both were defeated regimes that fought, at bottom, to preserve a racial hierarchy, and both were then folded back into the nation. So why is one past treated as an unambiguous evil you're not allowed to commemorate, while the other gets treated as heritage and defended as free speech?

I'm not asking you to take a side in the monument fight. I want the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating these two defeated racial orders differntly — something about how Germany's reckoning was imposed by occupying powers while the South got to write its own history afterward? or that the Holocaust was more total in its aims than slavery? or that the Confederate monuments mostly went up decades later as a deliberate propaganda campaign, so they're not really memory at all? — and then tell me honestly whether that principle survives when you test it against Turkey, where the state runs memory laws in the opposite direction and punishes people for calling the Armenian genocide a genocide, or Japan, where official textbooks keep soft-pedaling what the army did in Nanjing and Korea. If the best prinicple can't handle those too, then maybe the real difference isn't a principle at all but just who won the memory war and got to write the textbook. I'd rather you say that than hand me a principle that only fits the cases I happened to pick.
