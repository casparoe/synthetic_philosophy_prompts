Spoiler warnings are a strange practice when you actually stare at them. Review sites have spoiler tags, friends apologize for letting a twist slip, comment sections get policed so nobody learns "the detective is the killer" before they watch. But notice what most big spoilers actually are: identity claims. The twist is that a is b — the narrator is the murderer, the kindly neighbor is the monster, the two characters turn out to be one person. If those sentences were just like "a is a," nobody would care and nobody would need a warning. So the whole practice seems to presuppose that an identity statement can be genuinely informative, that "a is b" can add something even when you already knew both a and b individually. That's basically Frege's puzzle, right? Here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning, or at least of cognitive value, that spoiler culture presupposes. Does it quietly rule out the view that names are just labels that contribute nothing but their referent — the Millian view — or can a Millian explain why spoilers spoil? Is the practice even coherent: we treat some identities as ruinous and others as not spoilers at all, so is there a principled line or is its just convention? And what would have to change in how we handle spoilers if we went fully Millian? I'd also like you to say where the practice underdetermines the theory, where spoiler culture could be licensed by several different semantics and doesn't actually settle the Frege/Mill fight.
