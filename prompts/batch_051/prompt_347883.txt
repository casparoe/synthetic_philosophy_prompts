In physics, it is common for a theory valid at one energy or length scale—such as hydrodynamics or a low-energy effective field theory—to make extremely accurate predictions even though it is known to break down at much smaller scales, and even though its few parameters can often be fixed by measurements at the same scale without detailed knowledge of the underlying microphysics. Some philosophers take this to show that scales are partly autonomous: the successful higher-level theory is not merely a calculational shortcut that could in principle be replaced by a rigorous derivation from the fundamental theory. What are the five most important implications of this autonomy-of-scales picture for reductionism, understood as the thesis that higher-level sciences are in principle explained by and reducible to fundamental physics? For each implication, indicate whether it undermines reductionism outright or only qualifies the sense in which the reduction is possible "in principle." Present the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
