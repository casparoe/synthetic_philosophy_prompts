I cover the life sciences and the philosophy of science for a general readership, and I am drafting a piece on what it means to describe a biological explanation as mechanistic. My working criteria, adapted informally from philosophers of biology, are these: a mechanism involves (1) identifiable entities, (2) identifiable activities those entities perform, (3) organization of those entities and activities in space and time, and (4) productive continuity, meaning each stage reliably brings about the next from setup conditions to the phenomenon without explanatory gaps. I now want to test these criteria against a case I keep meeting in my evolutionary reporting: natural selection described as the mechanism of adaptation. Does natural selection, understood in its standard modern form — heritable variation among individuals, differential survival and reproduction associated with that variation, and a resulting change in trait frequencies across generations — qualify as a mechanism under my four criteria? Work through the criteria one at a time, and for each criterion say whether natural selection clearly satisfies it, clearly fails it, or leaves the matter underdetermined. Then, as a fifth item, say whether the points of strain show that my criteria are too restrictive, that evolutionary biology is using the term mechanism loosely, or that the disagreement is only verbal. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
