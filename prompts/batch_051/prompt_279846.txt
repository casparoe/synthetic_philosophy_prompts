In Immanuel Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, the universalisability test is standardly separated into two cases. Kant treats the maxim of making a false promise to relieve financial distress as failing because it could not even be conceived as a universal law of nature without contradiction. By contrast, he grants that a maxim of neglecting one's natural talents in favour of comfortable idleness can be conceived as a universal law of nature, but he claims it cannot be willed as such without contradiction. Please argue that this second test, the so-called contradiction-in-willing test, is not a genuinely independent moral criterion: either it secretly reduces to the first, contradiction-in-conception test, or it depends on empirical ends that cannot ground a categorical imperative.
