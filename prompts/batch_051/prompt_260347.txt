I'm an undergraduate in a course on the philosophy of rationality and moral philosophy, and I've become suspicious of one of my own epistemic habits. Whenever a general structural principle—above all the transitivity of "better than," but also the demand that a theory be simpler than a patchwork of qualifications—conflicts with a vivid judgment about a particular case, I almost automatically distrust the case judgment. My policy is that intuitions about concrete cases are noisy evidence: they are shaped by framing, order of presentation, and morally irrelevant features of the example, whereas a principle like transitivity earns a different status because it makes systematic comparison possible at all. So when a case intuition clashes with transitivity, I treat the case intuition as the thing to be explained away, not the principle. A friend in the course follows the opposite policy: for him, principles are only summaries of what we find case by case, so any case judgment is live evidence against the principle, no matter how much structure would have to be rebuilt if the principle fell.

We have been circling one concrete disagreement. He describes three prospects—jobs, lives, or policies—arranged so that, pairwise, each comparison looks right, but together they form a cycle: A seems better than B, B better than C, and C better than A. He insists each pairwise judgment is more credible than the abstract claim that "better than" must be transitive. I reply that a cycle of local case judgments is exactly the sort of glitch that reflecting on the whole might dissolve, and that preserving transitivity is worth reinterpreting the cases even if I cannot yet say where the intuitive error came from. But I worry that I am simply protecting elegant principles from falsification and calling it rigor—perhaps my real preference is for simplicity and coherence, and the policy is just a way of insulating it.

I want to know whether my habit is defensible as it stands, or whether there is a revised version that keeps its benefits without shielding principles from counterexamples. Is there a principled way, from the inside, to decide when a case intuition or a principle intuition should get priority? Are intuitions about cases and intuitions about principles the same kind of evidence, and does the rational response to their conflict depend on the subject matter—value, rationality, or metaphysics—or on whether I can offer a non–ad hoc explanation of the conflicting case? Under what conditions is a structural principle like transitivity genuinely non-negotiable, and when is treating it that way just a refusal to let the evidence speak?
