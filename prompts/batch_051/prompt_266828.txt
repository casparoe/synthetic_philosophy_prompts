Consider Quinn's self-torturer: an agent accepts each step of a long sequence of small trades — one barely perceptible increment of pain for a small payment — and yet, after sufficiently many steps, would pay a large sum to undo the whole sequence. One diagnosis says the agent's pairwise preferences are genuinely intransitive, so that rational choice over these options may cycle. A rival diagnosis says the preference order is perfectly coherent and the puzzle is instead a matter of discrimination: adjacent states differ below the agent's just-noticeable difference, so the agent cannot reliably rank nearby points on a continuous pain scale, and the sequence merely walks them across a vague boundary.

Are those two diagnoses genuinely distinct, and if so, what observation could tell them apart? Design the strongest concrete experiment you can. Specify what would be measured — for example, forced-choice comparisons between non-adjacent as well as adjacent pain levels, repeated presentation of the same pairs, and willingness-to-pay for one-step moves versus the whole package — and state the pattern of results that would favor each diagnosis, including what a mixed or noisy result would show. Then say what a clean result would still fail to settle: in particular, whether any observed cycles are properly located in the agent's preferences or only in their reports, and whether the experiment tests transitivity as an empirical regularity or merely enforces it as an axiom of interpretation.
