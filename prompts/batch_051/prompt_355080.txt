I'm trying to pin down what, if anything, makes the standard protected characteristics hang together — race, sex, disability, age, religion, sexual orientation — instead of being just a messy political list. The criteria I keep stumbling on are these:

(1) Immutability: you can't change it, so it would be cruel to make you.
(2) Irrelevance: it's almost never a legitimate factor in decisions about jobs, housing, credit.
(3) History of subordination: the group marked by the trait has been systematically excluded or kept down.
(4) Salience: the trait is easy to notice and sort by, so it turns into a cheap proxy.
(5) Faultlessness: you didn't choose it and aren't responsible for it, so you shouldn't have to pay for it.
(6) Political powerlessness: the group can't reliably protect itself through ordinary politics.

I keep having the feeling there's a seventh criterion that does work none of these does — something about the way the trait organizes people into ranks, or about what being sorted on that basis communicates about whether you're a full equal. But maybe that's just a foggy intuition. Give me one more candidate criterion that's genuinely distinct from the six I listed, not a rewording, special case, or combination of them. Then tell me honestly whether that criterion — or any single criterion on the list — handles the hard cases better than the others, like someone with a criminal record or someon who's currently a smoker, where the trait is partly changeable and partly chosen but the discrimination still seems to stick. If the honest answer is that no single criterion does all the work and the protected list is a coalition of different principles, say that and show how the pieces would fit.
