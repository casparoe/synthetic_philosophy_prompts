I am a PhD student working on the epistemology of legal proof, and my formal training is mostly in decision theory rather than law, so I have been trying to translate the problems I read about into models I already know. The problem that has me stuck is the one about naked statistical evidence. In the blue bus hypothetical, a pedestrian is hit by a bus and the only thing linking the defendant company to the accident is that it operates four out of every five buses on that route; no witness can identify the bus. In the gatecrasher example, a thousand people attend a ticketed event but only 499 have paid, and the organizer sues a randomly selected attendee on the strength of the resulting 0.501 probability alone. In each case the probability estimate clears the civil preponderance threshold, and yet the standard view is that a finding against this particular defendant would be illegitimate. The same difficulty is usually taken to arise, only more sharply, for proof beyond a reasonable doubt, but the civil examples are enough to show that the problem is not merely one of demandingness. Something beyond high probability seems to be required.

My working hypothesis is that the missing element is best modeled as decision under ambiguity rather than under risk. A market-share statistic gives the factfinder a sharp marginal probability but no distribution over the particular histories that could have produced the accident or the unpaid seat; one can think of each such history as a different prior, with all priors in the set consistent with the statistic but disagreeing about this defendant. On that picture, standards of proof are not thresholds on a single posterior probability but rules for choosing among acts when the prior is only partially specified — something like maxmin expected utility over a set of priors, or a more general alpha-maxmin or smoothing rule. I want to know whether this picture actually earns its keep, and I think the way to find out is to force a translation and inspect the losses.

Take the main positions in the debate, one by one: (a) the threshold view, on which standards of proof simply are posterior-probability cutoffs; (b) the individualized-evidence view, on which proof against a defendant requires evidence that bears on their own conduct rather than merely on a class frequency to which they happen to belong; and (c) the anti-luck view, on which a verdict is not proof when the factfinder could too easily have reached the same verdict in nearby cases where the defendant did not do the act — roughly, a safety or sensitivity condition on legal factfinding. Restate each position in the vocabulary of decisions under ambiguity, and for each say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression at all. Then give me the residue report: does the part that resists translation indicate that the ambiguity framework is getting at something real that the threshold view misses, or does it mark the place where the worry stops being epistemic and becomes something else — for instance, a moral constraint on treating one person as a statistic — that no decision-theoretic translation should be expected to carry? I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a specific verdict about where the translation succeeds and where it fails.
