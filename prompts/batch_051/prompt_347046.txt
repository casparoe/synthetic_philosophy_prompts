I keep running into a review essay that summarizes Cox's theorem like this: 'Cox showed in 1946 that any rational degrees of belief must obey the probability calculus. Starting from the assumption that plausibilities can be represented as real numbers and that the plausibility of a conjunction is a function of the plausibilities of its parts, he derived the sum and product rules. So probability is not just one option among many: it is the unique logic of partial belief, and any alternative—for instance allowing that two hypotheses might be only partially comparable or that belief might be interval-valued—is incoherent. And since rationality requires degrees of belief to be probabilities, Cox's theorem also shows that you must conditionalize on new evidence, on pain of inconsistency.' I'm suspicious of the last step, because the theorem looks like it's about what a coherent state of belief is at a time, not about how you should change it when evidence arrives; am I missing something? I'd like you to go through the summary roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a careful Bayesian would accept, what the theorem actually needs as assumptions, and wether the jump from 'degrees of belief are probabilities' to 'you must conditionalize' goes through. Also, if someone rejects the conclusion, do they have to deny something as basic as 'plausibilities are real numbers', or can they stay perfectly coherent by giving up one of the auxiliary assumptions?
