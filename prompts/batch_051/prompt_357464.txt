You are a decision theorist who specializes in translating formal results into procedures that people without training in decision theory can actually run under time pressure. Before you write the final procedure, brainstorm the most common ways Good's theorem gets misapplied in practice, and use that list to decide which checks the procedure must include. Think step by step.

I am a director of product at a software company. About once a week I decide whether to commission more information before making a decision we will be committed to for at least a year: a usability study, a pricing survey, a six-week engineering prototype, a limited market test, or another round of data analysis. I can roughly estimate what each study would cost in money and delay, but I have no formal decision-theory vocabulary, and my team makes two opposite mistakes: commissioning information that could never change what we would do, and refusing to wait for information that obviously could.

I know Good's theorem only as a slogan: the expected value of new information is never negative, so you should never turn down free information. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what Good's theorem actually says and in the assumptions it depends on, that my team can run in under an hour on a proposal to gather information. The procedure should specify:

1. The questions we ask, in order, before approving, rejecting, or conditionally approving a proposal.
2. What counts as a sufficient answer to each question, and what to do when we cannot get that answer.
3. Where the procedure stops with a no (do not commission the information), where it goes to a yes, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is about values rather than facts.
4. A treatment of costly information. Good's theorem concerns information that is free; every study we buy costs money and delay. The procedure must convert the theorem's logic into a rule for when the price is worth paying, and must mark the exact step at which the conversion stops being a theorem and starts being judgment.
5. Worked applications to three proposals: one the procedure approves, one it rejects, and one where it gives an answer I will not like even though that answer is correct. Make the three proposals different enough to show the procedure doing real work.

The procedure must also handle at least these hard cases, and say how Good's theorem bears on each:

(a) information that cannot change the decision but would make us more accountable or help us justify the decision afterward;
(b) information that might change which options we see as available, rather than only changing our beliefs about options we already have;
(c) information whose true cost is not money or delay but attention, morale, focus, or the risk that plans leak;
(d) decisions we can postpone and revisit rather than make once;
(e) situations where we cannot honestly assign probabilities to the study coming out one way or another.

Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire, and how to detect that the assumptions behind Good's theorem do not hold for the decision in front of us.

Finally, be self-critical. Tell me which part of the procedure you are least confident about, which argument or empirical finding would most change your mind about how the procedure should be built, and where a thoughtful person could reasonably disagree with you about what it should say.

Constraints: the procedure must be usable by people with no formal decision-theory vocabulary; if you need a term such as expected utility, explain it in one plain sentence the first time you use it. Keep the whole answer under about 2,500 words.
