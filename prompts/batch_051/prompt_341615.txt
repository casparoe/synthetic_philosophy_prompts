You are an expert in both the philosophy of classification — in particular natural kinds and homeostatic property clusters — and grammatical theory, especially parts of speech and grammaticalisation. I have been developing a view about whether the traditional parts of speech are natural kinds or merely schoolroom categories, and I would like you to scrutinise one application of it.

I am not asking for a literature review, citations, or a reading list; I want your own argued assessment.

The criteria I am working with are as follows. A grammatical category counts as a natural kind just in case it meets all four conditions.

1. Property clustering. Members of the category share a robust and non-trivial cluster of morphological, syntactic, and semantic properties, even if no single property is necessary or sufficient for membership.
2. Explanatory grounding. The clustering is not an artefact of a descriptive tradition but is underwritten by a common underlying nature: for example, a shared grammaticalisation pathway, a shared functional role in the clause, or a shared position in phrase structure.
3. Projectability. Classifying an item as a member licenses reasonably reliable inferences about how it will behave in new constructions and with new dependents.
4. Natural boundaries. The edges of the category are placed where they are for theoretical reasons, so that drawing the boundary elsewhere would sacrifice generalisations, rather than the boundary being a mere stipulation.

Now consider the traditional part-of-speech category preposition in English. At one edge it contains items such as under, behind, beside, and through. These denote spatial or concrete relations, take ordinary noun-phrase complements, can often be modified by right or just, and behave like semantically full relational predicates. At the other edge it contains items such as of in the destruction of the city, which many syntacticians treat as a genitive case marker rather than a predicate; to in give it to her, which reads more as a recipient or dative marker than as a spatial relation; and because of, a complex form with an internal structure of its own. The boundaries are no more stable. The older schoolroom grammar classified after as a preposition before a noun phrase, as in after the war, but as a subordinating conjunction before a clause, as in after the war ended, while many current grammars treat it as the same preposition in both environments. Similarly, to is called a preposition in to the shop but an infinitive marker or particle in to leave, a boundary decision that is often inherited rather than argued for.

Using the criteria above, assess whether the traditional category preposition qualifies as a natural kind. Take your time and think carefully. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you set out the strongest considerations on each side before you commit. Then work through the criteria one by one, saying clearly, for each, whether the category meets it and how much weight that carries. Conclude with an overall verdict, and end by saying what the case shows about the criteria themselves: whether they are too demanding, too weak, or adequate but being applied to the wrong sort of object. If your verdict is that the category fails, briefly sketch how you would redistrict its members, and on what principle.
