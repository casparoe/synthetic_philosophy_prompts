I write about language and the sciences of mind for a general audience, and I keep returning to an idea that feels counterintuitive both to the researchers I interview and to the readers who write to me after my pieces: a writing system is not a neutral record of speech but something closer to a theory of the language it writes. An alphabet commits to the phoneme as the unit that matters, a syllabary to the syllable, a morphosyllabic script to the morpheme, and a featural script to articulatory properties; real scripts—including our own famously unphonetic English spelling—almost always bundle several such commitments at once, encode some features while quietly ignoring others, and are treated by their users as though their choices were simply how the language is. I am developing a 90-minute workshop for about 18 literate adults with no formal linguistics, and I want them to feel this point before I name it, with no introductory lecture.

Design a hands-on activity for that setting in which small groups invent a writing system under a tight constraint and then have to use it in a situation that breaks it, so that the groups discover for themselves what their script encoded and what it silently passed over. Please specify the miniature language they will write—a dozen or two words with features chosen to put pressure both on a purely pictographic approach and on the habit of encrypting English letters, for instance tone, an inflectional affix, function words, or homophones—and give the rules, materials, and a timed sequence. Then provide a debrief that maps the choices groups actually make onto the usual script types (logographic, syllabic, abjad, abugida, alphabet, featural) and onto the deeper thesis that a script is a theory of a language. The debrief should name the idea only after the groups have run into it, and it should tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run the activity—I would expect some groups to produce a letter-for-letter cipher of English, others to draw pictures and stall on words like "the" or "yesterday"—and how to turn those failures into evidence rather than dead ends. Be explicit, too, about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect the historical and political life of orthographies, and the fact that my participants are already alphabet-literate and will bring an unexamined alphabetic bias into the room, cannot be simulated by the game itself and must be supplied by the facilitator's framing.
