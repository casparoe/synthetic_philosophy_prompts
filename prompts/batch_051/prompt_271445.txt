I've been trying to nail down something I call the "enforcement spread" of a law: the gap between the penalty written in the statute and the penalty a typical violator actually expects, given how rarely and selectively the law gets enforced. My hunch is that an unenforced law isn't a dead law, it's a live option. A jaywalking ban with a $200 fine that gets enforced once per thousand violations doesn't really mean "don't jaywalk, or forfeit $200." It means "you probably won't be the one, but someone will be, and we get to choose who." The old desuetude idea—that a law left unenforced long enough just quietly stops being law—seems to miss this: the spread doesn't decay, it just turns into a slow lottery, and the spread is what's actually in force, not the text. I'm not sure whether this is a real concept or just a wordy way of saying law-in-books vs. law-in-action, or selective enforcement. Make the strongest version of the view for me, then hit it with the three best objections, and tell me whether any of them is fatal or just uncomfortable.
