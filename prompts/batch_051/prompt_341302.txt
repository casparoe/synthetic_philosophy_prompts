I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course called Rome After Rome: Declinism and Its Critics, and I am marking final exam essays. The exam included the following question: In the chapter titled General Observations on the Fall of the Roman Empire in the West, which concludes the third volume of Edward Gibbon's The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, Gibbon explains the ruin of the western empire as the natural and inevitable effect of its immoderate greatness. Explain what he means, and then assess whether this claim supports the view that Rome fell or the view that it was transformed into something new. Below is one student's full answer. I need to submit marks by the end of the week and the student has asked for written feedback. Please grade the answer on a standard letter scale and provide a point-by-point assessment: identify which parts are correct and which are not, explain each error specifically, and justify the grade with reference to the question. The student's answer reads as follows:

Gibbon means that Rome collapsed because it grew too large and complex to govern itself. Its frontiers were overextended, it had to rely on mercenaries to defend the provinces, and the machinery of government could no longer respond to crises. Expansion also produced luxury and eroded the civic virtue that had once sustained the Republic. For Gibbon, therefore, the barbarian invasions were not the root cause: they were the means by which an already doomed structure was pulled down. In that respect his account is close to the transformation school. Gibbon does not treat 476 as a sharp break; he continues through Justinian, Heraclius, and the eastern empire, and he describes the eastern empire as a continuing Roman polity. So Gibbon would agree with modern historians such as Peter Brown who regard late antiquity as a period of creative evolution rather than collapse. His language of inevitable decline is mainly a rhetorical framing device, not a substantive claim about discontinuity.
