For a novel I am writing, I have been reading around philosophical debates on moral responsibility, and I keep returning to a version of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. My protagonist is a career prosecutor who, late in her working life, becomes convinced by something like this argument and finds she can no longer do her job. Before I let the character live inside the reasoning, I want to be sure I understand where the reasoning is vulnerable.

Here is the argument as I would reconstruct it, compressed from Strawson's version and put into my own words:

1. Whenever you act, you act as you do because of the way you are — your beliefs, desires, dispositions, and character — together with your circumstances.
2. To be truly or ultimately morally responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the respects relevant to what you did.
3. To be truly responsible for the way you are, you would have to have intentionally brought it about that you are that way.
4. But any intentional effort to bring it about that you are a certain way would itself be an action, and would therefore flow from the way you already are; the same point applies to any earlier effort, without end.
5. So you cannot be truly responsible for the way you are.
6. Therefore you cannot be truly morally responsible for what you do.

Taken one at a time, the premises strike me as hard to deny, and the reasoning seems to run cleanly to the conclusion. Yet the conclusion seems to prove too much: it does not merely complicate judgments about particular acts, but appears to pull the ground out from under gratitude, resentment, forgiveness, and the entire human practice of holding one another to account. If I am forced to say what I would most like to keep, it is the thought that people can properly be credited or blamed for some of what they do; but I do not want to keep it by fiat.

I would appreciate a careful diagnosis. Is there a questionable step here, and if so which one, and what is surrendered by rejecting it? If instead the argument is valid, then which premise is the least costly to abandon, and what do the main alternatives give up?
