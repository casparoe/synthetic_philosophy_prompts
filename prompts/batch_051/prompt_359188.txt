My climate-modeling reading group has spent the past few weeks working through deterministic chaos—strange attractors, Lyapunov times, sensitive dependence on initial conditions—and one member has begun treating the butterfly effect as a universal solvent for any claim about causal attribution in a chaotic system. Last week someone presented work arguing that a particular European heatwave was made significantly more probable by anthropogenic climate change. The objector brushed this aside: weather is chaotic, so no individual event can be traced to any specific prior cause; a butterfly flapping its wings in Malaysia could have been the difference between that heatwave occurring and not occurring. "That's just the butterfly effect," he said. I think this is too quick, but the slogan is elastic enough that I cannot tell exactly which premise I want to reject. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "the butterfly effect"—together with the associated image of a butterfly's wing-flap causing a storm half a world away—might be used to assert. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive empirical claim, a conceptual point about determinism and predictability, or something false or overstated. Then identify which of those readings, if any, would actually support the objector's conclusion that attributing an individual extreme weather event to climate change is misguided, and which would not. Finally, separate the senses of "unpredictable" that the slogan trades on; I suspect an equivocation between them is doing much of the argumentative work.
