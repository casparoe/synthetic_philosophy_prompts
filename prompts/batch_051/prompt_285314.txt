I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on a debate in the philosophy of language that has been running through recent work on offensive speech: where does the offensiveness of a slur live — in the conventional meaning of the word itself, or in what speakers do with it? I want to translate the debate for a general reader without flattening it, and I'd like a dialogue that models how the two sides actually argue under pressure. To be clear, both positions take for granted that slurs are harmful and that their ordinary use is generally wrong; the disagreement is diagnostic, about the mechanism by which the words do their harm.

Let me set up the two positions as I understand them. The encoded-meaning position holds that, for at least a core class of slurs, the derogatory force is part of the term's conventional meaning — whether realized as truth-conditional content, a presupposition, a conventional implicature, or an expressive component. What unites these variants is the claim that you have not stated the word's meaning unless you have included something derogatory, and they point to projection behavior as evidence: the offensiveness tends to survive when the slur is embedded under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, in questions, and in indirect reports, even when the speaker does not endorse the derogation ('He's not an S, he's a colleague,' 'If she's an S, I'll leave,' 'They asked whether the new hire was an S'). The use-based position holds that slurs are truth-conditionally equivalent to their neutral counterparts, and that the offensiveness is not encoded in the word's meaning but arises from features of its use — taboo, register, the history and currency of the term, speaker intention, and social context. On this view it is easier to explain why the offense is labile: in-group and reclaimed uses, the fact that a term can lose or gain its sting while its extension stays fixed, and the fact that mentioning or quoting the word while discussing language need not convey derogation.

Please write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated proponents of these positions, set as a recorded seminar exchange for my piece. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talking past one another, and push each other on at least these pressure points: the projection data, the use-mention contrast, and the reclamation data. Each should be forced to explain, in her own terms, the other side's strongest evidence, and each should have to make some genuine concession. Please don't resolve the debate — I want the dialogue to show where the disagreement is deepest and which considerations cut in which direction. To keep the piece readable and non-gratuitous, please use a placeholder notation such as S for the slur and N for the neutral counterpart rather than spelling out real slurring terms.
