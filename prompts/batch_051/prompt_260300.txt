I teach political philosophy, and whenever my seminar gets to secession — Scotland, Catalonia, Quebec — somebody always reaches for the same comparison: secession is like divorce. The anti-secession students use it one way: Catalonia leaving Spain would be like the richer spouse walking out and taking the good car, leaving the other one stuck with the mortgage. The pro-secession students use it the other way: forcing a stateless nation to stay inside a state is like a judge refusing to grant a divorce to a couple that's been miserable for years. I want you to audit the analogy itself, rather then re-argue the three cases. Start with the correspondence table: what in a secession dispute plays the role of the two spouses, the wedding, the shared property, the children, the prenup, the courts, the no-fault provision. Then say where the mapping breaks, and — this is the hard part — sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the intended argument and the ones that are just superficial. For example, a marriage is entered by the two spouses voluntarily, while a people usually never signed up for the state it finds itself in; is that a fatal problem for the unionist use of the analogy, or a fixable one? Does the analogy quietly import the idea that separation requires mutual consent, or that there's a neutral judge who can adjudicate the split, when real secession crises usually have neither? And at the end I want a verdict: do either of the conclusions people try to extract from the analogy — that secession should be a live option, or that it should be treated as a kind of betrayal — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the divorce framing doing all the work? If it was, give me a better analogy, or explain why no analogy like this can settle the question.
