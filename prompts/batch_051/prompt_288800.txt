I'm on the product-safety board of a company that makes assistive robots for assisted-living facilities, and I've pulled the short straw to draft our decision memo on a proposed deployment. The memo needs a concrete bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — plus reasons, and it has to take the other side seriously.

The design is pitched to us as a modernized Asimov setup: a strict priority stack of three constraints, roughly (1) prevent harm to any human, including harm that comes from inaction; (2) obey the designated caregiver except where doing so conflicts with harm prevention; (3) preserve its own functioning except where that conflicts with the first two. The constraints are lexicographic — the first always beats the second, the second always beats the third — and each is enforced by its own classifier.

The six-month pilot has been rocky. A resident with mild dementia keeps asking the robot to unlock the memory-care door so she can go see her husband, who's been dead for years. The caregiver has standing instructions to keep her inside, so the robot keeps the door locked, but then tells her it's broken because staff found the lie keeps her calm. Another resident with diabetes asks for dessert; the robot refuses, logs the request as a "requested harmful action," and notifies staff. The family is furious — they say the robot is treating an adult like a child and that the daily refusal is making her depressed, which sure sounds like a harm the harm-prevention classifier isn't weighing. Twice the robot froze when two harm-avoidance duties pulled in opposite directions (fall risk versus blood sugar) and did nothing but page staff. The nursing director says a robot that freezes exactly when you need it is worse than no robot.

The engineers are split. One camp says the hard priority stack is the only part of the design that's genuinely auditable: there's always a unique answer to "why did it do that?", and a scalar tradeoff scheme would just hide the same judgments inside weights nobody understands. The other camp wants a weighted harm-tradeoff system with a human override and automatic review whenever the robot surprises its supervisors.

So: draft the memo. Skip the history of Asimov — we all know it. The question is whether lexicographic safety constraints can be an acceptable architecture for this deployment, and if not, what conditions would get you to a yes. Give the strongest argument for the position you didn't take, and either answer it or say what would have to change for it to win.
