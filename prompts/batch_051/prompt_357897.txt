I am an analyst at a government AI safety agency, and every month I have to write a one-page brief on a specific AI race — between labs or between states — assessing whether the parties would likely be better off slowing down and coordinating, and whether they will race anyway. I want a reusable diagnostic procedure for this, built on the rationalist explanations for war: the puzzle is that rational actors with a common interest in avoiding a costly outcome should be able to find some bargain, so failure is standardly traced to one of three mechanisms — private information with incentives to misrepresent, a credible-commitment problem, or a genuinely indivisible issue. Give me a checklist or decision tree in words for taking a concrete race and assigning it to one of those mechanisms, or explaining why the framework does not fit, runnable in an afternoon from open-source reporting plus a few interviews by someone without game-theory training. The procedure should keep distinct what the theory keeps distinct — above all, the difference between being unable to verify what a rival is doing and being unable to trust any later promise — should flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated, and should say what to do when key facts about capabilities, progress, or internal decision-making cannot be obtained. Apply it to three toy cases: two state labs racing toward an agreed capability threshold with no monitoring or enforcement; a private lab that says it would share safety advances if it trusted its rivals, while quietly under-reporting its own progress to investors; and a race whose objective is winner-take-all, such as sole control of the first system above a threshold. At least one case should be one where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and the response should end by stating what the procedure cannot do. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the procedure and the reasoning, with no preamble.
