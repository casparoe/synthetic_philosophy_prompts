I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or linguistics, and I work mostly on text-to-speech and transliteration systems. A question that keeps surfacing in my work is whether the familiar description of alphabets actually fits English. The familiar description says that an alphabet is a writing system whose letters stand for the phonemes of the language, with English treated as a noisy instance: the mapping is irregular, but the underlying theory is still phonemic. Yet examples such as sign and signature, or heal and health, make me doubt this. In each pair the spelling holds a written unit fixed while the pronunciation changes, which looks like the system is tracking morphemes or word identity rather than surface sounds. So I have a claim — English orthography encodes phonemes — and I cannot tell whether it is a substantive empirical claim, a rough idealization, a historical description, or just a loose way of talking. Since the same question would arise for any alphabet, the deeper issue is what it means to say that a script encodes a level of language at all.

I would like you to help me separate those possibilities. First, operationalize the claim as concretely as you can: propose a measurement or experiment, for instance the error rate of a grapheme-to-phoneme predictor, the conditional entropy of pronunciations given spellings and of spellings given pronunciations (roughly, how much uncertainty remains once the written form is fixed, and vice versa), a behavioral reading experiment, or an artificial-alphabet learning task. Say what specific result would count as evidence that the phonemic description is doing real explanatory work, and what result would count as evidence that English spelling is better described as encoding morphemes or whole word forms. Then tell me what a clean result would still fail to settle: which part of the claim that an alphabet encodes phonemes is not captured by any such measurement, and whether that remaining part is a factual question, a conceptual question, or merely a decision about terminology.
