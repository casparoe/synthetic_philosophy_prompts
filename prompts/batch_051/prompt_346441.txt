I'm a bioethics PhD student and I do pre-review for our university IRB. We get a few protocols every month that pitch themselves as moral bioenhancement — intranasal oxytocin to boost empathy, transcranial stimulation aimed at making people respond more 'fairly' (their word, not mine), a phone app that uses evaluative conditioning to lower implicit bias, that kind of thing. The board has no consistent way to handle them: some members treat them like ordinary psych studies, some want a blanket no on the grounds that this is eugenics-lite, and the rest end up arguing about values. These are real approvals with real subjects, so the screener has to be usable by reviewers who havent read the theory and runnable in about 20 minutes.

My plan is to build the screener out of Persson and Savulescu's moral bioenhancement argument the way I understand it: human moral psychology is built for small-scale life while our technological power is planet-scale, so cognitive enhancement without a matching moral upgrade is a serious hazard; that makes moral bioenhancement urgent rather than optional; the target should be underlying dispositions like altruism, empathy, and a sense of fairness, not just behavior that looks nicer; and there's a standing worry about whose morality gets installed and whether the means wreck the end (coercively installing empathy would be self-defeating). I want the procedure to keep those commitments without making reviewers read the theory.

Write me a decision procedure our pre-review can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it has to kick the decision to the full board because it's a values question rather than a factual one. Flag the distinctions that have to survive simplification and the ones that can be dropped. Then run it on two proposals:

1. A trial giving intranasal oxytocin to adults with high psychopathy scores, recruited from a state prison, to see whether it increases empathy and reduces reactive aggression over six months. The consent procedures look solid; the main known risk is that oxytocin can increase trust in ways that might get these participants exploited.

2. A mandatory-for-the-cohort program at a small liberal arts college that pushes evaluative conditioning through a phone app during orientation, aimed at lowering implicit bias against three outgroups chosen by the administration. Low biomedical risk, but the outcome measure is the IAT, the effect is small and fades fast, and participation is basically required.

I also want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. One of the two should probably come out in a way i wont like — tell me straight when that happens, don't soften it.

Please write the answer in American English.
