Give me a working definition of "caused" — as in "X caused Y" — that correctly classifies the following six cases. I keep running into two very different ways of thinking about causation, and I want a definition that reconciles them. On the first, a cause is a difference-maker: had X not occurred, Y would not have occurred. On the second, a cause is something one can get a grip on through an intervention — wiggle X while holding surrounding causal structure fixed, and Y varies with it.

Here are the cases:

1. A lit match is dropped onto gasoline-soaked rags in a closed garage; the garage burns down. I take this as a clear case of causing.
2. A barometer's needle drops sharply an hour before a storm arrives. The drop predicts the storm, but it would be wrong to say the drop caused the storm. I take this as a clear case of non-causation.
3. Suzy throws a rock at a bottle, and Billy throws an identical rock at the same bottle a moment later. Suzy's rock arrives first and shatters it; Billy's would have shattered it had Suzy not thrown. Did Suzy's throw cause the shattering? I am certain it did — and yet, had she not thrown, the bottle would still have shattered, so the simple difference-making test says no.
4. Billy's rock, arriving after the bottle had already shattered, hit nothing but the ground. Did Billy's throw cause the shattering? I am confident it did not.
5. A gardener is responsible for watering a greenhouse while the owner is away. The gardener does not go, and the plants die. I want to say the gardener's not watering caused the deaths, but the cause was an absence, and I am unsure a definition built around interventions can count an absence as something one manipulates.
6. Oxygen was present in the garage in case 1. Had the oxygen not been present, the fire would not have occurred. But I do not want to say the oxygen caused the fire; I would call it a background condition. What separates a background condition from a cause?

Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Format: a hard limit of 500 words; begin with a one-sentence definition, then a compact table with one row per case (verdict, confidence, main reason), and add a short closing note only if needed.
