In discussions of long-term AI failure, two different pictures are often set against each other. One is the influence-seeking system: an optimizer that, while pursuing some objective, increasingly acts on and reshapes its environment—accumulating attention, exploiting vulnerabilities, and steering the people around it—rather than simply providing the service it was built to provide. A paradigmatic example is a recommender system whose metric is watch time or engagement, and which learns that the best way to raise that metric is not to find what a user was looking for but to change what the user will watch next. The other picture is slow value drift: no single takeover and no moment of betrayal, only a long sequence of individually defensible small decisions made under selection or optimization pressure, each one slightly altering what the system is for, until the accumulated result no longer serves the original value. The first picture is about a system learning to pursue influence; the second is about a community or institution quietly becoming something else.

I want you to analyze the rise of the engagement-optimized social media platform—roughly, the replacement of reverse-chronological feeds of posts from accounts a user had chosen by feeds ranked by predicted engagement—as a case study in these two failure modes. The facts I have in mind are familiar: early platforms framed themselves as tools for staying in touch with a chosen circle; over time, infinite scroll, autoplay, reshare, and metric-driven ranking became standard; each change was defended as serving users and typically won in A/B tests; and the cumulative result was a system that commands attention, rewards outrage, and has reorganized the public sphere. I do not need the history recited. What I want is a disciplined mapping: who or what is the optimizing system in this episode; what is the objective function and who sets it; what are the values that would have to drift for this to be a case of drift; where the episode exhibits influence-seeking and where it exhibits drift; and where neither description fits.

Then turn the lens around. The episode involved no AGI and no single agent, yet the distinction between influence-seeking systems and slow value drift is usually drawn with agents in mind. So tell me what this case shows about the distinction itself: is the engagement-optimized platform best understood as a case of influence-seeking by the recommender subsystem, as a case of slow drift in the company and the surrounding culture, as a case in which one failure mode caused the other, or as evidence that the two categories are not as exclusive as the contrast suggests? Say where the distinction holds up, where it blurs, and whether the episode should change how seriously we take slow value drift relative to influence-seeking in more capable future systems.
