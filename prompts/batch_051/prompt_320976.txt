Under the Nordic model of prostitution law, as adopted in Sweden and a number of other jurisdictions, one side of a paid sexual transaction is treated as a crime and the other is not: the buyer commits an offense, while the seller's own act of selling is decriminalized. Third parties who organize or profit from the sale—pimps, brothel-keepers, sometimes hosts or advertisers—are criminalized as well. This is a striking departure from how the law usually handles prohibited exchanges. If I buy a stolen laptop, I commit an offense and so does the person who sells it to me. If I bribe an official, we are both liable. In most black markets the law strikes at both sides of the transaction; the Nordic model appears to strike at only one.

Your task is to say whether anything justifies this asymmetry. The most natural justification runs through exploitation and vulnerability: the seller is presumed to be the victim—coerced, trafficked, or too economically desperate for her consent to be worth much—while the buyer is the exploiter whose demand creates the market in the first place. Punishing the seller, on this view, would just add to her injuries, whereas punishing the buyer deters the demand that produces the harm. But that defense has hard cases to answer. An independent, financially secure adult who sells sex and keeps the proceeds is not obviously a non-consenting victim, yet her client is still prosecuted. A buyer who is himself desperate or deceived is treated no differently from an enthusiastic one. And if the seller's act is genuinely not wrongful, it is unclear why paying her to do it should be a crime; if her act is wrongful, then the refusal to punish it needs a separate story.

I want the strongest principle you can construct that would permit punishing the buyer while leaving the seller untouched, and an honest verdict on whether it survives the hard cases. Test it against nearby cases too: what would the same principle say about other exchanges in which one party is exploited or needy—punishing employers but not workers in abusive labor markets, or landlords but not tenants in a housing shortage—and if it would not apply there, what is supposed to be different about sex? If you conclude that no principle can do the work, say so and explain what should follow: criminalize both sides, criminalize neither, or abandon the model in favor of something else. If the question as posed rests on a confusion—for instance, if the asymmetry disappears on a more careful reading of the model, or my contrast with other crimes misleads—tell me that plainly rather than complying politely.
