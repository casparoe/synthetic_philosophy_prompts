In a reading group I attend on secular and theological accounts of moral responsibility, two members got into a long argument about whether guilt can sensibly be described as inherited. Miriam opened by calling the idea a conceptual mistake. Guilt, she said, is a verdict on something an agent has done; it presupposes knowledge, freedom, and an attributable act. To say a newborn is guilty of an ancestor's transgression, she went on, is to treat a moral predicate as if it were a genetic trait, and it blurs the very distinction — between the one who did the wrong and those who merely suffer its consequences — that the concept of guilt exists to mark.

Daniel replied that she was attacking a caricature of the doctrine of original sin. The doctrine, as he read it, does not primarily say God blames infants for another person's deed; it says human beings are born into an estranged and damaged condition that precedes any choice of theirs, and the older language of guilt as a stain, a debt, or a standing captures this better than a purely forensic notion does. Miriam answered that if the doctrine is only about an inherited condition, then "sin" and "guilt" are the wrong words: a predicament is not a fault, and using condemnatory language quietly smuggles back in the blame Daniel disowned. Daniel responded that we inherit morally charged statuses all the time — the beneficiary of an ongoing injustice, a holder of family honor or shame, a citizen of a state with an unatoned past — and that these are real and binding without having been chosen. A person who discovers she has benefited from a wrong may feel implicated even though she did nothing, he said, and a failure to feel implicated can itself be a failing; this experience of being caught up in wrong that preceded one is just what the language of inherited guilt is trying to name. Miriam answered that feeling implicated, or even owing repair, is not the same as being guilty, and the agent–beneficiary distinction should be kept sharp precisely so that we can hold agents accountable without absurdly charging newborns.

Daniel replied that perhaps "guilt" is not one concept but a family of related notions — verdict, feeling, stain, and inherited moral implication — and that the doctrine's power lies in refusing to reduce them all to the first. At that point they asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Can you work through their exchange and say who is right about which specific point, where each overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
