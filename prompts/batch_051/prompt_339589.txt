I'm a retired geochemist who's spent the last couple of years reading formal epistemology, and the lottery and preface paradoxes have started to feel like my two favorite cases—but I keep finding people using them to pummel rival views without ever writing down what a theory of rational acceptance is supposed to do in the first place. So before I read another paper, I want the desiderata.

For background, since I know the details matter: in a fair lottery with a million tickets, it seems fine to accept, of any given ticket, that it will lose—the probability is .999999. But if acceptance agglomerates under conjunction, you end up accepting that every ticket loses, which contradicts your certainty that some ticket wins. In the preface case, an author accepts each sentence in her book (she checked them carefully) yet also accepts that at least one of them is false. Any account of acceptance has to say exactly which step in each of these goes wrong, or why the setup is misleading.

Give me the full requirements list for a theory of rational acceptance, adequate for a working scientist deciding when to treat a high-probability result as settled enough to build on. I mean a real spec: the cases it must get right (the two above, plus the lab version where you accept a batch of individually well-supported measurements while also accepting that at least one in the batch is off); the distinctions it can't collapse (graded confidence vs outright acceptance, acceptance-for-action vs acceptance as a standing commitment, and whatever else you think belongs); and the principles it has to take a stand on rather than dodge (closure under conjunction, consistency, what role stakes play). For each requirement, say why its a requirement and whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most standard views—my hunch is that the simple threshold view, accept whenever the probability clears some fixed cutoff like .95, gets killed by something in here, but I'd like that made precise rather than gestured at. Also flag any subset of the list that can't be jointly satisfied; I suspect there's a clean little no-go hiding in the lottery case, but I might be overreading it.

One specific thing I want settled: in the lab, the word accept felt different when I was acting on a result this afternoon (run the next experiment, send off the sample) than when I wrote it up as something I'd assert in a paper. Is that a real distinction the theory has to respect, or a confusion I picked up from applied statistics? If it's real, put it on the list; if it's confused, say so plainly.

And be direct with me: if the whole commission is misframed—say there's no well-behaved notion of acceptance worth theorizing, and the right move is to dissolve it into graded credence plus decision rules—tell me that and write the corrected task instead. For each requirement you do endorse, tell me how confident you are in it. Casual writing is fine, but I want the structure sharp.
