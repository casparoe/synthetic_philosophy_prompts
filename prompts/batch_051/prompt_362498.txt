I'm working on a paper for my jurisprudence seminar and I've been trying to turn Devlin's argument in *The Enforcement of Morals* into a set of workable criteria for when a society may use the criminal law to enforce morality. My professor gave us the lecture and Hart's reply, and I keep getting stuck on a case. The part of Devlin I keep circling is where he argues that a society is held together by a shared moral code, and that the criminal law can be used to defend that shared code in much the same way it defends the state against treason — because both are attacks on what makes the society a society. So here are the criteria I distilled:

1. The conduct would provoke intolerance, indignation, or disgust in the ordinary reasonable person, not just in moralists with a theory.
2. The moral norm being violated is one whose erosion would threaten the society's cohesion.
3. Criminalizing the conduct would actually help shore up that shared norm.
4. The conduct isn't something society has already implicitly agreed to leave to private judgment.

Now the borderline case. Suppose there's an organization, call it "Exit Morality," that recruits consenting adults to sign a mutual compact saying that, among themselves, they will treat promises, truth-telling, gratitude, and other moral obligations as optional conventions they can abandon whenever it suits them. They run public forums arguing that moral obligation is an illusion, and they publish a newsletter defending the view that no one owes anyone anything. They don't defraud outsiders, they pay their bills, and their members hold jobs including a few public-school teachers. A solid majority of the public, if polled, reports deep disgust at the group, and many say it shouldn't be allowed to exist. The group's defenders say it's just speech plus private association.

My question: under the four criteria, does this qualify as the kind of "attack on shared morality" that Devlin thinks the law may suppress? Or does the case show that criterion 2 collapses into criterion 1 — that we can't tell whether a moral norm's erosion threatens cohesion except by consulting the very disgust that's already doing the work? I don't want an essay defending Hart or Devlin — I want a close application of the criteria, criterion by criterion, and then a verdict on whether the criteria themselves need to be revised.
