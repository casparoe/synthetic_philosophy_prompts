Write a classification instrument for an upper-level seminar on distributive justice. The instrument should sort respondents into positions on a single question: under what conditions, if any, does a person deserve an economic reward? Deliver a decision tree, not prose. Each branch point must be a concrete yes/no question or a forced choice about a case, phrased so a respondent can answer without knowing the literature, and the first split should be whether desert claims can be true independently of human institutions or are instead constituted by the rules of a practice. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of position names: fix the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, locate actual positions among those combinations, and mark combinations that are internally coherent but unoccupied, noting in one clause whether each is unattractive, blocked by neighboring commitments, or merely unexplored. The tree must reach at least these leaves, under whatever names fit: a pre-institutional contribution view, a pre-institutional effort view, a self-ownership libertarian view that grants full entitlement to voluntary transfers even where desert is absent, a luck egalitarian view that substitutes responsibility for chosen effort for backward-looking desert, a forward-looking incentive view that assigns rewards solely for their future consequences, and an institutional conventionalist view. Use these probes at least once: (a) two employees produce identical measurable output but one found the work effortless and the other agonizing; (b) a farmer works harder than everyone else in the region and is ruined by drought; (c) an heir receives a fortune she did nothing to earn; (d) a worker meets every target in a bonus scheme the employer admits was set arbitrarily. For each leaf, give one sentence on the most awkward commitment its proponent cannot disown. Close by flagging any two leaves the tree cannot separate and stating the further question that would separate them.
