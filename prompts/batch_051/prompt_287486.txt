Our attitudes toward deliberately improving the human mind contain an asymmetry worth examining. Consider two safe interventions, each given to healthy adults. The first improves working memory and concentration. The second modestly increases empathy, patience, and the capacity to resist impulsive anger. The first is widely treated as continuous with education and coffee: people worry about safety, fairness in examinations, and unequal access, but not about the user's agency being undermined. The second is widely treated as more troubling: as an attack on authenticity, as engineering virtue, as making people good rather than allowing them to choose to be good, as changing who someone is rather than sharpening a tool they possess. Both are deliberate, artificial improvements of the mind. Yet we tend to welcome improvements to the capacities people use in pursuing their ends, and to recoil from improvements to the dispositions that determine which ends they pursue and how they treat others. Is there anything that justifies this asymmetry between cognitive and moral enhancement, or is it an inconsistency we are simply used to?
