Draft the joint statement that a committed epistemicist and a committed supervaluationist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about vagueness. Assume both participants are careful and charitable, and that each is willing to conclude that some of their dispute may be merely verbal.

The document should consist of two main sections.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should go beyond truisms and cover at least the following: the phenomena any account of vagueness must handle, including borderline cases, the sorites paradox, the felt pull of tolerance intuitions, and the apparent absence of a discoverable sharp boundary; the rejection of the unrestricted tolerance principle and a diagnosis of exactly what goes wrong with it that both sides can accept; and their shared logical common ground, including the status of classical tautologies and excluded middle, and the reason both sides hold that accepting excluded middle does not by itself settle the dispute. Where the epistemicist and the supervaluationist use the notion of a precisification or admissible sharpening in different ways, state the agreed mapping between their vocabularies rather than silently merging them.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give a neutral statement of the question; the epistemicist's answer, worded as an epistemicist would accept it as an accurate statement of their own view; and the supervaluationist's answer, worded as a supervaluationist would accept it as an accurate statement of their own view. Cover at least the following questions, and add any others you judge substantive: Do vague predicates have sharp but unknowable boundaries, or is there no unique boundary because several sharpenings are equally admissible? Do borderline predications have determinate truth values? Is vagueness fundamentally an epistemic limitation or a semantic phenomenon? And how should higher-order vagueness be handled—as iterated precisification, as iterated ignorance, or in some other way?

End with a cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer to them would move at least one side toward the other, or would force one side to revise the official statement of its view. For each crux, specify a concrete case, argument, or test that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Finally, if you judge that any part of the original disagreement is merely verbal, say which part and why.
