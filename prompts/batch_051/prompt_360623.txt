I help run a philosophy revision group at my sixth-form college, and as part of our final unit on philosophy of mind we have been peer-marking one another's short essay answers. Question.
Explain John Searle's Chinese Room argument and assess whether it succeeds in refuting strong AI. In your answer, describe the thought experiment, state what Searle takes it to show, and consider at least one reply.

Student answer.
Searle's Chinese Room argument, from his 1980 paper Minds, Brains, and Programs, is intended to refute what he calls strong AI: the thesis that a suitably programmed computer would not merely simulate a mind but would literally be a mind, with understanding and other genuine mental states. Searle imagines himself alone in a room. He knows no Chinese, but he has a large set of English instructions telling him exactly which Chinese symbols to output in response to the Chinese symbols passed under the door. From outside, the room produces perfect Chinese answers, so a fluent Chinese speaker would naturally suppose that the room understands Chinese. Searle's point, however, is that he is merely manipulating uninterpreted shapes according to formal rules; he has no idea what any of the symbols mean. Because a program, like the rulebook, is purely syntactic, running a program can never yield semantic understanding no matter how the symbols are arranged. Searle therefore concludes that no machine could ever think or understand anything: intentionality is a causal-biological property of brains, and since computers are inorganic, they are in principle incapable of mental states. The systems reply objects that although the man inside the room does not understand Chinese, the whole system, the man plus the rulebook plus the symbols, does. Searle responds that the man could memorise the entire rulebook and database and perform the manipulation in his head, and he still would not understand Chinese, so the extra machinery adds nothing. I find this response convincing because the burden is on the systems reply to identify where the understanding could be located, and Searle shows it cannot be located anywhere.
