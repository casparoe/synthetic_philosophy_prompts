I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, and there's an assumption I keep tripping over in economic reporting that I can't quite get a handle on. In policy world — central banks, finance ministries, the business press, my own editors — GDP growth is treated as an unambiguous good and recessions as an unambiguous bad, even when the slowdown is mild and mostly hits speculative construction. But when you push people on why growth is good, the reasons they give are all instrumental: jobs, tax revenue, money for hospitals, less poverty, more slack to deal with the next crisis. And yet almost nobody seems willing to say that if we could get all those things without growth, growth itself would stop mattering. The accounting is weird too: rebuilding after a hurricane adds to GDP, a spike in cancer treatments adds to GDP, cleaning up a chemical spill adds to GDP, and the resulting growth still gets reported as good news. So I want to know what theory of value this practice actually presupposes. Does treating growth as an end in itself commit you to thinking abundance — a bigger economy, more stuff, more production — has moral worth beyond what it buys: beyond the welfare, health, security, and leisure it procures? Or is the practice compatible with a purely insturmental view of prosperity, where more output is just a fallible proxy for those underlying goods? Reconstruct the implicit theory as honestly as you can, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Tell me where several different theories would license roughly the same growth-friendliness, and then tell me what would have to change in policy, in national accounting, and in how journalists cover recessions if we replaced the presupposed view with a purely instrumental one: growth is a tool, not a good.
