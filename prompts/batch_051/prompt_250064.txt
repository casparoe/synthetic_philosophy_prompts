I am a high-school student and a member of our school's philosophy society. We have recently been discussing the position associated with Augustine that lying is never permissible—not even when it would prevent serious harm—and I have been trying to reconstruct the rationale behind that position as a numbered argument. I intend to present this reconstruction at our next meeting. Several members find the conclusion hard to accept but cannot agree about where the reasoning goes wrong; one of them thinks the argument is valid and that we are only resisting the conclusion because it is unwelcome. I would be grateful for a close inspection of the steps.

(1) To lie is to assert something one believes to be false, with the aim of getting another person to believe it.

(2) Assertion is a rule-governed practice whose central rule is that one may assert a proposition only if one believes it to be true.

(3) An act is a bad instance of its practice when it breaks that practice's central rule.

(4) Every lie breaks the central rule of assertion, since the liar asserts what she does not believe. (from 1 and 2)

(5) Hence every lie is a bad instance of assertion, and therefore a bad act. (from 3 and 4)

(6) An act that is bad can never be made permissible by its consequences.

(7) Therefore no lie, whatever its consequences, can be permissible.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, please say so plainly and explain why the conclusion follows. If it is not, identify exactly which step fails and why, and state the strongest reading under which the troublesome step would be true and whether the argument still goes through on that reading. I have a strong suspicion that the word 'bad' changes its sense somewhere between steps (3) and (6), but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Please write your response in British English.
