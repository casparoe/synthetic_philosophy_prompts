Consider the following argument, which I keep coming back to but cannot decide about. It concerns the Church–Turing thesis in the form often called the "empirical" version: every function on the natural numbers that is effectively computable by some finite mechanical procedure is Turing-computable. Here is my argument that this thesis cannot be an empirical claim at all:

(1) To empirically refute the Church–Turing thesis, one would have to exhibit a procedure that is effective, in the intuitive sense, yet computes a function that no Turing machine computes.
(2) But any procedure that can be exhibited explicitly enough to be tested as a proposed counterexample must be given in some finite notation: a finite set of instructions, rules, states, or operations.
(3) Any procedure given in a finite notation can be recast as a single algorithm operating on finite symbolic configurations.
(4) Any such algorithm is representable by a Turing machine.
(5) So any proposed counterexample that is explicit enough to test would already be Turing-computable, and thus would not refute the thesis.
(6) Therefore the Church–Turing thesis cannot be empirically refuted.
(7) A thesis that cannot be empirically refuted is not an empirical thesis.
(8) Hence the Church–Turing thesis is not empirical; it is a definitional stipulation, a convention, or at most a mathematical axiom.

I am inclined to think the argument is valid and that people who call the thesis an empirical hypothesis are just not facing how much work "effectively computable" is doing. But I am told the argument cheats at step (3) or step (4), or that step (7) rests on a crude Popperian caricature. Which step actually fails, if any? For each of steps (3), (4), and (7), state the most plausible reading under which the step would be false, and say whether the argument can be repaired by qualifying the corresponding premise.
