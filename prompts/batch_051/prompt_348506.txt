For a paper in my civics class, I'm trying to work out what we should even be asking of a theory about how much risk a society should tolerate. I keep seeing people defend a policy by pointing at one bad outcome, or by saying "if it saves even one life, it's worth it," and I want the full specification before I read any actual proposals. Treat the deliverable as the list of requirements: what an adequate theory of a society's acceptable risk has to get right, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and which requirements are non-negotiable versus held only by particular camps. Please cover at least the following. The cases it has to classify or handle: a school adding security measures after a threat, a city lowering its speed limit, a factory emitting pollution near homes, a vaccine with a rare side effect, and a substance people use knowingly and voluntarily. The distinctions it cannot collapse: a risk someone chooses versus a risk imposed on them, a known probability versus a worst case whose odds nobody knows, a risk spread across a population versus concentrated on one group, and a risk that is reduced versus a risk that is merely displaced or replaced. The constraints it cannot contradict: zero risk is usually unattainable, and safety measures can change people's behavior in ways that create new risks. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which reflect the preferences of particular camps, flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to knock out the popular simple answers. Then briefly run three candidates against the list: "minimize total risk," "voluntary risk is all that matters," and "follow the precautionary principle." A table is fine.
