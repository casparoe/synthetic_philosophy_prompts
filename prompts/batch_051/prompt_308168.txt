Here's an argument I put together for a reading group on Wang Yangming, and now I'm half-convinced of a conclusion I don't actually want, so I need someone to find the flaw before I present it.

So Wang Yangming's whole thing is the unity of knowledge and action — the idea that if you genuinely know what's good, the doing of it is already underway, and that someone who says "I know I should but I don't" is just parroting words. To genuinely know filial piety is to act filially; otherwise you don't really know it.

Here's my argument:

(1) On this view, to genuinely know that one should φ is already to be moved to φ, absent coercion or external obstacles. "I know I should but I won't" always betrays a failure of knowledge, not a failure of will.

(2) So for any person and any action: if they don't φ, they didn't genuinely know they should φ.

(3) Now take ordinary weakness of will: a student who says, sincerely enough, "I know I should study, and yet here I am on my phone." By (2), the student is wrong about her own state: she never genuinely knew.

(4) But there's no way to check for "genuine knowledge" independently of whether the person acts. The phrase just tracks "the kind of knowing that inevitably produces action," so every apparent counterexample gets automatically reclassified as not-really-knowing.

(5) That means the doctrine's key claim — genuine knowledge cannot fail to act — is true by defintion, which is to say it says nothing about anybody.

(6) And if it is meant as a real psychological claim, it makes weakness of will impossible by fiat, so we can never blame someone for knowing better; every moral failure becomes a failure of knowledge.

(7) Either way, the teaching looks like it cant be both substantive and true.

I'd most like to keep (1), because it really does seem to be what Wang is up to, and I also don't want to give up on akrasia being real. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
