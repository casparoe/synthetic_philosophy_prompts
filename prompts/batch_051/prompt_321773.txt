I keep going round in circles on a question that probably has a name, but I've worked it up on my own. On one side is a view I find genuinely compelling: it's dishonest to put one number on how likely something is unless you really know that much. If I say there's a 40 per cent chance a supplier will be late, I don't mean a precise 40: I mean something looser, maybe thirty to sixty, and pretending otherwise turns a hunch into a measurement. On this view my beliefs should be imprecise — a range or a spread, not a point — and refusing to fake precision is a kind of intellectual honesty.

But then I think about actually acting, and a stubborn intuition gets in the way. When I have to decide whether to pay for express delivery or leave early for a meeting, I have to do one thing, not a range of things. And the choice seems to reveal that I've settled on some particular probability to act on. The trouble is sharpest when the range straddles the line where the action becomes worth it. Say I'll pay extra only if the chance of lateness is over 50 per cent, and my honest range is 40 to 60. The imprecise view now seems to desert me exactly when I need it, or to sneak a hidden precise number back in through the decision rule. I cant shake the feeling that acting forces a single number out of me, even when the evidence doesn't justify one.

I'm not asking you to just pick the winning side, because I don't know. I want to know what it would take for each side to give way: when should I conclude the acting-intuition is just confused and should be dropped; when should I conclude the imprecise-belief view is unworkable and has to be reworked or given up; and is there a reading on which my honest uncertainty stays a range while my choices still commit me to something sharper, so both can survive? Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
