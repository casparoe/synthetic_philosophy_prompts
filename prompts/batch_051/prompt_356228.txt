I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about language death, and my gut verdicts keep pulling me in different directions. I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher who works on value theory and the ethics of cultural loss, think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few candidate principles before you settle on one. I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, just the reasoning. Here are my cases and verdicts:

1. A language spoken by around six hundred people in the highlands is dying because, over a decade of town meetings, nearly every parent has decided to raise their kids in Spanish so they'll have better job prospects. The old language will probably be gone in two generations. My verdict: the community has the right to make that call, and it's less bad than if someone forced them, but I still feel something real is being lost that isn't just their private business.

2. Same language, same place, but this time the government bans the language in schools and harasses people who speak it in public. The language dies within a generation. My verdict: straightforwardly awful, and the loss of the language is part of the harm itself, not just the coercion.

3. A language hasn't been anyone's first language for three hundred years, but it survives fluently in rituals and scholarship among second-language speakers. My verdict: I don't mourn it much, and I don't really count it as "dead" in the tragic sense.

4. A language loses its last native speaker, but before she dies linguists record six hundred hours of her speech, write a grammar, and compile a big dictionary. My verdict: the loss is real, but the documentation takes a lot of the sting out of it.

5. A hobbyist invents a language with a full grammar, fifteen hundred words, and a handful of fluent friends who meet weekly to speak it. The group drifts apart, and within a year the language is gone. My verdict: not tragic; I dont really think anything valuable to the wider world was lost.

6. Two languages, same number of speakers, same degree of vitality. One dies because an earthquake kills most of its speakers overnight; the other dies because a state policy prohibits its use. My verdict: both are losses, but the second is worse in a way that seems to matter — even though, as far as the amount of linguistic diversity lost, the two cases look identical.

It feels like I could patch these together by saying a language has instrumental value for its speakers, plus some value as part of the world's diversity, plus a separate wrong when it's suppressed unjustly. But I'm not sure that actually adds up: if the language has value to the wider world, then cases 1 and 5 should bother me more than they do; if it mostly matters to the community itself, then the two halves of case 6 should feel the same. Is there a principle — or a small set of candidates — that gets all six verdicts, or close? State the best one precisely, say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed (a language with two million speakers whose government decides, without much deliberation and without any real coercion, to shift the whole country to English in a single generation), and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I have to give up. Please answer in American English.
