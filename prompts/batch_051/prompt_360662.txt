I am a novelist, and a recurring character in the book I am writing chairs a hospital clinical ethics committee. I have already drafted the rulings she gives in three earlier episodes, and I now need her to rule on a fourth case in a way that is consistent with what she has decided. In the first, she judged it permissible to withdraw ventilation from a patient with progressive neuromuscular disease whose breathing was entirely dependent on the ventilator, at the patient's own written request; the patient died soon afterwards of respiratory failure. In the second, she refused to authorise a nurse's proposal to give a lethal injection to a terminally ill patient whose family found his last hours unbearable. In the third, she refused to authorise the withholding of antibiotics from a patient with a serious but treatable chest infection, because the entire point of withholding would have been to let the infection kill him. The new case before her is this: newborn twins are joined at the chest and share a single heart and a common circulation. Left unseparated, both will die within weeks. A surgeon proposes to divide the shared circulation so that the stronger twin keeps the heart; the weaker twin will die during the operation, not because the surgeon aims at her death, but because the divided circulation cannot sustain her once separated. I need to know what this character should decide if she is to remain consistent with her own earlier rulings, which of those rulings is the closest analogue, and whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case. If it does, give me the candidate principles that fit everything she has already ruled, what each would require in the twin case, your best prediction of her ruling with the reasons for preferring it, and the further case that would best settle which principle she is actually running.
