I'm in a seminar on personal identity and we've been working through the origin-essentialism argument from Kripke's Naming and Necessity: the claim that a particular person couldn't have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones they actually originated from. The usual intuition pump is that if a different sperm had fertilised your mother's egg, it would have been one of your siblings, not you. Fine. But now consider where reproductive technology has actually gone: IVF already creates several embryos and lets parents choose one, and in vitro gametogenesis (making eggs and sperm from skin cells) is likely to make it possible to generate large numbers of embryos and score them for predicted traits before any of them is implanted. If Kripke is right, choosing embryo B rather than A doesn't make A worse off; it means A never exists and B exists instead. That move has been used to say designer-baby worries rest on a confusion. But I'm not sure the move survives when the thing being selected isn't a pre-existing embryo at all, but engineered gametes or a genomic line that was itself designed as part of the selection process. Does the necessity-of-origin argument still go through in that setting, or does the premise that there are particular gametes with their own essential origins get undercut? If it does survive, does it still have the same defusing force, or does it turn into something weaker? I'm interested in the argument's structure, not in the policy.
