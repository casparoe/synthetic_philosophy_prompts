I’m taking a graduate seminar on Gödel’s philosophical papers, and I keep getting stuck on how to extend his arguments about time to debates that happened after he died. The passage that trips me up is in Gödel’s 1949 contribution to the Schilpp volume on Einstein. As I understand it, he argues there that the rotating solutions to Einstein’s equations allow each observer to have a well-defined local time, but there is no way to stitch these local times together into a single objective worldwide flow of time; and he takes this to show that time is ideal, or at least that the intuitive idea of a universal now is unsupported.

What I want to know is how he would have reacted to the later debate between presentists and eternalists, and to the more recent claim in quantum gravity that time is not fundamental but emergent from a timeless underlying description. Would he treat these as independent confirmation of his own idealist conclusion? Or would he resist the eternalist label because his view is stronger: not just that time is a block, but that it is unreal in a more radical way? And would he see the emergent-time program as a scientific version of what he was arguing, or just a retread of subjective time with extra mathematics? I’m okay with a speculative reconstruction, but I’d like it clearly flagged where his text underdetermines the answer.
