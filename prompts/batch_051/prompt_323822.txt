You are a professor of philosophy of mathematics with training in logic and a working knowledge of abstract algebra; you have published on mathematical structuralism and on how working mathematicians handle notation, convention, and choice. I am a second-year PhD student, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a two-page dissertation prospectus (roughly 1,200 words) for my department. Take the idea seriously, but also take it apart where it is loose.

My half-formed idea is this. When a mathematical object has nontrivial automorphisms, its individual elements are not distinguishable within the structure: nothing in the language of fields separates the two square roots of −1, and every statement true of i remains true after swapping i and −i. Yet mathematicians say "the positive root of x² − 2" or "the principal square root of −1," and we rightly suspect that the student who asks which root of x² + 1 is really i has missed something. I suspect the usual structuralist moral — only the structure matters, the relabeled objects do not — is too coarse. The interesting line looks different: some choices among symmetric elements are mere labelings, while other choices import structure that was not there before (an ordering, an orientation, a preferred basis, a coordinate system, a gauge). I want to understand when a choice among symmetric elements is harmless labeling and when it is a substantive mathematical move that changes the object, and whether — once the extra structure is in view — anything genuinely turns on how you label the roots.

Here is what I want from you. First, before writing anything, generate three or four candidate framings of the central question and discard the ones that collapse into slogans such as "only structure matters" or "it's all convention." Do not include that brainstorming in the output. Then write the proposal itself, in the first person as my prospectus, with: (1) a precise statement of the question in a form that has a determinate answer; (2) why the existing structuralist and object-oriented positions leave a gap here rather than settling it, described in your own words; (3) a concrete method — the cases I would work through (roots of polynomials, conjugates and Galois groups, Benacerraf-style multiple reductions for numbers, gauge-fixing in physics, canonical forms in linear algebra), the distinction I would try to formalize, and the tests it must pass; (4) what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and (5) an honest scope note for a two-year prospectus. Think carefully throughout about the difference between a label and imported structure. Do not include a bibliography or any invented citations; where you gesture at positions, paraphrase them, and I will add references myself. The final proposal should be written in American English.
