So I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about information cascades, and by the end I'd backed myself into a position I actually want to keep. I've taken to calling it cascade compression. The usual setup: people decide in sequence, each sees what everyone before them did, and after a while it's rational to follow the crowd because those earlier choices encode private information. My claim is that the thing people copy isn't only the choice — its also the tempo of the choice. The first few movers hesitate, the middle movers move faster, and the late movers barely deliberate at all, because the public history no longer reads as an inference, it reads as an answer. So a cascade compresses decision time toward zero, and I want to say faster than the evidence itself is improving. You could then use that compression as a diagnostic: when you can't observe anyone's private signals, which you almost never can, you might still spot a cascade by the collapse in decision time. My friend torched this: decision time is noise about caution, stakes, identity, maybe whether someone is being watched, and I'm smuggling in the exact private information I admitted I can't see. I think he's wrong and I'd rather learn that with reasons than keep it comfortable. So: is cascade compression a real concept or just a metaphor with a stopwatch? What would the strongest version of it claim, how close is it to things people have already worked out under better names, and where does it break — in the definition, the causal mechanism, or the diagnostic hope? And give me the hardest objection or two without letting me off easy.
