I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book set in a small island confederation that's on the verge of joining a mainland customs union. The thing I keep circling is this: joining doesn't obviously take away your flag, your parliament, or your courts, but it does take away the tariffs and trade-policy levers you'd reach for the moment a crisis hits, so what's actually changed and what have you not lost? Act as a political philosopher who works on sovereignty and membership and also knows how to design games; I'm after the feel of the thing more than the law. I'm running a three-hour workshop at a writers' retreat: sixteen novelists, mostly drafting stories about breakaway provinces, port cities, and trade leagues. Design a structured role-play or negotiation game for that group — rules, payoffs, roles (a small island state, a big mainland member, a strait-controlling state, one with a failing industry, maybe a state that stayed out), a timeline, and a debrief — so the participants feel the difference between a genuine customs union and a loose confederation themselves, ideally by walking into an unplanned shock after the agreement and discovering they'd already given up a power they thought they'd kept. The debrief should map what happened onto pooled authority, exit rights, compensation, and what membership is actually membership in; say what will predictably go wrong and which part of real customs-union politics the game cant reproduce and has to be supplied afterwards. Dont give me a survey — brainstorm two or three possible mechanisms first, then give me the one you'd actually run and why.
