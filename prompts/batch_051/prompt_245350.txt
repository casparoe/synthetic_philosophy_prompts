I am working on a paper about the no-trade theorem and its implications for the epistemology of markets, and I should like a carefully developed argument for a position I am considering. The usual presentation of the theorem treats it as a negative or quiet-market result: when rational traders share a common prior and begin from a Pareto-efficient allocation, private information alone cannot produce mutually agreeable speculative trade. I would like you to argue for a different reading.

Please argue that the no-trade theorem is best understood as a fixed-point claim about the information carried by a proposal to trade: any price at which both parties would be willing to transact would itself reveal enough about their private signals to move the price away from that level, so the willingness to trade is self-undermining. On this reading, the theorem is not chiefly a prediction that rational markets will exhibit low volume; it is an account of how information can be transmitted through offers that are made and declined, rather than only through the prices of completed transactions. In making the argument, please contrast this interpretation with the view that observed volume is straightforwardly evidence against the theorem, explain why the argument does not depend on treating traders as secretive or unable to communicate, and work through one concrete micro-level example, with explicit posteriors and expected utilities, in which a single refused offer changes an outside observer's posterior more than the original quoted price did.
