I spend a good deal of my time in online forums where machine learning practitioners and philosophy-curious engineers argue about whether a system can be said to reason from experience. One claim I keep running into is that the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning are just Hume's problem of induction restated with the mathematics made explicit. As I understand the claim, the theorems say that averaged over all possible ways the world could be, no learning algorithm does better than any other — so an algorithm can beat random guessing in our world only because it already encodes some bias, such as the expectation that the future will resemble the past. The people making the claim then conclude that this is exactly Hume's point: induction cannot be justified from experience alone, because every successful induction has to import in advance the very uniformity it was supposed to earn from the data, and the theorem is offered as proof that there is no escape from that circularity.

My own rough attempt at the mapping goes like this: past observations correspond to training data, the regularities we project onto new cases correspond to the test distribution, and the choice of learning algorithm corresponds to our willingness to generalize in one way rather than another. But I suspect that rough mapping hides as much as it reveals, which is why I want the analogy audited.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher of science who also understands the technical content of statistical learning theory. Lay out the correspondence explicitly, correcting my rough mapping where needed: what corresponds to the learner, the training data, the unseen test cases, the inductive bias, the space of possible worlds, and the measure of success? Then identify the places where the analogy strains or breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the skeptical conclusion from the ones that are only superficial. Finally, tell me whether the intended conclusion — that the no-free-lunch theorems vindicate the claim that induction cannot be justified without circularity — actually survives once the disanalogies are in view. If there is a better analogy for what the theorems do and do not show, give it. Please start by brainstorming the correspondences before settling on your final assessment.
