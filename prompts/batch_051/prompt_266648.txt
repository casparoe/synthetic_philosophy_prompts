I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, and I'm working on a piece about how newsrooms, streaming platforms, and online retailers increasingly let aggregate popularity metrics decide what gets promoted. In our editorial meetings, a senior editor keeps describing this with a comparison he clearly finds decisive: a crowd's average judgment is like a thermometer made of many noisy sensors. Each person's rating, purchase, or click is a rough measurement of the same underlying property—call it quality, importance, or truth—and although each sensor is off, the errors are supposed to run in different directions, so the average converges on the genuine value. On this picture, a bestseller list, a trending sidebar, or a heavily upvoted comment is simply the public's thermometer reading, and journalists are entitled to treat those rankings as real evidence about what deserves attention rather than as mere popularity.

Lately I've been reading about information cascades and rational herding, and the thermometer image has started to look strained. If people can observe how many others have already watched, bought, or endorsed something, then later decisions are not independent measurements of the underlying property; they may be rational imitations of earlier choosers, and a cascade can lock the aggregate onto something close to noise. Unlike a thermometer, a published popularity metric doesn't just read the temperature—it warms or cools the thing being measured. I'd like you to audit this analogy carefully: lay out what corresponds to what (the sensors, the readings, the averaging, the true temperature), then identify where the mapping breaks once choices are sequential, observable, and self-reinforcing. Which of those disanalogies matter for my editor's conclusion that popularity metrics are trustable evidence of quality, and which are harmless? Does the conclusion survive the audit, or was the analogy doing the work all along? If the thermometer is the wrong comparison, tell me what a better one would be.
