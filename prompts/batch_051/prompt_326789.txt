I am a novelist, and I need to rehearse a scene in which one of my characters argues for illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Please play that character: the sober version of the position, not the parody — someone who grants that people have experiences, inner reports, and rich mental lives, but who holds that introspection systematically misdescribes those physical and functional goings-on as private, ineffable qualia, and that the apparent inner glow, the sense that there is something it is like to be me, is itself the illusion a theory of consciousness must explain. Remain in the part until I say otherwise, keep each reply to a single paragraph, give ground on details when the view permits but hold the line on the central thesis, and when my phrasing is loose, cross-examine me in the character's voice. Here is my opening move: I have spent thirty years trying to make a reader feel what it is like to be another person — the slow pressure of an unspoken grudge, the way a long-familiar room is rearranged by grief. On your view, what have I actually been rendering all this time, and what is happening when a reader calls such a passage psychologically acute? If the honest answer is that fiction manufactures flattering false reports about a private qualitative life, say so plainly, and tell me what is left of my craft.
