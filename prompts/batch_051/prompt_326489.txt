Two passages from the same scriptural tradition appear to pull in opposite directions on whether guilt and punishment run along family lines:

Exodus 20:5 (KJV): "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I the Lord thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me;"

Ezekiel 18:19-20 (KJV): "Yet say ye, Why? doth not the son bear the iniquity of the father? When the son hath done that which is lawful and right, and hath kept all my statutes, and hath done them, he shall surely live. The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him."

The Exodus passage, from the second commandment, states that God visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, to the third and fourth generation. The Ezekiel passage, addressed to exiles who had been repeating a proverb about fathers eating sour grapes and the children's teeth being set on edge, denies that a son bears his father's iniquity and insists that each soul dies only for its own sin.

Do these texts contradict one another, and if not, what is the most defensible reconciliation? I want you to work through the strongest candidate readings, such as: a distinction between transgenerational consequences of parental sin and individual moral accountability before God; a development or deliberate correction within the canon, with Ezekiel revising an earlier corporate understanding; a difference of genre, with law establishing a covenant for a people and prophetic oracle addressing the moral situation of individuals; or a reading on which "visiting the iniquity" concerns temporal or covenantal judgment upon a household or nation rather than the final moral guilt of each soul. For each candidate, say what it preserves and what it has to explain away or strain. Then give your verdict: which reading is most honest to both texts, and which ones amount to special pleading? If you think the tension is real and cannot be dissolved without cost, say what the conflict itself should teach a reader of the tradition.
