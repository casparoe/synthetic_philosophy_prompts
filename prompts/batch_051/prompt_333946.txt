I'm in a philosophy of language seminar and we just read Frege's 1892 essay "On Sense and Reference" right before Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'", and I keep getting stuck on how the two actually line up. Here's the passage I'm stuck on:

"It is natural, now, to think of there being connected with a sign (name, combination of words, letter), besides that to which the sign refers, which may be called the reference of the sign, also what I should like to call the sense of the sign, wherein the mode of presentation is contained. In our example, accordingly, the reference of the expressions 'point of intersection of a and b' and 'point of intersection of b and c' would be the same, but not their senses. The reference of 'evening star' would be the same as that of 'morning star', but not the sense."

I get the basic move: the reference of "evening star" and "morning star" is the same (Venus), but the senses differ because the object is presented under different modes of presentation. What I can't get straight is what a "mode of presentation" is suppose to be — is it something in the speaker's head, some private association, or something more objective? A few paragraphs later Frege says the sense is grasped by everyone sufficiently familiar with the language, which sounds like it's meant to be shared rather than private. But then my professor keeps saying the upshot of Twin Earth is that meanings aren't in the head: on Twin Earth, my molecule-for-molecule duplicate says "water" but refers to XYZ while I refer to H2O. If sense is the mode of presentation and sense determines reference, would my duplicate and I have the same sense for "water" (because we're internally identical) or different senses (because the references differ)? And if Frege's senses are supposed to be objective and graspable by anyone who knows the language, should I read him as already leaning externalist, or as exactly the kind of internalist picture Putnam is attacking? Could you walk me through what "mode of presentation" is doing in the quoted passage and help me see where Putnam's argument cuts against Frege's distinction and where it leaves Frege alone?
