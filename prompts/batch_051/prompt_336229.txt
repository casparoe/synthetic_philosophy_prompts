i've got a rule for reading philosophy that i use all the time, and i can't tell if it's a shortcut or a bias. whenever an argument turns on a thought experiment, i first rewrite the scenario as a numbered list of only the facts that are supposed to do actual argumentative work—no adjectives about the people, no vivid details like the color of the room or the hum of the duplicating machine. if the intuition survives the deflated rewrite, i keep the argument. if it doesn't, or the conclusion suddenly looks forced, i throw the whole thing out as an intuition pump doing illigitimate work, even when i can't say exactly which premise failed. my friend says i'm just punishing people for being good writers, and that a lot of philosophical insight only shows up when you imagine the case concretely. but i've noticed the details i strip out almost never show up in the premises—they're there to make the conclusion feel inevitable. so: is my policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as rigor? when does vividness in a thought experiment track truth rather than just persuade, and where's the line between a detail that carries content and one that's pure rhetoric? give me a committed answer—either the rewrite-test is worth keeping, maybe in revised form, or it should be dropped—and defend it rather than just laying out the options.
