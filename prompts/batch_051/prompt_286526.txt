Two colleagues of mine have been having a running argument about a decision that one of them has to make, and I have become the bystander audience for it. I know enough to follow the general shape of the dispute but not enough to tell which specific claims are right, and I do not want to take a side without understanding each step. The people arguing are not me; I am only reporting what they said.

The situation is this. One of them manages a small enrollment campaign for a professional training program and has to decide whether to scale the campaign up now or leave it at its current level. A statistician has offered to show him a preliminary internal survey of potential applicants before he decides, with the caveat that the survey is noisy and was not designed for decision-making. The disagreement is over whether he should look at it.

The exchange, roughly as it happened:

(1) One colleague said that Good's theorem settles the question: for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditioning, the expected value of information is nonnegative. Since the survey is free and can only sharpen the manager's beliefs, looking has no downside in expectation; whatever it says, he remains free to ignore it and take exactly the action he would have taken without it. So refusing free information is never rational.

(2) The other replied that this theorem ignores everything important in the actual case. The survey is noisy and the manager will overreact to it; the campaign team already fixates on bad numbers. If it reads pessimistically, people lose motivation and a recoverable campaign gets wound down. Information that demoralizes you, or that you process badly, can be worse than not looking, even in expectation.

(3) The first answered that this confuses ex post regret with ex ante value. Good's theorem does not say the news will feel good or that the manager will not later wish he had not looked; it says that, in advance, expected utility over outcomes cannot decrease, because "ignore the signal" remains an available policy. If demoralization is itself a real consequence of looking, then one has simply attached a non-monetary price to the information: the theorem concerns genuinely costless information, and the fact that costly information can have negative net expected value does not refute it. Likewise, overreacting to noise is a departure from the theorem's conditioning premise, not a counterexample to it.

(4) The second responded that this defense still will not rescue the original claim. Even granting the theorem's internal assumptions, extra information can reduce expected payoff in advance once other agents are present or a later self will act differently. If the survey leaks, a competing program sees the numbers and adjusts; and if seeing lagging numbers weakens the manager's resolve, he may later do something that his current self would prefer to bind him against. In game-theoretic and commitment cases, the expected value of information can be negative.

(5) The first replied that those are exactly the cases in which the theorem's premises are not met: the information is not private to a single decision maker, or the decision maker cannot count on carrying out the "ignore it" policy because his later self will not cooperate. That is not a failure of the theorem; it is the theorem doing its work. It shows that when information is genuinely free, private, costlessly processed, and available to be ignored, the purely informational component cannot have negative expected value. If the survey leaks or erodes resolve, those are separate harms bundled with the signal, and they are the reasons to hesitate, not the information itself.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, separate what is correct from what is overstated or conflated, identify the assumption on which the contested claim turns, and rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then state carefully what a correct version of Good's theorem says, and say how much of the original practical disagreement—whether the manager should look at the survey—is actually settled by the theorem alone.
