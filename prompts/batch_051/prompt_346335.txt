I keep going back and forth on what the Bayesian convergence results actually show. On the one hand, the theorem seems like the strongest possible answer to the worry that priors are arbitrary: Blackwell and Dubins showed, roughly, that if two agents’ priors are mutually absolutely continuous—so they agree about which events have probability zero—then as they condition on a shared sequence of data, their posterior probability assignments merge in total variation with probability one, no matter how far apart they started. That sounds like priors wash out, full stop. On the other hand, I have a strong intuition that this is not the right lesson: two serious people can look at the same economic data for decades and still rationally disagree about something like inflation dynamics, and it doesn’t feel like either is violating Bayesianism or being irrational. They just started with different models, or different prior weight over model classes, and the finite data never fully kills that. I don’t want to be told “the theorem is true but people are irrational” or “the theorem is irrelevant because infinite data is unrealistic.” I want to know exactly what the theorem’s conditions buy, and whether my intuition survives as a point about finite time, model incompatibility, or something else. Which should yield: the intuition that rational disagreement can persist, or the idea that the theorem shows priors are ultimately just a transitional feature? What would it take to give up either side, and is there a reading on which both are right? Max 600 words, lead with the bottom line.
