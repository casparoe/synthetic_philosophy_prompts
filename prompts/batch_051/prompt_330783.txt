I serve on a five-person disciplinary panel for a state board of nursing that hears complaints about conduct that may be unprofessional without violating any written rule—a nurse who was cold to a dying patient's family, one who hid a minor charting mistake until it was harmless, one who accepted an extravagant gift from a grateful family. We have about an hour per case, none of us has philosophical training, and our written reasons must survive appeal. I want to ground our decisions in Adam Smith's idea of the impartial spectator, but I need it turned into an actual procedure: an ordered sequence of questions we can put to the facts, tests for when our own sympathy is being biased by likability, status, or mere conventional feeling, and explicit stopping points where the procedure hands the matter back to judgment. Give me that checklist, flag the steps that are judgment calls disguised as technique, then run it on the three cases above, including at least one where it would push us toward a decision we will not like. Finally, say what the impartial spectator cannot do for a panel like ours, and where we would be fooling ourselves if we thought the procedure had removed our bias.
