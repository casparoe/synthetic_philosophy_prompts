I am a lecturer who works on risk communication, and for years I have enforced a private rule about my own expressed uncertainty: I never state a credence as a single number; I only give intervals. If a colleague asks how likely I think the funding application is to succeed, I will not say 0.7; I will say somewhere between 0.6 and 0.8, and I will not be moved beyond that. If someone presses me for a point value, I refuse, and I privately suspect that the pressing is a demand for manufactured precision rather than for warranted honesty. The rule also governed my formal judgements when I chaired a departmental committee: I reported only ranges for the probability that each proposal would meet its targets, which drove one member to distraction.

He says the rule is evasive. An interval, he argues, does not tell anyone what to do; and whenever I actually act — deciding which proposal to back, or whether to cycle rather than take the train — I behave exactly as if I had settled on some particular number, so the interval is just a way of refusing to own the commitments my actions already reveal. I reply that this begs the question: I can choose an action that is not dominated across the whole interval and decline to pick a point inside it, and the claim that action always reveals a hidden point credence presumes a picture of practical rationality in which precise credences are mandatory — which is precisely what is in dispute.

I would like a third-party verdict on the habit itself, not on my colleague. Is an interval-only policy a defensible epistemic discipline, a bias dressed up as a principle, or something that is right for some tasks and wrong for others? Please be specific about the difference between a credence and a betting rate, about the circumstances in which interval-valued credences genuinely fail to determine choice, and about when refusing to sharpen an interval is intellectual honesty rather than avoidance of commitment. Also tell me whether the rule could be restated so as to keep its benefits without its costs. Answer in British English. Finally, say what would change your overall verdict: which argument, formal result, or kind of counterexample would most plausibly push the assessment the other way, and where in your own reasoning you are least confident.
