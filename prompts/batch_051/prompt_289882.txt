I've been reading around the old metaphysic of science debate about whether causation is real or just a folk notion, and I can't figure out why it never moves. The eliminativist side says fundamental physics gives us laws and differential equations, and 'cause' never appears in the equations; causal talk is something we project onto events for convenience, and Russell made a version of this point over a century ago when he described the law of causality as a hangover from prescientific thought. The realist side says that is obviously wrong: you cannot run a single experiment, interpret a mechanism, or explain why anything happened without causation, and a world with all the same equations but no causal order would be a different world. The reductionist side says causation is real but not fundamental—it reduces to counterfactual dependence, or regularity, or difference-making under interventions.

What bothers me is that all three sides seem to agree on the same facts about what physics looks like and about what practicing scientists actually say and do. The disagreement is entirely about what those facts mean for the word 'causation'. Yet nobody moves. My working theory is that each side means something different by the word—one means a productive tie between events, one means whatever grounds explanation, one means difference-making—and so the debate is at least partly verbal, but each side insists the others are just changing the subject.

I don't want a survey of Hume and Lewis and interventionism. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a hidden verbal dispute, a clash of starting intuitions about what an explanation has to do, a dispute about what counts as "reducing" something, or a substantive metaphysical question we happen not to be able to settle yet? And under your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a formal result, a distinction everyone could accept, a change of question, or should we expect the stalemate to just continue? Be honest about whether the question is well-posed in the first place.
