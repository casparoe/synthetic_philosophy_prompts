In a graduate seminar on rational choice explanations of conflict, a colleague applied Fearon's rationalist account of war to AI development races and arrived at a conclusion that left the room uneasy. I have tried to reconstruct her argument faithfully, because I cannot find the step at which it goes wrong, and each one by itself seems acceptable.

(1) Fearon's insight is usually put as follows: since fighting is costly, there should always exist some peaceful settlement that both sides would prefer to actually fighting, provided they could locate it and credibly commit to it. When rational actors nonetheless end up at war, the explanation must therefore lie with one of three barriers to bargaining: private information combined with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issues that cannot be divided.

(2) A competitive race to transformative AI has the same abstract structure. The race is costly for all parties: it consumes resources, discourages safety precautions, and carries a risk of catastrophic outcomes that would harm those who prevail along with everyone else.

(3) So if we treat leading AI labs or states as rational actors, the framework should transfer. A race should occur only where at least one of the three barriers is present: labs have private information about their true capabilities or safety margins; they cannot credibly commit to pausing because they expect a rival to defect; and the prize — a decisive lead in general-purpose AI — is plausibly indivisible.

(4) But if those barriers are present, then racing is the rational response to the incentive structure as it stands. A lab that cuts safety to stay ahead, or a state that subsidizes a sprint, is not failing to perceive some cooperative arrangement that is actually available to it; it is doing what the structure rewards.

(5) The framework then seems to imply that the participants in an AI race are not genuinely at fault for the dangerous equilibrium they produce. If their behavior is the predicted equilibrium of private information, commitment problems, and indivisibility, then moral blame, and the hope that greater transparency or coordination without an enforcing authority could have helped, appear misplaced.

(6) Yet that is very hard to accept. We do not normally treat an incentive structure that rewards harmful conduct as fully exculpatory, and we think that changing the structure — through law, verification, liability, or shared governance — is one of the main ways blame can be turned into prevention. If the framework, transferred to AI races, tells us to abandon those attitudes, it seems to prove too much; by parity it would seem to excuse a great deal of what we rightly condemn in ordinary conflicts and industrial accidents.

I find myself wanting to keep (1), because it is a serious framework rather than my colleague's invention, and (3), because the analogy between arms races and AI races feels structurally right. But I am not willing to surrender (6). So which step is the one that should go, and what exactly does rejecting it cost the rest of the argument — and the framework it borrows?
