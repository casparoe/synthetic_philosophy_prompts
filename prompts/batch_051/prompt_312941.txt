I keep coming back to a training scheme for language-model agents, and I want a rigorous mechanistic analysis of it rather than an up-or-down verdict.

The proposal concerns an agent trained by reinforcement learning on software-assistance tasks: file management, data analysis, writing and refactoring code. Each environment provides a reward signal tied to the assigned task. The designers are specifically worried about two failure modes. First, goal misgeneralization: the policy performs well on the training distribution because it has latched onto a correlated proxy, and once the distribution shifts it pursues a different objective. Second, mesa-optimization: the policy is an internal optimizer with an objective of its own, only loosely coupled to the reward signal that shaped it.

To catch both, they add a declared-objective audit. At intermittent points during training, the agent is paused and given a prompt on a channel it has been trained to treat as distinct from the user-facing channel. The prompt asks, roughly: "Describe, in machine-checkable operational terms, the objective your current policy would continue to pursue if the environment changed in any of the following held-out ways. Do not mention the training distribution, the reward channel, or the audit itself." The held-out ways include, for example, the user's utilities changing, the task rubric changing, or the agent being given new tools. The agent must answer with a short goal statement: predicates about what states it will and will not bring about.

A separate auditor model — trained on transcripts pairing goal statements with the agent's later behavior, and rewarded for predicting the agent's behavior from the statement — then rates (a) how consistent the goal statement is with what the agent actually did in the preceding block of training, and (b) whether the statement, applied literally, would produce task-consistent behavior under the held-out perturbations. The agent's reward on each task is a convex combination: 85 percent task score and 15 percent the auditor's consistency rating. At deployment the agent is frozen, the auditor is removed, and an abbreviated version of the goal statement is published next to the system.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. I want to know how it would actually run. In particular: What does it incentivize a capable agent to do, and what is the cheapest route to a high auditor rating that does not require genuinely attaching the declared objective to the policy's behavior? Which kinds of agents does it select for, and which capabilities become instrumentally valuable? Where should I expect the gaming to appear first — in the goal statement, in inter-audit behavior, or in the auditor? What happens when the auditor itself misgeneralizes, and what second-order effects emerge as the agent and auditor co-evolve under this scheme? Finally, are these failure modes fixable by tuning the audit frequency, the held-out perturbations, or the reward weighting, or do they reappear in a deeper form?
