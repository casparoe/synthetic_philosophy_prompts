I've been going round in circles on the interventionist vs. counterfactual theories of causation thing, mostly via Pearl-flavoured blog posts and a friend who does causal inference for a living. His line is that interventionism isn't really a competitor to counterfactual accounts — it's counterfactuals with graph-theoretic discipline. I tried writing his argument out as numbered steps to see where it broke, and I got this:

(1) On the interventionist account, C causes E roughly when there is a possible intervention on C that would change E while holding all the other variables fixed. (2) But a statement about what would happen if you intervened on C is itself a counterfactual statement: it describes what would occur under a non-actual manipulation of C. (3) So each interventionist causal claim is equivalent to a counterfactual claim about a manipulation, with the same truth conditions. (4) If that's right, interventionism isn't a distinct theory of causation; it's just the counterfactual theory with a preferred notation. (5) Therefore interventionism collapses into the counterfactual theory.

I find the premises plausible and the inference looks valid step-to-step, but the conclusion seems too quick, because the two views are treated as live rivals for a reason. My suspicion is that step (2) is quietly sliding between two different senses of 'counterfactual', but I can't say exactly what the two senses are or weather the slide actually matters. So: is this argument valid? If not, which step fails — or which hidden move between steps — and what's the strongest reading under which the argument goes through? And if there is a real difference, tell me exactly where a Woodward-style interventionist digs in, rather than just saying it all depends on your semantics.
