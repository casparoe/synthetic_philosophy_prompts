I sit on the bylaws committee of a debating society. Our Code of Debate, adopted in 1972 for in-person meetings, contains this rule:

“Rule 9(c). A presupposition of a speaker’s remark that is not yet common ground becomes part of the common ground when the remark is made, unless a participant raises an objection before the next participant has finished speaking. Once the next participant has finished speaking without objection, the presupposition may no longer be challenged.”

The society has recently begun holding some formal debates on a text-based platform with a fifteen-minute edit window. During a debate over the society’s reserve funds, one member posted a long speech containing this sentence: “Since the reserves are already at the society’s free disposal, the only question before us is whether this particular grant is wise.” The proposition that the reserves are at the society’s free disposal was not in the common ground before the post, and it was not the main point of the sentence. Two participants read the post almost immediately (the platform records that they opened it); neither objected, and both began drafting replies that took the proposition for granted. No reply was posted before the edit window closed. Fourteen minutes after posting, the member edited the speech to delete the sentence, so that participants reading later never saw it. The chair, who was in a different time zone, did not open the platform until the next morning.

You are an expert on the pragmatics of conversation, working in the Lewis–Stalnaker tradition that treats common ground, presupposition, and accommodation as matters of conversational scorekeeping. I am less interested in what the society’s rules of order would settle in practice than in what Rule 9(c), read as a principle of scorekeeping, says about this case. Take the rule’s wording first, then its purpose and what it is there to protect, and then what the framers would plausibly have said if they had been asked to imagine asynchronous text with an edit window. For each of those three approaches, say whether the proposition entered the common ground, and whether it was still there after the edit. Then tell me which approach should govern, and what the case reveals about accommodation when a remark’s audience is fragmented and the remark can be retracted. Please reason through the alternatives carefully; start by brainstorming the candidate readings and their consequences before settling on a final assessment.
