I've been reading parts of David Hume's essay on the standard of taste, as an interested non-specialist, and I keep getting tangled up over how much agreement the position really predicts. The way I understand it, Hume grants that beauty is not literally out there in the object; it is a sentiment that arises in a properly prepared mind. Yet he also thinks taste is not arbitrary, because some responses are more authoritative than others. The standard, on my reading, is set by the joint verdict of true judges, where a true judge is someone who has five qualifications: delicacy, practice in the relevant art form, breadth of comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Hume seems to think that such judges will largely agree, and that the disagreement that remains is the relatively harmless kind that flows from differences of age, culture, or personal temperament.

I am collecting pressure tests for this idea, and I want you to build me a case with the following specification. Two people must both plainly possess all five of those qualifications, and yet they must flatly disagree about the beauty of a single painting. The case is for probing the gap between saying the qualifications usually produce agreement and saying they guarantee it, so the disagreement has to be generated by the setup rather than stipulated: you may not simply announce that one judge is more sensitive or that the painting is "really" beautiful. Constraints: keep the scenario realistic — a gallery, a private showing, a conservator's studio, nothing fantastical; make the disagreement about beauty, not about price, attribution, or moral content; and make the two judges alike in age, culture, and temperament so the dispute cannot be dismissed as Hume's blameless variation. Ideally, the disagreement should survive each judge hearing the other's full reasons, so it cannot be resolved by pointing to some overlooked detail.

Please work as an expert on eighteenth-century aesthetics and the internal logic of Hume's position, think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanisms for the disagreement before settling on the one a defender of the standard would find hardest to absorb. Then give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) a check that each of the five qualifications is met by both judges; (3) each judge's verdict and the reasoning that supports it; (4) which feature of the case is doing the work and why it is neither a failure of one of the five qualifications nor ordinary blameless variation; (5) what a defender of the standard of taste would say to stop the case from counting, and what would happen to the case if you removed the feature identified in (4).
