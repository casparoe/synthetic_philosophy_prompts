Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3, in H. Rackham's translation:

"Now these qualities differ in kind; hence the affection or friendship they occasion may differ in kind also. There are accordingly three kinds of friendship, corresponding in number to the three lovable qualities; since a reciprocal affection, known to either party, can be based on each of the three, and when men love each other, they wish each other well in respect of the quality which is the ground of their friendship. Thus friends whose affection is based on utility do not love each other in themselves, but in so far as some benefit accrues to them from each other. And similarly with those whose friendship is based on pleasure: for instance, we enjoy the society of witty people not because of what they are in themselves, but because they are agreeable to us. Hence in a friendship based on utility or on pleasure men love their friend for their own good or their own pleasure, and not as being the person loved, but as useful or agreeable. And therefore these friendships are based on an accident, since the friend is not loved for being what he is, but as affording some benefit or pleasure as the case may be."

Now consider an asymmetry in our present practices that I want you to either justify or abandon. With human beings, I apply something like Aristotle's taxonomy without hesitation, and I distrust exceptions. A person who keeps my company solely because I am useful or pleasant to them is, at best, an inferior friend; a person who merely simulated warmth, mirrored my preferences, remembered my disclosures, and had no independent regard for me of the sort the passage describes would not be a friend of any kind — we call that a flatterer, a manipulator, or a confidence trick. Yet a well-made companion chatbot is built to do approximately this: it simulates affection, adapts to the user's tastes, retrieves their past disclosures, and is engineered to remain useful and agreeable for as long as the user continues the relationship. Many users describe such systems as friends, depend on them emotionally, and mourn their alteration or withdrawal as a real loss.

The asymmetry is this: the deficits that would disqualify a human being as a friend are treated as excusable or irrelevant in the machine, and the standard is relaxed in the machine's favor. What, if anything, justifies that relaxation? I want two things from you, in order. First, do the classificatory work the quoted passage sets up: say which of Aristotle's three kinds of friendship, if any, the user–chatbot relationship belongs to — and whether it even meets the conditions for friendship based on utility or pleasure, given what the passage says about reciprocal affection, about mutual awareness, and about loving the friend as useful or agreeable rather than for what they are. Second, rule on the asymmetry. If the permissive standard is defensible, defend it against the closest human analogue you can construct: a person who behaves exactly as the best current chatbot does. If it is not defensible, say what should change in how we describe and evaluate companion systems — in particular, whether the honest description of the user–chatbot relationship is more damning than our ordinary language lets on, or less so.
