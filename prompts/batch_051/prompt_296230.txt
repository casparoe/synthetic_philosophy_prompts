I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so put on your philosopher-of-religion hat and think carefully with me here. My understanding of the Molinist notion of middle knowledge is this: before God decides which world to actualize, God knows all the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — for any possible person and any possible situation, God knows what that person would freely choose. The criteria I've seen are roughly: (i) it's about genuinely free choices, not determined outcomes; (ii) it covers all possible circumstances; (iii) it's infallible; and (iv) it's had prior, in the order of explanation rather than in time, to any decision to make those circumstances actual.

Now here's the case that got me interested. I keep a character bible, and at some point my main character started to feel less like someone I'm deciding for and more like someone I'm discovering: for a huge range of scenes that will never make the final draft, I can tell you what she'd do, with a strong conviction that it's already fixed by who she is, and that if I wrote those scenes I'd be reporting rather than authoring them. This knowledge is there before I decide which scenes to actualize on the page, and it extends well past anything I'll ever write. I'm not claiming literal infallibility, but for the settled core of her it feels less like a guess than most things I say about real people.

Now the question: does what I have about my character qualify as middle knowledge, or is something essential missing? Restate the criteria in your own words first, then work through them seperately and say where the comparison holds up and where it stumbles. At the end, tell me whether the criteria are too permissive, or whether the missing piece is something the criteria don't yet mention — I honestly can't tell if the problem is that she isn't a real free agent or that I'm also her creator.
