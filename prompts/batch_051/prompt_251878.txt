I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep running into the same problem: I'm expected to say whether a film, an album, an exhibition, or a book is good on a deadline, but I often havn't seen, read, or heard more than a few clips, pages, or photos. So I end up leaning on other critics, audience reactions, and the artist's own statements. I know philosophers have a whole debate about aesthetic testimony — roughly, whether you can justifiably form aesthetic beliefs on someone else's say-so. Kant is one of the hardliners. In section 33 of the Critique of Judgement, the section called the second peculiarity of the judgement of taste, he argues, to paraphrase, that taste is autonomous: other people's judgments cannot give your aesthetic judgment its basis, and no amount of testimony or authority can settle for you whether something is beautiful. I want a reusable decision procedure for when I can rely on other people's aesthetic verdicts in my writing and when I can't, grounded in that debate but usable without reading the journals. It should be a checklist in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure forces me to hedge or to clearly report someone else's judgment instead of making my own, and where it stops and I have to either experience the thing myself or drop the piece. Run it on three worked cases: a film I saw only a ten-minute excerpt of but two reviewers I trust have praised; a novel I read half of and found dull but whose ending everyone says redeems it; and an art show I only saw as installation photos. Make one of them a case where the procedure tells me not to do something I'd normally do without thinking. And as you go, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, so you don't present one camp's view as if it were the consensus.
