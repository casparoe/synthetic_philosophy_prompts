I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on chance, determinism, and the rise of statistical physics. We read the following passage from James Clerk Maxwell’s *Theory of Heat* (1871), where he states the second law of thermodynamics:

“One of the best established facts in thermodynamics is that it is impossible in a system enclosed in an envelope which permits neither change of volume nor passage of heat, and in which both the temperature and the pressure are everywhere the same, to produce any inequality of temperature or of pressure without the expenditure of work. This is the second law of thermodynamics.”

In one of the course handouts, a graduate student summarized what Maxwell is doing in the passages around this one as follows. Maxwell’s point is that the second law is not a genuine law of nature, only a statistical regularity that holds because we cannot track individual molecules. His thought experiment about a being who could sort fast and slow molecules shows that a sufficiently clever being could make heat flow from a colder to a warmer body and reverse entropy, so the second law has no real necessity. Since the macroscopic direction of heat flow is just an average over disordered molecular motions, Maxwell concluded that the microscopic world is indeterministic.

I want to know how fair that summary is to Maxwell’s actual text and argument. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence: identify which parts are accurate, which are overstatements, which conflate statistical, epistemic, and more absolute claims about the second law, and which conclusions do not follow. In particular, does the passage itself commit Maxwell to saying the second law has no real necessity, or to saying anything about microscopic indeterminism? What exactly would Maxwell be entitled to claim from the passage and the surrounding argument?
