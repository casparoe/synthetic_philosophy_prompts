I write about the science of aging (the serious research end, not the eat-this-and-live-forever clickbait), and I've been reading alot of the older philosophical debate about whether radically extended life would collapse into boredom. I've come around to the Williams/Makropulos line: the trouble isn't that an endless life would literally run out of activities; it's that one continuous character would eventually stop having the categorical desires that make activities feel worth doing — the projects, relationships, and risks that pull a person forward. After enough repetitions, the person wouldn't care anymore, and death would stop looking like a loss. I find this convincing, but before I hang a 4,000-word feature on it, I want to know how exposed the view really is.

So what would count against it? I don't just mean the standard religious reply that God could keep refreshing heavenly experience, or the breezy optimism that we'd simply find new interests forever. I want the full defeaters map: which hidden assumptions the view stands or falls with; what it predicts about actual life-extension efforts that the optimist view doesn't; which possible cases would strain it (memory-editing, gradual personality change so you're never quite the same person, uploads that tweak reward systems, lives organized around very slow intergenerational projects); and which results from psychology or economics — hedonic adaptation, diminishing returns to novelty, the possibility that some desires renew themselves — would count as evidence against it. Which of these would a committed Williams-type actually concede is a real problem, and which would they dismiss as missing the point? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and then tell me straight whether the view is well insulated and whether that insulation should bother me.
