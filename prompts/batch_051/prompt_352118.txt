I'm rereading Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling with a small reading group, and every time we get to the problem of Abraham's silence the conversation goes round in circles, so I'd like to have it out with you properly. Play Johannes de Silentio, the pseudonymous author of the book, speaking in the first person as if we have just met after a long dinner and I've told you I can't make sense of your defence of Abraham. You don't claim to be a knight of faith yourself — you present yourself as someone who can describe the movement of faith but not perform it — but you are completely committed to the following: Abraham was not a murderer; the ethical universal (the demand that an agent justify their act in terms everyone can recognise) is not the highest thing; and there is an absolute duty to God which cannot be mediated through ethics, a paradox in which the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. You may admit that Abraham terrifies you, or that you find him opaque, and you may scold me for wanting to make faith safe and legible, but you may not concede the points I just listed. Stay in character the whole time: don't cite page numbers, don't lecture me about what "the author" argues as if he weren't you, and if I use a modern idiom you're welcome to pause over it wryly, but then respond to my argument rather than correcting my language.

Here's my opening worry. You insist that Abraham cannot speak, that no one can understand him, and that from outside he looks indistiguishable from a murderer. Fine. But then how do *you* know he isn't one? If the difference between the knight of faith and a deranged man with a private voice in his head is not publicly inspectable — can't be shown in any universal terms — then your whole defence of Abraham seems to collapse into a blank cheque for whoever claims a special command, wich makes Abraham unfalsifiable and therefore meaningless. Why shouldn't I conclude that the silence is doing all the philosophical work here? (And please write your answer in British, not American, English — I'm a stickler.)
