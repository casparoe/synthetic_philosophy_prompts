A colleague of mine teaches a graduate seminar in the theory of interpretation and has, over the years, recorded the rulings she gives in class on disputed examples. I have kept a file of those rulings, and before I put a new dispute to her I am trying to work out what principle she has actually been applying.

Her recorded rulings, in order:

1. A short lyric contains an ambiguous stanza. Its most natural reading is P, and P is also the reading that makes the poem aesthetically stronger; however, in a published interview the poet said the stanza means Q, and Q is a reading the words can bear without outright distortion. She ruled that the stanza means Q.

2. A novel published in the 1960s contains a word whose ordinary sense has narrowed considerably since. She ruled that the word bears the sense it had at the date of publication rather than the sense a present-day reader would give it.

3. Asked whether the typographical arrangement of a poem — its unusual indentation, which groups certain words visually — is part of the poem's meaning, she ruled that it is: the printed form is part of the work and affects how the lines are to be read.

4. In an essay in a literary magazine, a short-story writer said she had intended the final chapter of her latest book to be read as irony, though the book itself leaves both an ironic and a straight reading open. She ruled that the chapter is best read as ironic.

5. A living novelist gave a public lecture in which she said that a recurrent image in her book signifies the protagonist's unresolved guilt. The connection is not made explicit in the text, but it fits the text once it is noticed. She ruled that the image carries that significance.

None of her recorded rulings has yet forced her to say what weight, if any, private evidence of an author's intention — an unpublished diary, a private letter, a confidential note — should carry.

The new dispute is this. A posthumously published novella has always been read as meaning R; all the public evidence, including the text, the author's published notes, and interviews given before her death, points to R. The executor of the estate has now found a private diary entry in which the author says she intended the novella to mean S. S is a reading the words can be made to bear, but nothing in the public record points toward S, and a reader working only from the public record would not arrive at it. My colleague has been asked to decide whether the novella's meaning is R or S.

What should she say if she is being consistent with the rulings above, and what is she most likely to say? Lay out the interpretive principles that fit her recorded rulings, state what each live principle requires in the novella case, identify the closest analog among the earlier rulings, and say which principle her record best supports. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what adopting each possible rule would commit her to, and describe the further case that would most cleanly settle which principle she holds.
