I am a novelist, and I teach a small seminar on point of view in first-person fiction. Last week I set the following short-answer question on Moore's paradox as an entry point into the craft of unreliable narration. Below is one student's answer. Please grade it as if you were the instructor: assign a mark out of 10, and then provide the specific point-by-point comments that justify that mark, noting any errors or confusions precisely rather than giving a general impression.

Question:

Moore observed that a sentence of the form "p, but I don't believe that p"—for instance, "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining"—can be true even though it is absurd to assert. Explain exactly what goes wrong in such an assertion, and then discuss what your account implies for one device available to a fiction writer: a first-person narrator who writes, of an earlier scene, "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was raining."

Student answer:

Moore's example is not a formal contradiction, since the first conjunct and the second can both be true: rain can fall whether or not I am aware of it. The trouble must therefore lie in what the speaker does in uttering the whole sentence. When someone asserts that it is raining, she presents herself as believing that it is raining; assertion, we might say, expresses belief. The second conjunct then retracts the very state the first conjunct projects, which is why the utterance is called a pragmatic contradiction. Because the two conjuncts cancel each other in this way, a sincere speaker cannot use the sentence to state a truth.

For the fiction writer this gives a sharp diagnostic. A first-person narrator who says "It was raining, but I didn't believe it was raining" is doing in one breath what Moore's present-tense speaker does: she vouches for the rain and then denies having the belief that vouching for it would require. The result is that the narrator comes across as insincere or self-deluded, someone whose relation to her own mind is no longer to be trusted.

There is a qualification, however. The omissive form "p, but I don't believe that p" and the commissive form "p, but I believe that not-p" differ in degree rather than kind: the commissive version is worse, because it sets a positive belief against the asserted content, whereas the omissive version merely withholds it. And once the sentence is shifted into the past tense—"it was raining, and at that time I didn't believe it"—the paradox dissolves, because a later self can report an earlier self's ignorance without conflict. So the paradox, as it applies here, is best seen as a present-tense phenomenon; the retrospective narrator can report a past failure of belief without falling into it.
