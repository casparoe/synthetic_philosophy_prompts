A line of argument I keep meeting in constitutional theory runs like this. In the Odyssey, Ulysses wants to hear the Sirens' song without being destroyed by it, so he has his crew stop their ears with wax and lash him to the mast, ordering them to ignore his later pleas to be untied until the danger is past. The argument then says a constitution — or at least an entrenched bill of rights enforced by courts — is a society's version of that device. Knowing in advance that it will sometimes be seized by fear, rage, or majority panic, a people binds itself by entrenching certain rights and removing them from ordinary majoritarian revision, and instructs the courts to ignore the majority's later demands. From this the conclusion is drawn that constitutional limits on majority rule are best understood and defended as a people's self-binding, not as rule by the dead or by unelected judges.

I want you to audit how far this analogy actually holds. Please lay out, in prose, what is supposed to correspond to what across the two cases. Then press on the disanalogies: what Ulysses knows about the temptation and about how long it will last; who does the tying and under what terms the person bound can later be untied; whether the crew and the ship are the right stand-ins for courts and citizens; whether a whole people across generations can stand in the position of a single agent binding itself. For each disanalogy you identify, say whether it matters for the self-binding conclusion and why. Finally, tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy if you think there is one. Please write in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a letter, and use American English.
