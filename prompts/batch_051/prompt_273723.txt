I've been turning over a slogan that keeps getting quoted in my local, and I want someone to actually take it apart instead of just nodding along. The phrase is 'an injury to one is an injury to all.' Last week, during a contract fight, one of the older members used it to end a debate about whether we should accept a two-tier wage scale for new hires. His argument was: if we let the company pay the new people less, that's an injury to them, and since an injury to one is an injury to all, rejecting the concession isn't optional. The room nodded and moved on, but I dont think the sentence actually did the work everyone acted like it did.

I can think of a few things it might be claiming. It could be a predictive claim: a pay cut for the next cohort eventually drags down everyone's baseline, so the harm literally spreads. It could be a moral claim about solidarity, that we have an obligation to treat harm to any member as harm to ourselves even when it doesn't cascade. It could be a claim about what the real unit is: that the 'I' who gets injured is the union or the class rather than the individual worker, so it's not a metaphor. Or it could just be a warm way of saying 'that seems unfair.' There are probably others. Can you tease apart the distinct precise claims packed into that one sentence, say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false if read literally, and tell me which reading the older member actually needed for the two-tier argument—and whether it survives scrutiny?
