Recently I had to decide whether to accept a medical treatment for which the clinical evidence was genuinely mixed, and a colleague pressed two essays on me: W. K. Clifford’s “The Ethics of Belief” and William James’s “The Will to Believe.” I was stopped by a distinction in James’s essay: “They may be (a) living or dead; (b) forced or avoidable; (c) momentous or trivial; and for our purposes we may call an option a genuine option when it is of the forced, living and momentous kind.” My decision seems to meet his test: declining to decide is itself a decision, both alternatives are live for me, and the stakes are not recoverable. On my reading, James thinks that when an option is genuine, the passional side of our nature may settle it. Does it follow that the balance of evidence is beside the point—that I could believe the treatment will work even when the studies lean against it, and that Clifford’s demand to proportion belief to evidence simply has no grip on options like mine? If that is too strong, what role does James still leave for evidence, and where exactly does my reading go wrong?
