An economist I work with ends most policy disagreements with the proverb "actions speak louder than words," and then treats people's answers to surveys about their own preferences as largely irrelevant. I have also heard the same proverb used to argue that an addict's relapses reveal what he "really" wants, that utility is nothing over and above whatever a person chooses, and that people always choose what is best for themselves. These cannot all be the same claim. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this proverb might be used to assert — about the reliability of self-report, about what preference or utility are, and about welfare — and say, for each, whether it is a harmless methodological heuristic, a substantive but defensible commitment, or an overclaim. In particular, tell me which reading, if any, actually licenses ignoring stated preferences in favor of observed choices, and which further readings would be needed to support the stronger welfare conclusions.
