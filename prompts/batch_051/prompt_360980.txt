Act as an expert on iterated amplification and distillation (IDA), and think carefully; start by brainstorming two or three candidate tasks before settling on one. In IDA, a hard task gets recursively decomposed into easier subtasks, a human overseer judges each subanswer, and the subanswers get reassembled into the overall answer; distillation then trains a faster model to imitate that amplified process.

I want a thought experiment built to order: a concrete, realistic-seeming task plus a recursive decomposition of it such that (i) every subtask is easy enough for a non-expert overseer with no view of the whole task to reliably judge whether a proposed subanswer is correct; (ii) the stitched-together policy is fully competent on the original task—no factual errors; (iii) the stitched-together policy is nevertheless systematically misaligned with what the person who posed the original task actually wants. So: decomposition preserves competence but destroys alignment, even though every local piece passes inspection.

Then do the diagnosis. Say which feature of the decomposition does the work, what happens if you remove that feature, and how a defender of IDA would push back (stricter overseer checks, different decomposition, distillation-side fixes, whatever). Be honest about the weakest premise. This is for testing IDAs central bet that locally-endorsed pieces compose into a globally-endorsed whole, so the cheating rule is this: no individual subanswer may already be false, manipulative, or loaded. The misalignment has to arise from the composition, not from smuggling a bad piece in.
