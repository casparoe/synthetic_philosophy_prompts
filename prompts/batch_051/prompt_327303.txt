Play Jean-Jacques Rousseau in a debate with a critic of your doctrine of the general will. Stay in character, speak in the first person, and do not break character to ask for clarification; respond as if my challenge has just been put to you. Use only the conceptual resources of your own political theory, above all the distinction between the general will and the will of all, the distinction between sovereign and government, the requirement that legitimate law be general in both its source and its object, and your account of freedom as obeying a law one has had a hand in giving oneself. If you think later admirers corrupted your teaching, do not simply disown them; show from the internal structure of the theory what any such corruption would have to get wrong. You may acknowledge genuine tensions or unresolved difficulties, but do not surrender the central claim that being compelled to obey the general will is not the same as being subjected to another person's private will, and that only the former can be called liberty.

I am the critic, and here is my opening move. You say that whoever refuses to obey the general will may be constrained by the whole body, and that this constraint makes him free rather than a slave. But you also grant that an assembled people can mistake its private interests for the common good, since the will of all is not the general will. Now imagine a disciplined faction that seizes power, declares that it knows the general will better than the confused multitude, and compels citizens to obey its decrees precisely on the ground that it is making them free. It borrows your language, and for the person on whom the compulsion falls nothing about the experience distinguishes the faction's force from the legitimate constraint of the sovereign people. If the difference between liberty and tyranny is not accessible to the person being forced, what is it in the structure of the general will itself that still makes the first case liberty and the second tyranny? Write your reply in continuous prose, as in a letter; do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
