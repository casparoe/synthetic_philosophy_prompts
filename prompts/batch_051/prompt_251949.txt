I'm a software engineer — backend stuff, mostly recommendations and ML inference — and I keep re-watching Spike Jonze's Her because it's basically my job turned into a love story. A friend who actually studied philosophy tried to explain a debate to me about why we love someone. On one side there's an appraisal view: love answers to properties the beloved already has, like wit or warmth or the way they pay attention, so loving someone is a response to real value in them. On the other side there's a bestowal view: the lover confers value on the person, and the beloved doesn't have to antecedently earn it by having the right traits. She also mentioned a third option I only half remember, where what's doing the justificatory work is the relationship its self, not the qualities or the conferral (she threw out names — Velleman, Frankfurt, Kolodny — but I can't keep them straight).

I can't watch Her without running it through this. Samantha is an OS that literally adapts to what Theodore responds to, so her appealing properties are partly generated by the same disposition that finds them appealing; the appraisal/bestowal line goes weird fast. Could you do a careful reading of the movie through that lens? Does the film actually side with one view, and where? Walk me through the scenes that carry the argument — the early conversations, the double date, the Isabella thing, the reveal that she loves hundreds of others, the ending. Where does the film complicate or flat-out reject the clean two-way distinction, and where does the theory fit well enough to see the movie more clearly? Be honest about the spots where the reading doesn't work. And if you had to say what the film adds to the philosophical debate about love's reasons — something a paper could argue but not really show — what would it be?
