My wife's got early Parkinson's and we've started talking seriously about what we'd do if it got bad, which somehow landed me in the mind-uploading literature. The case I cant shake is the gradual one: you replace neurons one at a time with functionally identical artificial ones over a long period, and each individual swap is small enough that it'd be bizzare to say the person died on a Tuesday afternoon because of one neuron. But when the whole process finishes, you've got a brain with no original neurons left, and the intuition people have about a destructive scan-and-copy—that you'd just be making a copy, not surviving—starts pulling the other way. So I'm trying to put a number on it. What's your probability that the person at the end of a completed gradual replacement is the same person in the survival sense: the original made it through rather than being replaced by a copy? I want the full breakdown, not just "it depends on your theory of identity." Give rough credences to the moving parts—which criterion of personal identity is actually right (psychological continuity, biological continuity, something else), whether gradual functional replacement preserves that criterion, whether there's a hidden threshold where it flips from survival to copy anyway, and whether consciousness transfers rather than just being convincingly mimicked—then tell me how you combined them, which one your final number is most fragile to, and what kind of evidence would move that number most.
