A municipal health authority proposes the following lexicographic decision rule for all public health and social-policy interventions. Each proposed intervention receives two estimates: (a) the expected change, relative to no intervention, in person-hours of pain at or above intensity 7 on a 0–10 scale, and (b) the expected change in net sub-threshold well-being, defined as total pleasure minus total pain below that intensity. The rule is: any intervention that increases the expected above-threshold pain of even one person is rejected, no matter how much sub-threshold well-being it creates for others; among the remaining interventions, the authority chooses the one with the greatest total net sub-threshold well-being. The rule is offered as an institutionalization of the view that severe pain and pleasure are not commensurable, while mild pain and pleasure are. Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a moral position to be endorsed. In exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion, answer in this order: (1) what the rule incentivizes patients to do, (2) what it incentivizes clinicians to do, (3) what it incentivizes administrators and the pain-measurement system to do, (4) how the intensity threshold, the person-hours accounting, and the net sub-threshold well-being index can be gamed or measurably distorted, and (5) how the rule behaves in triage cases where every available intervention leaves at least one person above threshold, and whether any of these failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the lexicographic priority.
