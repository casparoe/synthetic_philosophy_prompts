I have been reading about theories of distributive justice on my own, not as part of any course, and I keep returning to a chapter in an introductory book that explains the disagreement between John Rawls and John Harsanyi about what rational people would choose behind the veil of ignorance. I think I follow the basic setup: we are to choose principles of justice without knowing which position in society we will occupy, so the choice is supposed to be fair in some sense. But I want to check whether the chapter's account of why Rawls and Harsanyi reach different conclusions is fair to both of them.

The chapter says something like the following. Behind the veil of ignorance we have no basis for assigning probabilities to ending up in any particular position, so we face genuine uncertainty rather than calculable risk. According to the chapter, Rawls holds that, when probabilities are unavailable in this way, the rational rule is simply maximin—choose the arrangement whose worst possible outcome is least bad—and that is why the original position selects the difference principle rather than utilitarianism, since utilitarianism could permit the worst-off to be made worse off whenever doing so raises total or average well-being. Harsanyi then objects that a truly rational person behind the veil would assign an equal probability to being any person in society and maximize expected utility; the chapter says this is what a properly conducted original position yields, and that this is just average utilitarianism. It concludes that Rawls's only way out is to assume that people are extremely risk-averse, which the chapter dismisses as a psychological quirk rather than a requirement of rational choice.

I have no training in this material, but something about the move from "we cannot assign probabilities" to "so maximin is rationally required" strikes me as too quick, and the claim that Harsanyi's equal-probability move settles the debate feels like it is treating one contested step as an obvious conclusion. Are my suspicions reasonable? Could you go through the chapter's summary piece by piece and tell me which parts are accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, how a defender of Rawls would respond to the equal-probability argument and to the charge of resting on extreme risk aversion, and whether the chapter's final conclusion actually follows from the parts of its summary that are correct?
