I've been reading scattered rationalist-blog discussions of the frame problem for years, and I recently put the following chain of reasoning together for a reading group on AI and rationality. I can't find the step where it comes apart, and I don't think any of us is seeing all the costs.

(1) If an agent selects actions by reasoning — by computing the expected consequences of candidate acts from its beliefs — then it must be able to determine whether a candidate action would achieve the goal while avoiding unacceptable side effects.

(2) Determining that requires distinguishing the relatively few facts an action changes from the enormous number of facts it leaves alone. Otherwise the useful inference "the crate is now at the door" is swamped by indefinitely many inferences of the form "the wall is still white, the floor is still level, the books are still on the shelf — unless...", and the agent cannot conclude anything at all.

(3) For any particular fact f that held before the action, the claim that f still holds afterward does not follow from the action description alone. It follows, if at all, from f together with background knowledge: the wall stays white because pushing a crate is not a painting event and no paint is involved; the floor stays level because the crate slides rather than being dropped; and so on. Each such conclusion is a small piece of ordinary world-reasoning, and there is no finite list of them.

(4) So a reasoner either must perform a potentially unbounded amount of world-reasoning to certify that each fact it cares about is undisturbed, or it must already know, before it plans, which few of the indefinitely many candidate facts could be disturbed by the action.

(5) But knowing which facts could be disturbed is exactly the kind of open-ended commonsense judgment the frame problem asks for. A cheap default such as "assume everything persists except what the action description lists as changed" does not fix this, because the dangerous exceptions are precisely the unlisted effects that do occur — the crate overhangs a glass table, the route passes an unlit stair — and the listed effects that occur only under conditions the default does not track.

(6) Therefore the relevance problem cannot be solved downstream of planning, by refining the planner's inference rules. A planner either arrives with relevance selection already supplied from outside the planning process, or it never starts planning in the first place. Planning presupposes a solution to the frame problem rather than contributing to one.

(7) And that seems to prove too much. Human agents are finite, must acquire much of their sense of relevance from experience, and still plan successfully in novel environments — noticing the glass table while ignoring the million irrelevant facts. If (6) held in full generality, a finite learner could never get planning off the ground: it would need to already possess the relevance judgments its planning was supposed to let it form.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), because I want planning to be genuine inference from beliefs, and the finiteness claim in (7). Are those compatible, or is the cheapest exit to accept one of the horns — that a planner's relevance judgments are pre-given rather than learned, or that planning is not inference but something embodied and unarticulated? Give me the diagnosis that actually earns the escape, not just a restatement of "relevance is hard."
