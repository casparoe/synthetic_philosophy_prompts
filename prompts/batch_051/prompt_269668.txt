I spent my career as an entomologist, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of mind and animal ethics. A former colleague of mine—a comparative physiologist—has firm, settled judgments about which animals are sentient, and I have been trying to work out the principle that lies behind them.

Her recorded judgments are these:

- A healthy adult human: sentient.
- A dog: sentient.
- An octopus: sentient.
- A rainbow trout: sentient.
- An oyster: not sentient.
- A moon jellyfish: not sentient.

She has never, so far as I know, offered a judgment about any insect. That is precisely the gap I want your help with.

Consider a new case, whose relevant features I will stipulate so that the question does not turn on disputed entomology. A company raises black soldier fly larvae (Hermetia illucens) by the trillion for livestock feed. Stipulate the following about an individual larva: it has a centralized brain; it has identifiable nociceptive sensory neurons; and after a single pairing of a substrate with a noxious stimulus it forms a durable avoidance memory. That avoidance is not a simple reflex: it is suppressed by severe hunger, and it extinguishes only gradually after the threat is removed. Take all of this as given. The question is not whether the larva is in fact sentient; it is what her recorded judgments commit her to saying about it.

On the strength of her recorded judgments, would she judge an individual larva sentient? I want your answer to do four things. First, set out two or three rules that fit all six of her recorded judgments. Second, say what each rule predicts for the larva. Third, give your best prediction of her verdict, stating which feature does the deciding work on your account. Fourth, specify the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules that remain live.

There is a further complication I care about. Suppose your best reconstruction of her rule makes the individual larva's sentience improbable, or only somewhat probable. She has also said—independently of any particular species—that when an organism is used in numbers on the order of a trillion, a low probability of sentience cannot simply be set aside, because the expected quantity of suffering becomes so large. Does that conviction change what she should say about the facility, or does it merely change how she should act while leaving the sentience verdict untouched? I want to know whether the scale argument and her recorded judgments can be made to cohere, and, if they pull in different directions, which she would surrender first.
