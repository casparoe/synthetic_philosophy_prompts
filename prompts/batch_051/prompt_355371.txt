I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Moby-Dick, and I would like a more careful version of the disagreement than either of us managed. My friend claimed that Ahab's reading of the whale — as a conscious, malevolent agent that took his leg on purpose and now hunts him back — is a textbook violation of C. Lloyd Morgan's canon, which says animal behavior should not be explained by higher psychological faculties when lower ones, like fear, self-preservation, or blind response to pursuit, will do. I resisted, because the novel seems to me less settled than that: Ishmael often deflates over-readings of animal behavior, but the book also makes the whale's actions feel freighted with meaning in a way that resists a purely deflationary account. I would like a reading of Moby-Dick through Morgan's canon. Where does the novel dramatize or enforce the canon's warning, where does it complicate or refuse it, and does the book ultimately treat Ahab's attribution of malice as mere error or as something the canon cannot fully rule out? Point me to the scenes that should carry the reading, and try to say what Melville's treatment adds to the philosophical question of when an animal's behavior licenses cognitive or moral attributions that the canon by itself leaves open.
