I've been going around in circles about free-floating rationales—the Dennett idea that some explanations work by citing a reason that no one ever actually had—and I want you to play the philosopher who works on teleology, function, and the intentional stance and sort me out, because I keep giving inconsistent verdicts and I can't tell whether I'm confused or the concept is just wierd. I'm an anthro grad student, so I hit these cases in institutions and social explanation all the time, but the philosophical literature I half-remember isn't doing the work for me anymore. Here's where my gut lands. (1) The giraffe's neck: obviously a genuine free-floating rationale. Longer-necked ancestors left more descendants because they reached leaves better, and no giraffe ever represented the thought that a longer neck means more food. (2) My thermostat keeping the flat at 19 degrees: I won't call this a free-floating rationale. The reason lives in whoever designed the thing and set the dial; the thermostat just executes. (3) A company that has squeezed its suppliers for years gets quietly dropped by them one by one, and its stock suffers, so the outcome looks like a judgment the market reached even though no single trader holds the whole story: I'm tempted to say this really is a free-floating rationale, even though it's built out of a bunch of actual reasoners each holding a piece. (4) Termite mound ventilation: genuine, same family as the giraffe; selection on building behaviour, no termite intends airflow. (5) A department's committee rules that persist because they make decisions unattributable and thereby protect whoever chairs them, though no one ever designed them for that: I'd call this a genuine free-floating rationale too, a social one, and it's the kind of case that first got me interested. (6) A split-brain patient whose left hemisphere gives a clean explanation for something the right hemisphere did: I'd call that confabulation, not a real free-floating rationale, even though it's exactly a reason that no one, in the relevant sense, had. (7) A language model's chain-of-thought announces that it's converting the units so the comparison is on the same terms, and then solves the problem: I lean that this is closer to the split-brain case than to the giraffe, but I can't say why, and it's the case that keeps me up.

My working rule is something like: a rationale is genuinely free-floating only if citing it helps explain why the structure or behaviour is there or persists, rather than just making it look intelligible after the fact. But that rule seems to kick out the market case (or maybe the market is a selection-like process among firms—I genuinely don't know), and it doesn't explain the thermostat: the reason for its switching is the designer's, so is the designer's own reason just a free-floating rationale one level up?
