I'm in a seminar on pain and affect, and the professor has been going through cases and ruling on whether the experience itself is bad for the person having it, setting aside downstream consequences. Her verdicts so far: a runner's stubbed toe, a sharp and throbbing pain she grimaces at and wants to stop, is bad. A patient with pain asymbolia whose hand is exposed to the same kind of noxious heat says the hand stings and burns, but she doesn't mind it, doesn't withdraw it, and denies it is unpleasant; the professor says not bad. A severe itch that can't be scratched and that the sufferer is desperate to end is bad. The crushing, unwanted feeling of grief weeks after a death, with no localized bodily sensation, is bad. A cancer patient on morphine who can identify a distant stabbing pain in his abdomen but is untroubled by it and asks for no relief is not bad. And a hiker's cold, sharp sensation on stepping into a stream on a hot day, which she enjoys and lingers in, is not bad.

Next week she will rule on a new case: a masochist in a consensual scene experiences a sensation she calls painful — it stings and burns and is genuinely unpleasant in the moment — but she is glad to be having it, wants it to continue, and asks that it not be stopped. Predict whether the professor will, or should for consistency, say the masochist's pain is bad for her or not. I want the reasoning more than the bare prediction: identify the two or three distinct principles about what makes pain bad that fit her six verdicts, say what each predicts about the masochist, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case that would best tell the contenders apart.

Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the candidate rules; (2) their predictions about the masochist; (3) your best guess and why; (4) the discriminating further case and how each rule treats it; (5) the principle the professor would be committing herself to under each possible ruling.
