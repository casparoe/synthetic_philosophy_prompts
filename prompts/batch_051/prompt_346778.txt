In astrobiology, one persistent problem is that every attempt to define "life" seems either too narrow to include what we might one day find elsewhere or too broad to exclude things we do not want to call alive. Give a working definition of life that an astrobiologist could actually use to recognize life that does not share Earth's particular biochemistry. I want a criterion, or a small conjunction of criteria, that does real classificatory work rather than a list of familiar features of carbon-based organisms or a family-resemblance shrug.

Run your definition against each of the following cases and classify each one as alive, not alive, or something you are forced to argue about:

1. a metabolizing, reproducing cyanobacterium;
2. a wildfire spreading across a hillside;
3. a crystal growing in a saturated salt solution;
4. a bacterial endospore that is currently metabolically inert and not reproducing, but will resume metabolism after decades of dormancy;
5. a sterile mule;
6. a tobacco mosaic virus outside any host cell;
7. a hypothetical Martian organism that stores hereditary information in a mineral-like lattice rather than in DNA and metabolizes perchlorates;
8. a prion that propagates by causing other proteins to misfold;
9. a self-replicating robotic probe on an exoplanet that mines local material, builds functional copies of itself, and whose copies occasionally differ in ways that affect how well they replicate further;
10. the last surviving adult member of an intelligent alien species, which metabolizes and maintains itself but cannot reproduce.

The definition should get the clearly alive cases right, get the clearly nonliving cases right, and give you a defensible verdict on the borderline ones, though the borderline verdicts may need argument. Finally, identify which of your ten classifications is least secure, and say whether the difficulty reveals something about the concept of life itself or only about the limits of human-made definitions.

Please write your answer in American English.
