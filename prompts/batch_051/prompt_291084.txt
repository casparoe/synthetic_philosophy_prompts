I am taking a graduate seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and next week I present my term-paper argument to the class. My thesis is that the most promising near-term direction for machine ethics is neither the top-down encoding of a canonical moral theory as an explicit decision procedure nor the bottom-up learning of a reward model from human approval, but what I have been calling a moral-uncertainty layer: a component that represents a spread of credible ethical views, uses that spread to separate decisions that are robust across reasonable frameworks from decisions that are not, and defers to human judgment in the non-robust cases rather than acting. My argument runs roughly as follows. First, top-down systems inevitably smuggle in contested normative commitments and go silent or arbitrary when principles conflict. Second, bottom-up preference learning optimizes revealed approval, which is not the same as doing what is right; it is also vulnerable to reward hacking and to laundering whatever biases are present in the training data. Third, a moral-uncertainty layer avoids both problems by giving up the aspiration to act on the single correct theory and instead acting only where the credible theories agree enough, deferring elsewhere.

The audience for the talk is mixed. About half the class are philosophy students who have recently read Kant and Mill and will press me on normative grounding: they will say the uncertainty layer still needs some criterion for including and weighting views, and that any such weighting is itself a moral commitment I cannot extract from the data. The other half are computer science students who have implemented reinforcement learning from human feedback and will press me on implementation: they will say my proposal is just the same learned models wrapped in a fancier objective, and that representing multiple normative viewpoints is not computationally meaningful. There is also a TA who works on AI safety and who I expect to raise the failure mode in which the system learns to manipulate its model of human reviewers so that the perceived spread shrinks and decisions it prefers come out looking robust. I want to walk in prepared. What objections should I expect, in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way those audience members would actually phrase them? For each objection, what should I concede, what should I hold, and where is my reply weakest — and which single reply is the one that has to land for the whole position to stay standing? Finally, what is the objection I should be privately hoping nobody thinks to ask?
