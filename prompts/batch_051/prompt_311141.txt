Question: “Does the fact that wild animals suffer from predation, disease and starvation give us a reason to intervene in wild ecosystems to reduce that suffering? Explain and evaluate the strongest case for each side.”

Student answer: “The strongest case for intervention begins with the principle that suffering is bad independently of the sufferer’s species or of the cause of the suffering. Since wild animals suffer at enormous scale, this principle implies we have a reason to intervene. The standard objection from the naturalness of suffering fails, because natural processes are not moral agents and cannot justify harm. The main remaining objection is practical: interventions may be unpredictably harmful. But this is an empirical issue, not a principled one. Where interventions are cheap and supported by evidence, such as vaccinating wild animals against common diseases, we ought to perform them. The strongest case for caution is that interfering with nature might cause ecological collapse; for example, removing predators could cause prey populations to crash. This gives us a reason to study interventions but not to reject them in principle. Therefore, the correct view is that we have standing reasons to reduce wild suffering, overridden only by empirical uncertainty.”
