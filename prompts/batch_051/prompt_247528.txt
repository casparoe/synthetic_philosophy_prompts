At my political philosophy reading group last week, two members got into an argument that I have not been able to referee, and I would like a point-by-point verdict on it. The concrete question was this: suppose a government imposes a small additional tax on its highest earners and, by design, destroys the revenue rather than spending it — no one receives the money, no program is improved, the rich are slightly worse off, the poor remain exactly as they are, and the gap between the two shrinks. Is that change an improvement in any respect whatsoever?

Marta said yes. She argued that inequality is bad not merely because of the suffering or poverty it happens to track, but in itself; a less unequal world is, in that one respect, a better world, even if it is worse in other respects because the rich have been made worse off. The fact that no one benefits, she said, is beside the point: values other than individual well-being — justice, fairness, and on her view equality — can make a state of affairs better without making any individual better off. David called this absurd. He said a change with no beneficiary cannot be an improvement in any sense worth caring about, and that if the poor are no better off then nothing of moral importance has changed. He insisted he cares more about a given benefit when it goes to the poor than when it goes to the rich, so he is not indifferent to distribution; but he denied that the distribution itself has value. Making the rich poorer while helping no one, he said, is pure waste, and it only looks like progress because we have confused "benefits matter more for the worse off" with "the gap itself matters."

Marta replied that "pure waste" is just a redescription of one dimension of the comparison: everyone already accepts that a state of affairs can be better in one respect and worse in another, and if equality is intrinsically good, then leveling down improves the world along one dimension while worsening it along another. David answered with the reverse case: if the rich get richer while the poor stay exactly as they are, her view must say the world has become worse in one respect even though no one is harmed, and he finds that objectionable. His view, he said, handles both cases with a single principle: only well-being matters, and an improvement matters more the lower the level of the life it improves — a fact about each person's absolute level, not about the distance between one person and another. That, he said, is why he can both favor helping the poor first and regard leveling down as worthless. Marta shot back that this "single principle" merely rewrites a relational complaint as a complaint about absolute levels: the objection to inequality is the fact that one person can have so much while another has so little — the comparative standing, the relation itself — and one cannot refute that by substituting a theory that ignores relations and then congratulating itself for its elegance. She said he was assuming a form of welfarism — the view that only well-being ultimately matters — which was exactly what she was contesting.

They asked me to settle it, and I could not. Please go through this exchange move by move: evaluate each argument as it was actually made, say who is right about which specific points, identify where the disagreement bottoms out, and say who is more right overall, including anything both of them are missing.
