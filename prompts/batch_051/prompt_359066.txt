The final for my philosophy of physics course is sneaking up, and I want an essay question on the 'end of physics' that doesn't let students just recite the standard dialectic about unification versus effective field theory. I want the question built around a trilemma they haven't seen laid out quite this way: (a) a genuine final theory with no free parameters and no explanatory leftovers; (b) an interminable hierarchy of effective theories, each only valid above some energy scale; (c) two or more empirically equivalent theories that predict exactly the same observables but posit different underlying structure. The thing I most want to test is whether they can handle the from-inside problem: for each of (a)-(c), what observation or theoretical result could ever license believing we're in that situation, and whether the distinctions among the three are sharp enough for 'we'd know when we got there' to make sense.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of around 700-800 words that takes a definite stance on whether we could know from the inside, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, total 20. For each must-hit point, add one sentence naming the common student mistake it helps seperate out (for example, inferring (b) from the mere fact that current theories are approximate, or treating underdetermination as equivalent to 'no fact of the matter'). I'd like the difference between an A and a B answer to come down to defending a stance on the knowability question with an argument, not just laying out the three possibilities.
