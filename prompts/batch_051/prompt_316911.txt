i've been reading about dutch book arguments and i keep flip-flopping. the core argument feels airtight: if my credences violate the probability axioms — say i'm 0.7 that a bit will be 1 and 0.5 that it'll be 0 — someone can sell me a $1 bet on 1 for $0.70 and a $1 bet on 0 for $0.50, each of which i judge fair, and no matter what happens i'm out $0.20. as a software person, i love that it turns rational belief into something like a typechecker: incoherent credences are just a guaranteed runtime failure.

but the more i stare at it, the more the argument feels like it's smuggling in the conclusion. calling each bet 'fair' means i'm indifferent between taking it and not taking it, so refusing a single bet can't be irrational. and even if i'd take each one on its own, i don't see why i'm committed to valuing the bundled pair as exactly the sum of the parts — i might care about total exposure, not just expected value. the dutch book only works if there's a rule that says a rational agent has to accept every bet they judge fair, alone and in any combination, and that rule is doing an awfully lot of work.

so which should give? is my intuition that i can just walk away from the bundle exposing a real hole, or is it missing the point of what credences are meant to model? or is there a version of the argument that keeps the force without assuming fair bets are additive? walk me through what it would cost to drop either side, not just which side wins.
