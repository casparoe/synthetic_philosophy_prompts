I serve on the Public Monuments Advisory Commission of a small city in northern England. Our governing policy includes the following provision:

Section 9(c). A public monument, memorial, or commemorative place name may be established or renamed only in honour of a person, or of an event, that has made a significant and enduring contribution to the history, culture, or civic life of this City and whose commemoration would, in the judgement of the Commission, be likely to remain a source of public respect.

A neighbourhood association has applied to rename a small cobbled square—at present called Welders' Court—after the protagonist of my novel, a printer's widow who, in the 1860s, organises the women of the district against a collective rent rise and is arrested on that very square. The novel has become a fixture of the local literary tourist trail; guides bring visitors to the square, a mural of the character covers the gable of a nearby pub, and the association insists that the square's true civic significance now lies in this entirely invented woman. The drafters of Section 9(c) were clearly thinking of mayors, industrialists, war dead, and the occasional composer, not of characters from works of fiction. I have been asked to decide whether the application falls within the provision.

I am less interested in municipal procedure than in what it is actually right to say. What do the words of Section 9(c) require in this case? What does the purpose of the clause require? What would the drafters have said if asked, and what would a reasonable reader take the rule to mean? And which of those approaches should decide the matter when they diverge?
