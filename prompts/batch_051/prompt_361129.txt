Last week I watched two friends of mine argue about the French Revolution until the restaurant closed, and I honestly couldn't tell weather either was in the right, so I'm asking for a referee.

Rhea opened with the claim that 1789 through the Terror is a class-struggle story. The old regime fell because the bourgeoisie had money but no political power, and after 1789 the thing that kept driving events was pressure from below—peasants and sans-culottes. The Terror, she said, was the revolution defending itself: counter-revolution at home, invasion from abroad, and the people being defended were the popular classes.

Dan said that explains who benefited, not why the conflict took the shape it did. A class analysis doesn't on its own predict purges, public virtue campaigns, denunciation, and a cult of the general will. Once politics is redefined as the expression of a single people, he argued, anyone who disagrees becomes an enemy of the people, and the Terror is that idea working itself out.

Rhea replied that ideology can't explain timing. The revolution didn't radicalize at the speed of its ideas; it radicalized when the war went badly, when the king tried to flee, when bread ran short and plots seemed everywhere. That's contingency, not logic. Dan said circumstances push but they don't write the script; the same pressures in a different political culture wouldn't have produced the guillotine.

Rhea said ideas don't sign arrest warrants, coalitions with material interests do, and the factions that fell were the ones that had stopped being useful to the coalition running the war.

Then Dan hit what he clearly thought was the finisher: if the Terror was class self-defense, why did it keep turning on its own—the Girondins, then Danton, then Robespierre? A movement that repeatedly kills its own leaders looks less like a class defending itself than like an ideology eating everyone who falls short of its demands.

Rhea said that's exactly what class struggle predicts: when the counter-revolution presses, each phase purges its moderates and rides further left, so a revolution consuming its own leadership is a feature of the model, not an anomaly. Dan came back that this makes her account unfalsifiable—every escalation gets relabeled as the class struggle deepening, and nothing the Terror did could count against it. Rhea said his ideology account is the unfalsifiable one, since he relabels every material factor as mere circumstances.

They didn't finish before the restaurant closed. I'd like a move-by-move adjudication: for each move, say whether it's clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and why. Then say what both of them are missing. Please keep separate what's settled history from what's genuinely contested among historians of the period, and don't present one interpretation as the consensus. In the body, give the point-by-point call, and then make your very last line \answer{...} with a short phrase inside, like \answer{Rhea more right overall} or \answer{roughly a draw}.
