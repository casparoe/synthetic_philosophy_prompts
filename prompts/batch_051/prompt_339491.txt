Write a dialogue of about 900 words between two philosophers who disagree about what makes a practical choice hard. Call them the comparativist and the parity theorist.

The comparativist holds that whenever an agent faces a difficult decision, there is a fact of the matter about which alternative is more choice-worthy. On this view, the experience of difficulty is entirely explained by the agent's uncertainty about the relevant weights, probabilities, or consequences, or by the cognitive cost of the comparison itself. The parity theorist holds that some choices are hard for a different reason. In choices that draw on different, equally central parts of an agent's life—for instance, a demanding legal career in the city versus a quieter medical practice in a rural community—the alternatives are neither exactly equal nor incomparable, and neither is better than the other. They are on a par. On this view, the agent does not simply discover an antecedently correct answer; she must commit to one alternative and, in committing, create new reasons that make the chosen alternative the one to pursue.

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange, not a pair of alternating position statements. Each character should respond to the previous move, grant the concessions the other has earned, and then press the real weakness in the other's view. Both should be equally sophisticated and charitable.

Midway through, the parity theorist should press the small-improvement argument. Suppose two careers, A and B, are such that neither is better. If A were exactly equal in choice-worthiness to B, then a modest improvement in A's terms—for example, a small salary increase with every other feature held fixed—would make the improved A determinately better. In many hard cases it does not: the improved A is still not determinately better than B. The parity theorist should use this to argue that the original options were not equal on a hidden common scale. The comparativist should answer with at least two serious replies, such as explaining the result through vague or imprecise comparatives, treating the failure as evidence of incomplete preferences, or insisting that the original judgment was merely rough. The parity theorist should then respond to those specific replies, explaining why parity is not vagueness, not incomparability, and not mere uncertainty, and why it is the agent's commitment, rather than the discovery of an already better option, that does the rational work.

End the dialogue where the two characters locate the deepest divergence: whether practical reason is fundamentally a matter of detecting an independently existing comparative structure or partly a matter of conferring one through choice. The reader should be able to see precisely where their views of rational agency come apart; do not force a reconciliation.
