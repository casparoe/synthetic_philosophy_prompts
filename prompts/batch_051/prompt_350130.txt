Last night I had a long argument with a friend about a situation I am in, and I am still not sure what to do. I serve on the five-person board of a small housing cooperative. Our bylaws set board terms at two years and say nothing about what happens when a term ends and no successor has been elected. All five seats were filled in the same election two years ago, so all five terms expired at once last month. We attempted to hold an election, but it collapsed over a dispute about which residents are members in good standing and therefore eligible to vote. Each side accuses the other of manipulating the membership roll, no one was elected, and it is not clear when a lawful election can actually be held. The bylaws require a quorum of three board members to transact business, and we now have five people whose terms have all expired.

An emergency has since arisen. A pipe burst in the common building, and the insurance company will not renew the policy unless a current officer signs the renewal and authorizes the repair. Three of us, including me, have continued to meet and act as the board, on the theory that the alternative is paralysis and that the members, by failing to produce a lawful election, cannot reasonably intend for the cooperative to lose its insurance and its building. Two of the other former board members, along with a number of residents, say we are no longer the board at all. On their view, the cooperative has entered a state in which no one has authority, and anything we sign is a private act by three private individuals with no more standing than any other three residents.

My friend argued for two hours that my position is incoherent. He said that if authority comes from the rules and the rules have run out, then I have no authority, and that if I act anyway I am simply asserting a power I do not have and using the emergency as an excuse. I replied that a total vacuum is not a real option: someone will act, and the only question is whether it should be the people with the most continuity and competence, under whatever constraints they can honestly acknowledge.

I do not want to be the person who mistakes convenience for legitimacy. What should I do, and how should I think about whether the three of us may act? Under what principles, if any, can authority persist when the rules that created it have run out?
