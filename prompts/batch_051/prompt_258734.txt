Okay, here's a thing I keep going around in circles about. I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and most of the critical-thinking literature I read treats doubt purely instrumentally: you question your beliefs because that's how you end up with truer ones. But there's another strand—especially in public discourse—where doubt is a moral duty. You owe it to yourself, to other people, to the truth, to keep questioning; being sure of something gets framed as arrogance or laziness. I don't just want to know wether people talk about these differently. I want to know whether the moralized version changes anything measurable.

Here's the claim I'd like you to test: making doubt a moral duty changes what people do with their doubts—specifically, it makes them more likely to perform self-criticism while being no more likely (maybe even less likely) to actually change their mind, because the self-criticism itself is where the virtue is displayed.

Design the best empirical test you can of that, trying not to reduce it to self-report. Operationalize both "doubt as instrument" and "doubt as duty" (or tell me why my dichotomy is off), give me a concrete experimental paradigm with a manipulation and outcome measures, say exactly what pattern of results would count for the claim and what would count against it, and then be honest about what even a clean result could never settle—e.g., whether one version is "genuinely" more rational, or whether duty-talk is just a culturally specific way of praising doubt.
