I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in ethics, and I need one essay question on inherited guilt and original sin, treated as a problem in moral philosophy rather than as theological exegesis. Please produce three things: the essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question should be answerable in 90 minutes and marked out of 20. It should be written so that a candidate cannot pass by summarising familiar material—for example, the Augustinian contrast between original sin as an inherited condition and as imputed guilt, or stock arguments about collective responsibility and reparations. The question must require candidates to construct and test a distinction or principle of their own.

Please build the question around the following crux: the difference between guilt (a status properly assigned to an agent for that agent's own voluntary wrongdoing) and a transmitted normative stain or taint borne solely in virtue of descent. A strong question might ask whether the doctrine of original sin is best reconstructed as a thesis about guilt, about taint, about liability, or about benefiting from others' wrongdoing, and whether any version of the thesis survives once these are separated. You may use this structure or improve on it, but the question must force candidates to draw and defend distinctions among these notions, and it must not be answerable by a summary of any named position or text.

The model answer should exemplify a first-class answer: it should analyse the relevant notions (at least guilt, taint, liability, and benefit-based responsibility), state the conditions under which a normative status can be transmitted across persons, and defend a determinate verdict on whether inherited guilt is coherent and, if it is not, what the most defensible secular reconstruction of original sin is. The answer should argue from principle rather than appeal to textual authority.

The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. Each note should diagnose a specific failing—for example, treating guilt and taint as interchangeable, or assuming that because a doctrine has theological standing it automatically has a place in moral philosophy.

Style and constraints:
- Write the whole deliverable—question, model answer, and marking scheme—in American English.
- Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not make the question depend on recall of any particular text.
- Aim for a question that is clear to someone who has studied ethics but has not taken a specialist course in the philosophy of religion.
