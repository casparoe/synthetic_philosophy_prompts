I am a PhD student in epistemology working on the nature of reasons, and I need a short, rigorous essay for a workshop handout on a phenomenon I have been calling free-floating rationales: reasons or bodies of justification that appear to obtain independently of any actual reasoner who has them. Examples include a valid proof of a theorem that no one has ever read, a body of evidence for a hypothesis that is distributed across papers no single scientist has ever assembled, and a computer-generated derivation that no human has ever checked. The controversy to be addressed is whether such items can genuinely be reasons, or whether reasons are essentially tied to the perspective, aim, or activity of a reasoning agent; the essay must weigh the main considerations on both sides and defend a verdict rather than ending in a standoff. A strong essay will distinguish a reason from the act of reasoning and from a person's having a reason, and will say something about whether distributed or merely possible reasoning changes the answer. Please write the essay as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion outside the numbered points. Use American English.
