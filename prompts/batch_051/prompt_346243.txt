I want an audit of my views on cryonics before I act on them. I hold the following:

1. The probability that cryonics ever results in my being revived as the same person is low—between one and five percent—but not negligible.
2. Whether to pursue it should be settled by expected value: a sufficiently small chance of an enormous good, such as decades or centuries of healthy additional life, can justify a real financial cost now.
3. A future being with my memories who is not a continuation of me would be worth nothing to me; identity, not mere resemblance, is what matters.
4. Identity, in my view, consists in psychological continuity—overlapping chains of memory, intention, and character—not in an unbroken stream of consciousness; that is why I survive sleep and anesthesia.
5. I accept the deprivation account: death is bad because it deprives me of the goods I would otherwise have had, not because being dead is itself an unpleasant state.
6. When weighing those future goods, I discount them only for the probability that I will not be around to enjoy them, never merely for their futurity.
7. I reject Pascal's-mugging-style reasoning—a tiny probability of an arbitrarily large payoff is not automatically worth any finite cost—though I have no worked-out rule for where the line falls.

Tell me which pairs of these commitments are in tension, rank the tensions by severity, identify which pairings only look like tensions and why, and propose the least costly revision that restores consistency, plus the single change that would improve the set the most.
