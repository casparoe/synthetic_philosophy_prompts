I've been chewing on a variant of Nozick's experience machine and wanted to see wether it changes the standard arguments. In the original, you get simulated experiences and lose contact with reality. Here's my variant: nothing is simulated. You keep your actual life — same partner, same job, same losses, same death of a parent waiting somewhere down the line — but you have a permanent neural dimmer switch installed. It clips the amplitude of every emotional response, on both sides. Bad news no longer disturbs you: you register the loss, update, make arrangements, but the grief never lands. Good news and beauty lose their sharp edge too; nothing sweeps you up, you never ache with longing, music becomes just sort of pleasant. Your beliefs and practical deliberation are untouched, so this isn't lobotomy-lite — it specifically flattens the passions. The result looks, from the outside, a lot like Stoic apatheia, a steady and unsurprisable equanimity, except that it's manufactured by a device rather than achieved by working on your judgments.

Now, the familiar objections to Nozick's machine say we want to actually be in contact with a deeper reality, actually be a certain sort of person, not merely have experiences of one. Do those objections even land on the dimmer-switch version, where reality stays fully available and only the passions are flattened? And if freedom from disturbance is your goal, are you now under pressure to sign up? What would a Stoic say — is there a principled reason to refuse the machine precisely because it delivers apatheia through the wrong route, and what would that refusal reveal about whether apatheia is a felt condition or an achievement? I'm less interested in rehearing that the original machine is awful than in whether this variant splits the usual anti-machine camp.
