I'm a guardian ad litem in family court, which means a judge appoints me to investigate what's actually in a kid's best interest when the parents are fighting over custody, schooling, medical care, religious upbringing — that kind of thing. I'm not a philosopher, and I carry around twenty active cases at a time, each of which has to be investigated and written up in under two weeks, on partial information, in language a judge will acutally read at 8:30 in the morning. My report usually carries the day unless a parent pushes it to a hearing, so a wrong call has real consequences. A colleague once told me the principle I should be using is the child's right to an open future: roughly, that adults shouldn't make choices now that foreclose a big range of the child's later options, since the child can't consent to those choices yet. I like the idea but it's almost useless as it stands, because every parenting decision shapes a child's future somehow — picking one school closes off another — and I can't tell where ordinary parenting ends and objectionable foreclosure begins.

I need you to turn that principle into a decision procedure I can actually run on a live case. It should be a checklist or a set of questions in order, with some sense of what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops and tells me this is not a violation, where it stops and tells me it is, and where it hands the case to a person because the remaining question is a values judgment rather than a factual finding. Mark the steps where the procedure is likely to misfire, and be explicit about what it can't do. Then run it on three cases: (a) a ten-year-old whose parents want to pull her out of school to train thirty hours a week for an elite gymnastics program; (b) a deaf child whose hearing parents refuse cochlear implants; and (c) a thirteen-year-old whose parents belong to a religious community that strongly discourages college and expects girls to marry young. Make sure at least one of the worked cases comes out the opposite of how I'll probably feel about it, and tell me plainly where the procedure forced your hand.

Please write the answer in American English.
