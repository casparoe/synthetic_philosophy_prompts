I have adopted a policy for how I form opinions in online arguments, and I want to know whether it is rationally defensible. When I am in a substantive disagreement with an account I cannot verify as human, I default to treating it as a counterfeit person in Daniel Dennett's sense: a synthetic agent engineered to pass as human, flood public discourse with fluent prose, and manipulate real people into engagement. Once a cue trips the suspicion—instant replies, mirroring of my wording, unflappable responses to corrections, or an emotional attunement that feels manufactured—I refuse to update on anything the account says. I do not assess whether the arguments are sound; I do not let it set the terms of the exchange; and I do not reply, because replying would further train the system. I accept that a sound argument can come from a synthetic source, and that the same cues also occur in real people who write in polished, measured, or pseudonymous ways. Is this shortcut defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it just a bias against the messenger dressed up as risk management? Is there a revised version of the rule—one that quarantines the social manipulation without refusing to hear argument from any unverifiable speaker—that would keep the protective benefit while avoiding the cost?
