Hi — I'm a PhD student in medieval philosophy (working on Abelard's ethics), and I also sit as the graduate rep on my university's academic-integrity panel, which meets weekley in term time and has about twenty minutes per case. The stakes are real: students can be suspended or have degrees withheld, and the rest of the panel are mostly non-philosophers who don't care about Abelard; they just want a protocol they can use. Our current rubric is almost entirely organized around the severity of the outward act — what was copied, how much, whether it got submitted — and it treats "the student denied it" as an aggravating factor. I think Abelard gets this backwards. If I'm reading him right, culpability lives in consent: the sin is in consenting to do what you know you shouldn't, not in the deed; the desire itself isn't sin unless you consent to it; and if you consent and are then prevented from acting, you're just as culpable as if you'd done it. On that logic our panel should sort cases by what the student consented to, not by what happened to come out — which means we'd sometimes punish harder for a paper bought but never submitted than for one plagiarized carelessly. But I'm not naive: consent is inside someone's head, and all we usually have is testamony, a browser history, and what the instructor says. Whatever we produce also has to be signed off by the dean of students, who will veto anything that looks like letting a cheater off on a technicality.

So here's what I'm after. Please write me a reusable decision procedure for the panel, acting as someone who knows Abelard's distinctions well and also knows how an honor panel has to actually operate. Start by laying out the distinctions the criterion needs (consent, act, desire, ignorance), then turn them into a sequence of questions the panel can put to the evidence, in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no culpability under the Abelard criterion," and where it has to hand the case to a person because the criterion can't be settled on the available evidence. Mark the steps where a panel member has to make a judgment call. Then run the procedure through three cases: (1) a student bought an essay but never submitted it; (2) a student copied a friend's code and says they genuinely believed collaboration was allowed in that class; and (3) a student submitted an essay with plagiarized paragraphs and says they don't remember pasting them in. Include at least one worked case where the procedure gives an answer the dean will hate, and close by saying plainly what the procedure is structurally unable to do.
