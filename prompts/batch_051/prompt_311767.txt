I've been reading the Dennett–Caruso exchange on free will and punishment and realized I have no idea what I actually think, so I started going through cases and writing down my gut verdicts. Here they are. (1) A man who attacks a stranger because of a brain tumor: doesn't deserve blame or punishment, but if he's still dangerous he should be kept away from people until he isn't. (2) A repeat drunk driver who kills a pedestrian: deserves prison, and not simply to keep him off the road — the years are owed to him. (3) A teenager from a violent, chaotic home who robs a convenience store at gunpoint: I'd punish him less than a rich kid who did the identical thing on a dare, but I still think something non-trivial should happen to him. (4) A wealthy CEO who over a decade knowingly defrauds thousands of retirees: I want him punished harder than any prediction of future dangerousness would support. (5) An 85-year-old man who murdered someone at 30 but is now frail, demented, and harmless: I don't want him locked up, yet part of me still feels he *deserves* to suffer for what he did, and I can tell those two reactions are in tension. (6) A psychopath who feels no guilt or empathy and carefully plans and commits a murder: if he can't help being what he is, I don't see how punishing him is different from punishing the tumor patient, but my gut insists it is.

My half-baked principle is something like "you deserve punishment to the extent you knowingly chose the bad act from a fair baseline of options and capacities," but that rule seems to break on the psychopath and maybe also on the old man. Is there a principle — stated precisely — that gets most of these verdicts, what should it say about cases 5 and 6, what would it say about a case I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts do I have to give up to keep the rest? Please write the answer in American English.
