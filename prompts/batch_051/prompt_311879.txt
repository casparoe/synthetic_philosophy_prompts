Backstory: I've been involved in a couple forecasting-adjacent projects, and a friend keeps pushing a proposal for how a small foundation should decide which empirical claims are worth funding further research on. The rule would be this. Anyone submitting a claim has to break their evidence into "independent lines of evidence." For each line they state a likelihood ratio: how much more likely that line is if the claim is true than if it's false. The review committee then just multiplies the ratios together, with an explicit rule that independence is presumed by default. A reviewer can object that two lines aren't independent, but only by naming the common cause — same dataset, same original paper, same model family, same informant, one measurement nested inside another. Saying "these feel correlated" isn't enough; if no common cause is named, the multiplication stands, and the product becomes the official score the foundation uses to rank claims.

What I want is not an abstract "is this a proper scoring rule" take. I want someone to work through how the institution would actually deform around this rule. What kinds of proposals would get funded, what submissions would be selected for over time, and what would people learn to do to the "independence" step? Then push on the philosophy: is presumption of independence a defensible default when aggregating weak heuristic arguments — the way people informally multiply "multiple lines of evidence" in blog posts and expert elicitations — or does putting the burden of proof on the objector just gift-wrap a bias toward overconfident products? Where are the weird failure modes (weak lines that aren't independent in either direction, or independence that's itself an unstated modeling assumption), and in the long run does the mechanism select for truth, for the ability to package correlated evidence so it looks independent, or for something worse?
