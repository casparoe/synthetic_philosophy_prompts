I'm drafting an explainer on how the word random hides real ambiguity, and I keep returning to Bertrand's chord problem. I set out below a numbered version of the argument I would like to use in the piece. A mathematician I interviewed tells me the three probability calculations are all correct as conditional claims, but she also says my final step is doing more work than it admits. I now want to know whether the argument, as written, is valid, and if it is not, exactly where it fails.

Setup: a chord of a circle is selected at random, and we ask for the probability that it is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Consider the following steps:

1. A chord can be generated by choosing its two endpoints uniformly and independently on the circumference. No endpoint is singled out by the problem, so this is a faithful way to honor the instruction to choose at random.
2. Under that procedure, fix one endpoint. The chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle precisely when the other endpoint lies in the opposite arc spanning one-third of the circumference. So the probability is one-third.

3. A chord can also be generated by choosing a point uniformly throughout the disk to serve as its midpoint, and then drawing the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. No midpoint is singled out, so this too is a faithful way to choose at random.
4. Under that procedure, the chord is long precisely when the midpoint lies inside the concentric circle of half the radius, which occupies one-quarter of the disk. So the probability is one-quarter.

5. A chord can also be generated by choosing a radius uniformly and then choosing the midpoint uniformly along that radius, and drawing the chord perpendicular to the radius. Here, again, no radius and no position along a radius is singled out.
6. Under that procedure, the chord is long precisely when the midpoint lies within half the radius of the center. So the probability is one-half.

7. Since the three procedures are equally faithful to the instruction to choose a chord at random, the principle of indifference assigns the same event probabilities of one-third, one-quarter, and one-half.
8. Therefore, either the problem as posed has no determinate answer, or the principle of indifference is not an acceptable rule for assigning probabilities.

I want a step-by-step diagnosis, not a survey of the paradox. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, state the corrected version of that step, and say whether the conclusion in step 8 survives the correction. In doing so, please separate what is settled in the standard treatment of Bertrand's problem from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the principle of indifference, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
