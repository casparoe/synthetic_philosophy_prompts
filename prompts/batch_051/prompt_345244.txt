so me and my mate spent the whole evening arguing about money pumps, and I've come away feeling like his argument proves too much but I can't spot where it breaks.

His argument goes like this:

(1) A money pump is meant to show that intransitive preferences are irrational. If I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, then someone can start me with B, charge me a small fee to swap B for A (which I accept, since I prefer A), then charge me to swap A for C (since I prefer C), then charge me to swap C back to B. I end up where I started, just poorer, and every single step was a trade I preferred at the time — by my own lights I've been taken for a ride.

(2) But you can run the same kind of pump on someone just because their tastes change. Say on Monday I prefer A to B, so I pay to swap my B for A. On Tuesday I change my mind and prefer B to A, so I payed to swap back. I'm back where I started, poorer, and each trade seemed good when I made it.

(3) If the first case shows the agent is irrational, the second case has to as well — the victim is just as vulnerable to the series of trades.

(4) But the second case is just a normal change of preference. And preference change can't all be irrational; sometimes we learn something new, sometimes our tastes just develop, and it would be mad to say we must keep every preference forever.

(5) So either virtually any preference change is irrational, or money-pump arguments don't actually show that intransitive preferences are irrational.

My friend thinks the right lesson is the second option in (5) — that money pumps are overrated and don't prove much. I'm not willing to give up (4), but I also feel like there has to be a real difference between being intransitive and just changing your mind, because the first person seems exploitable in a way the second isnt.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? If you had to keep or reject one premise, which would it be, and what does that choice cost? Also can you write your answer in British English, mate.
