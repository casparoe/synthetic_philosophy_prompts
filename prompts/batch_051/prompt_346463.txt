I have been trying to get straight on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I keep finding myself stuck on a reconstruction that looks airtight but reaches a conclusion at odds with how evidence is actually used in science. The setup: a piece of evidence is old relative to a hypothesis when the evidence was already known before the hypothesis was formulated. The canonical case is the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, which had been recorded for decades before general relativity and is standardly treated as strong evidence for the theory. I want to know whether the following argument really does show that Bayesian confirmation theory cannot handle this.

Step 1: For evidence e to confirm a hypothesis h for an agent, the agent's probability of h after conditioning on e must be greater than their probability of h before conditioning on e. Step 2: By Bayes' theorem, P(h|e) = P(e|h)P(h)/P(e). Step 3: If e is old evidence with respect to h, then at the moment h is introduced and assessed, the agent already knows e, so P(e)=1. Step 4: If P(e)=1, then for any h with P(h)>0, P(e|h)=1; whether h is true or false makes no difference to the agent's certainty in e. Step 5: Therefore P(h|e)=P(h), and by Step 1 old evidence can never confirm a hypothesis. But the Mercury case looks like old evidence confirming a hypothesis.

So I have two questions. First, is the argument valid? If it is valid, exactly which ingredient of the Bayesian framework needs to be revised or enriched, and why? If it is not valid, which step fails, and on what precise reading of the terms?
