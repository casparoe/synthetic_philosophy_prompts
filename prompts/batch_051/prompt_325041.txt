A friend and I watched 12 Angry Men last weekend and got into a proper argument afterwards, and I'm still not sure who's right. I'd like a point-by-point referee. The argument was about whether the film is best read as a dramatisation of Dewey's idea that democratic publics form through shared inquiry, or whether it actually shows the limits of that idea. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dan said the jury room is a Deweyan public in miniature: a bunch of strangers with no common life are forced to turn themselves into a kind of community by investigating the same problem together. They don't just vote; they reconstruct the evidence, run little experiments with the knife and the apartment layout, and change their minds when the inquiry demands it. That's basically a public forming through communication and experimentation.

(2) Amira said that's too rosy. A jury isn't a public, it's a sequestered body with a verdict to produce under coercion. Dewey's idea is about publics emerging around consequences that ripple beyond a room; the jury has no stake in the consequences that matter, and the people actually affected aren't in the room. Calling it democratic is a category error.

(3) Dan replied that Amira was smuggling in a requirement that democratic inquiry has to be open to everyone. The film shows the conditions that matter: a stubborn minority (Fonda) refuses premature closure, dissents are not suppressed, and several jurors revise their views under the pressure of argument. The juror who won't revise (the bigoted one) is shown as the failure case, not as the truth of the process.

(4) Amira said no, the film mostly shows persuasion and rhetoric, not inquiry. Fonda plants doubts before he has evidence; he appeals to the old juror's sense of being overlooked and to the foreign-born juror's dignity; the angry juror is broken down by the group, not by evidence. If that's inquiry, then any well-run argument is inquiry, and Dewey means something narrower: checking a proposal by its observable consequences.

(5) Dan came back with a point about consequences: a Deweyan inquiry ends in a test. A trial ends in a verdict, which is a binary collective act, not a revisable experiment. So you could only read the film as Deweyan in the scenes before the final vote. The group does the experimental part, then the institutions slam shut.

(6) Amira finished by saying that's exactly her point: the film shows the tension between Dewey's open-ended, experimentalist ideal and the institutional clock of the law. It isn't a celebration of democratic inquiry; it's a demonstration of how ordinary people can do something inquiry-like even within a device designed to force a yes/no decision. But the device itself isn't experimentalist at all.

Please adjudicate this move by move: say which points from (1) to (6) are strongest and weakest, where each person is actually succeeding against the other, and how the film itself cuts for and against both of them. Then say who read the film better overall, and what a Deweyan critic should add to the argument that neither of them quite got to.

I'd like the response in British English, formatted like this:
