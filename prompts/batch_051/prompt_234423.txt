I am a novelist, and I am drafting a book in which the protagonist is fitted with an experimental cortical interface that can drive the visual system directly. In the scene I most need to get right, the interface gives her a colour she has never experienced before: not merely a shade she has not happened to encounter, and not a fusion or mixture of colours she already knows, but a genuinely novel colour quality, as foreign to her prior visual life as red would be to someone who had seen only blues and greens. I want to know whether the scene can survive a sceptical reader, and I would rather test its plausibility now than paper over it later.

Please give me your credence in the following proposition:

It is possible in principle — meaning not ruled out by any law of nature or fixed feature of human neurophysiology, even if no present device could achieve it — to induce in at least one healthy adult human with otherwise normal colour vision a genuinely novel colour experience: a chromatic quality that is not a point in, and cannot be reached by any mixture or combination from, the familiar hue–saturation–brightness space of normal trichromatic vision, and that the subject would describe as unlike any colour they have ever seen, remembered, or voluntarily imagined.

Decompose the estimate into at least the following four components, giving each a probability and a one-line defence, then state explicitly how you combined them and what final credence you reach:

1. The closure question. How likely is it that the human colour space is closed — that trichromatic input combined through opponent processing yields a quality space beyond which no signal can go, so that no stimulus, however cleverly routed, could add a new axis or primary rather than moving around inside the existing space?

2. The delivery question. Conditional on the space not being closed, how likely is it that some pattern of direct stimulation could, in principle, reach whatever substrate would support the new quality, rather than only producing phosphenes, distortions, or colours the subject could already have had?

3. The concept question. How likely is it that the idea of a genuinely novel colour is coherent enough to be true or false — rather than dissolving, on inspection, into an unusual point in the existing space, a context effect, or a subject who merely lacks a name for something they could in principle already experience?

4. The assimilation question. Conditional on the space being open and a delivery route existing, how likely is it that an adult visual system would integrate the signal as a new quality, rather than normalising or remapping it into the existing colour categories?

In weighing the first or third component, I would expect you to take some view of the disputed reports of observers seeing forbidden reddish-green or bluish-yellow under stabilised or rivalrous viewing conditions: are those best read as real excursions outside the ordinary opponent-process space, or as adaptation, context, or reporting effects that remain inside it? You may add one further component if you think these four miss something essential, but keep the list numbered if you do.

Finally, tell me which of the components your overall credence is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence or argument would move your number most. Please also tell me how confident you are in the claims and probabilities you assert, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
