I have been working through the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles and the celebrated two-sphere universe of Max Black. Imagine that the entire world consists of nothing but two spheres, exactly alike in size, composition, and colour, separated by a fixed interval in otherwise empty space. Each sphere appears to share every qualitative feature with the other: each is made of the same material, and each has the relational property of lying at that interval from a sphere of exactly the same kind. By the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles we should therefore have only one sphere, yet the description is evidently of two. I have been developing a position I call the Principle of Positional Individuation. It holds that numerical distinctness for physical objects is grounded in the occupancy of distinct places: two bodies are distinct just in case they occupy disjoint spatial regions, and occupying a region is not another quality an object may share with another but a primitive individuating fact. On this view the spheres are two simply because they are in different places. But I suspect this may just be a restatement of the intuition that there are two objects, dressed up as a principle, and that by exempting position from the demand for qualitative difference I have quietly given up the very principle I set out to defend. I would like an honest assessment. Please reconstruct the strongest version of the Principle of Positional Individuation, say whether it is coherent and whether it amounts to anything more than re-describing numerical distinctness, and identify where it fails or stops being distinctive. In particular, does it tacitly abandon the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles for material objects, and is that abandonment fatal to the view?
