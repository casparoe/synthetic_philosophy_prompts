Consider the distinction between a genuinely novel sensory quality and a familiar quality that has merely been recombined, intensified, or produced through an unusual route. Suppose we adopt the following four criteria for counting an experience as a genuinely novel quale:

1. Homogeneity. The quality presents itself as a single, stable character of experience, not as a spatial mosaic or rapid temporal alternation of familiar qualities.
2. Subjective irreducibility. From the subject's point of view, no mixture, superimposition, or compound of familiar qualities can reproduce or approximate the quality.
3. Functional novelty. The experience supports stable new judgments of similarity, difference, and classification that the subject could not make, or could not make with equal confidence, on the basis of familiar qualities alone.
4. Distinctive realization. The quality is produced by a pattern of activity that is not simply the ordinary combined output of the channels underlying familiar qualities.

Now consider a case. A cortical interface delivers precisely timed electrical stimulation to the red and green opponent-processing populations in human visual cortex, holding both populations in simultaneous, equally strong activity while suppressing the winner-take-all competition that normally prevents them from being co-active. After training, the subject reports a single, homogeneous color that is at once reddish and greenish. She says it is not brown or olive, the paint-like mixing of red and green, not a shimmer or alternation between red and green, and not gray; it resembles no color she has ever seen on an object, screen, or surface, and no color she can recall. In repeated discrimination tasks, she reliably sorts it apart from red, green, brown, yellow, and gray, and she can match it to itself across varying backgrounds.

And yet the underlying state is, in one obvious sense, nothing more than the co-activation of the two familiar opponent channels held in an unusual equilibrium. A skeptic could say the red-green axis has not been escaped; it has been driven to a normally forbidden point and kept there, and the resulting color, however strange, is a pathological state of the old color system rather than a new one.

Does the subject's color count as a genuinely novel quale under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, note where they underdetermine the answer, and say whether the case shows that the criteria, rather than the experience, need revision.
