I am a long-time reader of rationalist blogs, and I have spent years reading about foreign policy, forecasting, and the uses of historical analogy. I would like an audit of a cluster of positions I have drifted into. I want to know whether the set hangs together, rather than being five things I picked up separately that quietly pull against each other once they are made precise.

(1) Named historical analogies in foreign-policy debate — Munich, Vietnam, 1914, Suez — are usually rhetorical instruments for a conclusion already reached on other grounds; the same analogy is routinely wheeled out by different sides to justify opposite policies.

(2) Some of these analogies nevertheless gesture at real, repeatable causal dynamics. Appeasement of a revisionist power can embolden it, and quagmire dynamics can trap an intervener. These dynamics are specific enough to ground probabilistic forecasts.

(3) Munich was misapplied in Vietnam and Iraq, and Vietnam was misapplied in the humanitarian interventions and counterinsurgency debates of the 1990s and 2000s; but these misapplications are failures to respect scope conditions, not evidence that analogical reasoning as such is worthless.

(4) The Thucydides trap — the thesis that structural stress between a rising power and an established power makes war markedly more likely — is a respectable generalisation, and it is reasonable to assign a substantially elevated prior probability of conflict to the US–China rivalry on that basis.

(5) Policy debate should nevertheless be purged of named historical analogies. Arguments should be restated as explicit causal models with declared base rates, and the bare label Munich should carry no argumentative weight of its own.

Is this a consistent package? I would like a ranked audit rather than a lecture: identify which commitments pull against which, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. Please give exactly five numbered findings, ordered from most to least serious, each a sentence or two. For each finding, say whether it is a genuine tension and what would have to give, or an apparent tension and why it dissolves. Write no introduction or conclusion, and use British English.
