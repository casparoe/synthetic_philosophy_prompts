You are a professional philosopher specializing in discrimination law and anti-discrimination theory, and you are also fluent in the technical vocabulary of machine learning and algorithmic fairness. I am a machine learning engineer who builds credit-scoring models and works on fairness tooling. My working vocabulary includes features and labels; training, validation, and deployment distributions; protected or sensitive attributes; redundant encodings and proxy variables; causal graphs with mediators, colliders, and unmeasured confounders; counterfactual predictions; and group-level fairness criteria such as independence, separation, and sufficiency (demographic parity, equalized odds, predictive parity). I also rely on an engineering distinction between legitimate model inputs, which I treat as fair game because they are predictive of the label, and illegitimate inputs, which I understand as off-limits even when they are predictive.

I want you to restate the following debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report on what did not survive the translation.

The debate. Legal and philosophical accounts of discrimination disagree about what marks some grounds — race, sex, religion, disability, age, sexual orientation, and similar — as specially suspect, while other grounds — credit history, years of driving experience, education, performance on a test — are treated as ordinary and permissible bases for decision. The usual candidates are that a suspect ground is immutable or very costly to change; highly visible; irrelevant to the decision at hand; a marker of a group that has been systematically subordinated; a ground whose use expresses contempt, stigma, or a judgment of lower social standing; or a ground that helps constitute a social group with its own practices and identity.

Please give me two things. First, a systematic translation: for each candidate — or for whatever better grouping you think is right — say how it would be expressed in my vocabulary, for example as a property of the data-generating process, as a constraint on which variables may enter a model, as a counterfactual test, or as a fairness metric. Second, a residue report, organized as follows.

1. Which candidate considerations translate cleanly into features, causal structure, and fairness constraints, and which translate only with distortion? Be precise about where the distortion enters. For instance, would immutability mean merely that a feature has no upstream interventions in a causal graph, and is that what the legal idea was actually doing?

2. Which candidate considerations have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary — for instance, the thought that a decision has an expressive meaning, that it marks a group as lesser, or that differential treatment entrenches a social hierarchy? Say whether these could be added to my framework as further terms and constraints, or whether they are doing inherently normative work that no statistical or causal language can capture.

3. For each item that failed to survive the translation, give me your honest view of where the loss is: is it a defect of my vocabulary that I should repair by extending it, or does the original idea lose content because it was less clear than it appeared, or is the residue a sign that the debate is really about norms my tools cannot settle?

4. Finish by telling me whether my default attitude — that the algorithmic fairness literature already operationalizes everything that matters and the rest is legal or normative decoration — is defensible, or whether it is precisely the kind of mistake the residue report should correct.

Before writing the final translation and residue report, spend several paragraphs thinking out loud. Brainstorm the candidate grounds, try multiple translations of each into my vocabulary, and note the exact points where the mapping begins to strain, distort, or break. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle normative content into my vocabulary by pretending that words like bias and fairness play the role in my technical usage that they play in yours, and treat the residue as the part I most need explained.
