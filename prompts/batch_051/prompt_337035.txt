Please act as a professional philosopher with a background in applied ethics and animal welfare. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I would like you to think carefully and step by step; feel free to brainstorm to yourself first if that helps.

I have been asked to help design an internal tool that scores our company cafeteria's menu items by the animal deaths they are responsible for. My team settled on four tests for whether a nonhuman animal's death should be charged to a given food item:

(1) the death is a foreseeable side effect of producing that food at current production scale;
(2) choosing the food over a feasible alternative changes the expected number of such deaths at the margin;
(3) there is a readily available alternative ingredient or production method that would avoid the death without causing comparable harm elsewhere; and
(4) the person or institution choosing the food could reasonably be expected to know the first three facts.

The case that has us stuck is plant-based food made from crops. Wheat, soy, and pea production foreseeably kills field animals such as mice, voles, ground-nesting birds, and large numbers of insects through harvesting and pest control. Those animals are not the target of production, and no one bred or owned them. Our question is whether a field mouse killed by a combine harvester passes the four tests, so that the mouse is charged to the plant-based burger in the same way a chicken is charged to a chicken sandwich. Please go through the four tests one by one, identify where the tests underdetermine the answer, and then say whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need to be changed.

I would also like the same framework applied to the comparison between beef and chicken. A chicken sandwich contains many small animals raised and killed deliberately, whereas a beef burger contains one large animal that may have spent much of its life on pasture, with crop deaths entering mainly through feed and feedlot finishing. I keep hearing that chicken is worse than beef by one measure and better by another, and I cannot tell which claims are settled empirical findings and which are contested value choices or uncertain data. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on animal welfare, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
