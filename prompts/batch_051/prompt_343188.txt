Act as a philosopher of law who has thought hard about the rule of law and enforcement discretion. I want you to think carefully and not just give me the first-pass textbook moves; start by brainstorming the different ways my position could fail before you rank them.

A friend of mine was recently charged under a local ordinance that, as far as anyone can tell, hadn't been enforced against anyone in decades. It wasn't about public safety; it got dusted off as leverage in an unrelated dispute. So I've landed on a view I didn't used to hold: that a law can be a rule-of-law problem just because it has fallen into desuetude (it's never enforced but never repealed), even if its content is perfectly reasonable. The defect isn't in the substance; its a defect in the law as a guide to conduct — people's actual reasons to expect how the law will be applied don't match what's on the books, and the law is sitting there as a tool for selective use.

I'd like to know what would count against this view. Not just the obvious objections, but the observations, distinctions, possible cases, and theoretical commitments that would have to be true for my position to be wrong or to need serious qualification. I want defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd also like to know which of them a defender of my view would concede genuinely count against it and which they'd dismiss (and on what grounds). In particular, press on the line between desuetude and ordinary prosecutorial discretion: is that distinction as clean as I'm assuming? If someone can coherently accept broad discretion but reject dead-letter laws, what exactly does their view commit them to? And be honest about insulation: if my view can be patched in response to every counterexample, tell me whether that's a feature or a reason to distrust it.
