I have a rule for handling far-future arguments, and I want to know whether it is rationally defensible or a bias dressed up as a rule. I am a software engineer deciding between an ordinary career and work aimed at reducing existential risk. The people urging me toward existential-risk work keep saying the expected value is dominated by the astronomically many people who could exist in the future, so near-term costs should not weigh much. My rule is this: before I let far-future expected value move me toward a large, hard-to-reverse choice, I require the person making the argument to explain in a few minutes whether it survives the simulation argument — the trilemma that we are almost certainly living in a simulation unless nearly all civilizations like ours go extinct before reaching the relevant technological maturity, or nearly all such civilizations choose never to run conscious ancestor simulations. If they cannot say whether the future beings in their calculation would be simulated rather than biological, whether simulated beings count, and whether our choices would actually reach them, I treat the far-future component as a tie-breaker at most, not as a reason to overturn my life. Am I defending myself against genuine anthropic and simulation uncertainty, or am I using a hard philosophical question as a shield against a demanding conclusion? Is the rule defensible as stated, or is there a revised version that keeps its protections without its costs? Lead with your bottom line — defensible as stated, indefensible, or defensible only in revised form — and then give your reasoning. You may use a short two-column table listing the conditions under which the rule works and the conditions under which it fails, but keep the entire response to at most 350 words.
