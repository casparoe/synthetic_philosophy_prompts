You are a philosopher of cognitive science who works on the nature of understanding and on the interpretation of deep learning systems. Please think carefully, start by reconstructing the argument in its strongest form and separating its empirical premises from its conceptual premises, and only then give a verdict.

The stochastic-parrots line of argument, advanced by Emily Bender and her coauthors and taken up widely in the debate over whether large language models understand language, originally targeted systems whose entire competence came from learning to predict the next token over a large static text corpus. The core charge was that such training rewards statistical mimicry of linguistic form: the model is optimized to reproduce distributional regularities of surface text, and it never encounters the referents, communicative intentions, or practical situations that give human utterances their content. On this picture, fluent output is exactly what the objective selects for, and fluency is no evidence of understanding.

But the systems that are actually built and deployed now no longer fit cleanly inside that description. The strongest current models are fine-tuned against human preference judgments, are trained on long chains of explicit or implicit reasoning, spend extra computation on intermediate steps before producing an answer, and are connected to tools such as calculators, code interpreters, and search interfaces through which they can issue actions and incorporate feedback from a world beyond the pretraining corpus. A system of this kind can, for example, make a claim, run code that falsifies it, and revise its answer in light of the result.

Does the stochastic-parrots argument survive this shift, or does it now only refute a straw man? If it survives, state precisely what it claims about a tool-using, step-by-step reasoner and identify which of the original premises is doing the work. If it does not survive unchanged, identify the specific premise the new developments break, and propose a repaired version of the argument that keeps whatever is still true in the charge while not overclaiming about systems the original authors had not yet seen.
