I spent my career as an experimental physicist before retiring, and I have been reading decision theory for about a year. The problem I keep returning to is the diachronic money-pump argument against intransitive preferences. That argument seems to me to work only if a silent assumption holds: that an option keeps the same decision value regardless of the larger sequence or menu in which the trade is embedded. I want a thought experiment that isolates exactly that assumption.

Build me a money-pump case to the following specifications.

Form: one ordinary, realistic exchange setting—a market stall, a swap at work, an online resale platform, or something similar. No lotteries, no time discounting, no changing of tastes over the course of the encounter, no deception by the trader, no precommitment devices, and no appeal to the agent's ignorance. The agent is fully informed, at every step, of all the offers the trader will make in the sequence.

The agent, under forced binary choice with each pair presented alone, exhibits a strict cycle over three options: A over B, B over C, C over A. The trader may attach a small fee to each swap, but each fee must be small enough that the corresponding pairwise choice still favors the swap when the pair is offered alone.

The agent's preferences, however, are defined over whole choice situations—menus, sequences, or histories of transactions—rather than over bare options. The trader then attempts the standard pump: starting from A, trade to C, then to B, then back to A, collecting a fee each round.

The scenario must be engineered so that, on a revealed-preference reading of the pairwise data (choices define preferences and context is irrelevant), the trader confidently predicts acceptance at each stage. On a sequence-sensitive reading (the agent chooses over menus or histories and may refuse a trade because the sequence that contains it leaves her worse off), the pump stalls and the agent ends the encounter with her original endowment intact.

Then provide:

1. The scenario and the trader's exact sequence of offers.
2. A walkthrough under each of the two readings: which steps are accepted or refused under each, and why.
3. The single structural feature that does the work in blocking the pump, and why it is that feature rather than anything else. I suspect it will be the agent's entitlement to evaluate the first trade as an element of an entire sequence rather than as a bare binary choice, but the scenario must generate that result rather than stipulate it; correct me if I am wrong.
4. The pressure test: remove that feature—for example, allow the trader to present each offer so that later offers are not visible, or force the agent to decide each trade without reference to future trades. Does the pump then go through, and what does that show about what the original argument actually establishes?
5. An honest closing note: what argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about where the irrationality lies—in the cyclic pairwise preferences themselves rather than in the choice architecture—and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

This is for a short paper on whether diachronic money-pump arguments show anything over and above the fact that pairwise-choice data can mislead when options are embedded in sequences. So do not hand me a case that simply stipulates that the agent refuses the key trade; each verdict must fall out of the two readings. If you judge the full specification impossible, build the nearest satisfiable case and state exactly which constraint you had to relax and why the original specification failed.

Hard length limit: 1,200 words.
