Okay, so this got personal for me recently: I busted my wrist and spent a few weeks in physical therapy, re-learning to type and hold a pen and do all the tiny rotations the hand does without asking. What surprised me was that the skill didn't come back as instructions. The PT kept telling me to supinate and pronate and keep the wrist neutral, and the words did basically nothing; what actually worked was reaching for the mug or the key, and then my hand sort of found the shape on its own before I could say how. I've come around to something close to Merleau-Ponty's position: skilled movement isn't run by an inner model or by explicit knowledge, it's carried by a lived body with its own motor intentionality — the body grasps the task directly, pre-reflectively, and the reflexive I shows up late to sign the receipt.

I want to stress-test that, not have it praised. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this picture of motor intentionality to be wrong? I know some of the standard pressure points — deafferented patients who apparently have to watch their limbs to move them, ideomotor apraxia where someone can use an object but can't pantomime using it, motor imagery work that makes movement look like something the brain simulates — but I don't know how deep any of that cuts. Which of these should a defender of the lived-body view concede as counting against it, and which get dismissed as being about a different level of description? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. And tell me honestly wether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if any counterexample gets absorbed by saying it's all still pre-reflective or holistic — and whether that's actually a problem for a phenomenological claim or just the price of doing embodied cognition. The thing that would move me most is evidence that movements are routinely planned and corrected using internal representations when the body is otherwise healthy, not just in damaged or weird cases.
