I have been working through the dispute between Searle and Dennett over whether intentionality can be original as opposed to merely derived, and I want help mapping out what my position commits me to and what would count against it. Let me state the position precisely. I reject Searle's conclusion that artifacts have only derived intentionality and therefore minds cannot be computational; but I do not reject it by insisting that computational states could have intrinsic content after all. Instead, I deny that the contrast between original and derived intentionality marks a difference in kind. My view is that the mental states of human beings are themselves derived intentionality in the only sense of derived that survives scrutiny: their content is fixed the way the content of maps, sentences, and instruments is fixed, namely by an interpretative stance embedded in a history of usage, calibration, and selective pressure, except that for humans the interpreting system and the interpreted system are not cleanly separated. Genuine aboutness exists; it is just relational or positional, never intrinsic. I also think Searle's appeal to original intentionality is what Dennett called a two-bitser: either the notion of original intentionality is doing real explanatory work and is too mysterious to be a difference-maker, or it reduces to something that artifacts could in principle possess.

Now here is what I want. Tell me what would have to be true for me to be wrong — not merely wrong about Searle's rhetoric, but wrong that human intentionality is derived in the same ontic sense as a map's. What observations, thought experiments, or results in the theory of content would be decisive against my view, and which would merely be awkward? Which cases should worry me most: the apparent fact that I can intend things no one has ever interpreted, the semantic capacities of pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals, or the possibility of content-bearing states in a solitary mind that has never been part of any interpreting community? A defender of a view like mine might gracefully concede some complications and still not change their mind; what would a concession that is genuinely damning look like? I personally am most vulnerable to the intuition that a system whose content is grounded only positionally could not be wrong in the first place, because there would be no fact of the matter about what its states are about. Please say whether that is the point where the view survives or collapses.
