I am drawn to a strong version of Hofstadter's ant-colony picture: that an ant colony can literally have a mind — not merely that it coordinates as if it had one, but that the colony-wide flow of pheromones, antennal contacts, and division of labor realizes states with genuine intentional content, such as goals and an ongoing model of the nest's surroundings, even though no individual ant has a mind. Treat that position as the object of inquiry and tell me what would count against it. Which discoverable facts about real colonies would be hardest for the view to absorb? Which conceptual commitments does it stand or fall with — substrate neutrality, functionalism, the distinction between cognition and mere self-organization, the relation between mind and consciousness? Where should a defender draw the line between a minded system and a complex adaptive system, and what does it mean if that line cannot be drawn in a principled way? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me a committed verdict on whether the position still risks anything or has been made compatible with every conceivable finding — and whether, for a claim of this kind, that would be a defect. Open with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating of the question.
