I've been on my hospital's triage committee for a few years now, and I've read enough of the QALY-versus-fair-chances literature to feel like I'm watching two sides argue past each other in slow motion. On one side you've got the maximizers: when there's one ventilator and several patients, allocate it to whoever is expected to get the most QALYs out of it, because the whole point of triage is to get the most health from a scarce resource and a QALY is a QALY no matter whose it is. On the other side you've got the fair-chances crowd: they say that turns triage into a covert ranking of whose life is worth more, it quietly discounts older and disabled people, and the only defensible thing is an equal lottery, or a weighted lottery, or a prioritarian rule that favors the worst-off, or something like first-come-first-served so at least you're not pretending to be a god. Both camps look at the same cases — one 20-year-old with decades ahead vs one 80-year-old with a few years; one person vs five equally situated strangers; the patient who arrived first vs the one with better expected survival — and nobody's moved in decades.

I have a working theory: the real fault line isn't about the numbers at all; it's about whether you're allowed to aggregate small benefits across many people so they outweigh a larger benefit to one person, and underneath that, about what triage is for. Is it a health-maximizing algorithm or a social practice for distributing a shared bad fairly when there's no good option? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey.
