I teach a required media-literacy unit to first-year journalism students, and this term I want to build a session around an idea I have been calling the epistemic backstop. My students already understand the standard worry that a convincing deepfake might make someone believe something false. They have not yet considered the second-order problem I want to make the center of the session. Photographs, audio recordings, and video have long functioned as a shared, neutral backstop: evidence that can end a dispute because everyone, whatever their prior commitments, treats the recording as a record of what actually happened. The backstop fails not only when a fake is believed; it also fails when the mere availability of deepfakes gives anyone a standing excuse to dismiss an inconvenient genuine recording as synthesized. Once "it could be AI" is always available, even authentic evidence loses its power to settle disagreements, and the damage runs through institutions, journalism, the courts, and electoral politics. I want them to leave the room seeing this as a crisis of shared evidentiary trust, not a puzzle about detecting fakes.

I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and students with no background in philosophy, and I want a plan for teaching the idea rather than the explanation itself. Please lay out: the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; the single running example that should carry the idea all session — I had planned to use a clip of a politician apparently saying something damning, but I suspect that frames everything around whether the clip is real rather than around what evidence can settle once it might not be, so tell me whether that example works and what a better one would need to do; the misconceptions they will likely bring or form — I already expect "the problem is that we can no longer tell what is true" and "better detection tools will fix this" — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off; and a final question or exercise that would show me whether they have actually grasped the backstop idea rather than learned a slogan. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and why.
