As a health reporter for a general-audience magazine of science and ideas, I'm writing about whether a county health system's new cancer-screening outreach program worked. Two years after the rollout, survival among patients diagnosed at an early stage has risen, and survival among those diagnosed at an advanced stage has also risen. Yet five-year survival across all patients diagnosed since the rollout is slightly lower than before, because the program brought in many previously unscreened, high-risk patients who were more often diagnosed at an advanced stage. The health system insists the stage-by-stage comparison is the fair one; a statistician I consulted warns that stage at diagnosis is not a neutral variable to adjust for, since the program itself may change when and how patients get staged. My editor wants me to report either that the program improved survival or that it made survival worse. Which conclusion should I run with, and what principle should determine how I present the numbers?
