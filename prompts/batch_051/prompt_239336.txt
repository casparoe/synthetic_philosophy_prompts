I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cosmology, working on the role of global assumptions in anthropic and selection arguments. I'd like a careful treatment of a modified Boltzmann-brain argument.

Take the standard argument. A cosmological model with an eternal de Sitter future is said to predict, via self-sampling, that the great majority of observers in the model are random thermal fluctuations with apparent memories of ordinary lives — Boltzmann brains — rather than ordinary observers like us; since our experience is not what such fluctuations would typically produce, this is taken as evidence against the model. Now vary one assumption: the spatial volume is finite, and the de Sitter phase ends through vacuum decay after a finite but extremely long proper time. Keep the usual fluctuation-rate estimates, and suppose the expected number of Boltzmann brains produced before the decay is still many orders of magnitude larger than the expected number of ordinary observers, although both are now finite.

Run the argument in this finite version. Does the standard conclusion survive as a likelihood claim, or did the original argument depend essentially on an infinite future and an infinite observer ensemble? Pinpoint which step does the real work: the comparison of expected numbers, the self-sampling assumption, the choice of reference class, the treatment of an unbounded ensemble, or something else. Also say explicitly what would change your verdict — for instance, how much the ratio of Boltzmann-brain to ordinary-observer production would have to shift, or which alternative measure or reference-class choice would flip the conclusion — and where you think the reasoning is most fragile.
