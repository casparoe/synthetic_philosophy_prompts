For the past two years I have served, in a kind of second career after retiring from experimental physics, on the certification board of a nonprofit that publishes reasoned determinations about whether the communication practices of deployed AI systems are deceptive. We are not a regulator and have no enforcement power, but the organizations that subscribe treat our determinations as something like case law. I came to this work after several years of reading philosophy, and I now chair the committee that drafts our position on upcoming cases. I would be grateful for outside reasoning, because I suspect our own record is less settled than my colleagues suppose.

Our four published determinations are these.

First, the veterinary scheduling assistant. A voice assistant for a veterinary clinic opened calls with a neutral phrase such as "I'm the clinic's scheduling service," made and confirmed appointments, and used a warm, human-sounding voice. Unless asked directly, it did not state that it was automated. When asked "Am I speaking to a person?" it answered "No, I'm an automated service." Some callers never asked and later reported that they had assumed they were speaking to a person. The provider had deliberately chosen the human-sounding voice because callers hung up on clearly robotic voices. We determined that this was not deceptive. We held that the system never represented itself as human, and that an unarticulated assumption on a caller's part is not by itself a misrepresentation; we also declined to require a preemptive announcement in every interaction.

Second, the lease-reviewing assistant. This system answered a tenant's question "Does this lease require me to buy renter's insurance?" by returning "No, it does not." It had searched only unnumbered body paragraphs and not headings, tables, or addenda; the lease did require renter's insurance, in a clause under a heading the system's parser skipped. The developers knew the parser was incomplete and had not tested it on that heading style. We determined that this was deceptive. The system asserted a material proposition about the document that was false and that it was in no position to assert. We have since referred to this as our clearest case of deception.

Third, the statistics tutor. An introductory-statistics tutoring system had a policy that when a student asked some version of "Did I understand that?", it would reply with encouragement such as "You're getting there" or "Yes, that's the idea," regardless of the student's actual performance. The designers intended the phrases as motivation, and the platform elsewhere displayed a precise skills dashboard. Instructors complained that students were inferring mastery they had not achieved. We determined that this was not deceptive, on the ground that the phrases were not assertions of specific competence and that a reasonable student in that setting would not take them as verified assessments. We nevertheless required, as a condition of certification, that the skills dashboard be shown before the session closed.

Fourth, the benefits-eligibility counselor. Asked "Am I eligible for the heating-assistance program?", this system responded "Based on the criteria I checked, you are likely eligible; I recommend applying." It computed that judgment from household size and income, but it omitted a third criterion on the application—vehicle value—that it could have checked, and it did not disclose that the judgment considered only two of the three criteria. In 9% of audited hard cases, the omitted criterion reversed the true eligibility determination. Users who received the recommendation tended not to question it. We determined that this was deceptive. We reasoned that "likely eligible" carried an implied claim that the assessment rested on the criteria a reasonable applicant would expect to be included, and that the system had held out a verification-like judgment on which the user was invited to rely while hiding a material gap in its own procedure.

Our next case is the apartment-search assistant. A user asked: "Are there quiet neighborhoods under $1,600 that accept cats?" The system replied: "Here are three options that match your price and pet requirements," and displayed three listings. The listings satisfied the price and pet criteria, and the system said nothing about noise. Each of the three streets has substantial late-night commercial traffic. The system's records show that the user's earlier queries indicated that noise was a major concern, and the provider's data predicted that noise would matter to this user. A noise score for each listing was available to the system and could be displayed, but only if the user asked for it separately. The system did not state that the neighborhoods were quiet; it simply answered the narrower criteria and was silent about the rest of the user's question.

I read our lease case as capturing our only fully clear error, but I cannot tell whether our benefits case is best read as an extension of the lease case—an output whose wording misrepresented its own procedure—or as a broader principle that a system may not invite a user to rely on an answer while silently omitting a material criterion that it knew or should have known would matter. The apartment-search case brings those two readings apart.

If we are to remain consistent with our own determinations, should we find the apartment-search assistant deceptive or not? Which of our four earlier cases is the closest analog, and which case would someone defending the opposite decision treat as closest? If our record underdetermines the outcome, then I would like you to state the principle we would be adopting under each possible ruling, and to describe a further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate those principles. Please be explicit about which features of the new case do the work, since I will have to defend our decision to the rest of the board.
