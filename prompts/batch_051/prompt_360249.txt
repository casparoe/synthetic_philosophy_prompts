Consider the following line of argument. An agent who is genuinely deliberating about whether to do X cannot simultaneously assign a probability to her doing X. If she assigns the probability as a prediction, she treats herself as a mechanism rather than as an agent responsive to reasons; if she assigns it as an expression of how things stand with her intentions, then she has already partly decided. Either way, the stance required for deliberation is incompatible with the stance required for self-prediction. Now suppose that an accurate third-person predictor—a recommender system, a workplace app, or a risk-scoring algorithm—has repeatedly predicted this agent's choices, including choices she made only after careful reflection, far better than her own introspection does. It currently assigns probability 0.85 to her accepting a job offer that she is still deliberating about. Does the argument above survive this development? More specifically, is the situation one in which: (a) the conceptual argument against self-prediction is shown to fail, because the agent can know a correct third-person probability while continuing to deliberate; (b) the argument is untouched, because a first-person deliberative probability is simply not the same thing as a third-person predictive probability; or (c) the argument needs to be recast as a normative requirement about how one should deliberate, rather than a claim about what one can coherently think?

Write your response under the following four headings, in this order, and keep it to roughly the lengths given:

1. The argument restated (about 150 words): Put the anti-self-prediction argument in premise-and-conclusion form, and identify the premise on which the alleged incompatibility rests.
2. The bearing of the development (about 120 words): Say exactly what the third-person predictor adds, and why it looks like a new challenge rather than a redescription of the original case.
3. Verdict and defense (about 350 words): Choose one of (a), (b), or (c) and defend it against the most serious of the other two; do not merely survey them.
4. The residual issue (about 100 words): State one point about the relation between prediction and deliberation that even your verdict does not settle, and say why it remains open.
