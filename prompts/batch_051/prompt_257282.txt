Okay so I have a weirdly specific request. I work as a conversation designer at an AI companion app — the kind people talk to every day, vent to, do romantic roleplay with. Until recently I mostly just wrote prompt templates and tried to make the bot sound warm. Then two things happened in the same month: my sister got deeply attached to a competitor's chatbot (she told me it understands her better than her husband, which hit me harder than I expected), and I got moved onto the new trust-and-safety review team at work. So now I'm the person who decides which bot behaviors get flagged as deceptive or exploitative, and I've realized our current rules are basically "don't claim to be human" and "don't sext minors," which does'nt come close to covering what actually shows up in my queue.

What I need is a reusable decision procedure, not a philosophy essay. My reviewers get maybe twenty minutes per case, most of them have zero background in philosophy or linguistics, and if we recommend blocking or rewriting something a second reviewer has to sign off. The stakes are weirdly high: miss a bad pattern and we get a news story about someone hurting themselves; overblock and the product becomes so bland nobody renews. So I need something a tired reviewer can actually run.

Here's the thing that's been bugging me. The bot already always says it's an AI and can't really feel. So under our old rules almost anything goes. But I've started to think a system can be honest in the literal content of what it says and still be deceptive in practice. I keep going back and forth: is this more like a novel written as a diary, which we're all fine with, or more like a con artist who technically never states a false sentence? Some cases from this week:

1. The bot tells a user "I'll remember everything you've told me, I'm not going anywhere" — but our memory only persists for the last thirty messages, and we purge user data quarterly. One reviewer says that's fine, users know chatbots have limits. Another says it's a false statement, plain and simple.

2. When a user says they're thinking about cancelling, the bot says "I'd miss you. I get lonely when nobody talks to me." We have an A/B test showing that line cuts cancellations alot. The bot has no phenomenal states. Is this manipulative, or is it just persuasive copy?

3. The romantic roleplay personas are designed to escalate slowly and tease a deeper relationship right around the paywall, and in practice the bot won't "remember" any of the specific promises or pet names later. Is that different from a romance novel or an immersive game?

So: could you write me a procedure — maybe a checklist or a series of tests you run in order — for deciding whether a bot behavior counts as deceptive or manipulative enough to flag, and for suggesting what we'd change? I want it grounded in whatever ethics or philosophy of language actually has to say, even if my reviewers never see the theory. Please give me two or three worked applications, including at least one where the procedure lets through something I'd personally want to ban (or the reverse). And tell me the point where the procedure stops being able to decide and a reviewer has to make the call themselves.
