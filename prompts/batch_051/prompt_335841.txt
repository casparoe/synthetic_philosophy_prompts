I never studied history or philosophy formally, but I have read a great deal of narrative history, and over the years I have settled into a view that I would like you to stress-test as systematically as possible. The view is about whether the famous turning points ever really turned on anything. Put roughly: the broad shape of major historical outcomes — who ends up dominant, which institutions survive, what kind of economy or political order a region gets — was mostly fixed by slow background conditions: geography, population, economic incentives, available technology, and long-standing institutions. The famous contingencies, a lost order at a battle, an assassination, a storm that scattered a fleet, a commander's hesitation, an election decided by a few hundred votes, mostly changed timing and local detail rather than the long-run pattern. I have been calling these close counterfactuals: change the battle, keep the century. On my view, only much larger changes to the background conditions themselves, long-shot counterfactuals, would have produced a recognizably different course of events. I also suspect the view has an asymmetry built into it: the closer a counterfactual is to what actually happened, the more vivid and more nearly impossible to settle it is; the further it is, the more consequential and the less constrained it is — so the interesting questions may be exactly the ones we cannot answer.

I am not asking for a general essay in reply, and I am not asking you to argue me into or out of the view. I want a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false; which observations, cases, arguments, formal results, or rival approaches would count against it; what it predicts about the historical record that a contingency-heavy rival would not predict; and whether it has been insulated by its own qualifications. For each candidate defeater, I need to know whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, and whether someone who accepted the view would themselves concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it without cost. Do not restrict yourself to the obvious objection that we cannot rerun history; that is a starting point, not the whole map. And keep the discussion tied to counterfactual reasoning about actual historical processes, not to free will or general determinism.
