I'm a novelist six months into a draft about a war photographer back in a city that has no idea what shes seen, and I keep stubbing my toe on a question I cant think about straight on, so I want to think through a book instead. The question is roughly this: we call courage a virtue, but the paradigm cases are usually military — holding a line, going back for a wounded comrade, bearing up under fire. If the virtue gets its sense from that paradigmatic context, what happens to it in peacetime? Does a peaceful society still have a use for martial courage, does the virtue get domesticated into something like the courage to tell the truth at a dinner party, or does the returning soldier just become a misfit whose virtues function as symptoms now? I keep circling Mrs. Dalloway — Septimus the shell-shocked veteran, and Clarissa with her party and her nervy little endurance. Read the novel through that lens for me. What does Woolf actually show about whether martial courage remains a virtue outside war: does it survive, transform, get displaced onto civilian life, or just curdle into pathology? Where does the novel push back on my framing — maybe the martial/civilian split is one the book does not respect? And what does the book add to the philosophical question that arguing about virtue theory bare wouldnt give me? I dont need quotes or page numbers; I want the reading, and I wont mind if the honest answer is that my question was badly posed.
