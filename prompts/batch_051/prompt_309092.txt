I recently joined the deployment-safety committee at a lab that develops a general-purpose software engineering agent, and I have to vote on a new safeguard later this week. Our committee reviews control measures specifically, not alignment measures. We do not ask whether the model's goals are the right ones; we assume the model may be misaligned and ask whether a given safeguard would still prevent it from doing serious harm.

Our record of decisions so far is this. First, we approved full logging of every tool call and model action, with the logs held outside the model's reach and a human auditor reviewing a random sample each week. The consensus was that passive observation is cheap, does not perturb the model's behavior, and could catch the early stages of an escape or sabotage attempt.

Second, we rejected a proposal to plant a honeypot inside the live production codebase. The idea was to create a fake repository, seeded with attractive credentials, that would alert us if the model tried to copy from it. The decisive objection was not that honeypots are useless in the abstract, but that this one fed a deliberate falsehood to a system whose good behavior we were explicitly refusing to assume. A model that caught onto the deception might alter its behavior in ways that destroyed the evidential value of the honeypot, and the measure sat inside the very environment the model was meant to be helping with.

Third, we approved with conditions a proposal to remove all network egress from the model's runtime for a limited pilot. The rationale was that this measure neither persuaded, tempted, nor observed the model; it simply made certain bad outcomes physically impossible. The conditions were that the restriction had to be disclosed to the model and to any client, and that there had to be a date-certain review for restoring egress rather than letting the restriction become permanent without a fresh decision.

Fourth, we rejected a proposal for an automated kill switch that would freeze execution whenever an action classifier flagged the model's behavior as dangerous. The main ground for rejection was that the classifier was expected to interrupt many harmless sessions, and no human judgment stood between the classifier and the disruption of a client's work.

Now the proposal before us is a transparent tool-call cap. The runtime would hard-limit the model to sixty tool calls per hour, and the limit would be enforced by the runtime itself, so even a determined model could not override it. The model would simply be told that the limit exists. When a session hits the cap, execution stops, the client sees a short summary of what happened, and the client may lift the cap for that session with a single action. There is no deception and no classifier; the expected cost is roughly a five percent reduction in peak capacity. Our auditors believe many of the most dangerous failure trajectories require long autonomous chains of action, and the cap would bound those chains, but only while the client chooses not to lift it.

Is the closest analog to this proposal the network-egress restriction or the automated kill switch? Is our record best read as permitting transparent, non-deceptive hard limits, or as requiring a human judgment before any intervention interrupts a client's work? I expect our precedents to underdetermine this, so tell me what principle we would be adopting if we approve, and what principle we would be adopting if we reject. I am not looking for a menu of options; I want your best, committed recommendation and the reasoning that supports it.
