My roommate just took a medieval philosophy course and hasn't shut up about Abelard, who before this I only knew as the Heloise guy. Last night she summarized his whole view of sin for me like this: the sin is entirely in the intention, and the outward act itself is morally indifferent; even the desire — even enjoying the thought of the thing — isn't yet sin, because what matters is only whether you consent to it. So someone who consents to adultery but is interupted before actually doing it is exactly as guilty as someone who goes through with it, and someone who produces the "right" outward result by accident or for selfish motives gets no moral credit for it. From this she drew two big conclusions. First, that for Abelard, as long as your intention is good you basically can't sin — so if someone deliberately kills a person to end their suffering, out of compassion, Abelard would have to call that sinless. Second, that his view implies human punishment should stop caring so much about outward behavior and start caring only about people's hearts, since the deed itself adds nothing.

I know enough to be suspicious of a summary like that, because it sounds both too neat and too convenient. I half-remember that Abelard had a three-stage picture — suggestion, delight, consent — but I couldn't say what each stage does, and I'm especially unsure whether her two conclusions really follow from the accurate part. Could you give me an accuracy audit? In particular: what is she getting right, what is she subtly getting wrong, and does "sin is in the consent, not in the act" actually commit Abelard to either "good intentions can't sin" or "only punish the heart"? And on the compassionate-killing case, what would Abelard's own distinction actually say?
