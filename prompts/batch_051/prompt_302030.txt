Analyze the May 6, 2010 flash crash through the lens of the tool-AI/agent-AI distinction. To keep the analysis from depending on hazy memory, I will stipulate the salient facts: at about 2:32 p.m., a single large sell algorithm began working a several-billion-dollar order in E-mini S&P 500 futures under a volume-participation rule that paid no attention to price or time; high-frequency market makers first absorbed the order flow, then their inventories grew one-sided and they began trading among themselves in a hot-potato spike in volume; the Dow fell roughly 1,000 points within minutes and recovered most of the loss shortly after; and many plainly erroneous trades were later canceled. I know the standard market-microstructure account, so do not rehearse it. I want a disciplined mapping onto the tool/agent distinction: who or what was the agent, the individual algorithms, the firms that ran them, the designers, or the market as a whole? What does the distinction say the relevant humans should have done ex ante, and does it explain why nobody paused the system in real time? Where does the theory go silent or misdescribe the episode, for instance, was this an agent problem at all or an interaction problem? And what does the crash show about tool AI as a design target: is it stable, or does tool-like behavior become agent-like precisely under fast-moving, competitive conditions? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. As you go, separate what is settled about the episode and the tool/agent divide from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
