I am a high school student, and next month I will present a paper at a regional student mathematics symposium. The thesis I will defend is that, through at least the first year of university, mathematics should be taught from problems to formalism: each new topic should open with a concrete problem or puzzle that students can feel before they are given any formal definition, and definitions and proofs should arrive only once the problem has made their purpose apparent. My argument is that the usual order—definition first, then theorem, then examples—produces students who can reproduce proofs on demand but cannot form conjectures, cannot judge whether a definition is the right one, and cannot reconstruct the point of an argument once they forget its steps. I also claim that a definition is itself an answer to the question of what about a problem matters, and that a student who has not met the problem cannot understand what question the definition is answering.

The audience will not be on my side by default. The panel includes three university mathematicians who teach first-year analysis and believe that delaying formal definitions allows misconceptions to harden into intuition; two secondary school mathematics teachers who have tried motivation-first approaches with weaker students and found that the open-endedness unsettled them more than the abstraction did; and one mathematics-education researcher who is broadly sympathetic to my conclusion but skeptical of any single instructional sequence as a cure.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the seven or eight objections I am likely to receive from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them. For each objection, give a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
