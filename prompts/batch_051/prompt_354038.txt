You are a philosopher of biology who specializes in cultural evolution, and you are leading a graduate seminar on memetics — the project of treating cultural items ("memes") as units that undergo a Darwinian process of variation, selection, and heredity. Your students have assembled the following list of standard objections to that picture. You consider the list accurate and, as a list of the canonical objections, complete:

1. The individuation objection: there is no principled way to say how large a meme is, where one meme ends and the next begins, or which features belong to a given meme. Without well-defined units, talk of copying fidelity and of selection pressures on individual memes is hard to ground.

2. The fidelity objection: cultural transmission is largely reconstruction rather than replication. Each receiver produces a renovated version of a tune, story, recipe, or ritual, so mutation is the default condition, not a rare copying error.

3. The missing-genotype objection: genetic evolution separates a copied genotype from its expressed phenotype, so there is something stable and shielded from immediate modification to inherit. Cultural traits are typically expressed and modified at the same time, with no protected germ line.

4. The directed-variation objection: much cultural novelty results from foresight and deliberate design rather than blind variation, so the variation is not independent of later selection, and the distinction between variation and selection loses much of its force.

5. The reticulation objection: cultural traits blend, recombine, and spread horizontally among unrelated hosts, yielding a reticulated network rather than a branching, mostly vertical genealogy of the kind Darwinian explanations standardly assume.

6. The selfishness objection: attributing agency or interests to memes — saying they "exploit" hosts or "want" to spread — is at best an intentional-stance shorthand and at worst a redescription that masquerades as an explanation.

7. The unfalsifiability objection: evolutionary narratives for cultural traits can usually be reverse-engineered to fit whatever is observed, so memetic accounts risk being just-so stories rather than testable claims.

Your task is to supply one more objection that is genuinely different in structure from all seven: not a rewording, not a special case of any item, and not a combination of items. The objection may be one you have encountered or one you devise yourself; either way, novelty of structure matters. Begin by brainstorming several candidate objections and explicitly say why each candidate is either a variant of one of the seven or is too weak to use. Then present your single best new objection, state it precisely, and assess honestly how serious it is — for example, whether it is fatal to the memetic program, serious but repairable, or merely a boundary condition.
