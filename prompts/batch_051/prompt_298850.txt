In my physics class I keep noticing that when people get a question wrong it's not just random guessing—like, a bunch of kids will say a ball thrown straight up still has some of the throw's force in it pushing it upward until the force runs out, or that a moving object needs a constant push to keep moving at all. And they can sort of give reasons, it's not like they made it up on the spot. I read that some education researchers think of these as actual alternative theories: students have something like a stable 'impetus' theory of motion—a mini-theory about motion needing an internal supply of force—and that theory causes them to make systematic errors, kind of like the way a wrong scientific theory would. But then I saw a different claim that when you interview students really carefully, their ideas are way less coherent than that. The same kid will say one thing on one question and a totally different thing on a very similar question, and what looked like a mini-theory falls apart into a bunch of loose little intuitions that dont really connect to each other. So my question is whether the 'misconceptions as theories' idea survives that kind of evidence. If students' wrong ideas are mostly fragmentary and context-dependent, is it still fair to treat them as theories, or does that whole way of thinking about student error need to be repaired (or thrown out)? I want to know which specific part of the theories-view is actually under pressure, and whether there's a weaker version that still says something useful. Also, tell me what would actually change your mind: what kind of evidence or argument would make you switch sides, or where your reasoning is shakiest.
