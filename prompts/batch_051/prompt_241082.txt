I'm a retired evolutionary biologist — fiddler-crab courtship, mostly — and I've recently started reading the philosophy of religion for pleasure, which is probably why I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for. Take two cases. A small plain-dressing religious community near where I grew up expected young members to spend a year in a voluntary service posting, learn a minority dialect with no outside use, and keep wearing clothes that made them conspicuously odd to their neighbours. Seperately, a colleague doing fieldwork in South Asia described a temple sect where initiates fast for weeks, walk a long barefoot pilgramage, and sign over a sizeable slice of any inheritance to the community. Now, in animal communication, when a display is costly and hard to fake we treat it as an honest signal: a peacock's tail advertises quality because a sickly bird simply can't grow a decent one. But here the costs don't seem mainly to reveal a pre-existing quality — the people who finish weren't obviously more committed before they started; the costs seem to manufacture the commitment, while also making it uneconomical for a half-hearted free-rider to stay. And the very uselessness of the requirements is part of the point: a display with no outside payoff is one only a genuine insider would bother paying.

I want to understand whether this is one mechanism or two, and where the boundary between them lies. Is there an established name for requirements that simultaneously screen out free-riders and create commitment after the fact? If the closest thing is the handicap principle imported from biology, I can see the resemblance, but I keep feeling the peacock analogy papers over the difference between advertising quality and producing it. There also seems to be a second, more psychological idea in the neighbourhood — something about people coming to value what they suffered for — and I can't tell whether it's doing separate work or is just a paraphrase of the first. I'm less after a label than after the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and how one would tell the screening function and the commitment-making function apart in a real group.

If you could write the reply in British English, I'd be grateful — old habits.
