A friend and I keep having a low-grade argument about whether I'm weirdly rigid about being on time, and after a genuinly annoying evening where two people flaked on a dinner and one showed up forty minutes late without texting, I decided to write down what I actually believe about punctuality and small promises. It came out like this:

1. If I tell someone I'll do a small thing—bring the wine, show up at 6:30, send the link—I'm on the hook for it just because I said I would, even if it turns out the thing wasn't needed. The wrong isn't only the downstream inconvenience.
2. The main reason being late is bad is that the other person gives up time waiting, time they can't get back. If they filled the wait fine—got absorbed in a book, took a call—then I have a hard time seeing the lateness as more than trivial.
3. But you should still apologize and actually mean it when you're late even if the other person says it was fine and clearly didn't mind. 'No harm, no foul' feels like treating them as someone whose plans you can just rearrange.
4. There's nothing wrong with showing up at 9:30 to a party that 'starts' at 8 when that's the local convention. The soft start changes the content of the commitment, so you weren't late.
5. There's a big difference between a firm commitment ('I'll pick you up at 7') and a vague one ('we should grab coffee sometime'). The second is basically social noise, not a promise, and you can drop it without explanation.
6. The real fault in chronic lateness is at the moment of accepting the commitment, not the moment of being late. If you know you can't reliably be somewhere at 6:30, you owe it to people to say 'I'll be there between 6:45 and 7' or 'I can't promise a time.'
7. And yet there are people to whom I should say 'yes, I'll be there' even when I'm far from sure I'll make it—an old friend going through a rough patch who asks if I can stop by Sunday, a kid's school thing I might miss if work blows up. To say 'I can't commit' to those is to refuse to be the kind of person who shows up, and that refusal is sometimes worse than risking a no-show.

So here's what I want. Take that set and tell me whether it hangs together: which pairs pull against each other, which pairs only seem to, and how bad each real tension is—order them from worst to least bad. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would make the whole set cohere the most.
