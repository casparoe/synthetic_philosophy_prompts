I am wrestling with a claim about how to classify the false outputs of large language models, and I would like your help in determining whether the claim has real empirical content or is merely a façon de parler.

Let me begin with the distinctions I have in mind. On Harry Frankfurt's way of carving things, the liar departs from what he takes, rightly or wrongly, to be true, and he does so in order to induce a false belief in someone else; he is, in a particular way, answerable to the truth, because he must represent it well enough to avoid it. The person who commits an ordinary error is also answerable to the truth, but from the other side: he is trying to get things right and fails through ignorance, bad inference, or bad luck. The bullshitter is unlike both. What defines his output is not that it is false but that he does not care whether it is true; he selects words for some other purpose — to persuade, to impress, to fill the silence, to perform the role the occasion demands. On this picture, bullshit may happen to be true, and a false utterance may be sincere error. What distinguishes the categories is the speaker's relation to the truth, not the truth value of the utterance.

Now consider a language model that, in answer to a factual question, produces a confident, fluent, false claim — a hallucination. A colleague of mine is convinced that this is Frankfurtian bullshit in a strict sense. The system, he says, is not a failed truth-teller, because it was never in the business of tracking truth in the first place; it is trained to produce contextually apt language, and a hallucination is not a breakdown of that function but a fluent execution of it. Nor is it a lie, since there is no represented truth to betray and no intention to deceive. I find the claim plausible enough to bother examining, but I suspect the word "test" is doing a great deal of unexamined work in that verdict.

What I would like from you is an analysis with three parts.

First, sharpen the claim. Which thing is supposed to be the bullshitter — the model, the token output, the training objective, the developer, or the user who deploys the model? If the claim is best understood as analogical rather than literal, say exactly where the analogy is supposed to hold and where it would break.

Second, design the single strongest test you can. Choose one concrete manipulation or measurement — something that could in principle be run — and say in advance which results would support classifying hallucination as bullshit, which would push toward ordinary error, which would push toward lying, and which would suggest that none of the three categories applies. Be specific enough that someone could disagree with your experimental design rather than merely with your taste.

Third, say what the test cannot settle. I am prepared to be told that part of the question is not empirical at all: that "indifference to truth" and "intention to deceive" are attributions that outrun any behavioral signature, or that Frankfurt's categories apply in the first instance to speakers and only derivatively to systems. If so, tell me how much of the original claim survives the test, and how much must be settled by argument rather than evidence.

Finally, I want the response to expose its own commitments. Tell me which empirical result would most change your mind about how hallucination should be classified, and identify the step in your reasoning that you regard as most likely to fail.
