I keep running into phrases like "well-defined," "canonical," and "unique up to unique isomorphism" in mathematical writing, and I've realized I have no crisp criterion for when a labeling choice is benign and when it smuggles in real structure. The case that nags at me is roots of polynomials. Over the complex numbers, x² + 1 has two roots, usually written i and −i; complex conjugation swaps them and fixes every rational, so no statement in the language of fields distinguishes one from the other, and yet the machinery of Galois theory seems to care a great deal about permutations of roots. The software analogue I keep coming back to: two serialized objects that differ only in key order or internal identifiers are usually the same data, but hashing, reproducible builds, and any external contract that exposes bytes make representation part of the semantics.

I want to stop leaning on slogans and write down an actual specification. Give me the desiderata for a theory of when relabeling or choice-of-representation matters in mathematics: what any adequate account of the line between "mere labeling convention" and "choice that created real structure" has to deliver.

For each requirement, give the concrete mathematical case that forces it, and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as something I could reasonably relax for a working criterion. The specification should at minimum reckon with: the i/−i symmetry for roots of x² + 1 and how it generalizes; coordinates and change of basis in linear algebra, including which operations are basis-invariant and which are basis-dependent; orientation or chirality as a two-element choice with no preferred member; functions defined on equivalence classes or quotients that must first pick out representatives; and the standard facts that expressions symmetric in the roots can be rewritten in terms of the coefficients, while the splitting field of a polynomial is unique up to isomorphism even though the isomorphisms between particular copies are generally non-unique.

I also want the distinctions such an account is not allowed to collapse. For example: (a) a choice exists, (b) the choice is unique, (c) any choice yields isomorphic output, and (d) the choice is arbitrary but switching choices leaves behind an automorphism that must still be tracked. These feel importantly different, and I want a theory that keeps them apart. Flag any pair of requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, I can already feel a conflict between wanting every well-formed term to denote a single determinate object and wanting the two roots of x² + 1 to be treated as indistinguishable by the theory itself.

Then, only briefly, score the main idioms I am likely to encounter against that requirement list: the working algebraist's idea that "canonical" just means defined without auxiliary choices; the category-theoretic emphasis on universal properties and uniqueness up to unique isomorphism; and the structuralist view that mathematical objects are only ever defined up to isomorphism. Keep the scorecard short. The criteria are the real deliverable; where the criteria themselves rule out one of those idioms, say so, and identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular candidates.
