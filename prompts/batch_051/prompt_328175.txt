I work as a software engineer on data modeling and distributed systems, and I keep noticing a parallel that I would like someone to check. I gather that philosophers have a problem called the special composition question: under what conditions do some parts compose a whole object, such as particles composing a table, a dog, or a cloud? The standard positions, as I understand them, are nihilism (they never compose anything), universalism (any collection composes something), and restricted composition (composition happens only under certain non-arbitrary conditions). This looks structurally identical to a problem I face with entities. Inside one of our systems, a Customer is not a single thing; it is a cluster of rows across tables and fields across services, tied together by a shared ID and a schema. Whether those rows compose one customer is settled by the ID and a service contract, not by anything in the data itself. But when we ingest data from outside, there is no shared ID, and our entity-resolution logic has to decide whether a set of records is one person or many. That decision has exactly the features I see in the philosophical debate: vague boundaries, arbitrary cutoffs, and no fact of the matter unless we stipulate one. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching? In particular, does the schema/ID play a role with no counterpart in the material-object case, or is it simply an explicit version of what restricted-composition views mean by things like cohesion or being parts of a life? And does the parallel predict anything that comes out false on one side or the other? I cannot tell whether this is a real map between fields or a dead metaphor.
