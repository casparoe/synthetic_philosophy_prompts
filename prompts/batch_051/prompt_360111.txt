In my philosophy of medicine seminar I give students four criteria for a condition's being a disease, drawn from the naturalism/normativism debate:

1. Statistical atypicality: the condition departs from the statistically typical functional organization of the species, relative to an appropriate reference class such as age or sex.
2. Biological dysfunction: some part or process fails to perform the function for which it was selected.
3. Harm: the condition makes the person worse off than they would otherwise be, given the environment they actually live in.
4. Medical recognition: the condition falls within the recognized scope of medical investigation, and medical interventions can aim at it.

Now consider a case I have deliberately constructed so that it cannot be settled by looking anything up. A person is homozygous for a loss-of-function allele that disables the synthesis of a particular enzyme. In the ancestral environment, that enzyme prevented the accumulation of a toxic metabolic intermediate, and the deficiency would have been fatal by late childhood. In the present environment, however, a universally fortified staple food supplies the compound the enzyme would have produced. The person is asymptomatic, has a normal life expectancy, functions normally in every respect, and was tested only because she happened to enroll in a study. The enzyme remains nonfunctional. A gene therapy exists that would restore enzyme function but requires indefinite immunosuppression; she declines it.

Is this person diseased? Work through the four criteria one at a time and say honestly where each points. Then say what answer the naturalist is committed to, what answer the normativist is committed to, and which of the two positions you think this case puts under more pressure. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which variation of the case or which argument would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
