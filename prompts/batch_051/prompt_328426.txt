I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future in which adjudicated public argument has become a professional spectator sport, with a circuit, rankings, and televised matches. While building the logic of that world, I drafted the working notes below, which advance a mixed thesis: that making argumentation a sport would make the craft of reasoning more legible and broaden participation, but would simultaneously install incentives that nudge serious argument away from inquiry toward scorecard optimization.

I would be grateful for comments on the notes rather than a survey of existing positions or a bibliography. Specifically, please address: (1) where the argument is weakest, and the strongest counterexample a critic could press against it; (2) whether the distinction between scoring how well someone argues and scoring how likely they are to be right carries the weight I put on it, or whether it collapses under pressure; (3) the best version of the view I am arguing against; and (4) whether my pessimistic conclusion rests on hidden empirical assumptions about judges, audiences, or incentives that I have not made explicit.

Notes toward the essay:

The appeal of sport is that it makes excellence legible at a distance: a spectator can watch a performance and have a reasonable, if imperfect, grip on whether it was good. I want to imagine an institution that does this for argument. In the version I am considering, two advocates are assigned positions by lot from a fixed brief of shared evidence, then scored in two registers: a technical score and a stylistic score. The technical score rewards things like the accuracy of the recap of an opponent's case, responsiveness (the share of an opponent's distinct points that get a direct answer), economy (not spending time on weak objections), and concession (acknowledging when the evidence clearly favors the other side). The stylistic score rewards clarity, pacing, and the ability to make a chain of reasoning feel followable.

The optimistic case: such scoring makes virtues that are usually invisible visible. A point for accurate recap rewards the ability to state the opposing view well enough that its holder would accept the statement; a point for concession rewards updating in public. Training for the sport could teach epistemic discipline the way training for athletics teaches physical discipline, and spectators who could not follow an unstructured debate could learn to recognize distinctions through commentary.

The pessimistic case: once a rubric is attached to stakes, participants optimize the rubric rather than the underlying virtue. Responsiveness becomes answering every point, even in one thin sentence, rather than answering the points that matter. Recap becomes mirroring the opponent rather than understanding them. Concession becomes a performative, preplanned gesture. The judges' limitations become the real scoring function, and the contest selects for arguments that judges can recognize as good, which may diverge from the arguments that are good. This is the standard worry about gaming a metric, but it cuts deeper here because in many sports the performance is the point, whereas in argument the performance is supposed to be a means to something else: sounder conclusions.

That last sentence exposes the question I am actually chasing. There are two questions. The first is whether argumentation could be a sport; the second is whether making it one would improve argumentation. I can see how the first could be true while the second is false, and my notes argue, in effect, that the very mechanisms that make the sport legible (fixed criteria, public scoring, rankings) are also the mechanisms that would pull it away from its epistemic purpose. My mixed conclusion is that argument-as-sport would broaden the audience for careful reasoning while degrading the reasoning of the professionals at the top of the circuit, and that the net effect on public judgment is therefore genuinely uncertain.

That is where I have stopped writing. I am interested in whether the problem is in the architecture of the argument, not merely in my tone, and in what a sharper version of the opposing view would say.
