I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so forgive any imprecision. I keep noticing a resemblance I can't shake. In backend systems we hit the birthday paradox constantly: hash N items into M buckets and you should expect collisions around N on the order of sqrt(M), because there are N(N−1)/2 pairs and each pair gets its own chance to collide — which is why 64-bit IDs start colliding surprisingly early, around a few billion. It seems to me that the same move undercuts most "what are the odds of that?" reasoning in daily life. People compute the probability of this exact coincidence happening to them, when the correct denominator is the probability of any coincidence of roughly that kind happening, across all the candidate coincidences their life could have thrown up. The "one in a million" story is a survivor: it's the collision somebody finally noticed. My question is whether this is a genuine structural identity or just two things that share the word "unlikely." If the analogy is real, what transfers from one side to the other? For instance, if you observed hash collisions far earlier than sqrt(M) suggests, you'd suspect a bad hash function; is there an equivalent in the coincidence case — a point at which an anecdote becomes evidence that something is actively non-random rather than just a birthday-paradox effect? And where exactly does the parallel break? Counting pairs of people in a room is clean, but the space of possible coincidences in a life is not a well-defined N, so I suspect the transfer fails precisely there.
