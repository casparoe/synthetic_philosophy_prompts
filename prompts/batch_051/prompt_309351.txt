Sartre’s account of bad faith has always struck me as unstable, but I can’t locate the instability. In *Being and Nothingness* he describes a café waiter whose movements, manner, and voice seem studied and mechanical, as if he were performing the role of waiter rather than simply being one; Sartre says the waiter is not a waiter in the way an inkwell is an inkwell, and yet he plays at being a waiter, constituting himself as a thing-like identity. That passage is supposed to illustrate bad faith: the waiter denies his transcendence by identifying himself with a fixed social role. The argument I want to test, reconstructed for Sartre, runs as follows:

(1) Bad faith is self-deception, that is, lying to oneself.
(2) Lying requires a deceiver who knows the truth and a deceived who is ignorant of it, and these must be distinct.
(3) In self-deception, the deceiver and the deceived are the same person.
(4) Therefore, a single self cannot simultaneously know and not know the same truth in the way intentional lying requires.
(5) So bad faith cannot be intentional self-deception.
(6) But Sartre denies that bad faith is an involuntary state; it is a project freely maintained by the agent.
(7) Whatever is freely maintained but is not intentional self-deception cannot be a case of deceiving oneself at all; it is either sincere role-adoption or a muddled form of belief.
(8) Therefore, on Sartre’s own premises, bad faith is impossible.

I suspect the fault is in (2), because Sartre rejects the Freudian split between deceiver and deceived and replaces it with something like a single consciousness that is both the dupe and the duper, but then I can’t see how (6) is preserved. Is the argument valid, and if not, exactly which step fails? If it is valid, which premise would Sartre have to reject, and what happens to his distinction between bad faith and ordinary lying? Be specific about the step and give the strongest reading of it that would save Sartre’s position.
