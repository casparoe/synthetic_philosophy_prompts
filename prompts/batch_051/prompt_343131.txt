I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I'm giving a talk at a conference on addiction, criminal responsibility, and the brain. The piece I'm presenting is basically the Dennett move: ask which varieties of free will are worth wanting, not whether free will exists. My thesis is that the version of free will the "neuroscience disproves it" crowd attacks — a break in the causal chain, an uncaused cause — isn't worth wanting and isn't what blame or the law actually tracks. What's worth wanting is a messy bundle of trainable, scalar capacities: impulse control, foresight and stable temporal discounting, the ability to do what you'd endorse on reflection, the ability to revise your values over time. And I'll argue that the neuroscience of addiction doesn't show these are illusions; it shows how they're built, how they vary across people and situations, and how they get damaged or repaired.

The audience is mostly forensic psychiatrists and legal scholars who work on criminal responsibility, with a few computational neuroscientists who do intertemporal choice and delay discounting. They're not hard determinists; they think hard determinism is unhelpful for anything a court actually does. But they're also suspicious of compatibilism because it sounds like it just changes the subject. So they'll be primed to press me on whether my account has any actual work to do.

My argument, quickly, is: (1) the free will people worry about losing is a strawman; (2) what we actually hold people responsible for is the exercise or failure of capacities that are empirically real and often improvable; (3) the legal system already presupposes thresholds on those capacities — insanity, duress, immaturity — so the real argument is over where the lines go and weather they should be scalar rather than binary; (4) addiction research gives us traction on those capacities instead of dissolving them.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, with a note on which camp — the forensic psychiatrists, the legal scholars, the neuroscientists — is most likely to raise each one, phrased the way they'd actually put it. Say which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, and where my own position is softest — be brutal about that last part. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. The talk is 40 minutes and the Q&A is 25, so I need replies that work in two or three minutes each, not a seminar paper.
