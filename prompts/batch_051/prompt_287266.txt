I have been trying to work out what I actually think about what we owe the dead when we dramatise them, and my gut verdicts on cases will not settle into a principle. Here they are. A biopic of a recently dead prime minister invents a private ultimatum from his wife to explain a real decision: not fine. A historical drama merges three real advisers into one invented composite with made-up dialogue: fine. A series shows a dead scientist as having faked a result, although he was in fact cleared of that suspicion: not fine. The same series shows the scientist's documented neglect of his children: fine, because it is true and we do not owe him a scrubbed memory. A novel imagines an unevidenced love affair between two real dead film stars: not fine, though plainly less bad than the faked-result case. A play about a recently beatified nun gives her an invented crisis of faith: fine, though I feel uneasy saying so. My hunch is that specificity, harm to third parties, and public meaning are doing the work, but I cannot make them cohere. What principle, if any, captures all or most of these verdicts? Test it on the case I keep going back and forth on: a respectful film shows a real, decent, recently dead headmistress doing something she never did, namely quietly recording a pupil as present to spare the family a fine, as many teachers did at the time, while her family says it smears her honesty. Please say which of my verdicts your principle forces me to abandon, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and which consideration would most likely change your mind about the principle itself. End with a final line beginning 'Verdict:' giving a one-sentence statement of the principle you would defend.
