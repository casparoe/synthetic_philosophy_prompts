For a piece I'm writing about why evolutionary psychology claims are so easy to overstate, I need to pin down exactly what, if anything, is wrong with the following argument, which an interviewee pressed on me:

(1) If a behavioral tendency is common across human societies and currently benefits the people who have it, then it is a biological adaptation.
(2) The tendency to punish free-riders is common across human societies and currently benefits the people who have it.
(3) Therefore the tendency to punish free-riders is a biological adaptation.
(4) If a trait is a biological adaptation, then it exists because natural selection favored it for its current function.
(5) Therefore the tendency to punish free-riders exists because natural selection favored it for its current function.

Is the argument valid? If it is valid, tell me which premises are false and how the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique would correct each. If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which the inference breaks. Reply with exactly five numbered points, one for each of the five steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
