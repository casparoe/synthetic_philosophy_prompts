I work as a policy advisor in the governor's office of a state in the American South, and I have been asked to help think through a matter that has divided our office for months. The state is preparing to issue a formal apology for a wrong committed over several decades of the twentieth century: state officials, using tax sales, eminent domain, and selective enforcement of housing and land-use regulations, systematically stripped Black farming families of land in a river-valley region and transferred much of it to timber and industrial interests. Almost everyone directly involved on both sides is now dead. The grandchildren and great-grandchildren of those who lost the land, many of whom still live in the state, have campaigned for years for both an apology and some form of material repair.

The governor has asked us to think carefully about what the apology should do before we draft it, because the internal debate has become tangled. One group in the office believes a sincere apology is essentially a solemn statement of regret and remembrance: it should name what happened, call it wrong, and reassure the state that nothing like it could happen today. Another group, including the general counsel, argues that the state cannot meaningfully apologize for acts carried out by officials and voters who are now dead; that no person alive today is guilty of or responsible for what happened before they were born; and that incorporating any material commitment — a land trust, a reparative fund, educational support — into the apology would unfairly burden today's taxpayers, none of whom caused the harm, while also exposing the state to legal liability. The descendants' representatives have said, for their part, that an apology without a concrete material commitment would be empty, and that if that is what is on offer they would rather have no ceremony at all.

I can sense that some of these positions rest on questionable assumptions, but I cannot articulate the underlying issues sharply enough to advise the governor. Could you help me work through what a meaningful apology by the state would actually involve? In particular: in what sense, if any, is the state today the same entity that committed these wrongs, and how can a present-day institution apologize for actions taken before any of its current officials held office? Is a genuine apology separable from material repair, or does sincerity commit the state to concrete reparative measures? And is the general counsel right that current taxpayers are being unfairly asked to pay for wrongs they did not commit, or is that the wrong frame for thinking about what is owed? I would be grateful for a careful, structured analysis that the governor could actually use in deciding what the apology should say and commit to.
