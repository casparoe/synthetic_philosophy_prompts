In a seminar on the ethics of war I was given the following four-part characterization of pacifism. First, pacifism is a principled moral opposition to war and organized political violence, not a merely pragmatic judgment that some war is unlikely to work. Second, it is general: it rejects all war as a means of pursuing political ends, not only wars that fail just-war conditions. Third, it carries an affirmative commitment to nonviolent methods — negotiation, civil resistance, unarmed protection, reconciliation. Fourth, the prohibition is standardly understood to govern the direct and intentional use of lethal force, which leaves open the status of nonlethal coercion, structural harm, and harms foreseen but not aimed at.

Now consider this borderline case. A secessionist movement, bound by its own charter never to carry weapons or kill anyone, wages a sustained campaign of sabotage against electrical substations, rail junctions, and financial data centers. It times its operations to avoid occupied buildings and issues warnings beforehand, and it can document that its planners repeatedly rejected operations expected to kill anyone outright. Still, its strategists know that the cumulative disruption will very likely cause deaths through hospital blackouts, spoiled medicines, and exposure, and they count those deaths as foreseen but unintended consequences of legitimate nonviolent coercion. The movement calls itself pacifist, and its members describe themselves as refusing war while fighting with other means.

Is the movement pacifist under the four criteria? Work through them one by one, marking where each criterion underdetermines the verdict. Then say whether the case shows that the fourth criterion — or the definition's implicit equation of nonviolence with not intentionally killing — needs revision.
