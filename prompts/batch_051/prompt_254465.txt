In our seminar on mind uploading, one participant keeps defending the strongest version of the whole-brain emulation project by leaning on a computing analogy. The brain, he says, is the hardware and the mind is the software; emulating a brain is therefore like porting a program onto new hardware, and since we unhesitatingly say the same software is running on the new machine, he concludes that a sufficiently faithful emulation would be the same mind, and that someone who underwent destructive uploading would survive. Audit that analogy. Lay out the intended correspondences — program, hardware, port, copy, execution — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. Which features of software does it quietly import that may not carry over to minds, and which of these disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion about survival rather than being side issues? Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing the argumentative work? If the analogy fails, say whether a modification — for instance, treating emulation as reimplementation rather than porting — would do better.
