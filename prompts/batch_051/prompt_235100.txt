I run a reading group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence — about sixteen people, a mix of philosophy students and working engineers — and I have one ninety-minute session to make them feel why the "low-impact AI" problem is hard. I want them to discover, rather than be told, that the instruction to accomplish a narrow task while changing as little else as possible cannot simply be obeyed by an agent that lacks a full specification of what matters.

Design a self-contained tabletop game for the session. Split the group so that some participants play agents and the rest play a patron who holds a hidden schedule of costs attached to elements in a small artificial environment — a board of tokens, a deck of cards standing in for a room or a database, or something equally cheap to prepare. An agent receives a precise objective, such as moving a token to a target square or sorting a set of records, plus one standing instruction: change as little else as possible. Points are awarded for completing the task and deducted for incidental changes according to the patron's hidden schedule, which the agent cannot see. Then run a second round with a twist not announced in advance: the agent may pay points to ask a limited number of yes-or-no questions about which changes matter before acting. All scoring should use points or tokens, not money.

The rules, components, and timing for the ninety minutes are yours, but the game must be honest to the conceptual problem. The group should run into the fact that "minimal side effects" requires at least three things no instruction sheet supplies: a baseline of what would have happened had the agent not acted, a metric for saying how close two worlds are, and a decision about whether lost future options count as harms. Arrange the rules so that these choices surface in play before the debrief names them.

Then write the debrief. It should map what happened onto the difference between changes the task itself requires and changes that are incidental, onto why any computable proxy for low impact becomes gameable once it is announced, and onto the gap between knowing a few of the patron's values and having a full specification of value. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect someone will find a trivially minimal action, or object that the task itself should not count against the agent — and how the facilitator should respond without giving the game away early. End by saying honestly which part of the real problem this activity cannot reproduce — in particular, that in the real case no one, not even the designer, holds a complete hidden schedule — and what the facilitator must supply afterward.
