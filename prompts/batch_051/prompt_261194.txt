In my criminal law class we’re doing void-for-vagueness cases and I keep going back to Kolender v. Lawson. Justice O’Connor writes: ‘We conclude that the statute as it has been construed is unconstitutionally vague within the meaning of the Due Process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment by failing to clarify what is contemplated by the requirement that a suspect provide a “credible and reliable” identification.’ But it seems odd to me that the remedy for this is just to strike the whole law. My friend proposed something else: a court could uphold a vague statute only on the condition that the enforcing agency publish binding, public guidelines spelling out how it interprets vague terms like “credible and reliable.” A defendant could beat the charge either by showing the officer didn’t follow the published guidelines, or by showing the guidelines themselves are too vague. What would this mechanism actually do? Think about incentives for police departments, line officers, city attorneys, and people trying to figure out what’s legal. Where does it get gamed first? Does it just push the unresolved vagueness into agency rulemaking, or does it actually constrain discretion? I want you to flag where your analysis is most fragile and what kind of argument or evidence would make you flip.
