I keep coming back to the first couple pages of Benacerraf's essay Mathematical Truth (1973), where he lays out two constraints on any decent account of mathematical truth. As I remember them, the first is that the semantics should treat mathematical claims at face value: in the claim that there are infinitely many primes, the quantifier really ranges over some things, primes really refers, and the sentence is true in the same sense as a claim about tables. The second is that the account should not make it inexplicable how we know these things, since our best story about knowledge runs through causal contact, and the objects required by the first constraint dont seem to be the kind of thing you can have causal contact with. He argues that the going views of his day each satisfy at most one.

That passage has generated fifty years of argument between platonists, nominalists, structuralists, fictionalists, logicists, and so on, and as far as I can tell nobody moves. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not another survey of positions. My working hypothesis is that the impasse isnt really about mathematics at all but about what kind of explanation philosophy has to give, and that the two constraints are secretly answering different questions under one heading. But maybe I'm wrong, and there is a genuine empirical or formal question buried in there that could be settled. Which is it? In diagnosing it, I want you to say what each side treats as non-negotiable starting intuition, whether the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data, and what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a new distinction, a cognitive-science finding, a change of question — or, if none should be expected, why not. Keep it under 1200 words.
