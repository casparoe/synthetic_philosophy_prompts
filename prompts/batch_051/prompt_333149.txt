You're an expert on the philosophy of categories—natural kinds, family resemblance, conceptual roles, all that—and you know enough about type design and visual perception to talk letterforms without embarrassing yourself. I'm teaching a seminar on what holds a category together when every member looks different, and I keep coming back to letterforms and to the old idea that a letter has a spirit that survives whatever stylistic clothes you put it in. I want you to actually argue this one out, not just hand me the usual family-resemblance line. Start by brainstorming three or four plausible ways to decide whether a mark on a page is the letter 'a', and say which approach you're most tempted by and why. Then work through my case.

Here's the rough criteria I've been giving students for being a lowercase 'a' in a Latin-script writing system.

One, role: the mark plays the 'a'-part in the system—hit the 'a' key and that's what appears, and inside words it does the orthographic and phonological job of an 'a'. Two, anatomy: it has the usual letterform morphology—a roughly enclosed counter (the white space inside the bowl) sitting at x-height, plus some opening, spur, tail, or other right-side feature that keeps it from just being an 'o'. Three, perceptibility: a competent reader, reading at normal speed, can tell it apart from the other glyphs in its own font and sees it as an 'a' without squinting at a specimen sheet. Four, descent: the designer built it as a restyling of an earlier 'a' in that typeface's lineage; it's an 'a' by derivation, not an 'o' that got reassigned.

Now the case. Imagine a small pixel font called Dense, built for very low-resolution screens. Its whole lowercase alphabet is constructed from just three primitives: a circle, a vertical stem, and a horizontal bar, all on a tight grid. The designers intended the 'a' to be a circle with a one-pixel spur poking out at about four o'clock. The 'o' is just the circle. The 'e' is a circle with the right side broken and a little bar across. So far so good. But at the sizes the font is actually used, that one-pixel spur fails to render—there isn't room for it, so it disappears. The 'a' glyph is pixel-for-pixel identical to the 'o' glyph. Words that contain both make it obvious: in 'aloha' set in Dense, the 'a' and the two 'o's are the same plain circle. Readers mostly manage, since context does a lot of the work ('aloha' is a word and 'oloho' isn't), but in rare names and coinages the letters genuinely get confused.

So: is that circle in 'aloha' the letter 'a'? Go through my four criteria seperately and say what each one tells you. Then say where my list underdetermines the verdict. And I want your honest view, not a split-the-difference hedge: does the case show that the anatomy criterion (or the perceptibility one) isn't actually necessary for being an 'a', or does it show that Dense has simply built a defective 'a'—a letter that lost its letterhood by losing every distinguishing shape? If you think one of my criteria should be revised or dropped, give me the revised version and test it on this case. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your conclusion, and single out the claim in your answer you'd be least willing to defend in front of a room.
