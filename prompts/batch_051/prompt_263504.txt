I run the underwriting committee of a mutual insurer that writes long-tail liability cover for environmental risks—contaminated land, mining tailings, gradual pollution—where the claims record is thin, the causal pathways are complex, and the actuarial numbers are in many cases extrapolation dressed up as measurement. We meet fortnightly, the commitments run for decades, and the board expects every submission to carry a point estimate of expected loss and a probability range. I have come to think this practice systematically manufactures numbers the evidence does not support, and I want to replace it with something defensible.

Write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Keynes’s view of probability, that an underwriter can work through before any estimate is produced. Keynes’s view, as I understand it, supplies three distinctions my current process collapses. First, probabilities are relations of partial implication between evidence and hypothesis—degrees of rational belief—rather than frequencies in a class of similar past events. Second, many probabilities are non-numerical, and some are not even comparable: it can be an arbitrary matter whether an outcome is more likely than not, less likely, or as likely. Third, the probability of a proposition is distinct from the weight of the argument behind it—the amount of relevant evidence—so that a probability resting on a thin and patchy record is not the same epistemic state as the same probability resting on extensive, varied evidence. New evidence can even lower the probability while increasing the weight of the argument, and the procedure should preserve that distinction rather than flattening it into a confidence interval. He also warned that the principle of indifference cannot manufacture equal probabilities unless the evidence really is symmetric.

Translate this into a step-by-step procedure for my underwriters. It should tell them: when a numeric probability is permissible at all; when two risks may be compared ordinally but not assigned numbers; how to record and carry the weight of evidence separately from the probability; and what to do when the evidence does not support comparison. The underwriters who will use it are trained actuaries and environmental engineers, not philosophers; it must be usable within the half-day a senior underwriter has to review a submission, and the board must approve any recommendation that withholds a number. Include worked applications to two or three claims we might actually see—for example, a tailings dam where there is a good frequency record for the class of structure, a novel remediation technology that has never been deployed at scale, and a site where the relevant causal science is unsettled. Make one of the examples a case in which the procedure refuses to produce the number the board has asked for, and show how the underwriter should write that refusal up. Flag explicitly the points at which judgement cannot be eliminated, and say what the procedure cannot do for us.
