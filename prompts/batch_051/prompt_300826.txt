After I retired from the lab I started reading moral philosophy, and the thing I keep chewing on is how much blame attaches to eating animal products when the alternatives just aren't there. My friend Delia spent years doing agricultural development work and has fairly consistent instincts about this, so I've been recording her verdicts like a naturalist. Here are four of her earlier jugdments:

- Lena, a nurse in Hamburg, eats cheap factory-farmed eggs and dairy every day even though she has several supermarkets nearby and could easily afford tofu, lentils, and oats. Delia says she's blameworthy.
- Yusuf, a pastoralist in northern Kenya, keeps goats and drinks their milk; the nearest shop selling plant protein is a day away and unaffordable, and the milk is a staple for his kids. Delia says not blameworthy.
- Aiko, a student in Osaka, buys factory-farmed pork for lunch most days even though the convenience stores near campus sell cheap tofu and bean bento. Delia says blameworthy.
- Rosa, a subsistence farmer in highland Guatemala, keeps a few free-roaming hens and eats their eggs because beans are only available for a few months after harvest and the market is a long walk away. Delia says not blameworthy.

New case: Maria is a single mother in a small coastal town in the Philippines. Fishing has collapsed, the only store within walking distance sells cheap canned beef and eggs from industrial farms, and tofu or legumes are only sold in the city market three bus hours away. She feeds her kids the factory-produced eggs and canned beef because it's what's available and what she can afford.

What's your best guess about whether Delia would call Maria blameworthy? I can see at least two quite different rules that fit the first four verdicts, and I suspect they may not agree on Maria. What are the serious candidate rules, what does each one predict about her, which old case is the closest analog, and what extra case would best seperate them?
