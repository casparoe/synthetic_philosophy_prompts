Here is Edward L. Bernays in *Propaganda* (New York: Horace Liveright, 1928), near the start of the book:

> The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society. Those who manipulate this unseen mechanism of society constitute an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country. We are governed, our minds are molded, our tastes formed, our ideas suggested, largely by men we have never heard of.

I want to think about whether the argument compressed in this passage survives what has happened to persuasion since Bernays wrote. His picture seems to be of a small, conscious elite — people like himself, as he says later — who deliberately shape the habits and opinions of the masses for the sake of social coordination and, he thinks, the survival of democracy. Today, much of the shaping of attention, taste, and opinion is done not by a conscious elite pursuing a vision of the public good but by recommender systems, engagement-maximizing platforms, microtargeted advertising, and persuasive design in digital environments. These systems are automated, decentralized, and optimized for metrics such as watch time, click-through, or retention rather than for any social vision. They manipulate organized habits, often using the same psychological research Bernays drew on, but there is no "government," invisible or otherwise, deciding what the masses should be led to believe or desire.

My question is whether Bernays's argument still goes through in this environment. Specifically: does the new situation refute his claim that democratic society needs an invisible government of conscious manipulators, or does it only change who or what plays the role, or does it expose that the argument's real work was being done by the premise that the manipulators could be assumed to be benevolent and far-sighted? If some repaired version of his argument survives, what does it now support — e.g., that we should regulate the recommenders rather than remove them, or that we need a new class of trusted curators, or something else? Or is the argument dead, and we should say that Bernays was wrong about engineering consent being necessary for democracy?
