I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a weekly, and we're doing a one-off themed page on solipsism as a vibe — the main-character thing where everyone else on the pavement feels like an NPC. I'd like a poem to run opposite the explainer, and I want it to do actual conceptual work rather than just set a mood. Write a villanelle — the nineteen-line form with two refrains that alternate through the tercets and land together in the final couplet — from the point of view of someone walking through a crowded city at rush hour, with the two refrains as the two thoughts this person keeps cycling between. One refrain should be the sober epistemic point: roughly, the only mind I'm directly acquainted with is my own. The other should not be the careful 'maybe' — it should be the stronger, alienating certainty that the people around me are just rendered scenery running on scripts with no one home. The form should do the structural work: the refrains take turns through the stanza's, and by the time they meet in the closing couplet the reader should feel how the second claim keeps passing itself off as the first, even though the one doesn't support the other. Accuracy matters more than atmosphere — someone in philosophy of mind should recognise the difference between an epistemic gap and a positive claim about other minds actually being empty, and the poem shouldn't quietly conflate them. You can leave the tension unresolved, but the final couplet should make the gap visible rather than papering over it.
