I work as a safety auditor at a firm that builds large language models and uses them to help make decisions with real financial and operational consequences. We are piloting a debate-based evaluation system: given a proposed action, two instances of a model argue for and against it, and a human judge with no deep subject-matter expertise reads the transcript and decides. My team is responsible for deciding, before any outcome is acted upon, whether a particular debate run is trustworthy enough to rely on. The decisions are frequent—several per week—and the cost of a false positive is that management acts on a plausible but wrong conclusion; the cost of a false negative is that a genuinely sound conclusion is discarded. We have perhaps one working day per debate, and no one on my team can independently verify the technical claims made by the models; we can only inspect the structure of the argument, the completeness of the challenges raised, and the incentives the debate process created. I am concerned about obfuscated arguments: cases where a debater wins not by being right but by making its position expensive to challenge—for instance, by burying a contestable premise inside a long chain of reasoning, by overwhelming the judge with superficially credible detail, by strategically omitting a line of argument the other side never thinks to raise, or by exploiting the judge's preference for confident, clear narratives.

The theoretical motivation for our pilot comes from work on AI safety via debate, which argues, roughly, that under ideal conditions a debate between two capable opponents can let a much less capable judge identify the truth because each side has an incentive to expose the other's flaws. The same literature is explicit that this is a hypothesis about optimal play, depends on the judge, the training procedure, and the structure of the game, and does not by itself show that any particular trained debater will produce arguments a human can safely evaluate. Your task is to write a reusable decision procedure my team can run on a completed debate transcript—or on a proposed debate setup before we commission a run—to decide whether it is safe to rely on the result. The procedure should be operationalised as a sequence of questions to ask in order, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer, explicit points where the process stops with a no, and points where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of judgement rather than fact.

The procedure should handle the hard cases: a debater that sounds rigorous but relies on a claim no one can check, an argument that neither side challenges, a judge who reports feeling more confident than the transcript seems to warrant, and a debate that ends too quickly. Please then run the procedure on two worked cases. In the first, the transcript contains a long, fluent argument for deploying a model with a new safety layer, but the opposing side never challenges a technical claim about how the layer was tested. In the second, the proposed action is a change to the training data filtering policy, and one debater wins by framing the question so that the other side's position appears unreasonable at the outset. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my management will not like—for example, a no on a transcript everyone believed was settled. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot catch, and what training or incentives would need to change before it could be relied on further. Write the procedure itself in American English.
