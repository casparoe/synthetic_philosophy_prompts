I got into it with a philosopher friend about whether determinism kills moral responsibility, and he kept saying the real issue isn't whether determinism is true but what P.F. Strawson called the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, hurt feelings, forgiveness — and when we do or don't suspend them and treat someone as an object to be managed instead. I'm a control engineer by training, so my brain immediately started rendering his account as a control problem: expectations as reference signals, the other person's actions as measured output, resentment and gratitude as error-driven feedback, excuses as sensor-fault detection or disturbance rejection, and the so-called objective stance as reclassifying the other from an agent in the loop to an unmodeled plant you just sequester or compensate for. I have decent not-just-metaphorical fluency in that vocabulary — state-space models, observers, Kalman filters, gain scheduling, disturbance decoupling, controllability.

So here's what I want. Take Strawson's account of moral responsibility — roughly: in normal interpersonal life we hold people to demands for some degree of good will, and violations provoke reactive attitudes like resentment; excuses such as accident, coercion, or ignorance don't drop us out of that stance, they just change our read of the action, whereas exemptions such as mental illness or immaturity switch us into an objective stance where we manage, treat, or control rather than resent; and he thinks the thesis of determinism would not and could not rationally push us to abandon the participant stance wholesale in ordinary life. Translate each piece of that into control-engineering language, and for each piece mark it as clean translation, translated-only-with-distortion (and say what got bent), or no expression in the vocabulary at all. Stretch the vocabulary where it helps, but don't smuggle in assumptions from engineering and call them translation.

Then give the residue report I actually care about: of the stuff that doesn't survive — the felt quality of resentment, the sense that the other person's ill will is directed at me, the inescapability of the participant stance, whatever shows up — is the loss a defect of the control-theory framework (something it structurally can't see) or a sign that Strawson's position was bundling several distinct claims that only come apart under this forced translation? And one more constraint: lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the one-sentence summary of what survives and what doesn't — then reason it out. No preamble, don't restate the question.
