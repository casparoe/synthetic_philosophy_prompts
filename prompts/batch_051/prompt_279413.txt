For a reading group on the philosophy of pain, I have been asked to set down my position on how pain is related to its badness, and the other members will then pressure-test it. I would like an audit of whether the following five commitments hang together. First, the badness of pain is intrinsic to pain itself: part of what it is to be in pain at all is that the experience feels bad, not merely that it is associated with tissue damage or that it is ordinarily disliked. Second, first-person reports about whether an experience hurts and whether it feels unpleasant should be treated as authoritative unless there are strong defeaters such as confusion, inattention, or analgesia. Third, pain asymbolia should be taken at face value: the condition in which subjects describe a stimulus as having the spatial and intensive character of pain, where it is located, how strong it is, whether it is sharp, while reporting that it does not feel bad and that they feel no urge to withdraw from it, and this not being explained by confusion, inattention, or analgesia. Fourth, pain experience contains at least two dissociable components, a sensory-discriminative one and an affective-motivational one, and these are what come apart in asymbolia. Fifth, no experience can be bad for a person unless it includes some aversive felt push, however weak or overridden, toward stopping that experience. Please tell me which of these are in genuine tension and rank the tensions by severity; identify which combination looks jointly impossible but ceases to be once the terms are disambiguated; and say which single revision of my position would do most to restore joint tenability and what that revision would cost. Write in continuous prose, as an email or letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
