I'm a jouranlist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to make sense of the doomsday argument after reading Richard Gott's 1993 Nature piece on the Copernican principle and future prospects. I'm not a philosopher, so I want to stress-test the version I've built before I ever write about it. I'm paraphrasing Gott rather than quoting him.

Here's the cleaned-up argument I've been carrying around:

(1) The Copernican principle says that when you observe something, you shouldn't treat your observation as special in time unless you have specific evidence that it is.
(2) Applied to my own birth, that means I should treat my birth rank as a random draw from the set of all human births.
(3) If my birth rank is random among all human births, then there is a 95% chance that I am not in the first 5% of human births.
(4) I am roughly the 100-billionth human born.
(5) If I am not in the first 5%, then total human births must be less than about 2 trillion, because 100 billion is 5% of 2 trillion.
(6) So there is a 95% chance that the total number of humans ever born will be under 2 trillion.
(7) Given current birth rates, that means we have far fewer than 20,000 years left—much closer to the end than a galactic-scale future of billions of years.
(8) But the same reasoning, applied to the 1-billionth human, would have said with 95% confidence that total human births would be under 20 billion. That prediction was wrong. So the principle seems to prove too much.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to keep one thing, I'd rather keep (4) and (5) than (2). But I want to know what I'd be paying.
