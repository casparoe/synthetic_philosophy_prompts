In the introduction to the second edition of Walter Bagehot's *The English Constitution*, he writes:

> A new Constitution does not produce its full effect as long as all its subjects were reared under an old Constitution, as long as its statesmen were trained by that old Constitution.

Bagehot's more famous argument in the same book is that the monarchy belongs to the dignified side of the constitution: it exists to attract the people's reverence so the efficient side can govern without being stared at. The passage above generalizes that into a claim about political time: constitutions only work fully once the old generation is gone.

Now think about British constitutional monarchy today. Legally the monarchy is still nearly as powerful as in Bagehot's day, but the social setting is unrecognizable. Royal coverage is celebrity gossip, phone hacking has exposed private letters, and the palace runs social media damage control. A plausible reading is that no one under 40 was ever reared under the old deferential constitution; from childhood they've seen the Crown as content, not mystery.

So my question is whether Bagehot's dignified/efficient argument survives that change in the audience. Does his own old-constitution passage imply that the dignified function must decay once the socialized deference is gone? Or can the monarchy relearn how to produce reverence under new media conditions, and the argument just needs updating? Be explicit about which premise is under pressure and whether it can be restored.
