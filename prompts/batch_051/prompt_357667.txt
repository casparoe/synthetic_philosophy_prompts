I am a software engineer with no formal legal or philosophical training, and I am puzzled by a practice in common-law courts sometimes called the absurdity doctrine. Courts occasionally decline to apply a statute exactly as written when the literal reading yields a result they consider absurd. For a concrete illustration, take a statute that makes it a crime to damage another person's property: a passerby smashes a car window to free a small child from a locked vehicle on a hot day, and a court strains not to read the statute as criminalizing the rescue. My question is not whether the outcome is desirable; it is what this practice presupposes about what a statute is. Does it presuppose that a statute is fundamentally an expression of the legislature's intentions rather than a text whose meaning is fixed by its words? Does it assume legislators are rational enough that they never intend absurd outcomes? Can the doctrine be explained without invoking the legislature's intentions at all — say, as a default rule for resolving ambiguities or repairing broken texts? And is this practice consistent with the same courts' insistence elsewhere that judges may not rewrite statutes or correct drafting mistakes? I am most interested in the underlying conceptual commitments: which theories of meaning, authority, and interpretation the practice implicitly rules in or out, and where it leaves the issue underdetermined.
