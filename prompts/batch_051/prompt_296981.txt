I am a novelist, and I have drafted the following short piece on aesthetic testimony — roughly, the practice of forming aesthetic beliefs about a work on the word of another person. The draft is meant to defend a claim that strikes me as surprising and true. I would welcome your comments on it as a piece of argument. Please attend to three questions in particular. First, does the distinction between borrowed aesthetic verdicts and transmitted aesthetic know-how actually carry the weight I place on it, or does it collapse under some obvious counterexample? Second, what is the strongest reply available to someone who accepts the acquaintance requirement, and does the draft answer it or evade it? Third, is the final claim too strong for what I have argued, and, if so, what is the most interesting weaker version of it that the argument could still support? Where you identify a weakness, please say whether it is repairable and what the most natural repair would be.

Working title: Fictional Testimony and the Acquaintance Requirement

One familiar thought about aesthetic matters is that there is something defective in taking another person's word for them. If a friend tells me that a film is devastating, or that the pacing of a play goes slack in its second act, I may treat this as a reason to see the film or the play, but not as a ground for believing that the film is devastating or that the play goes slack. To hold such beliefs properly, the thought runs, I must encounter the work myself. Call this the acquaintance requirement.

Now consider a different and, I think, neglected case: the invented artworks that appear inside novels. A novelist may describe, in exact detail, a painting that does not exist — the way a particular gray holds the light, the way the composition pulls the eye toward an empty doorway. She may describe a fictional performance of a familiar kind of piece, with a precision about tempo and touch that a music critic might envy. The reader has never seen the painting and never heard the performance, and never will, because there is nothing to see or hear. Yet an attentive reader can come away from such passages with a real, new capacity: she can notice, in actual paintings and actual performances, qualities she could not have discriminated before reading. This looks like an enlargement of aesthetic competence produced by words alone, in the absence of any acquaintance with the described object. On the face of it, the acquaintance requirement should predict that this cannot happen.

An obvious reply is that the reader does not come to believe that any particular work has any particular aesthetic property, since the described works do not exist. I grant this. But I want to suggest that the reply keeps the letter of the acquaintance requirement while surrendering most of its point. The requirement is usually defended not as a bare epistemic rule about belief formation, but as a way of protecting the autonomy of the perceiver: one should not hand one's taste over to another person's say-so, because one's aesthetic sense is meant to be one's own. The novelist's invented descriptions, however, do not hand the reader a verdict. They install a manner of looking, a pattern of attention, a sense of which features are worth noticing. If the underlying worry is borrowed perception — seeing the work through someone else's eyes before one has looked for oneself — then fiction is in one respect the more potent instrument, precisely because it never issues a verdict to be accepted or rejected. The pessimistic view often treats a friend's recommendation as a temptation to be resisted while treating the novelist's description as an edifying exercise; but if the danger is borrowed perception, this attitude is unstable.

What I want to claim, then, is that the acquaintance requirement, as it is usually framed, runs together two different things. One is a reservation about borrowed verdicts — accepting that a work is good, moving, or unresolved without seeing or hearing it for oneself. The other is the transmission of aesthetic know-how — teaching someone what to look for, what to listen for, and how a judgment might be assembled out of noticed features. Fiction, at its best, does the second while refusing to do the first. If that is right, then part of what a novelist does is a peculiar kind of aesthetic testimony: testimony, not to how things are, but to how one might perceive them. And that, I want to suggest, is something we should value.
