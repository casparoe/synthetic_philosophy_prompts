I've been chewing on Hempel's ravens, and I put together this argument against the positive-instance idea. I keep thinking it's valid, but the conclusion is weird enough that I want a second pair of eyes. Tell me whether the argument goes through, and if not, which numbered step is the first to fail.

(1) Nicod's condition: observing an F that is G confirms that all F are G.
(2) So observing a black raven confirms that all ravens are black. This is the part I'd most hate to give up.
(3) Now suppose you know the actual world is exactly one of two possibilities, W_A or W_B, each with prior 1/2. In W_A: 100 black ravens, no ravens that aren't black, and 900 non-ravens. In W_B: 110 black ravens, 10 white ravens, and 880 non-ravens. Total birds is 1000 in both. Let H be "all ravens are black"; H is true in W_A and false in W_B.
(4) You draw a bird at random, uniformly from the 1000 birds in the actual world, and observe that it is a black raven. Call this observation E.
(5) P(E | W_A) = 100/1000 = 0.10, and P(E | W_B) = 110/1000 = 0.11. So E is more likely if H is false.
(6) By Bayes, P(W_A | E) = 0.10/(0.10 + 0.11) ≈ 0.476, below the prior of 0.5. So E lowers the probability of H; E disconfirms H.
(7) So a black raven disconfirms "all ravens are black."
(8) That contradicts (2), so Nicod's condition is false.

I suspect the trouble is either in (1) or in (7), but I can't say exactly what goes wrong without just refusing to accept the setup as legitimate.
