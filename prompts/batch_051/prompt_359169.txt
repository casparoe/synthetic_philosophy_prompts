I am a novelist at work on a story about a vow that appears to remain in force after the people who made it are gone, and I have been reading Hesiod's Theogony to think through how an oath can bind. Near the end of his account of the underworld, Hesiod describes the special oath by which the gods themselves swear, the oath taken over the water of the river Styx. The passage reads as follows in the English translation available through the Perseus Digital Library:

For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus.

What interests me is the particular mechanism of binding that this passage imagines. The perjury is not detected by a tribunal, and the penalty seems to follow from the false swearing itself; yet the text also presents the result as a fixed punishment, as though the oath were underwritten by a threat of terrible losses. I would like you to write an essay on the following controversy. In this passage, should the binding force of the Styx-oath be understood primarily on the model of a sanction-backed contract, so that even a god keeps faith because breach is ruinously costly? Or should it be understood as a constitutive performance, according to which pouring the water and swearing falsely does not merely trigger a punishment but places the swearer into a new condition, breathless and cut off, such that the penalty is the unfolding of the perjury itself? Weigh the strongest versions of each view, using the passage as your central evidence, and defend a conclusion rather than ending in a standoff. Since I am writing fiction rather than a scholarly article, I am less interested in historical philology than in what philosophical account of an oath's power best captures the logic displayed here.
