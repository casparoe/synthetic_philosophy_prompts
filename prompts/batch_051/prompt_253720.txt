I've been kicking around an essay on the wu-wei paradox and I'd like a second pair of eyes on the notes. The basic thought is that the paradox of trying not to try is usually presented as one problem, but I suspect there are actually two different mechanisms in play, and seperating them makes the standard Daoist remedies look more mysterious then they need to be.

Here's the idea. Wu-wei is normally glossed as effortless action or nondeliberate action: you act, sometimes extremely skillfully, without strain, self-consciousness, or a felt gap between decision and movement. The paradox arises because any deliberate program of becoming wu-wei seems to require exactly the effort and self-consciousness it is trying to eliminate. Trying to be effortless is a way of not being effortless, so the whole project can look self-undermining.

My move is that this describes two different ways trying can ruin wu-wei. The first is the centipede mechanism: attending to the parts of a skilled performance makes it collapse, the way asking a centipede which leg goes after which would trip it. Here trying is explicit, step-by-step control, and the cure is training until the skill sinks into the body. The second is the wanting mechanism: trying to be spontaneous means making spontaneity an object of desire, and desiring to be in a state whose whole point is that you are not monitoring it or aiming at it is self-referential trouble. This is closer to trying to fall asleep or trying to be surprised at your own party. I want to say the centipede problem is a problem of attention, while the wanting problem is a problem of desire and value, and that the standard Daoist stories about skill are aimed at the first, whereas something like the fasting of the mind passage is aimed at the second.

But I'm not sure the distinction holds. An obvious objection is that the wanting mechanism reduces to the centipede mechanism: wanting to be spontaneous just is a form of monitoring, because one is scanning oneself to see whether spontaneity has arrived, and that scanning is the self-consciousness that kills it. If that's right, the fasting of the mind is just an indirect way of getting the monitor out of the loop, and there is no separate paradox of desire.

What do you think? Specifically, can I defend the distinction as doing real work, or is it just a relabeling? Can you think of a case that fits one mechanism but not the other, say a case where a desire for a state undermines it even with no step-level monitoring, or a monitoring failure that has nothing to do with wanting anything? And is my rough mapping onto the texts fair, or should I weaken the claim to avoid putting a sharp distinction in the mouth of the Zhuangzi?
