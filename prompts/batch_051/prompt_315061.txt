You are a professional philosopher with expertise in analogical reasoning and the epistemology of decision-making under uncertainty. Think carefully before writing, and begin by brainstorming the distinct ways a historical analogy can distort a decision even when the people deploying it are competent and acting in good faith.

I am a philosophy professor, and a former student of mine now works as a policy analyst on a national security council staff. Several times a month she must evaluate a decision memo that rests substantially on a historical analogy — typically Munich 1938, sometimes Vietnam, increasingly the Thucydides trap. She has asked me to turn the philosophical critique of such analogies into something her office can actually use. Write, for her, a reusable decision procedure: a checklist in words, with questions in the order they should be asked, explicit stopping rules, and a note at each step about what counts as a sufficient answer. She is highly capable and reads policy continuously, but has no formal training in philosophy of history or probability. She has roughly one working day per memo, and she must be able to defend the procedure to a senior official who is emotionally attached to historical analogy as a way of thinking. The procedure should keep whatever distinctions do the real work in the critique of arguments from historical analogy, and should drop distinctions that would not change her conclusion in practice. Be explicit about which steps are genuinely judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the procedure hands a decision back to a person because it turns on values rather than facts.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical cases: (1) a memo arguing that State A's annexation of a small neighboring enclave must be met with immediate military deterrence because this is Munich 1938; (2) a memo arguing that continued support for a local counterinsurgency force will inevitably become a quagmire because this is Vietnam; and (3) a memo arguing that a recent naval standoff between a declining power and a rising power shows that a Thucydides-trap dynamic is already locked in. Make sure that at least one of the three applications reaches a conclusion the staffer and her senior official would prefer not to hear. Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: what it treats as settled that is in fact open to reasonable disagreement.
