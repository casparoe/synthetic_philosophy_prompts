I'm in an upper-level modal logic class and we've spent the last three weeks on the quantified version, specifically the Barcan formulas. The class sets everything up with a single fixed domain: there's one big set of possible things and it doesn't grow or shrink from world to world, and on that setup both formulas come out valid. BF says that if each thing is necessarily F, then necessarily every thing is F, and the converse says that if necessarily every thing is F, then each thing is necessarily F. The fixed domain is doing the work, since any object in the domain is in it at every accessible world, and since we've been reading 'exists' as 'is in the range of the quantifier,' the upshot is that everything that exists exists necessarily. So there's no room on this picture to say that there could have been an extra person who doesn't actually exist, or that I might never of existed. My prof keeps saying this is just the correct way to regiment modal talk, and that people who believe in merely possible objects are confused by surface grammar.

I can't shake the feeling that the result is built into the semantics rather than argued for, and I don't know how you'd even test it. So play it straight: what would have to be true for the Barcan formulas (one or both) to fail? What would the semantics look like instead — varying domains, treating existence as a real predicate, free logic, actualist quantifiers, whatever — and what does each escape route let you say that the fixed-domain picture can't? Which counterexamples actually have force, and which are just sloppy English? And give me a honest verdict on whether my prof's confidence is earned or whether the position has been quietly protected from refutation.
