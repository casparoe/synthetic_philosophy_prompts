I'm a junior legal officer at the Commission for Truth and Accountability in Arbelia, which was set up after the 1996 ceasefire between the old military junta and the two main rebel factions. I'm supposed to write the internal consistency memo for an amnesty decision we're making next week, and I keep going around in circles. The enabling law says an act is amnestiable only if it was committed with a political objective and was fully disclosed, but the commissioners have made clear that the statute isn't doing the real work, and our precedent file is what matters.

Our first five decisions are:

- Conscript K.T.: under direct orders, participated in executing two prisoners during a counterinsurgency sweep. He gave a complete account, led investigators to three mass graves, and was visibly shaken; amnesty granted.
- Guard M.N.: never personally killed anyone, but admitted she had denied detainees food and humiliated them. She identified the interrogators in her camp and apologized to survivors' families; amnesty granted on condition she pay into the reparations fund.
- Sergeant O.R.: beat a detainee to death during an interrogation. He disclosed everything and located the grave, but was flat through the hearing and said the detainee 'had information'; amnesty denied, referred for prosecution.
- Administrator P.D.: did not personally hurt anyone, but compiled the lists used to disappear people. She named thirty of those she had put on the lists and explained the chain of command, then said the detentions were 'necessary at the time'; amnesty denied, referred for prosecution.
- Militia member R.V.: took part in a raid in which four villagers were killed. He disclosed fully and testified against his commander, but said he would do it again because he thought the villagers backed the other side; amnesty denied, referred for prosecution.

The new applicant is a former intelligence captain who in 1997 personally tortured one detainee during a single interrogation session. He wasn't the architect of the program and didn't order anyone else to torture; he says he took the assignment voluntarily because he wanted promotion, and that it is the worst thing he has done. Since then he has disclosed the session in detail, cooperated with our investigators, expressed remorse that the victims' families apparently found credible, and spent the last few years doing humanitarian work.

If we want to be consistent with our own record, should we grant, deny, or impose conditions? Which of the five earlier decisions is the closest analog, and if they genuinely dont settle it, what rule would we be adopting under each option? I'd also like a sense of what future applicant would most clearly reveal which rule we've actually been running, because my boss keeps calling our standards 'principled' without saying what the principles are.
