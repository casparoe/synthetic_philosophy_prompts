I have been working through G. E. Moore's discussion in Chapter VI of Principia Ethica of the things good in themselves, especially what he says about the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. He is emphatic that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, and that one and the same state of consciousness can have very different value when combined with different cognitive elements. He also says that a friendship is better when the beliefs it involves are true than when they are false, and he treats knowledge itself as a good, though a lesser one than the affections. From these claims I find myself drawn into an argument that seems to prove too much, and I cannot tell whether the fault lies with Moore or with my formulation. Here is the argument.

1. A friendship between two people is among the greatest intrinsic goods.
2. If the affection depends on a false belief about the other person, then the whole is less good than it would have been if the belief had been true, because its cognitive part is defective.
3. Therefore, all else equal, more accurate beliefs about the other person should make a friendship better.
4. Accuracy is a single standard: if true beliefs about someone's virtues increase the value of a friendship, then true beliefs about their faults should also increase the value of its cognitive part, whatever else they do to the emotional part.
5. But in many actual friendships, learning a severe truth about the other person—a hidden act of cruelty, a persistent envy, a contempt for something one loves—would weaken the affection and leave the whole state worse than the warmer, more ignorant version of the same friendship.
6. So the theory seems to imply that we must balance the gain in cognitive value against the loss in affection before we can say whether a friendship is improved by full disclosure.
7. But Moore's doctrine of organic unities denies that we can compute the value of a whole by adding up the independent contributions of its parts. If we cannot sum the values of the parts, we also cannot perform the balancing in step 6.
8. Therefore Moore's theory leaves us without any principled way to decide whether the best friendship includes full knowledge of the other person or selective ignorance.
9. If a theory of intrinsic value cannot give guidance about whether a central instance of one of its greatest goods is better or worse under full knowledge, then it has proved too much: it has made the value of friendship in practice unknowable.
10. So either Moore's doctrine of organic unities, his claim that true cognition raises the value of love, or his insistence that knowledge is itself a good must be given up, or the conclusion that the value of friendship is unknowable must be accepted.

Where does this argument go wrong? If I have to choose, I would prefer to give up the doctrine of organic unities before giving up the idea that friendship and aesthetic enjoyment are the chief goods, but I suspect Moore himself would resist that more than I expect. Which step do you reject, why did it look right, and what does Moore's view have to pay if that step is the one that fails?
