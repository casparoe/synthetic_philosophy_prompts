At a seminar on early modern philosophy, a student ended a long dispute about whether Spinoza was really an atheist by writing the Latin tag Deus sive Natura—usually rendered God or Nature—on the board and saying that Spinoza himself tells us that God and Nature are one and the same. That has always seemed to me to close the debate only by leaving the claim imprecise. I would like a menu of the distinct theses the tag could be asserting, from the merely nominal to the radical. For each reading, please say (a) whether it is a platitude, a substantive metaphysical claim, or something false; (b) whether it is faithful to what Spinoza actually maintains in the Ethics; and (c) whether that reading, on its own, would decide whether Spinoza ought to be called an atheist, a pantheist, or neither.

Please include at least the following distinctions. First, the difference between saying that the words God and Nature pick out a single referent, in a purely extensional identity, and saying that God is immanent in nature rather than a transcendent creator standing outside it. Second, Spinoza's own distinction between Natura naturans and Natura naturata: does the tag identify God with nature as active cause, or with the whole collection of finite things that follow from it, and why should that difference matter for the label we give him? Third, the difference between saying that the natural order is fully intelligible and requires no supernatural supplement, and saying that the natural order could not have been otherwise because it expresses the divine essence with necessity. Finally, tell me which of these readings would actually support the student's use of the tag to close the debate, which (if any) would make Spinoza's position genuinely atheistic, and whether the necessity reading leaves room for the intellectual love of God that the Ethics treats as the mind's highest good, or turns that love into something closer to resignation.
