I retired from chemistry a few years ago and have been reading philosophy since, so I'm new to this and may be using words wrong. Here's what keeps confusing me. An AI company will say in its own launch materials that a model is aligned, helpful and harmless. Then it will describe a safety apparatus that seems to assume the model can't be trusted. There's a red team that spends all day trying to get the model to produce instructions for a bioweapon. There's a monitoring layer that flags outputs as deceptive or manipulative. And the model is locked in a sandbox: it can't send an email or touch a bank account unless a human explicitly opens the door.

People sometimes say these measures are about control rather than alignment, but I don't really see where one ends and the other begins.

My question is what philosophical commitments this collection of practices presupposes. Does running jailbreak evaluations commit a lab to the view that the model has its own goals, which might come apart from what the training was meant to produce? Or could you run all those same tests while still believing the model is just a next-token predictor with no aims at all, one that occasionally emits dangerous text when the statistics go sideways? And can a system with no beliefs or intentions still manipulate someone, or does a monitoring layer that looks for manipulation quietly presuppose that it can? I'd like you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where it's underdetermined between rival pictures of what the model is. If someone held that the model has no goals whatsoever, which parts of the practice would they have to give up or reinterpret, and would the remaining setup still count as alignment, or just as control?
