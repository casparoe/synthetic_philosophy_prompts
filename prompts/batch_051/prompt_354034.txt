I've been offered a job in another city and have spent weeks failing to decide. My usual way of handling a decision like this is to study myself from the outside: I review how I've handled similar choices, weigh my tolerance for risk and disruption, and try to assign a probability to what I will actually do. I catch myself thinking that I will probably end up accepting, or that I am more likely than not to turn it down, and it feels like I am gathering evidence about a fact.

A good friend has told me this is a mistake, and a conceptual one, not just a matter of anxiety. He says I cannot rationally assign a probability to my own future choice while the choice is still mine to make: a choice is not an event that happens to me and can be predicted, but an act I perform. Settling on a probability, he argues, either means I have already made the decision or I have handed it over to an imagined future self. On his view, trying to predict my own choice is not a neutral way to learn about myself — it alters the very choice it is meant to predict and turns deciding into passive spectatorship.

He might be right, but I can't tell whether he has located a real tension in my thinking or inflated a small point into a paradox. I would like advice that engages the actual situation, not a suggestion to list pros and cons. Is it incoherent, or in some way self-undermining, to hold a probability about my own future choice while I am still deciding? If it is, what sets ordinary decision-making apart from the self-forecasting I have been doing, and how should I decide instead? If it is not, what is actually going on when I forecast my own decision, and can I do it without distorting the choice?
