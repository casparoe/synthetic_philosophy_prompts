I sit on the compliance committee of a licensing board for industrial incident investigators. Our code of professional conduct, drafted in the 1970s and not amended since, contains the following clause, Section 4.2: "A certified investigator shall not include in any signed report a statement of fact that the investigator does not believe to be true at the time of signing." The clause was written with ordinary factual error, negligence, and deliberate fabrication in mind.

We are now reviewing a report submitted by a certified investigator. In the summary of findings, the investigator wrote: "The pressure-relief valve failed during the incident, but I do not believe that the pressure-relief valve failed during the incident." The rest of the report is unremarkable, and there is no dispute that the valve in fact failed. In a preliminary response, the investigator maintains that the sentence as a whole is true — its first part states a fact, and its second part accurately reports his own state of mind — and that the clause was never meant to reach a truthful compound statement. He adds that the drafters plainly did not have a first-person disclaimer sentence of this kind in view.

Our committee is split. We want to decide consistently with how the clause should be read and with reasons that could govern future cases. Please work through the competing approaches to interpreting Section 4.2 — the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what its drafters would likely have said if asked, and how an ordinary reasonable reader would parse the sentence — and tell us which approach should govern and whether the clause was violated.
