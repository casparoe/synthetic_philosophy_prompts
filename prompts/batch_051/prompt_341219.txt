Two friends of mine got in an argument over the old honor/dignity/victimhood-culture trichotomy, specifically whether any one of them is actually morally superior to the others, and they asked me to referee but I honestly couldn't. Here's roughly how it went.

Anna said dignity culture is the clear winner, and not just as a local equilibrium for WEIRD societies. Her argument: it's the only one of the three that doesn't make your moral standing something you have to extract from other people through performance. Honor culture ties standing to a willingness to retaliate; victimhood culture ties it to how convincingly you can perform being aggrieved. Dignity culture says your standing is just there, so you can shrug off insults instead of escalating them or advertising wounds. That's moral progress because it's the only one whose norms don't systematically incentivize feuds or the manufacture of grievances.

Ben pushed back that this is a rationalist delusion about impartiality. The "just ignore it" story only works if third-party enforcement actually shows up when the insult becomes real harm. Where it won't — or where invoking it costs more than absorbing the harm — dignity norms become "suck it up," and quietly favor whoever can take the most damage without complaining. Dignity culture doesn't escape the status game; it just changes the currency from toughness or victimhood to stoic self-sufficiency, and it punishes people who genuinely need help and say so. That's not obviously progress, just a different set of winners.

Anna came back that even if it isn't transcendence, we can still rank them by the incentives they install. Honor culture moralizes feuding. Victimhood culture moralizes fragility and public accusation. Dignity culture at least moralizes forbearance and third-party adjudication. Put societies behind a veil of ignorance and you'd choose dignity norms for the new society.

Ben replied that the veil-of-ignorance choice only shows dignity norms are safer among mutually suspicious strangers; it doesn't show they're morally better. A thick community can run honor norms without constant blood feuds, and victimhood culture was a real response to harms dignity culture makes invisible — especially for people with the least standing, who get told to "just ignore it." If dignity is better, it's better for a particular kind of society: large, diverse, trust-thin. Not better full stop.

Anna said "large, diverse, trust-thin" is the actual world, so that's good enough to call it morally preferable in practice — honor and victimhood norms don't scale, and they turn brutal or farcical when you try. Then Ben said she'd quietly swapped "morally superior" for "better adapted to modernity," and thats an ecological claim, not a moral one. They looked at me, but I don't actually know who's right about wich specific points.
