In discussions of mass atrocity I keep hitting the same impasse. One side treats "genocide" primarily as a legal category: the question is whether the acts satisfy the Genocide Convention's test — roughly, an intent to destroy a protected national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, wholly or partly — and applying the word without that finding is seen as a mistake, however terrible the events. The other side treats the word as a moral and political category whose point is to name extreme collective destruction; on this view, refusing the word because a legal test is not met can itself be a political act, and the category should track gravity rather than juridical criteria. The two sides often agree on the facts and disagree about the name. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a dispute about evidence, or even primarily about what happened, but about what the concept is for — fair legal labeling and accountability on one side, moral recognition and political mobilization on the other. The impasse has a history as well: Lemkin coined the term for something broader than the later Convention standard, including a group's cultural and social destruction, and the law narrowed it. Is my diagnosis too tidy? Is this a substantive disagreement with hidden conceptual or empirical stakes, a merely verbal disagreement, or a conflict between incompatible purposes that no amount of evidence will settle? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress? If the question or my diagnosis rests on a confusion, tell me that directly rather than accommodating it.
