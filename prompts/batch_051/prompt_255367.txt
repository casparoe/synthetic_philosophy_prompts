Two colleagues of mine, Nadia and Owen, work on platform policy at a video-sharing service, and last week I listened to them argue about a single moderation decision. A researcher posted a forty-second propaganda deepfake in which a mayoral candidate appears to accept an envelope of cash. The video had already been circulating on encrypted messaging apps; the researcher reposted it on the platform with a caption identifying it as fake and pointing readers to the tell-tale artefacts around the jaw and the shadow under the arm, along with a link to a frame-by-frame technical debunk. The platform's rule is that synthetic media impersonating a real person is removed unless it is clearly labelled and posted for criticism, education, or research, so the post was left up. A few days later the unlabelled clip was being forwarded again, and the candidate's campaign claimed the platform had laundered the smear.

Owen said the decision was right. Removing the researcher's copy would have left only the anonymous originals and no stable, public reference point for the debunk. A platform that removes the annotated copy while the raw clip spreads elsewhere is not protecting anyone, he said; it is merely refusing to host the version that helps people.

Nadia said the exception is self-defeating in this case. The label and the debunk do not travel with the file; the platform's own design allows the clip to be downloaded, clipped, and forwarded without the caption. What the platform actually hosted, she said, was a clean, high-quality copy of the very artefact its policy claims to remove, and the criticism exception had turned into a distribution channel.

Owen replied that the answer to context-stripping is to make context harder to strip, not to ban the annotated copy. If the concern is that the caption can be severed, then require a visible watermark and disable downloads for this exception; but do not remove the post, because the public needs to be able to compare the fake against the technical analysis rather than take a fact-checker's word for it.

Nadia answered that a watermark and a download block would help, but the platform cannot stop screen recording, and the deepfake's persuasive force does not depend on high fidelity alone. She said the researcher should have used degraded or partial reproductions, such as stills with the manipulated frames marked or a muted and cropped clip, which are far less useful to someone trying to spread the smear. Owen called that a false choice: a still or a cropped clip can be accused of misrepresenting the original, and it teaches the public less about how to recognise synthetic media. People who meet the full clip first inside a critical frame, he said, are better off than people who meet it cold.

Nadia said this assumes the critical frame reaches the same people as the severed clip, and it plainly does not: the re-forwarded clip reached exactly the audience that never saw the debunk. A policy, she said, should be judged by its effect on the actual distribution, not on the ideal reader. Owen replied that if platforms refuse to host such material even in annotated form, propagandists learn a simple rule: produce material too hot to republish, and the most manipulative content becomes the content that can never be publicly answered.

I honestly could not decide. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: for each claim, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me which of the two is more right overall, and whether the disagreement turns on any empirical question that the two of them are not in a position to settle. Please give a committed verdict rather than just laying out both sides, and write your answer in British English.
