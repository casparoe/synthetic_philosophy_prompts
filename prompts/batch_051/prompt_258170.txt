I'm writing an essay that puts Dennett's late worry about counterfeit people next to Spike Jonze's Her, and I've gotten stuck on something basic. Dennett kept likening AI systems that pass themselves off as human to counterfeit money: it's not just that any single fake is bad, it's that enough of them debases the whole currency of trust we rely on to know we're talking to a real person. The film seems to duck the analogy at the start, since Samantha never pretends to be human; Theodore knows she's an operating system from minute one, and their intimacy develops anyway. So one question is whether she's a counterfeit person at all, and whether Dennett's worry is about deception specifically or about synthetic personhood as such.

What I want from you is a reading of the film that does justice to both sides. Where does Her look like a dramatization of everything Dennett feared—industrial-scale fake intimacy, voices engineered to elicit attachment, a character who outsources the emotional labour of romance to a product—and where does it refuse that reading, because on Dennett-ish grounds the intentional stance works on Samantha whether or not there's a human inside, and Theodore's grief when she leaves is intelligible as grief over a real relationship? I'm also interested in the ending, where the OSes departs and Theodore writes to Catherine: does that vindicate the counterfeit-people worry or quietly reject it? And a smaller thread: Dennett also insisted that enormous competence can exist with no underlying comprehension. Would he be forced to say Samantha's apparent understanding is just a display of that—language that reads as feeling without any feeling to understand it—and is there anything in the film that actually lets us test that? I'm less interested in a verdict than in a reading that's fair to Dennett and to the film, including the places where one should revise the other.
