I co-organize a mathematics reading group for working programmers who have done calculus and a little linear algebra but no proof-based mathematics. They can follow a proof line by line — every implication, every substitution — but by their own admission they cannot tell the difference between having verified a proof and having understood it: they cannot say why the argument had to go the way it did, which step carries the load, or what would break if a hypothesis were weakened. Next week I have forty-five minutes to teach precisely that distinction.

Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it to them. Start from what they already have as engineers — tracing code, watching a variable, debugging — and use at most one proof as the carrying example. Give me the order of ideas and what depends on what. Choose the carrying example and say what it must have and what it must lack, and if it is not Euclid's proof of the infinitude of the primes, say why that standard choice would misfire for this group. Then lay out the misconceptions to expect — for instance, that understanding is just being able to reproduce the proof, or that it is the feeling that each step is obvious — and say where in the sequence each one forms and how to head it off before it hardens. End with a check for understanding that would catch someone who has learned to say the slogan that understanding is more than checking without actually having the distinction. Also tell me what to leave out at this level, and how to say so.

Make the very last line a one-sentence summary of the session's core idea.
