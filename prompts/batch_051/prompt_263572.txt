Two of my friends got into it at lunch yesterday about whether "what if everyone did that?" is actually a good argument, and I honestly couldnt tell who was right, so I want you to referee it.

Jake said cutting the line at the taco stand is wrong because if everyone cut, there'd be no line, and your whole reason for cutting is that you get ahead while everyone else waits. He said that's what makes it worse than other stuff with bad side effects: you're counting on everyone else not doing exactly what you're doing.

Priya said that proves way too much. If everyone became a doctor the economy would be a mess, but it's not wrong for one person to become a doctor. And if everyone stayed home from prom there'd be no prom, but staying home is fine. She said a bad outcome if everyone did X doesn't by itself make one person's X wrong, since one more person never tips the scale.

Jake said she was attacking a dumber version of his point. He wasn't talking about the literal consequences of everyone doing it. He said it's a logic point: if you say cutting is okay for you, you're committed to saying it's okay for anyone in the same position. But "anyone may cut whenever they're in a hurry" would destroy the line, so the rule you're acting on contradicts the practice you're relying on. That's the special pleading.

Priya answered that this just moves the problem into how you describe the rule. "I'll cut when I'm late and the line is more than ten people" can be universalized fine, and "I'll become a doctor" can be described as "I'll enter a profession that can't absorb everyone," which fails the test. She said unless Jake can explain which description is the real one, the test just gives back whatever answer you wanted from the start.

Jake said the thing that matters is what the action is for. Cutting is *using* the queue to get ahead of the people who made it a queue, so the maxim secretly depends on a practice it also undermines. Staying home from prom doesn't depend on other people going, so universalizing it isn't self-defeating in the same way. He said that's why the doctor case isn't parallel either: becoming a doctor doesn't exploit other people not becoming doctors.

Priya said that's now a different argument. He'd dropped the "what if everyone did that?" test and switched to a story about free-riding on practices, which needs its own account of when free-riding is wrong and why. And she said even if universalizability is a real consistency requirement, by itself it doesn't tell you which rules are right, so the slogan ends up doing almost none of the original work.

They both turned to me to settle it and I had nothing. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each turn, say what's right and what's off, and say who's more right overall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
