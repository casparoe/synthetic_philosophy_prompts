I'm a PhD candidate in formal epistemology with a side interest in philosophy of statistics, and I keep moving back and forth on whether objective Bayesianism can actually deliver what it promises. I want a number rather than a survey, but with the reasoning made explicit.

Fix a canonical case: you must put a prior probability distribution over a positive continuous quantity — say, a failure-time — and your total evidence consists of knowing its mean and its variance. Let P be the claim that, among all priors consistent with those two moment constraints, exactly one is rationally required, and that this uniquely rational prior is the maximum entropy distribution. I do not mean the weak claim that maximum entropy is one permissible choice among several; I mean that an agent who chose any other consistent prior would be making a rational error.

My own credence in P is around 0.45. A colleague in my department puts it around 0.75, and I suspect our disagreement is concentrated in a single sub-question rather than spread evenly.

Give me your own credence in P and the decomposition behind it. In particular, assign rough probabilities to the following sub-claims: (a) that epistemic rationality contains a norm strong enough to move from mere coherence to a unique prior — some genuine bridge principle from evidence to distributions; (b) that maximal entropy picks out a representation-independent property of being least informative, or else that the familiar re-description problem (the same quantity parameterized as a rate rather than a waiting time changes the maximum entropy prior) can be defused instead of being a fatal defect; (c) that symmetry or invariance considerations of the standard default-prior kind can select a prior without smuggling in a preferred parameterization or a hidden loss function; and (d) that the uniqueness claim does real work in finite-sample inference and prediction, rather than being washed out once the likelihood begins to dominate. Tell me how these combine — are they independent, or does your answer to the representation-dependence question largely screen off the others? — identify which sub-question most likely separates someone at 0.45 from someone at 0.75, and say what argument or result would move your credence the most. If you think P has no determinate truth value until a specific account of epistemic rationality is fixed, say which account you are using and how much of your residual uncertainty is about whether that account is the right one.
