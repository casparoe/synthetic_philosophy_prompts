A private research institute's animal-welfare code, unchanged since 1994, contains a single housing clause:

"Any animal maintained at the Institute shall be housed in conditions that allow it to display the normal behavioral repertoire of a typical member of its species."

The Institute now maintains a colony of mouse–human neural chimeras: mice in which a substantial fraction of the forebrain's glial cells are of human origin, created by a technique the drafters never anticipated. These animals are taxonomically Mus musculus, but their behavior falls well outside the normal mouse repertoire. They learn tasks at levels above the wild-type range, their activity cycles are atypical, and they develop repetitive behaviors in standard enriched mouse housing that do not appear in wild-type controls. Animal-care staff are divided: some read the clause as requiring only mouse-appropriate housing, because the animals are mice; others read it as requiring housing adequate to the animals' actual behavioral repertoire, whatever its source.

What does the clause require for these animals? Argue the question from four angles: the words of the clause; the purpose of the clause; what its drafters would have said if asked; and what a reasonable reader of the code today would take it to require. Then say which approach should win here and why. Conclude by drawing out what the dispute reveals about whether the code protects species membership or actual capacities.
