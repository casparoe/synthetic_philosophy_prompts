I have been trying to work out what I actually think about costly signaling and the handicap principle, case by case, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures my verdicts or whether some of them have to go.

1. Stotting. A Thomson's gazelle that spots an approaching cheetah sometimes leaps straight up with stiff legs, in full view, before running. I take this to be an honest signal of vigor: a gazelle that could not outrun or outlast the cheetah could not afford to advertise its escape ability this way, and the cheetah is not being fooled when it breaks off the hunt on seeing a stotting animal.

2. The peacock's train. I think the train is an honest signal of male condition. The cost is the point: a male in poor condition pays more, in survival terms, for the same ornament, so peahens are not making an error when they prefer elaborate trains.

3. A used-car warranty. A dealer who offers a strong ninety-day warranty is, I think, honestly signaling that the car is sound. The warranty is differentially expensive: it costs a seller little if the car is solid and a great deal if it is a lemon, so the signal separates the two types.

4. Cheap talk. If a friend says "Trust me, I'm good for the loan," I treat the words as almost no evidence of trustworthiness. Saying it costs the deadbeat no more than the reliable borrower, so it cannot separate them.

5. A counterfeit luxury watch. I think this is a dishonest signal, but not a counterexample to the handicap idea. It misleads only because observers mistake it for the genuinely costly article; the fake flourishes as a parasite on a costly-signaling system.

6. Two diplomas. A degree earned through years of demanding work signals diligence and ability. A diploma bought from a mill for a flat fee, with no real assessment, does not signal those things, even though the buyer also paid money and went through motions. I am confident about both halves, but I need a principle that explains why the mill diploma is not an honest signal when it, too, involved a cost. This is the pair that most bothers me when I compare it with the peacock's train.

7. An engagement ring. The convention that the ring should cost three months' salary puzzles me. I see why people treat it as a signal of commitment, but I cannot see that the size of the diamond tracks fidelity. A wealthy person who does not intend to stay can pay without strain, while a devoted person of modest means is made to stretch for months. Part of me says the sacrifice itself makes it an honest-enough signal; another part says it is a pure handicap, or a tax, because the cost is not differentially high for the type who would betray. I go back and forth.

What I would like from you is this. Is there one coherent account of when a costly display is an honest signal that delivers all, or nearly all, of my verdicts? State the principle precisely, and indicate which clause is motivated by which case. Then apply the principle to the engagement-ring case and to a case I have not listed: a person has a large, painful, difficult-to-remove emblem of a group tattooed on their body as a pledge of lifelong membership. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether a simpler principle that overturns one or two verdicts captures the rest better.
