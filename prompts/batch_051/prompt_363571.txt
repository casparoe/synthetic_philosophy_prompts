I've spent a lot of time reading rationalist-adjacent material, so I'm comfortable with steelmanning and suspicious of distinctions that sound precise but might not be doing any work. I keep using a distinction I invented about personal data, and I want it stress-tested by someone who knows the philosophy of property, privacy, and information.

Call "emanations" the records whose existence and content are fixed mainly by facts about what a person does or is: a location ping, a purchase log, a heartbeat reading, a browser fingerprint, the fact that your face appears in a photo. In these, the subject's action or body is the main cause of the record's existing with that content. Call "ascriptions" the records whose existence is fixed mainly by someone else's act of judgment or interpretation: a credit score, a performance review, a psychiatric note, a profile in a newspaper, a model's prediction about your future purchases, a diary entry about you. In these, the recorder's act of interpretation is the main cause.

The claim is this. The debate over whether information about you belongs to you conflates two different questions. Property, if it attaches anywhere, attaches to emanations, and it does so in proportion to the subject's causal contribution to generating the record — not because the record is about you, but because you did the work of acting or being the thing that was recorded. Ascriptions, by contrast, belong to whoever did the interpretive work, and you can no more own them than you can own someone else's opinion or memory of you. What you have over ascriptions is a different kind of right: privacy- or dignity-type rights against harmful or exposing uses, not ownership.

So the slogan "does information about you belong to you?" dissolves into two claims. First, you may have a property claim over the behavioral facts you generated, in proportion to your causal contribution — a labor-mixing claim applied to data. Second, you have non-property rights against abusive uses of both emanations and ascriptions. On this view, much of data-protection law has the logic backwards: it hands you legal control over data because the data refers to you, which is ascription-like logic, while failing to give you a property stake in the value created when your behavior is logged.

I want an honest assessment, not agreement. Specifically: Is the emanation/ascription boundary doing real work, or does it collapse once you notice that even a purchase log encodes someone's choice of categories to record? Does "proportional to causal contribution" mean anything determinate, or is it just labor-mixing with the same vagueness Locke is standardly accused of? Does this view already exist in the literature under a name I would recognize, and does it look stronger or weaker once stated properly? And where does the strongest counterexample break it — aggregated data built from millions of emanations, a classifier trained on my emanations without recording them, or an emanation that cost me nothing to generate?
