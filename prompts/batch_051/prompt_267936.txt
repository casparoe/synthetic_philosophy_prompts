Ok so I'm not any kind of philosopher but I ended up reading Plato's Meno in the Lamb translation on Perseus and this bit got me stuck. Around the end Socrates is saying why knowledge is better than true opinion. He says right opinion is just as useful for leading someone to Larisa, but then he says true opinions won't stick around: "they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. ... But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels."

So the standard story is the difference between knowledge and true opinion is that knowledge is tied down by causal reasoning. Now suppose there are two guides who both always get people to Larisa. The first has actually worked out the route from landmarks, distances, sun position, whatever, and can tell you why each turn is right. The second has a totally false explanation: he thinks the roads are rearranged every night by a local god, and the god only reveals the route to him if he prays in the right way. In fact the roads are fixed and his prayers do nothing, but the explanation is really stable for him—he uses it to answer questions, he has a story about why detours work, and he's never going to lose his belief. If anything the second guy's belief is more stable because he can't be argued out of it.

My question is what Socrates' criterion says about the second guide. Has his true opinion been fastened with causal reasoning or not? The prayer story feels like a causal explanation, just a false one. If Socrates only needs the person to have some causal reasoning, then the second guide counts as having knowledge, which seems wrong because knowledge would rest on false reasons. If Socrates needs the causal reasoning itself to be true, then what exactly excludes the demon story? Is it just that the premises are false, or is it that the explanation doesn't connect in the right way to the actual route? And if a true opinion can be just as stable as knowledge because the person has a false but psychologically unshakeable explanation, does that break the whole reason Socrates gives for prizing knowledge? I'm less interested in what the text says next then in whether the Larisa case actually supports the conclusion once you vary it this way.
