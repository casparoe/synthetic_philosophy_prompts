I'm in a political philosophy seminar and we spent last week on Rawls. A friend of mine, coming from an econ background, keeps insisting that Rawls only looks plausible because nobody states the Harsanyi side as a proper arguement. He wrote out his version for me and, annoyingly, it's more compelling than I expected:

(1) Behind the veil of ignorance you have no information about which person in society you will turn out to be. (2) You therefore have no reason to treat any one person's life as more likely than any other, and rationality requires assigning equal probability to each possible life — anything else would be arbitrary. (3) With equal probabilities assigned, the original position becomes a decision under risk, and rational choice under risk requires maximizing expected utility. (4) With equal probability on every life, expected utility is just average utility. (5) So the rational choice behind the veil is the society with the highest average utility. (6) And since justice is whatever rational people would choose behind the veil, justice requires maximizing average utility, not the difference principle.

He claims the only way out is to quietly drop the self-interest assumption, but that Rawls needs that assumption anyway to get the difference principle going. I have a nagging sense there's a trick in here somewhere, but I can't locate it. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and on what reading of it? If a Rawlsian can accept (1) and (2) and still resist (6), where exactly do they dig in?
