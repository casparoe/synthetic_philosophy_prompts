My friend offered this one-sentence summary of Bentham's critique of natural rights in Anarchical Fallacies: "Bentham's argument is that rights exist only as creatures of positive law, so talk of pre-legal natural rights is literally meaningless, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man is refuted once you notice this semantic point." I suspect this makes Bentham sound like he is raising a purely verbal objection, and that it flattens his actual argumentative strategy, but I don't know enough to say where the distortion lies. Below is a passage from Bentham's *Anarchical Fallacies; being an Examination of the Declarations of Rights issued during the French Revolution*, from his discussion of the proposed declaration:

"I can conceive but four purposes which a discourse, of the kind proposed under the name of a Declaration of Rights, can be intended to answer: the setting bounds to the authority of the crown; - the setting bounds to the authority of the supreme legislative power, that of the National Assembly; - the serving as a general guide or set of instructions to the National Assembly itself, in the task of executing their function in detail, by the establishment of particular laws; - and the affording a satisfaction to the people."

Is my friend's summary fair to the argument Bentham is actually making in this passage and in the work more broadly? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, separating what is accurate in the summary, what is subtly misrepresented, and whether the "refutation" follows from the accurate parts.
