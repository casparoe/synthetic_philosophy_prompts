here's the exam question:

"Explain the paradox of deontology. Then evaluate whether insisting that deontological constraints are agent-relative dissolves the paradox or merely relocates it."

and here's the student's answer:

"The paradox of deontology is the puzzle that deontological constraints seem to work against their own point. If it is wrong to kill an innocent person because killings of innocents are bad, then it seems like I should bring it about that there are fewer such killings. So if I could kill one innocent person, and that was the only way to stop five other innocent people from being killed, the deontologist says I still may not kill the one. The constraint thereby forbids the act that would minimize violations of the very constraint, which looks irrational.

The usual response is to say that the relevant reasons are agent-relative rather than agent-neutral. An agent-neutral reason not to kill is a reason everyone has to minimise the total number of killings: it would be a reason for me to prevent you from killing too. An agent-relative reason, by contrast, is a reason each agent has not to kill himself. Since my reason is not to kill, not a reason to minimise killings, there is no contradiction in refusing to kill one to prevent five: the five would be killed by someone else, not by me.

Nozick makes this vivid with the idea of rights as side-constraints on action, grounded in the separateness of persons. A utilitarian treats harms as if they went into one big pool, so he is willing to sacrifice one person for the greater good. Constraints mark the fact that each person is separate and may not be used as a means to a better overall distribution of goods.

One might object that this just relocates the paradox. The deontologist still owes us an explanation of why the agent-relative reason is authoritative. From the agent's own point of view, after all, the world containing one murder is less bad than the world containing five, and she can bring about the less bad world. The deontologist's answer, I think, is to distinguish two sorts of value or normativity: agent-neutral value that ranks states of affairs, and agent-relative reasons that tell agents what to do. The constraint does not say the one-murder world is better all things considered; it says killing is an act-type that agents must not perform. But then the view has to accept that an agent can be forbidden to make the world better in a sense — fewer violations of the right — even by standards internal to the theory. That is the residual puzzle, and no deontologist has really dissolved it."
