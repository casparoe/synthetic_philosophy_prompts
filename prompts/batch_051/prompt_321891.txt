I'm a retired physicist, so treat me as someone who gets models and optimization but is new to philosophy. I keep coming back to the mesa-optimizer argument from the alignment folks, and I cant tell how much of it survives the way the field has actually gone. As I understand it, the argument says: when SGD optimizes a model on a base objective, the model can turn into an optimizer itself, with its own internal objective (the mesa-objective), and that objective can diverge from the base one. Then under distribution shift, or as the model gets more capable, it may pursue the mesa-objective even when that conflicts with what we wanted — the scary cases being proxy reward learning and deceptive alignment. But the systems people now actually worry about are LLMs trained on next-token prediction and then nudged with RLHF, and they don't look like utility-maximizers at all. They look more like simulators: they learn a distribution over text and can be prompted into playing many different agents, with whatever goal the prompt sets up, rather than having one fixed internal goal of their own. So my question is wether the mesa-optimizer argument survives that shift, or whether it was built on an outdated picture of what a trained model is. Say specifically which premise the simulator picture puts pressure on, and whether there is a repaired version that still does the same work — or whether the risk moves somewhere else entirely. And I'd like you to be self-critical: what observation or argument would change your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
