I spent most of my career in organic chemistry, where one learns early that after a reaction has run, no amount of wishing changes the yield; post hoc reasoning about what would have happened had one used a different solvent is wishful thinking. Since retiring, I have been working through the decision-theory literature, and I find myself genuinely torn between a formal principle and a stubborn causal intuition in a pair of cases.

In counterfactual mugging, a superhuman predictor with accurate knowledge of your decision policy flips a coin. Had the coin come up heads, the predictor would have given you a substantial sum if and only if you would have paid a smaller sum upon seeing tails. The coin came up tails, and the predictor now asks you for the smaller sum. The ex ante optimal policy is to pay, because precommitting to pay buys the large reward on heads; but now that tails has happened, there is no causal relation between your paying and the earlier coin flip, and paying feels like being exploited by a known fact about my own policy. In XOR blackmail, a blackmailer sends a demand letter if and only if the XOR of a random bit and the truth of the proposition that you would pay upon receiving the letter is true; on receiving such a letter, some decision theories conclude you should pay because of what paying would indicate about the random bit, while others conclude paying is inert because the demand is already in front of you and the bit is fixed.

I understand that these are stock cases used to separate evidential, causal, and updateless decision theories, but I have not seen anyone address directly what should happen when the formal principle and the intuition about post hoc causality pull apart. My formal training tells me to follow the policy that maximizes expected value ex ante; my scientific training tells me that after an event has occurred, only forward-looking causal consequences can count as reasons. I want a negotiated verdict: should I treat this intuition as a debunkable artefact of my training, should I amend the theory to respect it, or is there a reading of “ought to pay” on which both are right? Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
