Consider two features of current British public policy side by side. Employees are automatically enrolled in workplace pension schemes unless they opt out, and organ donation operates on an opt-out basis; in both cases the default is set to the result the state favours, opting out is possible but deliberately made somewhat effortful, and the design is partly justified by appeal to inertia and framing effects. The same system also treats explicit, informed consent as a hard requirement for most medical treatment. What account of welfare, autonomy, and rational agency is implicit in the first pair of practices? Do they presuppose that a person has genuine preferences distinct from what she actually chooses under a default, and can that be reconciled with the presuppositions behind informed-consent requirements? I am less interested in the official rationales than in whether these practices are philosophically coherent together: what each strictly presupposes, where each is merely consistent with several theories, and where the apparent tension is real.
