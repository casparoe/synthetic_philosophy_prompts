I teach health policy ethics and have been asked to circulate a short statement of my own position to my graduate seminar before our first meeting. I assembled the statement below from different literatures over several years, and I have lost any feel for whether its parts still fit together. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in distributive justice in health care and in the measurement literature behind quality-adjusted life-years and the value of a statistical life. Work through each pair of commitments carefully before you write; a useful audit tells me which tensions are real and which are merely apparent, not merely that everything is connected to everything else.

Here is the position as it stands.

(1) Coverage decisions in a public health system should be governed primarily by cost-effectiveness measured in quality-adjusted life-years. An intervention should be covered when, and normally only when, its incremental cost per QALY falls below the system's threshold; if it does not, disinvestment is in order even when identifiable patients will lose access to a treatment they want.

(2) A QALY gained by a seventy-five-year-old counts no less than a QALY gained by a twenty-five-year-old. Age should have no independent role in allocation or triage decisions.

(3) The Rule of Rescue, the felt imperative to spare no expense for a named and visible patient while comparable statistical patients go without, is a documented cognitive bias, and allocation policy should screen it out rather than institutionalize it.

(4) In mass-casualty triage it is permissible, and often required, to withdraw ventilatory support from a patient whose chance of surviving to discharge is very low, so that it can be given to someone with a substantially better chance, provided the criteria are fixed in advance and applied without regard to social role, age, or disability.

(5) Conventional QALY methods are not disability-neutral, because health-state valuations are often elicited from people who do not inhabit the states they are rating and therefore sit below the valuations that those living with the condition would give. Under standard measurement, a policy of maximizing QALYs will tend to rank extending a disabled person's life below extending a nondisabled person's life, and I find that result unacceptable.

(6) In regulatory settings I am comfortable using a willingness-to-pay-based value of a statistical life as a rough measure of what society should spend to reduce mortality risk, even though it implies a monetary value per QALY far above the threshold I accept for coverage decisions.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with which, and which pairs merely look tense? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, identify the least costly revision and what it would cost; then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set cohere. I do not need references or citations. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in an email to a colleague.
