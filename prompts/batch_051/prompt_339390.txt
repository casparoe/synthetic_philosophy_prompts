I sit on the review board of a small press that specializes in biographical fiction: novels about real, named people who are dead, whose public lives are kept recognizable. Our standing instruction has long been that a writer may be true to the record without being bound by it, but the content of that instruction has been set by a short run of precedents. We approved a novel about an eighteenth-century composer and political organizer whose documented speeches and public acts were kept accurate, while her private conversations and a wholly invented confidante were written into the empty spaces, because no letters or diaries survive. We approved a novel about a mid-century poet whose cruelty to his wife was dramatized almost scene for scene from his own letters and his ex-wife's unpublished memoir, over the formal objection of his daughter; we held that surviving relatives cannot veto documented private truth. We rejected a novel about a nineteenth-century scientist that inserted a public accusation of plagiarism for which no evidence exists, on the ground that it would add a false episode to the public record. And we rejected a novel about a twentieth-century diplomat that invented a warm lifelong friendship with a political rival, because the archives show the two men detested one another and the invention flatly contradicted the record.

The proposal now before us concerns a civil-rights lawyer who died six years ago and is well known in her state. The manuscript keeps her recorded cases, victories, and speeches accurate. It invents a private, years-long affair between her and the opposing counsel in her most celebrated trial. There is no documentation of the affair. The novelist's entire evidence is a single undated line in her journal recording only that she had seen R. again and that an old feeling had returned; the novelist reads it as proof of an affair, while her two adult children say the affair never happened and have asked us not to publish the book. In the novel, the affair is the emotional engine of the trial, and a late scene implies that she prevailed in part because of information the opposing counsel quietly passed to her.

I need a recommendation for what our board should decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents. Please identify the closest analog among the four earlier decisions, spell out the candidate principles under which this proposal comes out differently, say what principle we would be adopting with each available decision, and name a further case that would most sharply separate the live options.
