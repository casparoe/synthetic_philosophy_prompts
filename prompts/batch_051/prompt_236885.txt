I am preparing a recorded Q&A for a philosophy podcast with a visiting political theorist who defends a recognisable but not much-discussed version of meritocracy. On his account, institutions may legitimately select people by competitive merit because merit, understood as the competent and conscientious exercise of a developed ability, is a good predictive proxy for the contribution a person will make; and, second, people who perform well under a fair competition deserve the rewards attached to that performance, even though no one deserves their native talent, their temperament, or the early environment that shaped their capacity for effort. His signature reply to the familiar objection that talent is unearned luck is that the objection confuses what a person is given with what a person does with it, and that desert ranges only over the doing, never over the endowment.

Please write me six to eight interview questions, each followed by a short follow-up to be used after the guest has given his standard reply. Avoid generic scene-setting questions; make them cut into the load-bearing joints of the view. Among other things, the questions should force him to specify what makes a competition fair and who bears the cost of making it fair; ask whether the luck objection can simply be re-deployed against the capacity for sustained effort and self-discipline; challenge whether the doing can be separated from the given as cleanly as his reply assumes; and ask what the view owes to people whose poor performance traces to disadvantages that fair procedures have not yet corrected. End with a question about what evidence or argument would lead him to abandon the view rather than merely amend it.

After the questions, please add a note stating how confident you are that they track the genuine stress points of the position, and identify the single question you are least sure actually damages it. Please use British English.
