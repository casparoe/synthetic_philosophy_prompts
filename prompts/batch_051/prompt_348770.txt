Here is a parallel I can't decide about. In the debate over why some nations are rich, the institutions view leans heavily on the fact that the countries that became rich overwhelmingly have relatively inclusive, secure institutions, while its critics reply that this fact is generated by a filtered sample: we observe only states that formed, survived colonization or escaped it in particular ways, and ended up rich, so the institutions correlated with wealth may be effects of wealth, byproducts of other processes, or merely correlated with the true cause. This looks to me structurally identical to the old adaptationism dispute in evolutionary biology, where the temptation is to explain any trait in a surviving lineage by telling a story about what it was selected for, and the spandrels critique answers that the trait may be an unselected byproduct of something else, with the apparent fit between organism and environment partly an artifact of looking only at what survived. Is this a deep point about inference from filtered samples, with the nation-wealth debate as one instance of a problem the biologists diagnosed, or does it break because economic success is not reproduction, institutions are copied horizontally between societies rather than inherited vertically, and human agents can anticipate and manipulate the filter? Be precise about what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between them, and name something the parallel would predict about the cross-country evidence that turns out false.
