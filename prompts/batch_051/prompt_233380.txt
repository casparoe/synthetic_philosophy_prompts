I'm taking a seminar on the moral psychology of injustice, and the professor has been running us through a distinction she keeps calling (after Rawls) the difference between envy proper, excusable envy, and justified resentment. Each week she gives a little vignette and then rules on which box it goes in. We've now built up a running list of her verdicts, and for a short paper we have to predict what she'll say about a new case.

Her verdicts so far:

- A junior associate envies the colleague who was promoted over her because he's the managing partner's nephew, and everyone in the office jokes about it; she's also underpaid and keeps getting the worst assignments. The professor called this excusable envy, not a vice — she said it'd be unreasonable to expect her not to feel it.
- A contestant envies the winner of a baking competition that was judged blind and fairly. Professor: plain envy proper, a vice — there's no injustice for it to track.
- A homeowner resents the contractor who took her deposit and never did the work. Professor: justified resentment — a properly moral response to being wronged, not envy at all.
- A grad student envies a peer who won a prestigious fellowship that the committee awarded on a close but fair reading of the files. Professor: envy proper, a vice.
- A tenant envies the neighbor one floor up who has a slightly bigger apartment at slightly lower rent because of a seniority policy everyone in the building accepts as fair. Professor: envy proper, a vice.

The new case we have to rule on: two remote customer-service reps do identical work. One finds out the other makes about nine percent more, purely because the manager is his old college friend. The lower-paid rep is comfortable — he can save, he's not worried about bills — and no one mocks or humiliates him; the whole thing is just kept quiet. When he finds out, he gets that familiar hot dislike of his coworker's extra margin. Would she call this excusable envy, envy proper, or justified resentment?

What's tripping me up is that I can see two rules she might be running that both fit all five verdict's. Rule (a): envy is excusable only when the unfair inequality is big and humiliating enough, or the person is badly enough off, that its unreasonable to expect them not to feel it. Rule (b): envy is excusable whenever it tracks a real unfairness, and the humiliation stuff is just one way that unfairness can show up. Which rule is more likely, what does each one say about the rep, and what's one extra vignette I could ask her about that would cleanly settle which one she's running?
