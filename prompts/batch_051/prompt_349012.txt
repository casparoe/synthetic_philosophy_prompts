You're a political philosopher who works on sovereignty and international institutions. Think carefully and from first principles, and before you settle on a verdict, brainstorm a couple of ways the idea could be made precise. Ive been playing with a concept I call membership bleed, and I want an honest assessment of it, not reassurance.

The claim is that joining a customs union, a confederation, or anything in that family is not a one-time transfer of sovereignty; it's the start of a standing renegotiation over what counts as trade policy at all. The treaty text may only hand over tariffs, but tariff policy is tangled up with product standards, subsidies, tax policy, visa rules and the rest. Once you can't use tariffs, you start regulating trade through those other channels, and soon the union has to legislate them just to stop members from cheating. Sovereignty therefore doesn't get transferred on signing day; it leaks out issue by issue as topics get reclassified from domestic to common.

I call the rate at which this happens the bleed rate. A plain treaty organization has a bleed rate near zero; a customs union has a moderate one; a federation is just what you get when the bleed rate has stayed high for long enough. The strong version: you sort international organizations more truthfully by bleed rate than by their founding documents, and a confederation is just a customs union with a high bleed rate plus some of the political machinery to cope with it.

Example: a country joins a customs union thinking it only gave up tariff-setting. Ten years later it's arguing about whether its corporate tax breaks are an illegal subsidy, because they shift trade exactly the way tariffs would. No treaty was renegotiated; the union's reach grew anyway. That growth, I say, is the bleed.

I know integration theory already has an idea called spillover — roughly, that cooperation in one sector keeps pulling in neighboring sectors — so maybe Ive just reinvented that with a new label, or maybe something dumber. Start by steelmanning the idea and saying what, if anything, it gets right. Then tell me: is it coherent? Is it distinct from spillover, or just spillover with a measurement gimmick? And where does it break — what does it get wrong about sovereignty or membership?
