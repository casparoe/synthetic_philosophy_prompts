I lead a small research group — three or four people, mostly philosophers and decision theorists, with about two years of runway — and I am weighing whether to spend that time on the conceptual foundations of pre-AGI bargains: agreements among AI developers, states, or other actors, negotiated before transformative artificial general intelligence exists, that are intended to shape or constrain what happens once it does. The bargains could take several forms — reciprocal verified limits on training or deployment, escrow of models or weights, advance agreement on how access or surplus would be shared after a capability threshold is crossed, or precommitments to build particular constitutional constraints into an AGI's objectives. The group cannot do empirical AI research, and whatever we produce should be usable by people actually trying to design or evaluate such bargains, not just by other academics.

The conceptual difficulty that draws me to the topic is that a pre-AGI bargain must bind parties who will later be enormously powerful, or able to create enormously powerful systems, while whatever enforces the bargain after AGI exists must itself have been put in place before AGI exists. That makes a cluster of questions live: what could make such agreements credible when the parties' future capabilities and preferences are asymmetric and partly unknown; whether the most upstream problem is enforcement, verification, or the content of the deal itself; how far mechanisms designed to bind sovereign states or firms transfer to actors whose later selves cannot be assumed to honor the bargain; and whether certain bargains are coherent only if the parties already agree on what post-AGI outcomes they value.

What I would like is a defended agenda, not a survey. What are the main open conceptual questions in this area, and which three or four should the group prioritize, and in what order? For each question you select, give the reasoning: why it is upstream of the others rather than downstream of them; how tractable it is with the tools we actually have — mechanism design, decision theory, bargaining theory, political philosophy — as opposed to tools we would need to acquire; how much it has already been worked over elsewhere and therefore how neglected it is for us; what a successful two-year project would concretely deliver; and what evidence or developments over the next couple of years should move it up or down the list. Finally, name at least one question that looks central from the outside but that we should explicitly deprioritize, because settling it would not change whether or how anyone tries to strike a deal.
