I keep noticing a move in arguments about big theories, and I'd like to know whether it has a name — and more importantly, where it ends.

The shape is this. Person A is defending a theory. Person B brings a counterexample from a pretty far-out thought experiment: 'your theory says X in this case, but X is clearly wrong.' Person A replies that our intuitions about far-out cases just aren't reliable, since they were built for ordinary situations, so an exotic case shouldn't count against a theory that handles normal cases well. Fine. But then, often in the same conversation, person A turns around and points to a different far-out thought experiment where the theory gives the answer that feels right, and treats that as a real point in the theory's favor. The rule about exotic intuitions being unreliable only ever gets aimed at intuitions that cause trouble.

Two instances I've run into. One was about mind uploading: someone dismissed the intuition that you'd survive a destructive scan-and-copy by saying it's an exotic case where intuition shouldn't be trusted, then a few minutes later leaned on the intuition that you'd survive gradual neuron replacement — equally exotic — as support. The other was in decision theory: a person waved off a counterexample about a near-perfect predictor by saying our intuitions misfire at extreme accuracies, and then used a different exotic predictor scenario to motivate their own view. In both cases I couldn't find any stated reason why the first intuition was untrustworthy but the second worth invoking.

So: is there an established concept for exactly this pattern — not confirmation bias in general, not plain cherry-picking, but the specific move of applying a suspicion-of-intuition standard only to intuitions that cut against your theory? If there is a recognized concept, how exactly does it differ from special pleading or cherry-picking or confirmation bias? If there isn't one that fits cleanly, what would you name it, and how would you define it so that it includes the cases above but excludes a case where someone gives a principled, stated reason for treating one intuition as more reliable than the other? I'm not just after a label; I want a precise characterization of where the pattern starts and stops. And please say what would change your mind about how you classify it or draw its boundaries — for instance, if the person does offer a reason, does the phenomenon disappear, or does the reason itself have to meet some further standard?
