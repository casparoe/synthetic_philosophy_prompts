A friend and I spent most of last evening going in circles about whether introspection is a reliable source of knowledge about one's own mind, and I came away convinced the debate is stuck in a way that isn't just about the two of us being stubborn. He takes what I'll call the deflationary view: introspection is a loose, error-prone process of constructing stories about ourselves, and the classic experiments in which people confidently give false explanations for their choices and judgments, plus the sampling studies showing how little of their own moment-to-moment inner experience people notice, are enough to strip first-person reports of any special authority. I take the other side: those studies show that people are bad at explaining the causes of their behavior and that ordinary attention is limited, but they don't touch the kind of direct knowledge I seem to have of what I am currently thinking, feeling, or deciding, which still enjoys a first-person privilege that third-person guesses about me lack.

What bothers me is that after three hours we hadn't moved. Every time I gave a case — noticing that I'm anxious while calmly asking myself whether it's really anxiety — he redescribed it as inference from bodily cues and context rather than direct awareness. Every time he gave a case — someone confidently offering a false explanation for a choice — I granted it and said it was a failure of causal explanation, not of knowing one's current state. Part of the trouble was that he kept treating every report as a hypothesis about an inner object, whereas I sometimes wanted to say that knowing what I believe is often just reading it off the world in front of me — which he then said was giving up on introspection rather than defending it. We even agreed on a fair amount: that we confabulate about causes, that we can be wrong about our own traits and long-term motives, and that there is a difference between knowing you are in pain and knowing why you are in pain. But we still could not say what the actual disagreement is. Is one of us making a hidden empirical prediction — for example, about whether there are mental states whose presence can be known without inference? Is one of us holding the other to a standard of reliability the other never had to meet? Are we using introspection to pick out two different things, one a faculty and the other a social practice of avowing states? Or have we simply hit bedrock intuitions about how much of the mind's own operation the mind can observe?

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, engaging the actual moves each side makes, and an honest verdict on what, if anything, would count as progress: a distinction, a kind of experiment, a better-posed question. Please be specific about where the real fault line lies, and be willing to say if you think part of the dispute is merely verbal.
