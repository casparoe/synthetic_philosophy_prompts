Ok so a friend showed me a cleaned-up version of Gödel's modal ontological argument and I can't shake the sense that it proves way too much. Here's the reconstruction we came up with:

(1) A property is positive just in case its negation is not positive (love and hate can't both be positive).
(2) If a positive property entails another property, the entailed property is positive too (if being just entails being at least sometimes fair, and justice is positive, then being at least sometimes fair is positive).
(3) God-likeness — having all and only the positive properties essentially — is positive.
(4) Necessary existence is positive (depending on nothing else for your existence is a perfection).
(5) In S5, if it's possible that something necessarily exists, then something necessarily exists.
(6) God-likeness entails necessary existence (from (3) and (4)), so a God-like being, if possible, exists necessarily.

Now here's the problem: nothing in (1), (2), or (5) cares what gets called positive. So define positive* the way a demonic cult would: cruelty is positive*, benevolence is its negation, domination is positive*, and so on. Run the same argument with positive* and, since the cult takes those properties to be possible, you seem to get a necessarily existing maximally cruel being. I cant accept that conclusion, so something must have gone wrong. But the S5 step and (1)-(2) look like pure logic to me, and the only places real content sneaks in are (3) and (4), which are just claims that something happens to count as positive. Thats where I'd point if I had to. So: where does the argument actually go wrong, and what does rejecting whichever premise cost? I'd rather keep (5) and (1), and I'd give up (3) before (4).
