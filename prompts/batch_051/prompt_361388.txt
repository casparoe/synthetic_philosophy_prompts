I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I am trying to get clear on a point in Scanlonian contractualism that strikes me as almost contradictory. The view is standardly described as anti-aggregative: one may not justify failing to aid, harming, or sacrificing a person by appealing to the summed or aggregated benefits, preferences, or claims of many other people. Yet in the familiar rescue cases, Scanlon seems to hold that one should save five people rather than one. I do not see how this conclusion is compatible with the anti-aggregative stance. If the five claims cannot be aggregated into a stronger combined claim, why does the presence of five equal claims license saving the five? And if the numbers do make a difference, is that not simply aggregation under a different description? Presumably I am misreading something, so I would like an explanation of the structural distinction between the individualist or pairwise reasoning Scanlon permits and the aggregative reasoning he rejects, and of how that distinction yields the conclusion that the five should be saved. Please also indicate how confident you are in the explanation and single out the point you are least sure about.
