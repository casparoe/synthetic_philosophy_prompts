I chair the compensation review committee at a unionized private college. Last month the administration, acting on a new transparency policy, published the full salary schedules for all employees, and the fallout has reached our committee as a formal charge. Long-serving associate professors and senior staff are describing their situations in overtly moral language: their pay has risen only by cost-of-living adjustments for a decade, while a small number of recent external hires in a few units were brought in at two to three times the campus median. The clearest anomalies do not align with the college's own role classification system, and some internal comparators for those positions earn nearly 60 percent less.

The chief financial officer circulated a memo arguing that the resulting sentiment is mostly envy — hostile displeasure at another's good — that it is a destructive personal attitude, and that the remedy should be communications, counseling resources, and no reopening of individual salaries. A group of faculty senators replied that the feeling is not envy but resentment: anger at a perceived injury, namely that the structure rewards departure and negotiation leverage over sustained contribution, and that it injures the self-respect of those below the median. A third faction on our committee accepts that much of the feeling is envy, but holds that envy is excusable when it is a response to the loss of self-respect produced by institutional arrangements — which would shift the question from the character of the employees to the justice of the pay structure itself.

Our written compensation principles commit us to four things that now appear to conflict: (1) pay enough to hire in the national market; (2) maintain internal equity across comparable roles; (3) be transparent about pay; and (4) preserve the dignity and self-respect of every employee.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame — reopen the anomalous salaries now, compress the structure over a multi-year plan, treat the resentment as an attitude problem to be managed, or some fourth option you specify — and it should state criteria for distinguishing pay differences the college should correct from those it should leave alone. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it, and do not simply restate the four principles: say how they should be ordered or reconciled in this case. The memo should be written in American English.
