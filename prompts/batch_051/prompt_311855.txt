Each year, a public agency decides which treatments my country's health system will fund. On paper, its method is arithmetic: it calculates the cost per quality-adjusted life-year, or QALY, each treatment is expected to produce. A QALY turns a year spent in less than full health into a fraction of a year spent in full health, so a drug that keeps someone alive in a state scored 0.6 for ten years yields six QALYs, and those six QALYs count the same no matter whose life produces them. The agency covers treatments that fall below a cost-per-QALY threshold and rejects many that fall above it. On its face, this looks like a practice committed to commensurability: health states, treatments, and the gains of keeping people alive all go on one numerical scale and are traded against money.

Yet the same agency makes exceptions that seem to repudiate the arithmetic. It funds very expensive drugs for a small number of identifiable and severely ill patients even when the cost-per-QALY calculation says the resources would produce more health elsewhere, on the rationale that a known person in immediate need should be rescued. It funds services that add almost no QALYs, such as palliative care for patients near death, because those services are regarded as owed regardless of the sum. It rarely applies the QALY arithmetic outside the health sector when deciding about road safety, environmental protection, or disaster relief. And its own staff often insist that the threshold is not a ranking of values at all, merely a pragmatic instrument for managing a fixed budget.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of value that makes the best sense of this patchwork. In particular: does the routine use of QALYs strictly presuppose that health states and lives are commensurable, or could the system be run this way even on the view that some values resist a common scale? Does the rescue exception presuppose incommensurability, or is it only a political concession compatible with a fully commensurable picture? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely permits, say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what a health system would concretely have to change if it accepted that the value of health and the value of a person are genuinely incommensurable.
