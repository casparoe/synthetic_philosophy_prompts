The debate over chain-of-thought faithfulness seems to have reached a standstill. One camp argues that a model's step-by-step rationale should be treated as a report of the reasoning that produced the answer: after all, intervening on the stated steps often changes the output, and models can sometimes be trained so that their stated reasoning tracks their behavior more closely. The other camp treats the rationale as a post hoc narrative constructed alongside the answer: models whose inputs encode a biasing feature will answer in line with that feature while their rationales cite only innocuous considerations, and one can improve the stated justification while leaving the behavior largely unchanged. Both sides have evidence, and neither has moved. My working hypothesis is that the impasse comes from the word reasoning being asked to do two jobs at once—a causal-psychological account of how the answer was computed, and a normative account of what would justify the answer to a reader—and that chain-of-thought can be one without being the other. Is this the right diagnosis of the disagreement? If not, what kind of disagreement is it: a hidden empirical question, a difference over standards of evidence, a verbal dispute, or something else? And given the correct diagnosis, what would actually count as progress?
