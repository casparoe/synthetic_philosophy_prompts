I’ve been rereading Alan Turing’s 1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* for a course on the history of AI, and I keep getting stuck on a sentence I had never noticed before. In section 2, while defending the imitation game, Turing writes: “The question and answer method seems to be suitable for introducing almost any one of the fields of human endeavour that we wish to include.” He then adds that the game should not penalize a machine for failing to shine in beauty competitions, or a human for losing to an aeroplane.

I want to apply that sentence, as he wrote it, to a case he could not have had in mind. Suppose a contemporary frontier model is evaluated not by its physical abilities or its subjective experience, which no one knows how to test, but by a long-form natural-language examination in a field such as bioweapons risk assessment. It answers detailed technical and strategic questions at least as well as qualified human experts, and it does so consistently across thousands of adversarial prompts. But the model has no independent goals, no sense of stakes, and no capacity to refuse a line of questioning except in response to its training. On Turing’s stated view, is the question-and-answer method still “suitable” for deciding whether this system has entered the field of bioweapons risk assessment? Or does the modern test draw the boundary differently, because the machine is being used as an instrument rather than a respondent? I’m not asking whether Turing would have supported AI-safety policy; I’m asking what his text, read strictly, commits him to about whether such a system should count as a participant in the field. Please say how confident you are in your central claims and identify the one interpretive step you are least sure of.
