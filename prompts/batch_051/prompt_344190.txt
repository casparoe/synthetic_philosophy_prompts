Here's a mechanism I've been turning over, and I want someone to pick it apart without just telling me whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Take a state with an ordinary citizen-initiative process, like California's, and assume initiatives can only create statutes, not constitutional amendments. Now add this rule: any law passed by initiative automatically goes back on the ballot for a straight yes/no retention vote every six years. If it gets 50%+1 it lives another six years; if it fails it's repealed immediately, and no substantially similar measure can qualify for the next two election cycles. Between retention votes, the legislature can't amend it, except by a two-thirds vote plus a formal declaration of emergency. The ballot pamphlet for each retention election is drafted jointly by the original yes and no campaigns, and an independent citizens' fact-check panel can strike any claim it finds factually false by a supermajority. What would this actually do in practice? I'm interested in the mechanics, not the constitutional law: who starts putting things on the ballot once every proposal has a scheduled death date, how agencies behave when a law they administer might lapse, how the legislature routes around the amendment ban, what happens to the fact-check panel over time, whether low-turnout retention elections get captured by motivated groups, and whether the whole thing just becomes a permanent campaign with extra steps. Which failure modes are fatal and which can be patched without gutting the basic setup?
