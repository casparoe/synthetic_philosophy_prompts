I teach a one-week policy summer school for about 24 undergrads (mostly PPE and econ), and I keep failing to get one thing across. In the mornings we do cases: food deserts, ER overcrowding, housing. In the afternoons they design interventions. Every single group, even after I've explained "systems thinking vs. tinkering at the margins," defaults to adding another staff member or tweaking an eligibility threshold or increasing a subsidy by 5%. They can all repeat the slogan afterwards, but it doesn't stick. So I want to stop lecturing and run a two-hour session that makes them feel the difference between an intervention that relieves pressure inside a system and one that changes the system's structure — and why the first kind so often gets undone by the system's own feedback.

Please design a runnable activity for the 24 students, 120 minutes, no reading beforehand and no framing lecture up front. By the end they should come away seeing three things: well-intentioned marginal fixes tend to get "eaten" by balancing feedback (things look better for a round or two, then revert); structural changes (a rule, a goal, an information flow, a delay) are slower and less visable but move where the whole system settles; and leverage depends on where you intervene, not how much effort or money you spend. The mechanic is up to you — a card game, a spreadhseet sim, a role-play of a city department's weekly budget meeting — but it should involve actual choices under time pressure and some visible score or trend line. Give me the full setup: materials, setup time, rules, round structure, a payoff or score scheme that doesn't need real money, facilitation notes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto stocks/flows, feedback loops, delays, and leverage. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the real idea the activity can't reproduce and I'd have to supply myself.
