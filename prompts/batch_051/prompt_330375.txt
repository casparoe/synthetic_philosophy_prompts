Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether eternal conscious torment could ever be a proportionate punishment for wrongdoing, and we kept talking past each other because neither of us had fixed what "proportionate" was doing in the sentence. I want to repair that before we resume. Give me a working definition of proportionate punishment — a definition of the relation "P is a proportionate punishment for offense O" — that I can carry back into the argument about hell.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle, with a definite verdict on each:

(1) A shoplifter steals a $10 item and is fined $10 plus a day of community service.
(2) A person who systematically abused a child for years receives the same fine and the same day of community service.
(3) Two employees embezzle the same amount from the same firm; one is a first offender, the other has a decade of similar convictions.
(4) Two drivers text through the same intersection at the same speed; one hits and kills a pedestrian, while the other is stopped by a red-light camera a few feet before the crossing. Their conduct is identical, but only one has a death on his hands.
(5) A man commits a serious assault, then genuinely repents, makes full restitution, and twenty years later is, by any ordinary measure, a different person. Should what he deserves at trial have changed?
(6) A woman fires a gun at her neighbor intending to kill him, but the shot lodges in a wall; the same day, a man texting while driving swerves and kills a pedestrian. I want the definition to say who deserves the heavier punishment, and whether that answer is a fact about desert or merely about the law's need for deterrence.
(7) The case that began the argument: a finite person commits an act of rebellion or contempt directed at a being of infinite worth. Some say the offense thereby has infinite gravity, so that endless torment is exactly proportional. I cannot tell whether this is a hard case or a definitional mistake, and that is what I most want the definition to settle.

I am confident that (1) is proportionate and that (2) is not. I lean toward saying the two drivers in (4) deserve the same punishment, though I also think the law may reasonably punish them differently; I want the definition to tell me whether that gap between desert and justifiable punishment is real. On (3), (5), and (7) I have no settled view. You may find it necessary to distinguish proportionality in amount from proportionality in kind — for instance, to ask whether a punishment's length is even the right currency for some offenses — or to distinguish desert from what the state may permissibly impose. Do not settle it by appeal to authority. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, say where it gives an implausible answer or no answer, and revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Then say what the resulting definition implies for the original disagreement: does it place eternal conscious torment on the same scale as finite punishments at all, and where does it draw the line among eternal conscious torment, annihilation (the wicked being destroyed rather than kept in torment), and the view that no one remains punished forever?
