Consider the claim that the relation "x is better than y" is transitive: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. This is easy to treat as a merely conceptual or normative principle, not something an experiment could bear on. But transitivity also functions as a descriptive hypothesis about evaluative comparison across a series of pairwise judgments, and spectrum arguments put that hypothesis under pressure. Take a long chain of options A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ in which each step trades a small loss on one dimension for a slightly larger gain on another, so that people judging adjacent pairs in isolation regard each successor as better than its predecessor, while also regarding Aₙ as clearly worse than A₁. If those pairwise judgments all report one underlying "better than" relation, the relation is intransitive; if the relation is transitive, at least one adjacent judgment must be an error. Please design the strongest concrete test you can of whether such apparent intransitivities are real features of evaluative comparison or artifacts — for example, of presentation order, vague thresholds, or insufficient incentives. Specify the manipulation or measurement, the result that would support transitivity by explaining the cycles away, the result that would support genuine intransitivity, and then say exactly what each outcome would and would not settle, especially whether any descriptive finding can establish or refute the normative principle that rational betterness rankings must be transitive.
