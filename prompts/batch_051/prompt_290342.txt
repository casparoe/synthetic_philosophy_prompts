I'm a new attorney at a tenants' rights clinic, and I've been thinking about a case in which our client completed an online lease through a property-management portal. The portal displayed a "lease addendum" only after she had already clicked through several screens, and the management company now says her click on "I agree" was indistinguishable, as a legal matter, from a handwritten signature. I went back to David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section V, "Of the Obligation of Promises," and found this passage:

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

Our reading group has built an argument from this that seems to prove both too much and too little. Reconstructed:

(1) If the obligation of a promise cannot consist in an inner act of mind, then what gives a promise its force is a public convention that attaches normative consequences to a recognized outward act.
(2) Clicking "I agree" is a recognized outward act within the existing convention of online contracting.
(3) Therefore clicking "I agree" can create contractual obligation even if the clicker did not read, understand, or consciously assent to the terms.
(4) By parity, any outward act that the relevant convention marks as acceptance—clicking, scrolling, continuing to browse, checking a box, or perhaps anything else the platform designates—could in principle create the same obligation.
(5) But if contractual obligation can arise without any inner act of assent or even awareness of the terms, then the distinction between "agreeing" and merely being subjected to a privately imposed normative scheme becomes hard to sustain.
(6) So either online clickthrough is agreement in the fullest sense, or no promise—signed, spoken, or clicked—is ever more than a convention-imposed liability. Both alternatives seem unacceptable: the first licenses obviously exploitative adhesion contracts, and the second makes the very idea of contractual autonomy disappear.

I want to know where this argument goes wrong, or which premise I should abandon. If the flaw is in premise (1), I need to see why Hume's conventionalism about promising does not extend the way our reconstruction says it does. If the flaw is later, I need to know what distinguishes a click on "I agree" from a wet signature or an oral promise, without reducing my client's objection to mere paternalism. Please diagnose the argument step by step, and explain what the main positions—voluntarist, conventionalist, and more doctrinal accounts of contract—each have to give up to block the conclusion.
