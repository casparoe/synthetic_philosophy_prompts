I've been trying to systematize my intuitive verdicts about what grounds what, where 'grounds' is the non-causal "in virtue of" relation — what it is in reality that makes a fact obtain. My verdicts:

1. The fact that the ball is red and round is grounded in the fact that it is red, together with the fact that it is round.
2. The fact that the ball is red or round is grounded in the fact that it is red, when the ball is red.
3. The fact that something is red is grounded in the fact that this particular ball is red.
4. The fact that the cloth is red is grounded in the fact that it is scarlet.
5. The fact that a table exists here is grounded in the fact that certain particles are arranged table-wise.
6. The fact that it is not the case that the ball is both red and green is not, I think, grounded in the fact that the ball is red — and I can't comfortably name anything else it is grounded in. Yet I also don't want negative facts to be fundamental.

I also feel the pull of three general principles:

- If a fact has grounds, it is nothing over and above those grounds.
- Grounding chains must terminate: we eventually reach facts that are not grounded in anything further.
- When a fact is grounded, its obtaining is thereby fully accounted for, with no explanatory residue left over.

These don't all sit comfortably together. I'm uneasy saying the disjunctive fact in (2) is nothing over and above its true disjunct, even though I judged it grounded. And my negative-fact verdict seems to leave negative facts neither grounded nor happily basic.

Is there a single coherent account of grounding that delivers these verdicts and respects the principles? Which verdict or principle should I abandon? And what does the account you favor say about a case I keep going back and forth on: if sets exist, is the fact that {Socrates} exists grounded in the fact that Socrates exists, or is it fundamental, or is neither answer right?
