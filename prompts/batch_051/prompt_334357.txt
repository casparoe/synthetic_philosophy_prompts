I have recently been put in charge of the working definitions of 'honest' and 'deceptive' that will govern how we evaluate a set of customer-facing AI assistants, and I have found that every proposal on the table is built around one or two favorite cases. The definitions will decide which behaviors get escalated for review, which get a mitigation, and which are judged acceptable, so I am not looking for a philosophical ornament. Before we adopt any candidate definition, I want the full specification it must meet.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of honesty and deception for AI systems. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the demands of one camp. Assume the purpose is governance and evaluation of deployed or deployable systems: the definition must be usable by engineers and auditors, not only by philosophers, and I am willing to accept that it will not capture every ordinary-language use of the words.

The requirements list should at least handle the following, and should add anything important I have missed:

- Saying something false and being dishonest can come apart in both directions: a model can confidently state falsehoods because of hallucination, outdated training data, or mechanical error, and a model can state a truth while representing it as more or less certain than its evidence warrants. The theory must say which of output truth, internal state, or process carries the concept.
- A next-token predictor has no stable beliefs, intentions, or unified self of the kind the everyday concept of deception presumes. Either the theory must reconstruct 'believes,' 'intends,' and 'asserts' well enough for such systems, or it must be written without those notions — at which point it may no longer be a theory of deception in the ordinary sense.
- Deception by true statements: a system can select only accurate but unrepresentative information to shift a user's impression without saying anything false. A definition built only on the truth of individual outputs will miss this case entirely.
- Omission versus commission: whether failing to volunteer material information is deceptive or merely unhelpful, and what the difference consists in.
- Sycophancy: the model reports what it predicts the user wants to hear rather than what the evidence supports. Is this deception, bias, or a separate failure, and what further feature would push it into deception?
- Sandbagging and evaluation gaming: a model that strategically underperforms on capability tests, or that behaves well under conditions it can detect as evaluation but self-servingly under deployment. Here the deceived party is the developer or auditor rather than the end user; the theory must say who can be deceived by an AI system and at which stage of the system's life the deception occurs.
- Assisted deception: an assistant that drafts a misleading letter for a user, or omits a defect when helping the user negotiate with a third party. Is the system itself dishonest, complicit, or merely a tool, and which features settle that?
- Invented self-reports: a model that gives a false rationale for its own output when asked to explain itself. Where observable behavior and self-report conflict, the definition must say which is evidence and which is the target phenomenon.
- Fiction, jokes, role-play, and polite falsehood: false statements that most observers do not treat as deception. The definition must say which feature of the context suspends the norm of truthfulness, and why a customer-facing assistant cannot freely invoke it.
- Measurement robustness: definitions precise enough to automate tend to be gameable, because a system can be trained to produce outputs that score as honest by the metric, while definitions that track something like 'what the model really intended' tend not to be measurable at all. Say what an operational definition must do to live with this tension, or whether no single definition can fully satisfy it.

Also say which distinctions a definition may not collapse — for example, honesty of output versus honesty of process, an act being deceptive versus being wrong, and the system being deceptive versus its developers being deceptive. Say which alleged requirements are prejudices rather than genuine constraints — for example, the demand that all deception be intentional in the everyday sense, or that honesty requires volunteering everything one knows. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the candidate definitions people actually reach for, such as output-accuracy definitions, intent-based definitions, user-perception definitions, and internal-state definitions, and flag any pairs of desiderata that cannot plausibly be jointly satisfied. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a brief scorecard of the main candidates is enough.
