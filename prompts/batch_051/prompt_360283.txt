Design a 90-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in bioethics and health policy (16 students) that makes participants feel, before it is named, why the choice of a maximizing rule in allocating scarce medical resources — expected lives saved, expected life-years saved, or expected quality-adjusted life-years saved — is a substantive moral choice rather than a technical detail. The central idea should emerge from what the groups do: each group should be able to defend its allocation as the straightforward application of a reasonable rule, yet the groups' selections should visibly clash, forcing the question of which rule was the right one to count as "saving the most."

The activity should center on a scarce-resource decision with small teams acting as decision-making committees. Use a deck of 12–16 patient cards, each listing a patient ID, age, a one-line clinical situation, three actuarial values — expected additional life-years with the intervention, a health-related quality-of-life weight on a 0–1 scale, and probability of surviving to those additional years — and, on the back, three precomputed scores: survival probability alone (the "lives" score), probability times additional life-years (the "life-years" score), and probability times additional life-years times quality weight (the "QALYs" score). Construct the deck so the top-ranked patients genuinely diverge across the three scores. Include four anchor cards that guarantee one pivot: an older patient with the best survival probability but few remaining years, a young patient with a lower survival probability but many high-quality remaining years, another young patient with different survival or quality numbers, and a middle-aged patient with more remaining years but a low quality weight. These anchors should ensure the lives rule favors the older patient, the life-years rule favors the young, and the QALY rule reveals the distinctive effect of the quality weight. Adjust the remaining cards to sharpen or soften the contrasts as needed.

Assign four committees of four students each. Committees 1–3 are each given one of the three maximands and may choose only by maximizing it; Committee 4 is told to decide by their own lights and to write down their reasoning. Each committee gets the same deck and must select the six patients who receive the single scarce intervention. In a second round, rotate the three maximands among Committees 1–3 and let Committee 4 revisit its choice after hearing the first-round reports, so that at least some groups see a rule different from their own reverse their earlier selection. Do not announce in advance that the rules will diverge; let the divergence arrive as a surprise during the side-by-side posting of selections.

The deliverable should provide a timeline of roughly 90 minutes: setup, first-round deliberation, side-by-side reporting, the second round, and a debrief of at least 25 minutes. In the debrief, map specific posted choices to the structure of the idea: the lives rule treats each expected person as equal and tends to favor the highest survival probabilities; the life-years rule treats each expected year as equal and tends to favor the young with the longest remaining lifespan; the QALY rule treats each quality-adjusted expected year as equal and adds a further valuation that can deprioritize patients with disabilities. Then connect the observed divergence to the practical difference between a value-of-a-statistical-life logic, which sums small survival chances, and a QALY-based cost-effectiveness logic, which sums quality- and duration-weighted benefits, noting that both can be presented as "maximizing health" while selecting different patients or programs.

Also specify what will predictably go wrong and how to handle it. In particular: some group will miscompute or misrank the scores, so pre-sort the deck and give the facilitator a master table; some group may refuse to use the QALY rule or may rebel against its implied ranking, and the facilitator should not override the refusal but keep it visible as evidence in the debrief, since that refusal is itself part of the phenomenon; and the divergence may fail to materialize unless the deck is test-run in advance with a small group. Finally, state candidly which feature of the underlying issue the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot recreate real mortality stakes, institutional pressure, legal liability, or grief; it treats probabilities, life expectancies, and quality weights as precise point values when in practice they are uncertain, time-discounted, and contested, including the disability critique of quality weights; and because the patients are identifiable cards rather than statistical lives, the activity cannot on its own reproduce the identified-versus-statistical-lives asymmetry, which the facilitator must supply explicitly.
