I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of AI, and I need help turning a recent argument into a short research proposal. Last weekend I spent a long evening arguing with a friend of mine who does machine learning. His view was that the value-alignment problem is, underneath everything else, an aggregation problem: gather enough people's preferences, reconstruct their utility functions, and aim the system at a suitably weighted sum, with the genuinely hard questions being mostly empirical and technical. I kept pushing back that a public of people who reasonably and persistently disagree about what makes a life go well is not just a collection of preferences to be summed. Part of the point of liberal political institutions, I said, is that they do not take sides among those contested visions of the good, and an AI system that purports to serve everyone may need something like that kind of neutrality rather than a maximizing rule. By the end he had talked me into one concession: the liberal-neutrality idea sounds right in the abstract but is frustratingly underspecified, and unless I can state exactly what it rules in and rules out as an alignment target, it is indistinguishable from a slogan.

That is the half-formed idea I want to turn into a proposal for a six-month doctoral fellowship in the philosophy of technology. The central question would be roughly this: for an AI system that serves a pluralistic public, is liberal neutrality a genuine alternative to value aggregation, or is it at best a set of constraints on aggregation that are themselves just as contested as the values they are meant to bracket? I would like the proposal to state the question precisely; to explain why the standard reliance on aggregating preferences or values does not settle it, and why standard formulations of neutrality do not settle it either (including whether the relevant conflict is among preferences, which aggregation tools can represent, or among values that may be incommensurable, which those tools represent poorly); and to describe how a philosophical rather than empirical or technical project can make progress. Please also include a concrete account of what a useful deliverable would look like, what would count as failure after six months, and what I would say to a skeptical reviewer who asks why a funder should pay for conceptual work here rather than for more investigation of bias or reward modeling. Please refer to positions and arguments by their content rather than by citation; I will add references myself.

At the end, add a short note saying how confident you are in the proposal's characterization of the positions it mentions, and single out the point you are least sure of. I would like the whole thing in American English.
