I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I will put this in terms of estimates and base rates. A chain of reasoning I began using for project planning now seems to me to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the faulty step.

(1) For project forecasts, the outside view — the base rate for a suitable reference class of similar projects — is reliably more accurate than the inside view, the detailed account of why this particular project will go well.
(2) The outside view wins because first-person reasoning about one's own case is systematically biased: the inside view draws its confidence from details that do not improve the forecast.
(3) A general principle of epistemic modesty is therefore warranted: when my own reasoning conflicts with a suitable outside view, I should defer to the outside view.
(4) But my acceptance of (3) is itself the product of first-person reasoning about my own case, since I have worked through the argument and found it convincing.
(5) By (3), I should therefore defer to the outside view that concerns people who work through general epistemic arguments and find them convincing, and the relevant base rate there is, to put it mildly, not encouraging.
(6) So the principle either exempts its own endorsement from its own standard, or it tells me to expect itself to be unreliable; either way, it seems to prove too much to be acceptable.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) in some form, because they have made my estimates markedly better, but I cannot see how to do so without arriving at (6).
