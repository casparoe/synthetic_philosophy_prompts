Since I retired from experimental physics, I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mind, and last month I sat in on a seminar that ended in a long, unresolved argument about what Benjamin Libet's experiments actually show about free will. The two participants — a neurophysiologist who works on motor control and a philosopher who thinks about agency — kept arguing after the session ended. I took notes and have reconstructed their exchange as faithfully as I can below, though I may have compressed some turns. I was a bystander, not a party to the dispute, and I honestly cannot tell who got the better of it. I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a verdict on free will itself.

The neurophysiologist began roughly this way. The classic Libet result, he said, is a timing gap: the readiness potential begins to build on the order of half a second before a self-initiated movement, while the subject reports the conscious intention to move only about a fifth of a second before the movement. The brain has therefore already begun the act before the subject is aware of deciding. Whatever conscious will is doing, it is not initiating the movement. The natural conclusion is that the felt decision is a late report of a process the brain set in motion earlier.

The philosopher replied that this inference is too quick. First, the readiness potential is a slowly rising signal, and choosing its onset is a technical matter: much of the early phase may be preparation or spontaneous fluctuation rather than anything we should call a decision. Second, the reported time of conscious intention comes from subjects watching a rotating spot and reporting when they first felt the urge; that is a retrospective judgment about a private event, not a direct measurement of when the intention occurred. Third, Libet's veto experiments complicate the picture: when subjects prepared to move but then refrained, the readiness potential had already begun, yet the act was cancelled, which suggests consciousness is not merely trailing behind as an epiphenomenon.

The neurophysiologist granted the veto point but said it does not recover free initiation. A veto is itself preceded by neural processes, so the conscious veto is just another event in the causal chain. At most the experiments leave room for a purely negative power of suppression, a long way from the ordinary idea of free will. Libet's own position, he added, ended up close to that restricted conclusion: a capacity to withhold rather than to initiate.

The philosopher answered that this is where the equivocation occurs. The experiment measures the timing of one report about one highly constrained movement; it does not show that the brain decided in the everyday sense in which deciding involves weighing reasons and settling on a course of action. Neural preparation is not a decision, and calling it one imports the conclusion. That every mental event has neural antecedents follows from our being embodied agents, not from a discovery that the conscious self is bypassed. If the worry is determinism, Libet adds nothing; if the worry is whether conscious intentions are causes, this experiment does not test that.

The neurophysiologist then sharpened his claim. The empirical finding, he said, survives that philosophical point. Even if we refuse to call the early activity a decision, the felt experience of deciding to move now corresponds to a stage in an already unfolding neural process, and that is a real constraint on any theory of agency. The modeling that reinterprets the readiness potential as the drift of spontaneous activity toward a threshold does not restore conscious initiation: on that model the movement is still triggered by a neural threshold crossing, and the conscious intention remains an event within the process rather than the cause of its beginning.

The philosopher made a last move. That only lands, he said, if one assumes that for an intention to be a genuine cause it must be the first event in the chain — a philosophical demand rather than a neuroscientific result. The threshold model shows the averaged readiness potential may be an artifact of how trials are aligned to the movement, which undercuts the claim that the brain had already decided several hundred milliseconds before awareness. And the fact that the report of intention is late, if it is late, shows at most that introspection is a poor chronometer, not that the intention is causally inert. What the experiment supplies is timing data about one narrow kind of action; everything else is interpretation dressed up as an experimental result.

I would be grateful if you would go through this exchange in detail and say which of them is right about which specific points, where each overstates or slides between senses of a term, and whether there is a distinction both of them have missed that would resolve part of the dispute. If you think my reconstruction of the scientific results is inaccurate, or that the disagreement as I have framed it presupposes a confused idea of what Libet tested, I would much rather you say that plainly than try to answer on the assumptions I have supplied.
