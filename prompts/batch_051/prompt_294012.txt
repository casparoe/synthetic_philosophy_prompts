I teach a philosophy of probability seminar, and there is a distinction I keep gesturing at but cannot put a name to. Maybe you can help.

Consider three people who each say a claim is 70% likely. Person A has run a large, careful survey. Person B has one striking anecdote and feels good about it. Person C has not looked at anything relevant but is splitting the difference between two experts who disagree. The number is identical. Yet something about their confidence differs: one of them is in a better position to make that judgment than the others, and it seems like an epistemic difference, not just a personality trait. It is not the usual risk-versus-uncertainty point — I do not mean that some probabilities are frequencies and some are judgments. I mean that even when the judgment is the same, the amount, quality, and completeness of the evidential backing can vary independently of the judgment itself.

I half-remember Keynes saying something in this neighbourhood, but I cannot recall whether he gave this dimension its own term or whether it is tangled up with his other claim that not all probabilities can be given exact numbers. So three questions. First, is there an established concept for this dimension — the thickness or robustness of the evidence behind a given probability, as opposed to what the probability says about how the evidence tilts — and if so, whose concept is it and exactly how was it defined? Second, how does it differ from a second-order probability, a probability distribution over possible first-order probabilities, and why can the phenomenon not just be absorbed into the first-order number? Third, what are the boundary cases: does more evidence always increase this dimension, or only relevant, discriminating evidence, and what should we say when the evidence is abundant but flatly contradictory, so the balance stays at 50/50 but the backing is massive? If the established name does not quite fit the phenomenom I am describing, I would rather have a sharp definition and three boundary cases than a label.
