For a graduate seminar in moral psychology, write the specification that any adequate theory of the role of disgust and the sacred in moral judgment must meet. By a theory I mean an account of what, if anything, these responses contribute to moral cognition — whether they detect wrongs, constitute wrongs, license judgment, debunk judgment, or merely accompany it. The specification should be usable, as a seminar exercise, to see which families of theory survive honest scrutiny and where each one strains.

Produce the list of requirements, each with the motivating case, experimental finding, or conceptual pressure behind it. Do not merely list bullet points: give the consideration that forces the requirement. The cases and findings must include at least the following. Harmless taboo violations, where people judge actions wrong while insisting no one is harmed, and where the wrongness seems to inhere in the action's category rather than in its consequences. The moralization and demoralization of the same conduct across time and social groups, such as smoking or same-sex relations, where the same act moves between moralized and non-moralized status. Disgust amplification, where incidental disgust from an irrelevant source — a foul smell, a dirty room — makes moral judgments harsher. And the standing use of collective repugnance as a reason in public bioethical argument, where some parties treat widespread revulsion as itself evidence of wrongness and others treat it as a contaminating heuristic to be debunked.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable — a test any minimally adequate theory must pass — or whether it is a demand only one camp imposes on the dialectic. For example, the demand that a theory vindicate ordinary judgments of the sacred as tracking real normative facts is very likely a camp preference rather than a neutral requirement, as is the opposite demand that all purity-based judgment be explained away as malfunctioning pathogen-avoidance. Flag every requirement that smuggles in a verdict on whether these values are legitimate.

Then supply the part of the assignment I care most about: identify any subset of requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by making controversial commitments. Press hard on the tension between the occasional reliability of disgust — it sometimes tracks genuine contamination and danger — and its easy corruptibility, since the same response can be recruited by social learning toward targets unrelated to harm and is largely insensitive to harm-based evidence. If a theory must respect both the partial reliability and the easy corruptibility of disgust, say exactly what it is committed to and what it must give up. Finally, state which two requirements, taken together, do the most work excluding the existing families of theory, and explain why those two are the load-bearing ones. I do not want a scorecard of named theories; the deliverable is the list, the rationale for each entry, and the tension analysis.
