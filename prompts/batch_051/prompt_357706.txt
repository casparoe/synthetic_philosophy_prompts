I am a novelist at work on a book whose central relationship is between a woman and the advisory machine installed in her house. The machine is deliberately designed to judge and never to act. It takes in a running description of the protagonist's situation, forms verdicts about what she ought to do, and can display a brief message on the one screen to which it is connected. It has no effectors, no access to any person or device other than that screen, and a separate moderation layer can suppress a message before it appears. In a pivotal scene, a character argues that the machine is not the kind of thing one can sensibly resent, forgive, or hold responsible for its verdicts, because it is not a moral agent at all. I want to put a genuinely strong version of this argument in his mouth, not a straw man, but I suspect one of its steps quietly trades on an ambiguity in the phrase "acting on its judgments." Please tell me whether the following argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step does the work and how it fails.

(1) A being can rightly be praised or blamed for a judgment only if it is capable of acting on that judgment.
(2) This machine is not capable of acting on its judgments: it cannot alter the world, intervene in events, or carry out the conduct it judges right; its only output is a string of text, and even that string may be suppressed before it reaches anyone.
(3) Therefore the machine can never rightly be praised or blamed for any of its judgments.
(4) What can never rightly be praised or blamed for any of its judgments is not the kind of thing toward which resentment or gratitude can appropriately be directed.
(5) What is not the kind of thing toward which resentment or gratitude can appropriately be directed is not a moral agent.
(6) Therefore the machine is not a moral agent.

I need the precise diagnosis rather than a general verdict. If one of the premises is false as stated, give the strongest reading under which it is true and say whether the inference goes through on that reading. If the argument slips between two senses of "acting on a judgment," identify the step at which the slip occurs and describe the two senses. I would also like to know whether the conclusion would be true in the variant where the moderation layer suppresses every message, so that none of the machine's verdicts ever reaches the protagonist, and what that variant reveals about which step is load-bearing.
