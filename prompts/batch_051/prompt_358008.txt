I keep going back and forth on a thought experiment and would like you to help me work it through. Take the Ring of Gyges story: you get a ring that makes you invisible, and the challenge is whether you'd still act justly if you knew you'd never get caught. A friend who's into social psychology pushed back on this, saying that "never getting caught" isn't one psychological condition — it's a bundle of situational cues (anonymity, no eye contact, no audience, no immediate victim), and those cues don't necessarily travel together. So here's the version I've been stuck on. Suppose the ring doesn't make you invisible whenever you want. Instead, it only activates in situations with two features at once: you're surrounded by strangers, and the interaction is one-way (shoplifting from a big box store, taking credit for someone else's work online, dumping trash where nobody sees you). In face-to-face situations — someone asks you for help, a cashier hands back too much change, a neighbor's groceries spill — the ring stays dull and you're fully visible. And suppose the ring gives a faint warmth whenever its active, so you always know exactly which kind of situation you're in, and everyone with a ring has the same experience.

Now, how does this change the standard analysis? The usual virtue-ethics reading is that the ring tests whether a just person has a stable character trait that expresses itself across situations, while the unjust person is just waiting for an opportunity to get away with it. But in this version, "getting away with it" only exists inside one situation-type. Walk through what the virtue ethicist should say about the modified case, and then walk through what the situationist critique of virtue ethics would say — the view that behavior tracks situations more than global traits, and that cross-situational consistency for something like honesty is a lot weaker than we tend to assume. To make it concrete: imagine we run this modified ring on a thousand people across twenty different settings, and we find that their just or unjust behavior correlates highly within each situation-type but only weakly across types. Does that confirm the situationist, or does the virtue ethicist have a principled way to say the ring never tested the right thing in the first place? And does the thought experiment still work as a test of whether justice is worth having for its own sake, or has it quietly become a test of something more local? Try to give both positions their strongest form, and point out anywhere they might really just be talking past each other.
