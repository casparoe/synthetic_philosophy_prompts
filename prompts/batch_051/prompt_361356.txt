Most people regard a civilian who kills five strangers to seize their land as a murderer, yet regard the leaders of a state whose war predictably kills thousands of foreign soldiers and civilians to keep or take territory as military commanders rather than mass murderers. The only obvious difference is that one act is private and the other is official. Hegel offers a candidate justification for this asymmetry in the Philosophy of Right: the state is not a mere instrument of individual interests but the actuality of the ethical Idea, and war is the moment in which the state's particularity is asserted and its citizens are raised above attachment to private property and finite life. Give the strongest Hegelian defense of treating state-organized killing as fundamentally different from private killing, then test it against an aggressive war of conquest by a powerful state. Does the defense survive that test, or is the asymmetry merely a prejudice in favor of organized power? Answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
