I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I build systems where individual consumer choices—purchases, searches, flights—are logged, featurized, and used to update demand forecasts and recommendation models. I have a question about whether a standard philosophical argument about individual moral responsibility survives a change in the way individual choices actually produce harm.

The argument is the inefficacy objection, applied to consumer-level harms such as buying factory-farmed meat or flying. As I understand it, the objection runs as follows: no particular animal is killed and no additional tonne of CO2 is emitted because of my single purchase or flight. Production and emissions—the harmful outcomes—are determined by aggregate demand, often through step functions or thresholds; my one transaction is not necessary or sufficient for any part of the outcome, so its causal contribution rounds to zero. If the act makes no difference, then the usual consequentialist reason to refrain from it does not apply, and any remaining moral pressure must come from another source—complicity, integrity, example-setting, or the like.

The development I'd like to test this against comes directly from my work. An individual transaction is no longer merely one more unit added to an aggregate. It is also a training example: it updates a demand-forecasting or recommender model whose parameters then influence decisions at scale—how much inventory a retailer commits to, which routes an airline schedules, which consumers are shown what. Through that channel, a single choice has, in principle, a nonzero causal effect that is not confined to the physical quantity consumed but propagates through a learned model that governs the behavior of millions of downstream decisions.

Does the inefficacy objection survive this development? Please reconstruct the argument carefully enough to identify the exact premise it needs about the causal channel between one person's act and the eventual harm, and then say whether that premise still holds once individual choices also flow into trained models. Tell me whether the objection emerges intact, needs repair, or is dead, and if it needs repair, state the form in which it could be restored. I also want you to separate cases where a model update is monotone and expected—a purchase that reinforces a trend—from cases where the update is swamped, clipped, or sampled out, because I suspect the objection's force may begin to depend on contingent details of the training pipeline rather than on a general fact about large numbers.
