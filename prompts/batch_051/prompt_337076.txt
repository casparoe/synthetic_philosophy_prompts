I've been trying to put into words something I saw at a previous job that I think is a more general pattern, and I want to know whether the idea holds up. In my last role, I worked for a company that had acquired a widely used open-source orchestration tool and then spent several years turning it into a proprietary platform: single-vendor marketplace, custom APIs, exclusive extensions, and a big push to migrate users onto managed services. The division reported strong revenue growth, and executives used that revenue as proof that the acquisition was a success. But I had access to internal cost data, and once I allocated shared infrastructure, opportunity costs, support burden, and the cost of maintaining two increasingly divergent codebases, the division looked like a net drain on the parent company. The only people who clearly benefited were a small group whose compensation was tied to the division's gross revenue. I started calling this "empire accounting": the practice of treating a subsidiary, platform, or captured ecosystem as an asset by counting the rents extracted through a privileged position while hiding the costs that the parent organization actually bears.

What struck me is that Adam Smith seems to describe the same mechanism in the Wealth of Nations, in his discussion of colonies and the mercantile system. As I understand him, Smith argues that Britain's American colonies appeared profitable to the public because the monopoly on colonial trade enriched a narrow merchant interest, while the empire as a whole was a net cost to the mother country, not a source of real wealth. He even suggests that Britain would be better off giving up the colonial project rather than continuing to pay for a scheme whose benefits are mostly imaginary. I may be misreading him, but I think he is saying that the accounting was the illusion: the empire looked like an asset because the gains were concentrated and visible while the costs were diffuse and off-book.

My coined concept, empire accounting, is meant to describe any situation where an organization mistakes its ability to extract rents from a platform, ecosystem, or dependency for actual value creation, and where the metrics it reports make that mistake systematically by counting gross captured revenue as if it were net profit to the whole. I think it applies to cases beyond literal colonies: a company that makes its open-source project closed, a government that treats a regulated utility as a revenue source, a team that keeps a legacy system alive because it generates internal chargebacks even though it slows everything else down. The central claim is that the apparent profitability of a privileged position can survive only because the costs are allocated elsewhere or not allocated at all, and that this is the same error Smith identified in Britain's colonial monopoly.
