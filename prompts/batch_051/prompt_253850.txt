Two groups of moral philosophers have spent a year in an adversarial collaboration on the structure of moral progress. One group defends an expansionist account: moral progress is best explained as the widening of the class of beings whose interests are treated as inputs to moral justification. On this view, the circle of moral concern has expanded, unevenly and with reversals, from kin and tribe through nations and all humans, and more recently toward some nonhuman animals; the underlying moral machinery remains comparatively stable, and progress consists chiefly in applying it beyond old boundaries. The other group defends a revisionist account: the most significant episodes of moral progress are reconfigurations of moral categories rather than additions of members to an existing circle. On this view, the abolition of chattel slavery, the decline of honor-based norms, and the emergence of ideas like structural injustice did not merely move a boundary; they changed what it is to be a moral subject, created or retired whole categories of evaluation, and altered the forms of reasoning that count as moral.

The two groups already agree that both extension-like changes and concept-revision-like changes occur in history. Their dispute is over which relation is explanatorily fundamental, and whether the metaphor of an expanding circle of moral concern is a good master metaphor or a systematically misleading one.

Write the joint statement this collaboration should produce. It should contain the following. First, a numbered list of substantive agreed claims, phrased precisely enough that neither side regards them as vacuous or slanted. Second, a disagreements table giving the residual disagreements in neutral wording, with each side's own position stated in wording its proponents would accept. Third, a section on cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; this section should address at least the following suspected cruxes: whether the abolition of chattel slavery is best represented as adding enslaved people to an already available category of full moral personhood or as reworking the operative concept of personhood itself; whether the decline of honor-based norms is best represented as an expansion of protection to the previously excluded or as the replacement of a status-based scheme of evaluation by a different one; and whether moral concern for ecosystems and species is an extension of moral status to nonhuman wholes or a shift from individualist to systemic moral categories. For each crux, give a concrete body of historical or conceptual evidence that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence will show. Also state, for each side, at least one kind of evidence or argument that its proponents acknowledge would count against their own view. If any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, say which part and why, after identifying the substantive remainder. Do not adjudicate; the document is one both camps could sign. Write in American English.
