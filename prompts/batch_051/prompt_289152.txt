In a seminar on value theory, I recently encountered spectrum arguments that are meant to trouble the assumption that 'all things considered better than' is transitive. The strategy, as I understand it, is to walk through a series of pairwise comparisons in which the relevant ground of comparison shifts slightly with each pair—say, a slight reduction in the intensity of a pain traded for a very large increase in its duration, or a small change in the number of people affected—so that we are led to judge each adjacent option better than the next, and then to notice that a direct comparison of the far endpoints points the other way, generating something like a cycle of betterness judgments. I had always assumed that transitivity is part of the logic of any comparative relation: if x is better than y and y is better than z, then x must be better than z, in the same way that 'taller than' could not fail to pass the test.

So my instinct was that such arguments, if valid at all, can only be reductios showing that one of the local pairwise judgments was mistaken, or that the word 'better' is being used in different senses at different points in the chain. When I then read authors claiming that 'better than' may itself be a nontransitive relation, I couldn't see how that is a coherent thing to assert rather than a simple confusion. Is there a reading on which a relation that flouts transitivity can still accurately be called 'better than,' and, if so, what constraints would remain on using it to guide choices? Or should I take the arguments as pressure to reject at least one of the pairwise premises—and, if I do, what exactly is left of the force of a spectrum argument? Please separate what is settled about this debate from what is genuinely contested among those who work on it, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
