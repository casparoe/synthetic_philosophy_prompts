A colleague defending a deflationary view of consciousness keeps invoking the same analogy: conscious experience is the brain's user interface. On his telling, the felt stream of thought and perception stands to underlying neural processing roughly as a computer's graphical desktop stands to the machine-level operations beneath it — icons, windows, and a pointer being a simplified, manipulable rendering of processes that are in themselves nothing like that rendering. The work the analogy is meant to do is specific: just as a user restricted to the desktop can be systematically wrong about what the machine is actually doing, a person restricted to introspection is systematically wrong about what the brain is doing; so first-person reports are appearances rather than evidence about cognitive architecture, and the apparent unity of the self is a useful fiction. I want an audit of this analogy. Give the explicit correspondence table — what maps to the user, the desktop, the icons, the underlying code, and the hardware — then mark where the mapping breaks. Pay particular attention to what the computer side quietly imports: a real user standing outside the interface, and a designer who chose the mapping between interface and machine. Does the analogy smuggle a homunculus and a designer into the brain? Which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which are incidental? And after that, tell me whether the conclusion — that introspection fails as evidence about cognitive architecture — survives, or whether the desktop analogy was carrying it the whole time.
