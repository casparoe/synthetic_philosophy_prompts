I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on decision theory and diachronic rationality. We have been working through money-pump arguments: the classic version is that an agent whose preferences are intransitive — who strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — can be led through a sequence of pairwise trades, each costing a small fee, and finish back at A with less than they started with; the less settled part is whether a sophisticated agent can refuse the sequence and what that resistance costs, since the natural defence seems to require abandoning the principle that if you prefer X to Y you should always take a trade from Y to X. I also help run a weekly undergraduate philosophy-and-economics discussion group, usually around 15 people for 75 minutes, and I want to run a session in which participants feel the force of the pump before they are told its name.

Design a runnable classroom game for that setting. Use small real stakes — wrapped sweets, stickers, or tokens with negligible but nonzero value — so that choices are real but nobody is harmed. The rules, incentives, or roles must be arranged so that participants generate the problematic pattern themselves: some of them should end up with less of something than if they had simply refused every offer, and they should notice this loss before the facilitator names the structure. I would prefer a two-stage design if it serves the goal: first, a naive stage in which the house (a facilitator or an assigned student) runs the trades and extracts fees; second, a defensive stage in which participants know the sequence of offers in advance and may refuse, precommit, or otherwise try to protect themselves. The debrief should then show what it costs to resist the money pump, not merely that the pump exists.
