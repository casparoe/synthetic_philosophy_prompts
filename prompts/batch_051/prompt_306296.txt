hey, so i do political philosophy — rights theory mostly — and i've been trying to find a way to test whether the concept of a right does real work or is just a compact way of talking about duties, liberties, powers and immunities. here's the counterfactual i keep chewing on, and i'd like you to reason through it as a prediction exercise.

in 2028, a mid-sized common-law country — call it anglostan if you want — passes a constitutional amendment creating an independent re-expression commission. the commission must rewrite every legal provision (constitutional, statutory, common law) that currently frames something as a right. the rewrite rule: a clause saying 'A has a right to X' gets replaced by (i) a duty on some identified person or body to do or refrain from specific acts, enforceable by A; (ii) a liberty of A to do X, plus an immunity against removal of that liberty; and (iii) nothing else. after the rewrite, no court may decide a case by invoking a right as a freestanding ground; every holding has to bottom out in a named duty, liberty, power, or immunity and the ordinary reasons behind it. assume the commission does the work honestly over five years — no sabotage, no intentional gutting.

i want your best argued prediction of what the law actually looks like in 2078, and the reasoning more than the verdict. where does the substitution merely re-label a result, and where does it bend the result? which areas of law — free expression, criminal process, privacy, social welfare, tort — do you expect to move, and in what direction? when there's a clash between A's claim and a serious public good, does the new text make it easier or harder for the state to justify the intrusion, or does it just make the justification talk in a different vocabulary? where do judges smuggle rights-talk back in, and what does that show about what the old concept was doing? and the big-picture question: is the hohfeldian re-expression lossless, or does the law loose something — and if it does lose something, does that loss look more like 'rights were trumps', 'rights were side constraints', or 'rights were instruments'?

also mark which predictions you're most confident about and which are guesses, and say what would have to be true — about judicial culture, about how litigants frame claims, about how the public talks — for your prediction to flip. and call me out if the setup begs the question, because i go back and forth on weather it does.
