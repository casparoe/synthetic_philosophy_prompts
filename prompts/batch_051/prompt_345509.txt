Last night I got into a long argument with a friend who teaches social psychology. He spent an hour on what he called the situationist evidence: the study in which seminary students who were told they were running late stepped around someone slumped in a doorway on their way to give a talk; the obedience experiments in which ordinary subjects were led to administer what they believed were dangerous shocks; the finding that people who happened to find a coin in a phone booth were far more likely to help someone pick up scattered papers. His conclusion was blunt: there is no such thing as a stable character trait, and talk of someone being an honest or compassionate person is folk psychology that systematically fails to predict what people will actually do.

Then, this morning, he described his own department's admissions procedures with no sense of contradiction: they ask integrity questions, solicit letters attesting to applicants' character, and deliberate about whether someone is the sort of person who would falsify data. And, of course, it isn't only his department. Universities run honor codes; professions conduct character-and-fitness reviews; hiring panels and courts make assessments of character every day.

I want you to do two things. First, reconstruct the theory of character that these practices are actually committed to. Do they strictly require global, cross-situationally stable dispositions of the kind my friend thinks the experiments refute, or can they get by on something thinner: local traits tied to specific circumstances, base rates from past conduct, or a purely normative fiction that treats people as responsible agents without making empirical claims? Separate what the practice presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the empirical evidence leaves it underdetermined. Second, tell me what an institution that genuinely accepted the situationist critique would have to change in practice, and whether any recognizable version of these practices could survive that revision.
