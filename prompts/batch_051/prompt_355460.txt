I'd like you to write a short piece of philosophical satire aimed at Sen's liberal paradox: the result that no social choice rule can simultaneously guarantee both Pareto efficiency and a minimal form of liberalism in which each person is decisive over at least one protected personal matter. The piece should take the form of the minutes of a constitutional convention held by a small housing co-operative that has just, in a burst of good intentions, adopted two principles into its governing document. The first is Paretian: if every member prefers one state of affairs to another, the co-operative must rank it above the other. The second is minimally liberal: each member has at least one protected personal sphere in which her own preference is to be socially decisive, even if every other member would prefer the opposite. The minutes should follow the committee as it discovers, through a series of ever more precise proposed amendments and clarifying clauses, that the two principles cannot be jointly satisfied. Use a concrete invented example (a shared library book, a parking space, or something similar) in which the members' actual preferences force the co-operative into a ranking cycle: one alternative is judged better than a second, the second better than a third, and the third better than the first, with each step following from one of the two principles the committee has just adopted. Write around 700 words in a flat, procedural, deadpan clerkly voice, with numbered agenda items or clauses. The comedy should come from the committee's conviction that the trouble is mere imprecision in drafting. The target of the satire should be the unexamined assumption that being consistently liberal about personal spheres and consistently Paretian about collective welfare is just a matter of good faith, and that any apparent conflict would dissolve if only the clauses were more carefully worded.

After the piece, add a short note of two or three sentences, explicitly outside the fictional minutes, in which you commit to a definite interpretation rather than hedging. Say which of the two principles the minutes are really designed to put pressure on, and defend that choice as the fairest statement of where the deep problem lies.
