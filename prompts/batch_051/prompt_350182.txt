The following problem and student response come from an advanced undergraduate course on algebra and the philosophy of mathematics. Grade the response as specified at the end.

Problem. The complex numbers are often introduced by adjoining a square root of −1 to the real numbers: one forms the field R[x]/(x²+1), identifies it with C, and writes i for the residue class of x. But the polynomial x²+1 has two roots in this field, namely i and −i, and complex conjugation is a field automorphism of C that swaps them. This prompts a worry. If no property expressible in the pure language of fields distinguishes i from −i, then the symbol i does not pick out a determinate object, and a construction that simply chooses one square root of −1 is arbitrary, hence not well-defined.

Assess this worry. Your answer should distinguish at least the following:
(a) whether the quotient construction R[x]/(x²+1) is itself well-defined;
(b) whether the residue class of x is a well-defined element of the resulting field;
(c) whether there is a canonical, structurally distinguished square root of −1 in C, and whether complex conjugation supplies one;
(d) what exactly is conventional about the notation i, and whether statements using i — for example, i² = −1 — can be meaningful and true independently of the convention.
Where helpful, contrast the square roots of a positive real number, where positivity distinguishes one root.

Student answer. The quotient R[x]/(x²+1) is indeed a field, since x²+1 is irreducible over R, and the residue class j = x + (x²+1) satisfies j² = −1; so the construction certainly produces some field containing a square root of −1. The difficulty is that this field contains two such roots, j and −j, and the automorphism j ↦ −j shows that no statement in the pure language of fields separates them. The choice between them is therefore arbitrary, and a definition that rests on an arbitrary choice is not well-defined. It follows that the i of the usual presentation of the complex numbers is not well-defined either: the phrase the square root of −1 is a definite description with two equally good referents. Complex conjugation does not repair this, since conjugation sends i to −i; but the property of being sent to one's negative is possessed by both roots, so the two remain indistinguishable. Positivity cannot help, because C admits no ordering compatible with the field operations, whereas the ordering on R canonically selects the positive square root of 2. The quotient construction merely relocates the arbitrariness: j is equally a stand-in for what we would call −i and for what we would call i, so the construction fails to deliver a determinate object. Consequently, until a convention is stipulated, a sentence such as i² = −1 is not yet a mathematical statement, and after the convention it is true only relative to that convention.
