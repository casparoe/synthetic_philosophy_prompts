I'm TAing a third-year philosophy of language class and in two weeks I've got a 75-minute session on presupposition, accommodation, and the scorekeeping picture of conversation. The class has done basic truth-conditional semantics so they know entailment, but they've never met presupposition as anything but a word. I don't need the material explained to me—I need a plan for how I should teach it.

I specifically want:
- the order the pieces have to go in and why that order is forced, i.e. what depends on what;
- the one carrying example for the whole session: what it must have, what it must lack, and why not the king of France is bald;
- the misconceptions they'll bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off—I'd bet on at least "presupposition is just what the speaker privately believes" and "accommodation is the listener repairing a misunderstanding after the fact";
- a last-five-minutes check that would show me whether they've actually got the scoreboard picture rather than just the labels;
- what I should defintately leave out at this level and say so, rather than half-mentioning it.

The thing I most want to avoid is them leaving thinking accommodation is a special cleanup operation that only kicks in when presuppositions fail. I want them to see ordinary updates and accommodation as moves in the same game, governed by the same kind of scorekeeping rule. So plan the arc around that.

When you write it up, start with the one-sentence spine of the lesson, then the plan and the reasoning for each choice underneath it—no preamble, no restating the request.
