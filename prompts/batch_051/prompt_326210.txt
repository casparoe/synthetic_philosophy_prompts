I'm a philosophy professor working on eighteenth-century moral psychology, and I've been troubled by something in the way our legal institutions think about impartiality. In common-law jury trials, jurors are routinely instructed to decide the case solely on the law and the evidence and to set aside sympathy, partiality, or emotional identification with either side; sentencing judges receive similar warnings. Yet the very same legal systems measure negligence, provocation, and self-defense by the reasonable-person standard, which asks the fact-finder to imagine how an idealized person, placed in the defendant's circumstances but without that person's actual temperament, fears, or weaknesses, would have perceived and responded. That standard appears to demand exactly the kind of imaginative occupation of another person's standpoint that Adam Smith made central to moral judgment. For Smith, impartiality is not achieved by suppressing sympathy but by exercising it in a disciplined way: we judge conduct by bringing our passions into line with those of an imagined impartial spectator, a well-informed but disinterested figure whose feelings set the standard of propriety. The institution therefore seems, on its face, to treat sympathy as a contaminant to be excluded from judgment, while Smith treats refined sympathy as the only route to impartiality.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and in the philosophy of law. Take your time and think carefully. Your object is the existing practice I've just described, not the law's official self-understanding. Investigate what theory of impartial moral judgment the practice presupposes. Begin by brainstorming the several theories the jury instruction and the reasonable-person standard could each be read as presupposing. Then reconstruct the practice's implicit theory as charitably as you can, carefully separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, address whether the apparent tension between the anti-sympathy instruction and the reasonable-person standard is real, or whether the practice can be made coherent on a Smithian reading on which the juror is being asked to trade partial sympathy for the sympathy of the impartial spectator. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in jury instructions, in judicial training, or in the formulation of the reasonable-person standard — if one actually accepted Smith's claim that impartiality is achieved through sympathy rather than by its suppression.
