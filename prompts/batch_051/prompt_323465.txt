Last month, over a long dinner, two friends of mine fell into an argument about a small incident from earlier that evening. I have not been able to decide which of them was right, and I would like you to work through the exchange in detail.

The incident: another guest, Dara, had been rerouted to an unfamiliar train station. A stranger approached her, unprompted, and told her to turn left at a pharmacy and walk two blocks, where she would see the metro entrance. She had no way to check the man's reliability, and she had no specific reason to trust him beyond the fact that he had addressed her; but she accepted what he said at face value, followed the directions, and found the metro exactly where he had described. When she recounted this later, she said she had been fully rational to believe him before checking.

Marcus objected that she had simply gotten lucky. Elena defended her. The argument went through several rounds, roughly as follows.

Marcus: A belief is rational only if it is supported by evidence available to the believer. Dara had no evidence about this particular man. What she did have was standing background evidence — that people who give directions to strangers in public places are generally accurate, and that nothing in the situation indicated incompetence or deceit. Her acceptance was rational, but only because of that background evidence. The bare fact that someone told her something is not by itself a reason.

Elena: Then you cannot explain basic cases. Children accept what parents and teachers say before they have any track record of testimony's reliability, and the track record itself has to be learned largely through testimony. If every testimonial belief needs prior evidence that testimony is reliable, the structure never gets started. The sensible view is that a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told unless there are specific reasons not to — defeaters, as with an obviously confused or obviously interested speaker. Dara had no such reason, so she was rational.

Marcus: The child case does not settle anything. A child may be a well-functioning learner without every belief it forms being justified in the full sense. And the track record is not circular: over a lifetime, particular reports are checked against observations and consequences, and the generalization that testimony is a reliable kind of thing becomes standing evidence. Dara is not a child; she has that standing evidence, even if she could not articulate it on the spot. So her belief was justified by evidence after all.

Elena: That is retrospective rationalization. At the moment Dara accepted the stranger's word, what made her belief rational? If you say her implicit background beliefs, I can ask what justified those; trace the chain back and you will eventually find testimonial beliefs accepted without prior reliability evidence. More importantly, your picture treats the stranger as a sort of instrument — a barometer of the geography — whereas a speaker does something quite different. When someone tells you that the metro is two blocks away, he offers you an assurance and makes himself answerable for your believing it. That is what Dara relied on, and it is a reason of a different kind from a reading off an instrument.

Marcus: Now you have moved from epistemology to ethics. It may be true that speakers take responsibility, and that we would be rude to cross-examine every helpful stranger, but courtesy does not make a belief likely to be true. Epistemic justification concerns what makes a belief truth-conducive, and Dara's belief was truth-conducive only because of the general regularity she had learned about how people give directions. Your assurance adds a normative relationship between persons, not a probability of truth. Without that regularity, accepting the stranger's assurance would be a recipe for believing whatever anyone says.

Elena: You misdescribe the alternative. I am not saying trust is rational no matter how unreliable the world is. I am saying the default is trust, and it can be defeated by evidence of unreliability. Nor is the assurance point mere politeness: when someone lies to you, you can later reproach them with "you told me," an attitude that would be nonsense toward a malfunctioning barometer. That normative relation is what testimony is, and the probability story cannot get started without it. For how did you learn the regularity about directions, if not through reports whose acceptance already presumed the very practice you are trying to justify?

Marcus: I learned it through reports that were themselves confirmed over time. The fact that they were once accepted unreflectively is irrelevant to whether they now constitute evidence. And to find out whether people generally give accurate directions, we do not need to solve the philosophy of testimony; we simply observe how often their directions lead somewhere. So the regularity is independently testable, and that is what does the epistemic work.

At that point they turned to me, and I found I could not sort out who had the better of which moves. Please adjudicate the exchange step by step. For each stage of the argument, say which party has the better of the specific point; identify any conflations, unnoticed distinctions, or places where one side has overreached; and then give an overall verdict about which position is stronger, and what each would need to concede to move the debate forward.
