A friend and I spent the evening arguing about Euclid’s treatment of the unit in Book VII of the Elements, and I came away dissatisfied with both of our positions. Euclid introduces the unit before any number: a unit is, in the usual English paraphrase, that by virtue of which each existing thing is called one, and a number is then a multitude composed of units. My friend insisted that this makes the unit a primitive measuring rod, not itself a number, so that one is not a number but what makes other things countable. I pushed back that this seems to collapse into a kind of mystification: if the unit is not a number, what sort of thing is it? A thing, a property, a relation, a convention we bring to things? He replied that Euclid also treats units as indivisible, and that the whole edifice depends on treating the unit as more fundamental than the numbers built from it. I am not sure what work fundamental is doing there.

Could you explain the conceptual structure of Euclid’s distinction between unit and number, and then sort out what has to be true about one for that distinction to be coherent? I am especially interested in two tensions: first, the unit seems to be both outside number and yet the first element from which all numbers are composed; second, even if one refuses to classify the unit as a number, the definition of number as a multitude of units already seems to smuggle in a notion of how many units there are, which invites the very numerical thinking the definition was meant to ground. I care less about the history of Greek mathematics than about whether the distinction can be made to work conceptually.
