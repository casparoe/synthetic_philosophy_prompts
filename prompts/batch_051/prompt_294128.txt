Suppose that in 1950 a new, lavishly endowed National Council for Social Research opens in a mid-sized country and announces a binding methodological charter for everything it funds. The charter permits researchers to describe and measure group-level regularities—inequality, institutional persistence, class-based voting, cultural diffusion—as the phenomena to be explained. But it forbids any explanation that cites a group, structure, institution, culture, class, or norm as a cause unless the same paper spells out the individual-level mechanism through which that factor operates: the beliefs, desires, information, opportunities, incentives, and interactions of particular persons. A genuinely sophisticated review board enforces the rule strictly for the next forty years, rejecting hand-waving reductions such as "people internalize the norm" unless the mechanism is actually specified. The surrounding society is otherwise ordinary, with competitive universities and the usual political pressures on research funding.

I want a reasoned prediction of what this discipline looks like by 1990, built from what you know about how scientists respond to incentives and how much social explanation really can be reduced to individual action. Which fields and questions flourish under the charter, and which quietly atrophy or migrate elsewhere? What evasions or creative adaptations do researchers develop—redefining the "individual" to include firms, households, or other composite actors; smuggling structure back in as preferences, beliefs, or "perceived constraints"; or building a de facto holism under individualist vocabulary? Where does the reduction genuinely succeed, and where does it stall or quietly change the questions being asked? And does the charter deliver what its architects presumably wanted—greater precision, microfoundations for all social claims, cumulative theory—or does it mainly select for a different set of topics? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the chain of reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Be explicit about which links in your prediction you regard as robust and which depend on assumptions about how fast researchers adapt, how reducible social facts are, and how well the board holds the line.
