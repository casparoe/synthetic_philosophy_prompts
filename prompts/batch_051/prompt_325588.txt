I've been arguing with a friend who volunteers for a land trust. They restore wetlands, mostly because they want to help wildlife, but over the last year he's gotten worried that what he's actually doing is creating a lot of short, brutal animal lives: a restored marsh fills up with voles and frogs and nestlings, and almost all of them starve, get eaten, or die of exposure before they reproduce. A former member of the group now insists that, from the animals' point of view, draining a marsh might be the less cruel option. My own credence that a typical wetland drainage reduces total suffering among the wild animals who would otherwise have lived there—over, say, the next fifty years, and including the pain of the animals displaced or starved by the drainage itself—is around 0.4. My friend's at 0.7.

I don't want "it depends." Give me your number, and then decompose it: what sub-questions does it actually hinge on? For example (don't limit yourself to these): is the average wild animal's life in a marsh net-positive or net-negative; when the marsh is drained, do the animals mostly die, or do surrounding habitats just absorb them; does the drained land go on to support more or fewer small animals than the marsh did, and with what quality of life; and how much acute suffering does the act of destruction itself cause? Assign a rough probability to each, show how you combine them, and say which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what kind of evidence—field counts, survival data, even a philosophical argument about whether natural predation counts as suffering—would move your number the most. Also, since we're at 0.4 and 0.7, tell us which sub-question most likely explains our gap.

I'm not asking for citations or literature pointers; I want to see the reasoning. And if you think the question as posed is confused—e.g. because "total suffering" isn't something you can just sum across a frog and a heron, or because reducing the number of lives isn't obviously a way to reduce suffering in the morally relevant sense—say so plainly rather than giving me a fake-precise number.
