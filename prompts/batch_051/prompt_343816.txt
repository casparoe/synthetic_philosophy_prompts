The question on the exam was: 'Distinguish divine impassibility from divine apathy in your own words. Then give the strongest argument you can for the view that a perfectly loving God must be able to suffer, and assess whether that argument succeeds.' The student wrote:

'Impassibility is the doctrine that God can't be affected from outside; nothing a creature does can cause a change or an emotion in him. Its easily confused with apathy, but the difference matters: apathy would mean God doesn't care at all, whereas impassibility says God cares completely, just not in a way that makes his care a painful passion he undergoes. The strongest passibilist argument starts from the nature of love. Love, at least perfect love, means feeling what the beloved feels. If God loves us perfectly, then when we suffer God feels that suffering too, and to feel suffering just is to suffer. So a perfectly loving God must be able to suffer. The only way out for the impassibilist is to deny that God has emotions at all, but that makes God an unfeeling stone and contradicts all the passages where God is said to be angry, grieved, or jealous. So the argument succeeds. It also shows that God must be mutable, since a being that goes from not-suffering to suffering has to change.'
