I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I'd appreciate a structured breakdown rather than a short gloss.

In a discussion group I attend, we were arguing about whether a person in an emergency should pause to run an impartial check before acting. The example on the table was a man who pulls his wife out of a burning car without hesitating. Another member asked whether the man should at least have stopped long enough to register the thought that the stranger on the far side of the road might have an equal claim to be rescued, and then act anyway. A long-standing member shut the question down by saying that if the man stopped to have that thought, it would be "one thought too many." The group treated this as decisive, but I never worked out exactly what was being claimed.

I've since noticed the phrase doing different work in different arguments. Sometimes it seems to mean only that it's unreasonable to demand explicit deliberation under time pressure. Sometimes it seems to mean that the man's merely having the thought would itself be a failure of character or a sign of something wrong in him, quite apart from what he then does. Sometimes it seems to be a broader slogan against applying detached, calculative reasoning to intimate relationships at all. And sometimes it seems to be aimed at ways of doing ethics rather than at individual people—a complaint about any approach that would treat the unhesitating rescue as something that needs an impartial justification.

I'd like a menu of the precise claims "one thought too many" could express. For each reading, state the exact proposition, classify it as trivially true, substantive and defensible, false, or too vague to evaluate, and say which of the observed uses it best fits. Then tell me which reading or readings the long-standing member would need for his dismissal to actually answer the original question about the man and the stranger, which readings would be too weak to support that dismissal, and which would be too strong or beside the point. I'd also be curious to know which of the candidate readings people who use the phrase casually usually have in mind, if they have any single one rather than a loose bundle of several.
