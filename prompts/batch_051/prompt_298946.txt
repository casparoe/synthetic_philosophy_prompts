Readers readily accept bizarre descriptive stipulations in fiction — dragons, invisibility, the dead returning to speak — but they resist stipulations that run against their moral commitments, such as a story that simply stipulates that a certain kind of cruelty is admirable. This is imaginative resistance. One view is that it reveals a distinctively imaginative limit: we cannot make the morally deviant state of affairs vivid to ourselves. A rival view is that it reflects unwillingness, or an ambiguity about what the story has really made fictional, rather than a failure of imagination. Treat the claim that morally deviant content imposes a constraint on imagination specifically, as opposed to on evaluation or interpretation, as a claim with empirical content. Design a concrete test — an experiment or observation, real or realistic — that would help separate these readings.
