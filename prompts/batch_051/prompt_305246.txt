I'm an undergraduate taking a course in constitutional theory, and I've been turning over a pattern that is usually presented as natural but seems to me to carry a heavy unacknowledged philosophical load. Many constitutional systems bind their own future selves: a constitution adopted at one time entrenches certain rights and procedures so that a later ordinary majority cannot easily alter them—amendment requires supermajorities, delayed or repeated ratification, or is ruled out entirely for specified provisions—and the same idea operates less visibly whenever elected politicians hand interest rates or the running of elections to an independent body they cannot readily overrule. The canonical justification is the Ulysses-contract story: the polity ties itself to the mast now because it distrusts its own future judgment, as Ulysses distrusted how he would respond to the Sirens' song.

What I am trying to work out is what this practice strictly presupposes about a polity as an agent persisting through time. A contract binds one party to another, but here both parties are the same polity at different dates—and unlike Ulysses, a polity is not a single continuing person; it is a sequence of overlapping generations whose later members never consented to the earlier arrangement. So does entrenchment presuppose that the polity at time one and the polity at time two are literally the same agent, so that the later majority is bound by its own earlier act rather than by a predecessor's? Does it presuppose that the future majority, when it tries to undo the entrenchment, will be in the grip of a predictable defect—panic, faction, short-term desire—rather than acting on values it has come to hold after honest reflection? And the practice sits uneasily with a principle those same systems also affirm, at least rhetorically: that legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed here and now, and that one generation ought not to rule another from the grave.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, or theories, of diachronic agency, autonomy, and rationality that the practice presupposes; to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely makes it rhetorically attractive; and to say what in constitutional practice would have to change if one thought, instead, that each generation is a distinct agent that cannot legitimately bind those who come after. I am drafting an essay arguing that defenders of entrenchment are quietly importing a metaphysics of the polity across time that they seldom defend, and I want a precise statement of the commitments they would need to own.
