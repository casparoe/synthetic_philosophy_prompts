I have been trying to make sense of a puzzle about how legal systems treat the same reproductive acts. In much of the United States, and most visibly in California, a woman can be paid for her eggs on a market that openly varies prices by donor profile, past donation outcomes, and intended characteristics, and she can also be paid to carry a pregnancy under a commercial surrogacy contract in which the intending parents become the legal parents from birth. In Spain, by contrast, compensated egg donation is routine and lawful—donors receive roughly a thousand euros, and the resulting child is legally the recipient's—but any surrogacy contract is void, and the woman who gives birth is the legal mother. France, meanwhile, allows only unpaid gamete donation and treats surrogacy as forbidden. So Spain will pay a woman for her eggs but will not enforce a contract for gestation, France will pay for neither, and California will pay for both.

The pattern does not sort cleanly by any single obvious principle. If the worry is commodifying reproduction, then paying for eggs seems as much a form of commodification as paying for gestation; if the worry is protecting women from exploitation, regulation would seem to be an alternative to an outright ban; if the worry is the welfare or identity of the child, it is unclear why gametes and gestation should differ. Reconstruct the implicit theories—of the body, of reproductive labor, of commodification, and of what makes someone a parent—that each pattern presupposes. In particular, assess whether there is a defensible philosophical distinction between providing eggs and providing gestation that can underwrite paying for the first while banning or refusing to enforce the second. Also say how much the actual practices strictly require a unified theory and how much they are compatible with several rival views, and explain what each legal regime would have to change if its presupposed theory were applied consistently. Please answer in American English.
