I have been thinking on my own about an old idea, usually called euhemerism, that the gods were once real people—kings, conquerors, lawgivers—whose lives were exaggerated and embellished until, after enough generations, a formidable mortal had swollen into a god. On this view a myth is not a lie and not an allegory but a distorted memory: behind figures like Zeus or Heracles there was probably some actual powerful person.

A later historical case seems to put that theory to the test, and I keep turning it over. After Julius Caesar was assassinated, the Roman Senate formally declared him a god, Divus Julius, and gave him a temple, priests, and a cult. The practice then became routine for many emperors after their deaths, good and bad alike, until the line between a political honor and actual worship became hard to draw. What fascinates me is that this was not the slow, organic exaggeration euhemerism imagines. The people who voted Caesar a god had known the man; almost the whole transformation happened within a lifetime, by legal decree, in the full light of a sophisticated city. Yet it was not, for everyone, mere theater: the temples, sacrifices, and oaths were real.

I would like you to analyze the deification of the Roman emperors as a case study for euhemerism. What should a euhemerist say is going on in this episode, and which features of it support the theory, which features it cannot accommodate, and which it explains only by stretching? In particular, I want to understand the difference between a myth growing around a man over centuries and a state deliberately manufacturing a god, and what that difference does to the claim that the old pantheons began as memories of mortals. Does the Roman case show euhemerism working faster and more openly, or does it show that imperial deification and the stories about Zeus are two different phenomena? Be honest about where the theory fails rather than making the case fit.
