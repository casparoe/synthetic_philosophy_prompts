I'd read the rationalist explanations for war (the Fearon framework stuff: private information plus incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, indivisibilities) and it hit me that the whole thing maps pretty cleanly onto why AI labs end up in a race they'd all prefer to avoid. My friend kept insisting it's just a loose analogy, that states and war are one thing and firms running R&D races are another, and that I'm pattern-matching. He might of had a point, but I also think he was mostly annoyed that I kept bringing up the same three mechanisms.

Here's the mapping as I see it. The puzzle in the war case is that war is costly ex post, so rational states should always be able to find some bargain they both prefer to fighting; the three mechanisms are supposed to explain why they sometimes can't. The AI version feels the same: everyone involved says a crash effort toward AGI is dangerous and the labs would genuinely prefer a slower coordinated schedule, so why don't they just make that deal? Private information: no lab really knows how close the others are to a capability jump, and each lab has every incentive to downplay its own progress so it doesn't trigger a crash effort by everyone else—so you get the incentive-to-misrepresent thing, and a race even when every player would rather go slow. Commitment problem: even if all the labs agreed to pause or slow down, none of them can credibly commit to not quietly speeding up once the others slow down; and since whichever lab wins can't credibly commit to not exploit the resulting power shift, any pre-race deal is unenforceable. Indivisibility: the prize looks winner-take-most—you can't really slice "being first to AGI" into shares—so there's no stable compromise. That third one is where I'm shakiest, and where my friend got traction: maybe you could split control or profits even if you can't split "first."

So I want to know: is this a real identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion?
