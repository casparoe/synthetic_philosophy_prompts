I am a PhD student working on psychiatric classification, and one idea I keep returning to in my own thinking is what Hacking calls the looping effect. The basic thought, as I use it, is that human kinds behave differently from natural kinds because the people classified are aware of the classification: they adjust their self-understanding and conduct to the label, clinicians notice the adjustments and rewrite the diagnostic criteria, and the category moves under its own feet. The usual illustrations are dissociative identity disorder, fugue states, and borderline personality disorder, cases where the availability of the label plausibly changed what got expressed. Lately I have found myself turning this into a working rule. When I try to judge whether a psychiatric category is a natural kind for the purposes of causal explanation — whether depression, autism spectrum disorder, or PTSD, say, is the sort of thing that could figure in a robust, mind-independent explanation — I first check for looping. If I find solid evidence that the category has looped even once, I stop treating it as a live natural-kind candidate. I do not conclude that it is unreal; I just decline to use it as a stable explanatory kind in my work, and I hold any argument that its present empirical coherence is evidence of a real, mind-independent kind to a considerably higher bar than I would for a category with no such history.

My worry is that this shortcut may do exactly what I accuse others of doing: let a broad metaphysical commitment drive day-to-day judgements. It is easy to tell a story on which looping and natural-kindedness are compatible — a label, by changing people's behavior, might set off a self-selecting process that stabilises the category rather than destabilising it, or the loop might change only the concept of the condition, not the condition itself. It is also easy to tell the opposite story, on which my rule is too lenient because it still lets looped categories serve in explanations once the bar is met. Is my rule rationally defensible as a policy for someone in my situation? If not, what is the most defensible restatement of it, and which feature of the rule is doing the damage? Where is my reasoning most likely to fail, and what argument or evidence would most likely make you reverse your verdict — either toward thinking that a looped category can still serve as a natural-kind explanation, or toward thinking that even the weakened rule should be abandoned?
