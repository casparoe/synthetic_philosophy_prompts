I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, so I tend to press on arguments the way I would press on an inconsistent requirement spec. I've been reading Frederick Douglass's 1860 speech in Glasgow, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", and two of his claims keep looking like they cannot both hold. Early in the speech he writes: "Again, it should be borne in mind that the mere text, and only the text, and not any commentaries or creeds written by those who wished to give the text a meaning apart from its plain reading, was adopted as the Constitution of the United States." That sounds like a hard textualism: the words on the page control, not the framers' secret motives and not later pro-slavery court rulings. But he also writes: "I, on the other hand, deny that the Constitution guarantees the right to hold property in man." The apparent conflict is that the written text itself contains clauses—I am thinking mainly of the Three-Fifths Clause and the Fugitive Slave Clause—that many people, including abolitionists of Douglass's own time, read as recognizing or protecting slavery. If the text alone is authoritative, and we are barred from appealing to outside intentions or interpretations, how does Douglass's anti-slavery conclusion follow from the bare text? I want to know what candidate reconciliations could close this gap: e.g., different senses of "guarantee," a structural reading of the whole document, an argument that those clauses deliberately avoid recognizing slave property, or some distinction Douglass draws later in the speech. More importantly, I want a verdict on which reconciliation is the most honest to Douglass's argument and which is special pleading. If the two passages cannot be fully reconciled, which one better expresses the position he actually holds in the speech, and does his textualism collapse under the strain?
