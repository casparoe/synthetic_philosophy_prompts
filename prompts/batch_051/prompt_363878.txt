I've been trying to get a handle on presuppositions for a while now, and I keep ending up with a pile of gut reactions that don't line up. Let me lay them out; maybe you can tell me what principle I'm groping at, or which of my verdicts I have to give up.

The loose picture I've been working with is the familiar scorekeeping one: a conversation keeps a running list of shared background assumptions, and each utterance has to fit against that list. But when an utterance doesn't fit, sometimes the list silently grows to accomodate it, and sometimes it doesn't, and I can't find the rule for which is which.

Here are my cases.

(i) "The present king of France is bald." I don't say this is false, and I don't say "The present king of France isn't bald" is true. Both just misfire, in the same way. So negation isn't flipping some commitment around.

(ii) "Dan doesn't know the meeting was moved" and "Dan knows the meeting was moved" both commit the speaker to the meeting having been moved. "Dan doesn't believe the meeting was moved" commits to neither. So it's something about know versus believe, not about whether the main verb is negated.

(iii) "If Maria has kids, her kids are already in bed." The speaker plainly isn't commited to Maria having kids. The if-clause seems to catch the kids-commitment before it can get out, but I can't say exactly how.

(iv) "Either Maria doesn't have kids, or her kids are already in bed." Same: no commitment to Maria having kids. This one is weirder to me, because the first disjunct says she does *not* have kids, so how does that license the second disjunct's talk of her kids?

(v) A new coworker says "I have to leave a bit early to pick my daughter up from school." I didn't know she had a daughter. I just absorb it. It would be bizarre to accuse her of sneaking something past me; it's not an assertion exactly, and it's not a lie.

(vi) But if someone I barely know says "After my third divorce I moved to Tulsa," I can absorb that too, but it feels clunkier, like a lot of biography arrived through the side door at once. Still, I don't treat the sentence as broken.

(vii) During a project postmortem, a colleague asks me "When did you stop sabotaging the project?" I don't answer the question; I challenge the embedded bit, something like "I haven't been sabotaging the project." Structurally this seems to resemble (v) and (vi) — some background content plus a main point — but here the background content doesn't get absorbed. And it's not that I can't follow him; I just refuse to treat that part as background.

(viii) "Even your mom thinks you should apologize." If it's already common ground that my mom always takes my side, fine. But if that's not settled, I want to say "don't just slide that in." So whether something gets accommodated seems to depend on what's already uncontroversial in the conversation.

(ix) "The critic who panned my novel is at the party." I might never have been told there was a critic, but my reaction is "wait, who panned your novel?" rather than "I didn't agree to that." That question feels like catching the background up, not objecting.

(x) "Since the city is bankrupt, the mayor should resign." If I don't believe the city is bankrupt, I don't quietly absorb the bankruptcy, I say "wait, since when is the city bankrupt?" This looks a lot like (v) in shape — a subordinate clause gluing a main clause to some assumed background — but my reaction is completely different.

(xi) "Before she died, she told me to keep painting." If I didn't know the woman died, the death itself arrives as background. And this one I genuinely hate: it doesn't misfire, but it feels like the speaker cheated by presupposing the very thing that was the actual news. Yet in (v) the background content — that she has a daughter — was also new, and I called that fine.

(xii) Finally, one I keep going back and forth on: "I don't know whether the present king of France is bald." My first instinct is that it still takes the existence of a king of France for granted, the way (ii) does with know-that. But "know whether" isn't asserting anything about him, so maybe the presupposition gets dissolved inside the question.

My rough working principle so far is that an utterance presupposes P when the speaker would be out of line unless P was already accepted by everyone in the conversation; if P isn't already accepted, we quietly add it to the common ground unless P is contested or it's really the main point. But (x) and (xi) pull against that line in different ways, and (iii)/(iv) versus (i) suggest that some connectives don't just leave a commitment in place but actively cancel it, which the principle doesn't cover at all.

So: is there one precise account of presupposition and accommodation that delivers all, or almost all, of my verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I drop? And what does your account predict for "The only person who solved the puzzle is Brazilian" said to someone who didn't know anyone solved it — and about (xii), which I still can't settle?
