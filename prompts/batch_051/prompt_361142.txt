here's a mechanism i've been turning over. a forecasting tournament — a couple hundred yes/no questions, each resolving somewhere over the next twelve months — scores people by forced kelly staking instead of brier score. everyone starts with 10,000 points. whenever you enter a probability p on an open question, the platform automatically stakes |2p − 1| of your current bankroll on whichever side you favor, at even odds. so p = 0.75 means 50% of your bankroll goes on YES: if yes, bankroll ×1.5; if no, bankroll ×0.5. p = 0.2 means 60% goes on NO. p is capped at 0.01 and 0.99 so you can't fully zero out, and to stop the bookkeeping from exploding you can only hold one open position at a time; when yours resolves your bankroll gets compunded and you can enter the next question. the leaderboard is just final bankroll, and the top ten get flat prizes.

How does this actually shake out? Does it elicit people's real probabilities, or does it elicit something else — and does that depend on whether players are trying to maximize expected log, expected points, or their chance at a top-ten prize? Where does it get gamed first (the p = 0.99 maniac on every 55% question, the last-week disaster near the prize cutoff, something about the one-position rule)? What does the even-odds structure do to which questions are even worth touching? And which failure modes can you patch without just turning it back into a brier score?
