I'm a TA for a philosophy of science course this term, and I've got a pile of midterms to grade. The unit was on the laws of nature debate, mostly Lewis's Best Systems Account on the Humean side and Armstrong-style necessitation between universals on the anti-Humean side. The question they had to answer was: "Explain why a simple regularity theory of laws struggles with the distinction between laws and accidental regularities, and then explain how a Humean Best Systems Account and an Armstrong-style anti-Humean view each improve on it; for each of those two views, say what makes a true generalization a law and whether laws supervene on the non-nomic facts." I've pasted one student's answer bellow.

Student's answer: "A simple regularity theory says a law is just a true universal generalization of the form 'all Fs are Gs.' The problem is that accidental regularities, like all the coins in my pocket being copper, are also true universal generalizations, so the theory can't distinguish laws from accidents. The Best Systems Account fixes this by saying laws are the axioms of the true deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength in systematizing the whole mosaic of local matters of fact. A generalization about the coins in my pocket buys no real strength and costs simplicity, so it won't make the cut, whereas F=ma earns its place. So on the BSA, lawhood is a matter of how simple and informative a generalization is, not really of what the world is like — it ends up being an epistemic or pragmatic status rather than an ontic one. Armstrong's anti-Humean view instead posits a second-order necessitation relation N between universals: N(F,G) guarantees that all Fs are Gs, so a law doesn't merely summarize the instances — it produces them. Since laws are added on top of the mosaic rather than supervening on it, the anti-Humean can say laws genuinely explain the uniformities, which a mere description can't do."
