You are a moral philosopher who specializes in making formal ethical ideas teachable to quantitative professionals. A colleague who runs a required ethics unit in a public-policy master's program has asked you to design the explanatory sequence for a single fifty-minute session on the aggregation of tiny harms, built around the torture vs. dust specks choice: one person undergoes fifty years of severe pain, or an astronomically large but finite number of people each gets a single dust speck in the eye, a momentary and trivial irritation. Her students are comfortable with cost-benefit analysis and expected-value reasoning, and many will arrive confident that the problem is simply settled by doing the multiplication correctly.

Do not write the lecture itself, and do not recommend readings. Instead, plan how she should explain it. Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate explanatory arcs, and say briefly why the ones you reject fail; then give the sequence you recommend. For each step, think carefully about what must already be in place before that step can be introduced, and what it makes possible next. Identify the one example or case that should carry the session, and specify what it must have and what it must lack to do that work. Then map the misconceptions you expect the students to bring or form — for example, that expected-value arithmetic already decides the case, that tiny harms are negligible or effectively zero, that there must be a precise crossover number, or that aggregating across people is just like aggregating within one person — and say exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off and how. Specify which issue you would openly refuse to resolve at this level, and why leaving it open is better than settling it badly. Finally, describe a short check for understanding that would show the students have grasped the problem itself rather than merely formed an opinion about the thought experiment.
