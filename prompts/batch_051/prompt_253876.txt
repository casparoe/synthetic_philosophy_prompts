I have been thinking on my own about a tension in the justification for unelected judges overruling legislatures. In Federalist No. 78, Alexander Hamilton writes: "Some perplexity respecting the rights of the courts to pronounce legislative acts void, because contrary to the Constitution, has arisen from an imagination that the doctrine would imply a superiority of the judiciary to the legislative power." His surrounding argument, as I understand it, is that the court is not asserting its own will against the legislature; it is giving effect to the people's earlier constitutional act, which stands above both the court and the legislature. That suggests the practice depends on the court's being a faithful agent of the constitution rather than another political actor. I want to test that with an imagined arrangement. A country's constitution protects a list of personal rights. Its constitutional court may invalidate ordinary statutes that infringe those rights, and its judges hold office for life. But the constitution also allows the legislature, after a court invalidation, to wait one year and then re-enact exactly the same statute by a simple majority, adding a preamble that says the law is enacted notwithstanding the court's decision. The re-enacted statute is then immune from further constitutional challenge for ten years, after which the court may review it again as if the earlier case had not occurred. In practice the legislature re-enacts nearly every invalidated statute in this way, and the court almost never reviews the re-enacted versions, saying the later enactment is a new fact it must respect. Does this arrangement still qualify as the judicial review Hamilton was defending, judged by the criteria in the passage and the argument around it? I would like you to set out those criteria concretely, work through the arrangement one criterion at a time, and say where the criteria themselves leave the question undetermined rather than pretending the case is obvious. Please also say how confident you are in the overall verdict and identify the single step in your reasoning you are least sure about. Write it as continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
