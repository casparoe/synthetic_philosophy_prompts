Working on a paper about supervaluationism--the view that a sentence is true iff true on all admissible precisifications of its vague terms, false iff false on all of them, and neither otherwise. Here are the objections I've already collected, so please don't just feed me one of these:

(1) Hidden sharp boundaries. The sentence "there is an n such that n grains makes a heap and n+1 doesn't" is true on every admissible precisification, so the view says there is a sharp cutoff while also implying no particular n is the cutoff. It commits you to a boundary it also says you can't state or know.

(2) Higher-order vagueness. The account explains truth in terms of admissible precisifications, but whether a precisification is admissible is itself a vague matter, so the sorites just reappears in the metalanguage.

(3) Non-compositionality. A disjunction can be supertrue even though neither disjunct is supertrue, and an existential can be supertrue even though no instance is supertrue. The semantic value of a compound stops being a function of the semantic values of its parts.

(4) The logic worry. Supervaluationism keeps all the classical tautologies, but once you add a "definitely" operator, ordinary inference rules like conditional proof, proof by cases, and contraposition can fail. So it buys back classical theorems without giving you a classical way of reasoning with borderline sentences.

I want a new objection to supervaluationism: not one of those four, not a rewording or special case of any of them, and not a combination of two or more. Lay it out in enough detail that I can see what the objection actually is, then tell me honestly whether you think it's any good.
