I'm a novelist, so I spend a lot of time constructing selves out of sentences, and in my circles people lean hard on the idea that a person just is the story they tell themselves about themselves — you're the protagonist of an inner narration, and the practical upshot is supposed to be that you can change by rewriting the story: new point of view, edit the backstory, recast the villain, all that. But I've also been reading about the view that the self is a model the brain runs, and the key bit is that it's transparent — you don't experience the model as a model, you just experience yourself as directly being the one who sees and acts. Those two pictures seem to be doing different work, and I want to know how far the story analogy actualy holds once you hold it up against the transparent-model view. Give me the correspondence table — narrator, plot, audience, revision, continuity, whatever else belongs in it — then the places where it breaks down most badly. I especially want the disanalogies that matter for the edit-your-own-story conclusion as opposed to the ones that are harmless. Does that conclusion survive the disanalogies, or is it being carried by the metaphor the whole time? And if the story picture is mostly carrying it, what's a better analogy?
