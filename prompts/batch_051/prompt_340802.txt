I'm putting together a one-page statement of my commitments for a formal epistemology reading group, and before I circulate it I'd like someone to stress-test the package for internal coherence. The group is comfortable with Bayesian vocabulary, so technical terms are fine. Here is the view I keep wanting to endorse, stated as precisely as I can:

1. Jeffrey conditionalization is the correct general rule for updating my degrees of belief when evidence arrives without making any proposition certain.
2. I accept radical probabilism: no ordinary empirical proposition ever has credence exactly zero or exactly one, and an update can begin with a shift in confidence across a partition; the shift does not need to be justified by some underlying certain observation.
3. The order in which I receive two pieces of uncertain evidence should make no difference to the credence I end up with, as long as the same evidence arrives; if order matters in principle, my rule is tracking something about my biography rather than about the evidence.
4. I accept a Reflection-style principle: my present credence in a proposition should equal my expectation of my future credence in it, and I should not now regard my future self as systematically less reliable than my present self.
5. Forgetting, in the ordinary sense of coming to trust my earlier memory less—including cases where I can predict in advance that my memory will degrade—should be handled inside this same updating framework rather than treated as a special epistemic event.

I honestly cannot tell where the strain in this set is. Please go through the commitments pairwise and tell me which ones are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each tension is. Rank the tensions rather than treating them all as equally serious, specify the cheapest repair for each, and say which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent.
