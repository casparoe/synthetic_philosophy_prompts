The founding deed of the (fictional) Harland Relief Trust, executed in 1883, contains the following clause, which still controls every grant the Trust makes:

"The income of this Trust shall be applied by the Trustees to the relief of such persons as are in sober and industrious circumstances and whose need, in the judgment of the Trustees, arises from misfortune rather than from improvidence."

The last of the original Trustees died in the 1940s. Since a court-approved reorganization in 2001, the Trust has had no human trustees; an algorithm trained on the decisions of earlier trustees scores each applicant on signals that include "external cause of hardship" and "voluntary departure from employment." A bookkeeper with twenty years of steady work left her position to become a self-employed decorative painter. Her one-person painting business survived for three years and failed only after a prolonged public-health closure; she has now spent her savings and needs short-term relief. The algorithm ranks her just below the grant line, mainly because the closure followed a voluntary departure.

Does the clause require the Trust to refuse her application, or does it permit a grant? Address the question under (a) the plain words of the clause, (b) its purpose, (c) what the founders would have said if asked to rule on this case, and (d) what a reasonable modern reader would take the text to permit. Then say which approach should govern here and why, and whether the clause is best described as continuing to apply a reason that no current person actually holds. Lead with your bottom line, and keep the response to no more than 650 words.
