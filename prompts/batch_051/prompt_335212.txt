On my team we model domain facts for a knowledge graph as triples of the form (subject, predicate, object) — for example, (Alice, loves, Bob). A colleague recently argued that this is more than a storage detail. He said it dissolves the old puzzle he called Bradley's regress: the worry that if a relation has to connect itself to the things it relates, then you need a second relation to make that connection, and then a third, and so on without end. In a triple store, he argued, the relation is simply a first-class item sitting beside the subject and object inside the row, and the row itself is the fact. Nothing is left over that needs to "glue" the pieces together. He then generalized: any theory of facts or propositions that treats a relation as a kind of connector requiring further connectors is making a category mistake, and a graph database's edge is a better picture of how facts hold together than the philosophers' relation.

I have no formal philosophy background, so I cannot tell how much of this mapping is carrying the argument. I would like you to audit the analogy: a fact or proposition is like a triple (or an edge, or a row in a relational table). Be explicit about what corresponds to what, where the datastore picture quietly imports features the philosophical picture lacks, which disanalogies actually matter for my colleague's conclusion, and whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table. If there is a better computing-flavored analogy, say what it is and why it is better.
