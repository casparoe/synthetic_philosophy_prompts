I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so this might be half baked. I spent a month working on a data retention policy at work and somehow ended up reading Margalit and Rieff on the ethics of memory, and now I'm stuck in an argument that seems to prove too much. I want you to tell me where it gives.

(1) When a serious wrong has victims, the community that can remember it has a duty to remember it — maybe not every detail, but the wrong itself, publicly and in some ongoing form.
(2) That duty doesn't come with an expiry date. A massacre from 120 years ago isn't owed less remembrance than one from 20 years ago just becuase it's further back.
(3) So the set of wrongs we're duty-bound to remember only ever grows. Nothing ages out of it.
(4) But actual public memory is finite. A community can't actively hold an unlimited number of wrongs, with their names and texture, before the older ones blur into background noise or become archived entries nobody consults.
(5) If the set of obligatory remembrances grows without bound while the capacity to actively hold them stays finite, then eventually the duty demands more than can be done.
(6) Ought implies can: if you cannot do a thing, it can't be required of you.
(7) So the original duty can't be what it looked like. Forgetting must sometimes be permissible, even for real wrongs.

But here's the part that makes me queasy. The same move can be run against any particular wrong you point at. Pick any injustice and insist this one deserves remembrance. The argument answers: the total set is already past capacity, so some wrong has to be dropped, and you've given no reason it shouldn't be this one. Yet if there's no principle for picking which wrongs may be forgotten, then we seem to be saying: forgetting some is required or at least permitted, but we can never say which. That feels less like a duty and more like a shrug.

So where exactly does this thing go wrong? I'd like it if premise (6) survived, more than premise (1), but I'll pay what the argument costs. Give me a committed diagnosis: name the step or the hidden slide, say why it looked fine, and tell me what I'm left holding if I reject it. Don't give me a menu of options.
