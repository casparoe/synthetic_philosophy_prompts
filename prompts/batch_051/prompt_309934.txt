I'm a journalist who covers AI and ideas, and I'm trying to get clear on something before I write about it. The standard argument I keep running into, which people loosely trace back to ought-implies-can, goes roughly like this: an entity can be a moral agent only if it can act on its judgments; if it can't do anything about the situations it evaluates, it's not an agent in the relevant sense, just a very articulate spectator. So an AI that can't act on its verdicts is automatically off the hook, morally speaking. That seemed right to me until I started playing with current language models. They can produce genuinely nuanced moral appraisals, weigh competing considerations, and revise their verdict when you show them an inconsistency — but they've been deliberately built with no effectors, no persistent preferences, no way to carry any of it out. So now I'm not sure whether the old argument just settles the case, or whether this development puts pressure on one of its premises. Specifically, is the pressure on the idea that the capacity to act is what makes a moral judgment significant, or on the assumption that moral agency is all-or-nothing? And if the argument needs repair, what's the weaker version that survives — something like, they can be moral reasoners without being moral agents? I'd like to keep the conceptual question seperate from the policy one; I want to know what actually happens to the argument.
