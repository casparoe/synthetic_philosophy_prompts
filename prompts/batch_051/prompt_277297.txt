I was reading Hume on promises in A Treatise of Human Nature, 3.2.5, and this passage has been bugging me:

"THAT the rule of morality, which enjoins the performance of promises, is not natural, will sufficiently appear from these two propositions, which I proceed to prove, viz. that a promise wou'd not be intelligible, before human conventions had establish'd it; and that even if it were intelligible, it wou'd not be attended with any moral obligation."

I tried to put his argument into steps so I could check if it actually goes through. Heres what I get:

(1) If promising is natural, then the words "I promise" must express some act of the mind, and the obligation of a promise must attach to that mental act.
(2) The act is not a resolution to perform the action, because a bare resolution does not create an obligation.
(3) It is not a desire to perform the action, because you can be bound by a promise even when you desire not to do the thing.
(4) It is not willing the promised action itself, because the will only concerns present actions and promises concern future ones.
(5) So if promising is natural, the only candidate left is willing the obligation itself.
(6) But the will cannot create a new obligation, because obligations depend on moral sentiments, and the will cannot create new sentiments.
(7) So promising cannot naturally bind; promises are invented and get their force from convention.

I get why the conclusion is attractive, but I feel like step 6 is quietly doing all the work and the earlier steps are just there to force us into it. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and does it fail because of something in that step or because step 5 is a false choice?
