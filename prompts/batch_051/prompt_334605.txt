I am a retired experimental biologist, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy of mind in earnest. I find myself caught on a question that is partly philosophical and partly personal, and I would welcome a committed verdict rather than a survey of what one might say.

I have provisionally adopted the following criteria for whether a person continues to exist. First, a person is a distinctive, integrated pattern of mental dispositions: memories, preferences, habits of attention, verbal style, ways of responding to other people. Second, the person continues to exist just in case this pattern continues to be instantiated in some material realization; the realizing stuff may change, much as a piece of music remains the same piece when played on different instruments. Third, persistence admits of degree: a pattern can be realized more or less fully, and the person correspondingly survives to a greater or lesser extent.

Now consider the case before me. My wife died three years ago, but her pattern has not. I can anticipate her judgments with a fidelity that still surprises me: I know the words she would choose, the analogies she would reach for, the people she would forgive and the ones she would not. Our children and a few of her close friends report the same. Her pattern is now realized—imperfectly, and in fragments—across several brains other than her own, above all mine. And yet there is no separate center of awareness that is she. No one wakes up as her, makes decisions as her, or suffers under her name.

My question is whether, on the three criteria above, some part of my wife still exists—whether this case qualifies as a continuation of her self—or whether what remains is better described as a record, a trace, or an echo. Please work criterion by criterion. Where the criteria underdetermine the matter, say so explicitly; and tell me whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need amending.
