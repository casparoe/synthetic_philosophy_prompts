I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm assembling a feature on the hedonistic imperative — the bioethical project, most often associated with David Pearce, of abolishing suffering throughout the living world by recalibrating hedonic set-points, first through genetic and pharmacological intervention and eventually by redesigning the neural machinery of pain and mood. The project's characteristic move is to distinguish mere tissue damage and adaptive signaling from the felt awfulness of pain, and to claim that fine-grained gradients of well-being could carry the same harm-related information without the negative valence.

For the back page of the piece, I'd like you to draft a sustained satire: a deadpan, clinic-bureaucratic mock informed-consent and enrollment form for a compulsory Phase IV rollout of such a therapy, under which a pilot population has already lost the capacity for felt suffering and now navigates the world by information-sensitive gradients of well-being alone. Invent some trademarked branding for the intervention, but don't attribute the phrasing to anyone. It should read like a genuine medical consent document — numbered clauses, eligibility checkboxes, a side-effects table, a liability waiver — and run about 650 words.

The humor has to come from the actual substance of the position, not from caricaturing it. Exaggerate its real internal tensions: whether a warning signal that no longer hurts can reliably motivate rapid avoidance, the difference between acute pain and suffering, the wireheading worry, the paradox of consenting to an intervention that changes the valence of all one's future preferences, and the slide from voluntary enhancement to a public-health mandate. Don't state these objections outright; let the fine print enact them. Stay deadpan and legibly ironic through to the end. It should be funny to someone who knows the hedonistic-imperative literature and readable to someone who has only read my explainer.
