John Stuart Mill argues in the second chapter of Utilitarianism that the principle of utility can accept that pleasures differ in more than amount: some pleasures, he holds, are higher in quality and not merely larger in quantity. He supports this by analogy with other objects of evaluation, where quality is weighed alongside quantity, so it would be odd to make pleasure the one case where only amount matters. Interpret this move. Is Mill's qualitative ranking of pleasures a stable monism about value—one ultimate principle with internal complexity—or does it introduce a plurality of standards that no longer reduce to a single currency? Compare Mill's position with a value pluralist view on which goods such as liberty, justice, and beauty can conflict without a common measure or a single right resolution. What is the strongest argument that the two positions are closer than they look, and at what precise point do they come apart?
