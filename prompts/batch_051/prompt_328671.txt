I am a novelist, not a philosopher, though I am fluent in the technical vocabulary of narrative craft: focalization and point of view, free indirect discourse, diegesis and mimesis, scene and summary, interiority, description, ekphrasis, and defamiliarization. I want to understand Molyneux's problem in those terms. As I understand it, the problem is this: a person blind from birth has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch alone. Sight is then restored. Before reaching out to touch them, the person sees the two objects side by side at arm's length and is asked which is the cube and which is the sphere. One tradition says the person cannot tell, because the visual and tactile ideas have never been connected; another says the person can, or could in principle, because both senses track the same spatial structure; modern experimental work complicates either clean answer.

Restate this problem, and the main positions one might take on it, in the vocabulary of narrative craft. Treat the moment of first sight as a scene to be written. For each position — the empiricist denial, the rationalist or nativist affirmation, and the qualified modern view — say how it would be expressed in terms of focalization, free indirect discourse, description, interiority, and the limits of what prose can render. Then give me an honest residue report. Which elements of Molyneux's problem translate cleanly into narrative craft, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in that vocabulary at all? Is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, or does it show that the philosophical problem is less clearly posed than it first appears? I am especially interested in whether narrative craft can mark the difference between correctly identifying a shape and undergoing a new visual experience, and whether that difference matters to the original question. Do not give me citations or a reading list; I want the translation and your assessment of what does not survive it.
