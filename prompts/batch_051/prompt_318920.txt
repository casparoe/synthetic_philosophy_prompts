I have been thinking about the French Republican calendar — the calendar introduced during the Revolution, which replaced the Gregorian calendar in France for a little over a decade. You probably know the contours: twelve months of thirty days, each named for a seasonal or atmospheric phenomenon rather than a Roman deity or emperor; a ten-day week called the décade, which did away with Sunday; five or six complementary days set aside at the end of the year; and a short-lived experiment with decimal time. What interests me most is the dating system itself: the revolutionaries refused to keep the Christian era as the hinge of the year, and Year I was backdated to the autumn equinox of 1792, so that time itself would seem to begin from the founding of the Republic. Napoleon's government later repealed the calendar and restored the Gregorian one. I know the plain history; what I want is the conceptual analysis.

Consider this as a case study for a particular thesis about calendars: that a calendar is not a neutral measuring instrument but a piece of normative infrastructure — an arrangement of time that declares what time is for. On this thesis, the choice of epoch is the thesis in its purest form, because it selects the event after which all other events are dated, the one point around which political, religious, and natural time are made to turn. The internal structure of the calendar — the number and names of the months, the length of the week, the placement of rest days and festivals — encodes a further set of judgments about which rhythms are natural, which are merely customary, and which human activities deserve to be carved into recurring time.

Analyze the French Republican calendar through that lens. Specifically: what does the thesis say the designers were doing, and on its own terms what should they have done? Which features of the episode does it illuminate — the republican epoch, the equinoctial new year, the décade, the seasonal month names, the complementary days — and which features does it misdescribe or leave unexplained, such as the practical coordination costs with every neighboring state that kept the Gregorian week, the mismatch between a decimal-rationalist calendar and the work rhythms of a mostly agrarian population, or the fact that the calendar survived as rhetoric much better than as a functioning civil institution? Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about the thesis? Is the revolutionary calendar a confirmation of the claim that dating systems are political all the way down, or does its repeal reveal that the thesis overstates what a calendar can carry — that a calendar must first solve a coordination problem before it can succeed as an assertion of values? Be disciplined about the mapping: distinguish the choice of epoch from the choice of internal structure, say what counts as evidence for the thesis in a case like this, and flag the points where the thesis is silent rather than making every feature of the calendar do one kind of work.
