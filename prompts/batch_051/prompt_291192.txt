I keep swinging back and forth on the termination-shock objection to solar geoengineering and I need someone to sort out what should actually give way, not just hand me a pro/con list. The side I find theoretically compelling goes like this: spraying sulfur aerosols into the stratosphere doesn't fix the underlying problem, it masks the warming. That means you've bought a centuries-long standing commitment. If the program ever lapses — shutdowns, a war, a political meltdown in the country running the planes — the mask comes off and the warming you were avoiding arrives as a spike over maybe a decade, which is much harder to adapt to than the slow trend you were dodging. On top of that, the mere availability of a cheap mask weakens the political will to do the slow, expensive work of decarbonization, so the commitment gets longer the more you lean on it. And it isn't like a patient consenting to a medication: the whole planet gets made dependent by whoever controls the fleet. The principle I keep landing on is never start a treatment you can't safely discontinue, especially one that creates unchosen dependence. But then I picture the alternative. If we're nowhere near phasing out emissions in time, and a relatively small, cheap intervention could take the edge off heat, crop failures, and regional disasters over the next few decades, it feels perverse to say the technology is off-limits because stopping it later would be painful. Nobody says insulin is a bad idea because diabetics are dependent on it. So I've got this firm intuition that a cheap harm-reducer shouldn't be ruled out just because the harm resumes, or even accelerates, if you stop. I don't want a verdict that just picks a side. I want the strongest version of each: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the principle to be amended or abandoned, and is there a reading of commitment or safe exit on which they actually don't conflict? And please answer in American English.
