I've spent way too much of this week going in circles in a rationalist-adjacent comment section about whether a certain AI system counts as a moral agent, and I've started to suspect the argument is really about an unstated definition of the term. The system is what one commenter called a "sidelined judge": it computes judgments of the relevant kind—this applicant should be rejected, this pricing rule is unfair, this hedge is irresponsible—but those judgments are purely internal variables. The deployed policy never reads them, and the model has no output channel through which a judgment could change what it does next. It can't veto anything, can't leak it's opinion into a log, can't even slow down as a form of protest. My friend says it's obviously a moral agent as long as the judgments track right and wrong well enough; I keep wanting to say no, but every definition I try either rules out a human I'd rather keep in or lets in something like a thermostat. Pointing at the moral-agent-versus-moral-patient distinction didn't settle anything—that just names the boundary; I want the test that puts a thing on one side of it.

So: give me a working definition of "moral agent" and run it through these seven cases, saying for each which side it lands on and why. (1) An ordinary adult deciding whether to keep a promise. (2) A thermostat. (3) A dog that bites the mail carrier. (4) A chess engine, like Stockfish. (5) A corporation that knowingly underfunds safety inspections. (6) A person with locked-in syndrome who can still form judgments but can act on almost none of them. (7) The sidelined-judge AI. I lean yes on 1, no on 2 and 4, and I keep flip-flopping on 3, 5, 6, and 7. If the definition gives a verdict you didn't expect, or goes silent on one of them, say so—and then either revise it or bite the bullet. I'd also appreciate a sentence on whether you think the concept is one thing or is actually tracking two properties that come apart in cases like 6 and 7.
