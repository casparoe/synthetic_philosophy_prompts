I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm writing a scene in which one of my characters, a psychologist who thinks phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, tries to sort another character's philosophical commitments using a kind of informal decision tree he keeps in his head. I want the tree to be real enough to steal for the scene, not just a list of labelled positions.

The scene is built around a passage from Keith Frankish's paper "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" — specifically the part where he argues, roughly, that introspective representation doesn't passively detect phenomenal properties but actively creates a misleading appearance of them, like an instrument panel that misrepresents its own cause. I don't have the exact wording in front of me and I'm not asking you to quote it back, but the first branch of the tree should make someone confront that passage directly: is it even possible for introspection to be that wrong about what it seems to reveal, or does the very seeming guarantee that there is something qualitative there?

So what I'd like is a decision tree, maybe 6 to 8 leaves, that takes a person from first-person reports and concrete cases to a named position: strong illusionism (Frankish-style), weak illusionism, type-B physicalism, conservative qualia realism, panpsychism, and mysterianism should be leaves; add one more only if the logical space actually demands it. Phrase every node as a question someone could answer about their own experience, a thought experiment, or a concrete case without already knowing the labels. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, say briefly whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate. I'm less interested in having the positions defined for me than in the diagnostic questions, but the leaves do need to be recognizable enough that a reader can hear the difference in my character's dialogue.
