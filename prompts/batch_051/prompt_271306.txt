I am a PhD student in philosophy of action and decision theory, and I keep returning to the following problem. Buridan's ass — the animal placed exactly between two equally adequate provisions, with no stronger reason to take one over the other — is usually treated as a puzzle about will and reasons, but it can be recast as a claim with empirical content: when an agent is genuinely indifferent between two options, the selection that occurs is not the agent's doing in any interesting sense; it is arational, a happening inside the agent rather than a choice. I want to know how much of that claim an experiment could get a grip on, and how much would remain a matter of redescription.

Design the strongest experiment you can to separate three accounts of choice under apparent indifference. Use human subjects choosing repeatedly between two payoff-equivalent options whose presentation varies in both obvious and subtle ways (position, colour, latency, stimulus history), recording choices, response times, confidence ratings, and post-task reports of whether subjects felt a leaning, followed a tie-breaking policy, or had no experience of choosing at all. Alongside this, consider a reinforcement-learning agent initialized with exactly symmetric value estimates and explicit random tie-breaking, and say which features of the human data would warrant the claim that the human system implements something like that tie-break rather than a hidden preference or a reportable heuristic.

For each of the three accounts — genuine indifference resolved arationally, a hidden or introspectively unavailable preference, and a stable explicit tie-breaking policy — specify the pattern of results that would favour it, and then say which comparisons genuinely separate the accounts and which merely re-describe the same data under different vocabulary.

Now address what I suspect no experiment reaches. Suppose the cleanest pattern emerges and we are tempted to say that a subject really chose without preference. Does that conclusion rest on the operationalization of preference — response times, confidence, post-hoc report, stochasticity of choice — and could a sceptic always insist that a preference too fine for our instruments was doing the work? Is that residual unobservability a limitation of the experimental program, or a defect in the notion of choosing without preference itself? Finally, would it be more honest to retire Buridan's puzzle in favour of the empirical question of how tie-breaking is implemented, or does the empirical question quietly presuppose the very notion of arbitrary choice it claims to test?
