I sit on the Capital Allocation Committee of the Northgate Resilience Authority, a public body charged with spending a one-time state appropriation on infrastructure to protect three neighborhoods against possible extreme-weather failures in the coming season. The appropriation must be committed by next Tuesday, and once committed, the plan cannot be revised mid-season.

The three neighborhoods differ in ways that matter. Harbor Flats is the most physically exposed: it sits below sea level and has the oldest drainage system, and a failure there would strand roughly 4,000 residents, disproportionately elderly. Millbrook is the most populous, with about 15,000 residents and the only emergency shelter serving the surrounding area. Alderwood has the highest concentration of medically fragile and low-income households but has so far seen fewer failures than the other two.

Staff have prepared three investment packages, each spending the full appropriation:

- Plan M would harden drainage and backup power in Harbor Flats alone. On staff's analysis it produces the best worst-case outcome of the three plans: the single neighborhood at greatest risk is better protected than under any alternative.
- Plan E would spread smaller improvements across all three neighborhoods, sized so that each dollar buys the largest reduction in aggregate risk if the three main failure scenarios are treated as equally likely. It fully protects none of the neighborhoods in a severe event.
- Plan R would retrofit the existing system to minimize, across scenarios, the largest gap between what is achieved and what the best plan for that scenario would have achieved. It is designed to make the Authority regret its choice as little as possible whichever scenario materializes, but it deliberately leaves Harbor Flats exposed to a bad, though not maximally bad, outcome.

Three years ago, after a near-miss in Harbor Flats, the Authority adopted a resolution requiring that, where probabilities cannot be assigned to possible outcomes, proposals be assessed by their worst-case outcomes. Our climate models still cannot responsibly assign probabilities to this season's failure scenarios.

Our enabling legislation says only that the money must be spent to protect life, health, and the continuity of essential services; it says nothing about how to weigh neighborhoods against one another.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting. It should recommend one of the three plans, or a clearly specified modification of one, and open with a one-paragraph bottom-line motion the Authority could vote on, including any time frame for subsequent review or monitoring the Authority should commit to. The justification should explain which rule for choosing without probabilities — maximin, minimax regret, or treating the scenarios as equally likely, or some explicitly constrained combination of them — is the right one for this body, given its charge, its prior resolution, and the differences among the neighborhoods, rather than merely reciting what the rules say. It should also give an honest statement of the strongest consideration favoring the plan that is ultimately rejected, and explain why that consideration does not carry the decision.
