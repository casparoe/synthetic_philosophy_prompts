We spent a week in my ethics seminar on whether the numbers count when you’re deciding whom to save, and I wrote up the argument below for a response paper. Now I think it proves too much, but I can't find the step to blame.

(1) In a one-versus-five rescue case, if you ought to save the five rather than the one, it can only be because five deaths are worse than one death.

(2) But for five deaths to be worse than one, there would have to be something bad about the five-deaths outcome over and above what is bad for each of the five. And no one suffers five deaths; each of the five loses exactly one life, just like the one.

(3) So the losses of the five don't add up to a bigger loss. There is no unit of "five deaths" that can outweigh "one death"; the only units there are are individual lives, and those are all equal.

(4) Therefore the mere fact that more people would die gives no reason to save the larger group.

(5) And the reasoning doesn't stop at five: it is no worse, in just the same sense, for a million people to die than for one.

(6) So even in a million-versus-one case, the numbers give no reason. But that seems absurd.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd prefer to hang on to (2)'s picture of harm — that harm has to be harm to some-one — but maybe that's exactly what's doing the damage. Also, could you separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and not present a contested view as the consensus?
