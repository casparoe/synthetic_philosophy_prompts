Okay here's the thing. I'm a PhD student in political economy, mostly working on multipolar traps and races to the bottom, and I keep going back and forth on a concrete competition-policy proposal. Could you write a dialogue between two characters for me? Here's the setup.

A national competition authority is considering what it calls a "standards coordination exemption." In any industry where at least four firms agree, those firms may collectively set a floor under some socially costly form of competition — e.g., minimum pay and maximum shift length for subcontracted gig workers, or a maximum emissions intensity per unit of output — and that agreement is immune from antitrust prosecution for five years. Renewal requires audited evidence that the agreement has not drifted into price coordination beyond what the floor itself entails. Any signatory can leave with six months' notice.

Character A is an institutional economist who thinks this is one of the only workable levers for breaking a multipolar trap: no firm can unilaterally improve standards because it gets undercut on price, but all would prefer the world in wich everyone improves. Character B is an antitrust lawyer who thinks the scheme is either useless or dangerous: in competitive markets the exit clause means a low-cost entrant defects and undercuts everyone; in concentrated markets it's just a cartel with extra paperwork. Neither should be a strawman. Make them respond to each other's actual points, and have them dig into at least three things: whether the exit clause makes collective discipline impossible or just screens out fake cooperation; how a regulator could distinguish a real standards floor from disguised price-fixing; and whether competition would migrate to quality and innovation, or just to evasion. Don't tie it up too neatly — leave the residual disagreement visible.
