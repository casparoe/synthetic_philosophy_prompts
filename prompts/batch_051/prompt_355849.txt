The debate over Frege's puzzle about identity statements has been running for well over a century, and in its modern guise it has looked nearly static for decades. On one side, Millians hold that a proper name's semantic contribution is exhausted by its referent, so that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' expresses the same singular proposition as 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and any apparent difference must be explained pragmatically or psychologically. On the other, neo-Fregeans hold that names contribute a sense or mode of presentation, and that this finer-grained component explains why a rational, competent speaker can find the first identity statement informative and the second trivial. The two camps largely share the data — the two identity statements differ in cognitive value, and someone who believes that Hesperus appears in the evening may doubt that Phosphorus does — yet each side regards the other's handling of those data as strained or ad hoc. I am after a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another verdict for a side. My working hypothesis is that the two parties are answering different questions under a single word: Millianism as a view about truth-conditional content and reference, neo-Fregeanism as a view about what captures cognitive significance, with 'meaning' doing double duty. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a genuine incompatibility underneath? Either way, what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive but about the explanatory target of semantics, methodological, or a clash of bedrock starting points — and what would count as progress: a division of theoretical labour, an experimental finding from psycholinguistics, a formal constraint on belief ascription, or, if the question is ill-posed, an argument that no progress should be expected?
