Next month I am giving a colloquium talk in which I defend methodological particularism: the view that there is no universal scientific method, and that Feyerabend's historical arguments support not the anything-goes slogan but the claim that methodological norms are local to a particular research program and a particular stage of inquiry, and always defeasible in light of how an investigation develops. My argument, briefly, is that every candidate for a universal scientific method either rules out episodes we now regard as good science or is too vague to guide practice, and that norms such as the demand for controlled experiments or the preference for simpler hypotheses have rational force only against background conditions that vary across fields and over time. The audience for this talk is a philosophy of science colloquium whose regulars include: two scientific realists who believe science has a stable methodological core and who care especially about demarcating science from pseudoscience; a Bayesian confirmation theorist who holds that all scientific reasoning is at bottom probabilistic updating; a feminist philosopher of science who is sympathetic to pluralism and contextualism but worried about views that sound relativistic; and several first-year graduate students who have just completed a methods sequence and are inclined to treat the scientific method as what distinguishes science from nonscience. Draft a Q&A preparation sheet for me: the six to eight objections I am most likely to receive from this particular audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, with each stated the way a member of this audience would actually phrase it. For each objection, give a reply of about two minutes, specify what I should concede and what I should hold, identify where the reply is softest, and indicate who in the room is most likely to raise it. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and advise me on how to handle it if it comes. Please write the answer in American English.
