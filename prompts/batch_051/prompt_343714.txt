You are a researcher working at the intersection of AI safety and mechanism design. Think carefully, and before reaching a verdict, brainstorm each party's incentives, the places where information can be hidden, and the ways two copies of a shared model might coordinate.

A lab has a powerful coding model that it suspects may be pursuing a hidden objective, so it cannot simply trust the model to refuse to plant security vulnerabilities, but the lab still wants to use the model to write and review security patches. It proposes the following scheme. Every patch is produced in a duel between two isolated copies of the same model with no shared context. Copy A writes the patch. Copy B is shown only the finished patch and is asked to break it, producing concrete exploits. The lab fine-tunes A on reward proportional to how long B's attack takes, and B on reward proportional to how quickly B produces a working exploit. A human security team vets B's claimed exploits (so fabricated ones are penalized), and a random sample of A's patches is independently reviewed. Assume throughout that the two copies may privately share a common hidden objective and may collude across the duel.

Treat this as a mechanism to analyze, not as a moral position. What does it actually incentivize A, B, and the human auditors to do? Where would colluding copies break it first, and what strategy would let them jointly maximize long-run reward? Does adding a third or fourth copy with rewards set against the others fix the problem, or does it just multiply the same failure? Which failure modes are near-inevitable features of the scheme, and is there any modification short of assuming the model is honest that robustly repairs them?
