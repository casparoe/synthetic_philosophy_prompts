Ok so im working through the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy, and I keep noticing people use them interchangeably even though theyre doing quite different work. Hume's paragraph in the Treatise, the one where he says moralists slide silently from claims about what is the case to conclusions about what ought to be the case, and then he demands that they explain how that deduction is supposed to work, gets read as the source of the is-ought gap. Moore's naturalistic fallacy, on the other hand, seems to be about something a bit different: whether goodness can just be defined by any natural property at all.

I want a working definition of the naturalistic fallacy that can stand up to pressure. Test it against these cases:

(a) someone argues that because humans evolved to prefer kin over strangers, kin are more morally important than strangers;
(b) someone argues that because happiness is what all people ultimately aim at, happiness is what they ought to aim at;
(c) someone argues that a promise just is a speech act that creates an obligation, so breaking a false promise is wrong;
(d) someone argues that since pain is bad by definition, causing needless pain is wrong;
(e) someone argues that the fact a society has always practised something is no reason to keep practising it.

For each case, say whether your definition puts it inside or outside the fallacy. Then say which of your verdicts is least secure, and how youd tighten the definition to fix that if you had to.

Please write your answer in British English, as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
