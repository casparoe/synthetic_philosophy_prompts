I've been going down a history-of-physics rabbit hole and keep running into the same pattern, but I don't have a name for it and I'm not sure it even is one thing. Whenever a big theory gets replaced, it's almost never a clean break: something always survives, and what survives is usually the mathematical machinery, while the story about what the world is made of gets thrown out. Fresnel worked out reflection and refraction by treating light as vibrations in a solid elastic ether, and later Maxwell dropped the ether, but the Fresnel formulas for how much light bounces off a surface stuck around nearly unchanged. The Hamiltonian apparatus from classical mechanics got carried into quantum mechanics even though the underlying picture—little billiard balls with definite trajectories—was abandoned. The parts that die are the ontology: the ether, caloric, phlogiston, absolute space. The equations get kept; the stuff gets replaced.

I can see this might just be instrumentalism, or the pessimistic induction run backwards, or plain survivorship bias (maybe I only remember the cases where the math did survive). But it feels more specific than any of those: it's an asymmetry claim, that the relational or formal or quantitative content of a theory is the part actually latching onto the world, and the substance story is a disposable user interface. I figure there must be a term for this in the philosophy of science, but the adjacent terms don't quite fit. Does the pattern or the position have a name? If it does, what's the actual argument for it apart from pointing at examples, and how do you draw the line between the kept part and the discarded part in a principled way? Not every equation survives and not every bit of ontology dies, so the boundary can't be as clean as the slogan makes it sound. I care more about the carving then the label. If the whole pattern is just retrospective cherry-picking and the literature treats these as unrelated cases, say so straight rather than forcing a name onto it.
