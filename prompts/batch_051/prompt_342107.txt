I'm a therapist — mostly narrative therapy, a little ACT — and a few times a month I hit the same tangle. A client tells me, with total sincerity, that they are not the person who did the thing: not the person who made that vow, took on that debt, signed that DNR, hurt that relative. Sometimes it's after a conversion or a transition or a depression that finally lifted; sometimes after a brain injury or a long bereavement; sometimes just years of slow drift, no drama at all. The framework I keep thinking through is the idea that the self isn't a thing inside us but more like a center of gravity for a story — real in its effects, like a center of gravity is real, but not a substance, and something built partly by the story the person tells and partly by the stories other people keep telling about them. That dissolves the question of whether some hidden self-stuff survived the change, but it doesn't yet tell me what to do in the room.

So here's what I want: a decision procedure I can actually run in a 50-minute session, for deciding whether to treat the person-before and the person-now as the same center of narrative gravity or two different ones. Give me a flow of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient yes or no at each step, where the procedure stops with 'same self,' where it stops with 'different self,' and where it has to hand the call to a person because the theory just won't settle it. Assume neither I nor the client has any philosophy background. My supervisor is cautious and will only sign off on this if it's explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on two or three made-up clients, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure spits out the answer I'm going to hate — maybe it forces me to treat a client as the same self when they're desperate to be done with that person, or treat someone as a new self when that lets them off a hook I think they still own. Tell me where the procedure will systematically misfire.

The cases I actually see: a client who deconverted and says the person who made their marriage vows is dead; a client a year out from a stroke who tells a patchy, third-person story about their own past and says it feels like it happened to a stranger; a client whose family keeps holding them to a self-story they say they've explicitly repudiated, and who wants the procedure to give them permission to walk away from that person entirely.
