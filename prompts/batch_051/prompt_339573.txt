I'm a retired physicist — thirty-odd years in quantum optics, almost none of it foundations. Since I stopped working I've gotten really into philosophy of physics, and I let a friend talk me into giving a department colloquium next month on the Born rule problem in many-worlds. The position I'll be defending is that the Born rule does not have to be bolted on as an extra postulate. My rough line: once you grant that the wavefunction branches, a rational agent deciding before a measurement faces bets whose payoffs land in different branches. If her preferences obey the usual decision-theoretic axioms, and if she doesn't care about branch labels but only about what her successors experience inside each branch, then the only coherent weight she can put on a branch is it's squared amplitude. Any other weighting makes her preferences exploitable across branches, so the |amplitude|^2 rule isn't a fact about objective chances out there — it's the only set of degrees of belief a rational agent in a branching universe can hold.

The room is a physics department colloquium: mostly condensed matter and quantum information people, a few cosmologists who actually take Everett seriously, and two philosophers of physics who'll be in the audience. The physicists are hardline empiricists — they think probability means relative frequencies of measurement outcomes, they think interpretations are for after-hours beer, and their first reaction will be that if every outcome happens then probability talk is meaningless. The cosmologists are friendly to many-worlds but skeptical that my argument buys anything over just postulating the Born rule from the start. The philosophers know the foundational literature and will press on whether I've helped myself to a preferred basis, or to an 'I' that survives branching, before the argument even gets going.

So here's what I want: prepare me for the question period like a prep sheet. What objections am I actually going to get, in roughly the words these people will use? Order them by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, tell me which have real force, what to concede on the spot, what to hold the line on, and what I should say back in a minute or two — and be honest about where my reply is weakest. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and include the one objection I should be hoping nobody in that room asks.
