I run a human-factors group at an aerospace manufacturer. We are drafting design standards for a next-generation cockpit in which many functions now performed by pilots will be automated: fuel monitoring, fault detection, avionics configuration, flight-path management. I want a decision procedure, usable by working engineers who have never read Heidegger, for deciding when an automated function should withdraw into the background and when it must keep soliciting explicit attention, and for deciding how failures should announce themselves. The conceptual spine is Heidegger's distinction between readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit) and presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit), and his point that equipment discloses itself most sharply in breakdown. Translate that into a review checklist of roughly ten items, with pass/fail or evidence criteria, that can be run in an afternoon for each proposed automation feature. The checklist must specify (a) what we must know about a pilot's situation and skill before judging whether a function may disappear; (b) which failures must be brought to attention and which may pass silently, and why; (c) how the mode of presentation should alter in failure rather than merely adding an alarm; and (d) the hard cases where the distinction gives no clean answer or cuts against what pilots say they want. Then apply the checklist to three features: auto-trim, an automated engine-failure display, and a proposed silent autopilot that runs continuously and announces itself only on disconnection. At least one resulting answer should be one our engineers will dislike. Finally, state what the procedure cannot decide—for example, what counts as a meaningful skill rather than busywork—and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated.
