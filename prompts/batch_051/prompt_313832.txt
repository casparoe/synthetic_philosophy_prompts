In the chapter on chance in Henri Poincaré’s *Science and Method*, Poincaré reflects on the way a cause too small for us to notice can grow into an effect large enough to force itself on our attention; because the initial difference escapes our powers of discrimination, the later outcome strikes us as chance, even though the underlying evolution is law-governed. I’ve been turning this over while teaching a philosophy of physics unit on chaos, and I want to press on a common but suspicious inference that sometimes gets drawn from his observation. A colleague tells me the following argument captures the real lesson of Poincaré’s point, but I suspect it overreaches in a way I cannot quite pin down.

(1) In a deterministic system, the exact initial state fixes the entire future trajectory.
(2) In a chaotic deterministic system, initial states that are indistinguishable to any finite measurement can lead to macroscopically different futures.
(3) If two candidate initial states are indistinguishable to our measurements, then no observation made at the initial time can settle which trajectory will occur.
(4) If no observation made at the initial time can settle which trajectory will occur, then the future of the system is not predictable from our actual measurements.
(5) If the future of a system is not predictable from our actual measurements, then the future is not determined by the actual initial state.
(6) Therefore, in chaotic systems the actual initial state does not determine the future; the apparent chanciness Poincaré describes is real, not merely epistemic.

Is this argument valid? If it is, which premise should I defend against students who reject the conclusion? If it is not, which step fails, and what is the strongest reading on which that step is true? Does the argument faithfully develop Poincaré’s observation, or does it turn an epistemic point into an ontological one?
