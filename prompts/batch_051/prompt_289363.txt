As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been reading across military ethics and the psychology of risk, and I have settled on a working list of criteria for valour, which I take to be a martial virtue and therefore narrower than everyday courage. On my account, a valorous act must: (i) be undertaken against a credible threat of death or serious bodily harm; (ii) be deliberate and composed, not panicked or impulsive; (iii) be directed at a purposive adversary, not at an impersonal hazard such as a fire, a storm, or a disease; (iv) push back against that adversary in some way rather than merely enduring what it does; and (v) serve a good beyond the agent's private interest.

Now consider this case. A civilian epidemiologist in a country drifting into authoritarian rule refuses to falsify an outbreak dataset. She knows the regime has already made inconvenient people disappear; she declines offers to leave, publishes the true figures under her own name, and coordinates with researchers abroad, fully expecting to be arrested or worse. She is unarmed, holds no military or protective role, and her only counter-blow is the continued publication of accurate numbers.

Run this case through my criteria honestly, and tell me whether she counts as valorous, merely courageous, or something else. Where the criteria do not settle the matter, say whether that exposes a weakness in the criteria or in the case. I would also like to know whether the martial notion of valour can be stretched to the unarmed confrontation between a citizen and her own state without losing the very thing that made it a distinct virtue in the first place.
