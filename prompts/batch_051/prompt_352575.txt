Avicenna's flying man thought experiment, the one in his On the Soul (the psychological part of al-Shifa'), has been bugging me. For anyone who hasn't read it: he asks you to imagine someone created all at once as an adult, floating in empty air with his eyes veiled and his limbs spread so he isn't touching anything, with no sense of his own body and no memory of it. Avicenna says this person will still affirm his own existance, but won't affirm any part of his body. I want to use that as an argument that the self is not the body, but I don't trust one of the steps.

Here's the argument:
(1) A human created in that state can affirm his own existence.
(2) In that state he cannot affirm any part of his body, or his body as a whole.
(3) If he were his body, or any part of it, then in affirming his existence he would be affirming that body or that part.
(4) So, from (1) and (2), he is not his body or any part of it.
(5) Therefore the thing he affirms, the self, is distinct from the body.

I half-suspect (3) smuggles in a transparency assumption about self-identification, and maybe the argument only shows something about what the subject can say or notice, not about what he is. But I also worry that if the argument fails, Avicenna's whole point about the soul being directly known to itself goes with it. So: is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, what the strongest repaired version of the argument is, and whether that repaired version proves something weaker, stronger, or just different. If it is valid, tell me what exactly it does and does not establish about the soul's nature. Take a definite position and justify it; don't just lay out options.
