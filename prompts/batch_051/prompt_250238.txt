In my forecasting group, arguments about whether to update on each other's probability estimates keep ending the same way. Someone invokes Aumann's agreement theorem and says the upshot is that rational agents cannot agree to disagree, so if our numbers still differ after we have talked through our evidence, at least one of us must be failing at rationality. I suspect that one-liner is doing more work than the theorem can bear. Lay out the distinct precise claims that line could be expressing — for instance, claims about common priors, common knowledge of each other's posteriors, idealized versus bounded reasoning, and what rationality recommends rather than what it entails — and say for each whether it is a theorem, a truism, a substantive empirical claim, or false. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually license using it to shut down disagreement the way my group does.
