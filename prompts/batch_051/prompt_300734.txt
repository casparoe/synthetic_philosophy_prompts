I am a retired geologist who has recently begun reading political philosophy, and a habit I developed over four decades in science is now causing me difficulty.

The habit is this. When a question of public policy turns on empirical findings from a mature science, I set aside my own lay reading wherever it conflicts with the central tendency of the relevant expert community, and I let that expert position fix my factual beliefs. Further, when a non-expert continues to reject the consensus, I treat their persistence as evidence about their judgment in the domain rather than as evidence about the question. Last month this arose over a local ballot measure concerning the underground storage of spent nuclear fuel: I found myself discounting my neighbor's worries about groundwater because they contradicted the consensus of geochemists and hydrogeologists, although I could not reproduce the models myself.

A friend tells me this is not merely a personal epistemic policy but the underlying attitude of epistocracy, the view that the more knowledgeable should carry greater weight in political decisions. He argues that if I genuinely regard my neighbor's factual premises as inferior, I am already treating his vote as worth less, and I should either defend competence-weighted voting or abandon the habit. I want to resist that conclusion while keeping the habit.

Is the habit defensible on its own terms? Does consistency require me to endorse competence-weighted voting, or is there a principled line between deferring to experts in my own belief formation and distributing political power equally? If the habit as stated is too strong, what would a defensible version look like?
