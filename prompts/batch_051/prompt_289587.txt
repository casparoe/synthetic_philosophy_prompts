I keep getting myself tangled up in a question about what it means for a part of a living thing to have a 'function.' The obvious answer is that a heart's function is to pump blood because that's what it does that matters for staying alive. But then I start thinking about a heart that got transplanted into somebody: it wasn't shaped by natural selection inside that person's body, a surgeon put it there, and yet it still seems flatly right that its function in that body is to pump blood. And then there's the opposite kind of case, where a trait evolved doing one thing but is now used for another — like bird wings, which were once for something else and are now for flying, or feathers, which seem to have started out doing something other than powered flight before they got repurposed. When we ask what a trait is 'for' today, are we asking about its past (what it got selected for) or about its present (what it does in the organism's current economy, the way parts of a machine each do a job)? I've picked up somewhere that philosophers have two broad camps around this, something like a historical/etiological answer and a present-contribution/causal-role answer, but I don't really understand how they handle the hard cases: transplanted organs, exaptations, artificial body parts, and newly discovered organs whose history nobody knows yet. Could you explain each view, where they agree, and where exactly they come apart on cases like these? This isn't just word games to me, because it seems to touch whether a vestigial organ still has a function, and whether a titanium heart or an artificial limb does too. I'm not a biologist and I'm not after citations; I just want the conceptual shape of the thing, with as little jargon as you can get away with.
