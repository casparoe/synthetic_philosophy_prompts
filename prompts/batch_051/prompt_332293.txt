I work on an autonomous system that keeps a probability distribution over a bunch of state variables and updates it from noisy camera and lidar feeds. The standard move is plain Bayes: when you observe E, you condition on E, which means E's probability goes to 1. But our observations are never actually certain, and a friend pointed me to Jeffrey conditionalization, where an observation doesn't push anything to 1—it just shifts the probabilities on some partition of possibilities (say "obstacle ahead" goes from .6 to .8) and holds the conditional probabilities on that partition fixed (P_new(H) = sum over the partition of P_old(H | E_i) * P_new(E_i)).

The claim I keep running into is that Jeffrey conditionalization is the right rule for uncertain evidence—not just a convenient approximation because our sensors are noisy, but the normatively correct update. The pushback I've seen is that this is just bookkeeping: you can always redescribe the evidence as a proposition about your own perceptual state (e.g., "the sensor returned this exact waveform"), and that proposition you can be certain of, so strict conditioning on the redescription is enough and Jeffrey's rule is redundant.

I honestly can't tell weather this is an empirical claim about how people update, a normative claim about how an ideal agent should update, or two notations for the same computation. So here's what I want:

1. The claim, unpacked (~150 words). Separate the descriptive claim about actual human updating from the normative claim about rational updating, and say which one you're going to test. Also say whether the redescription move (condition on a certain proposition about your own experience) is a genuine rival to Jeffrey conditionalization or just a relabeling of it.

2. The strongest test (~350 words). Design a concrete experiment or formal check that could actually distinguish "Jeffrey conditionalization is the correct rule for uncertain evidence" from "strict conditioning on a suitable redescription is correct, and Jeffrey's rule is only a convenience." Give the setup, what you vary, and what you measure. It can be behavioral (people's probability judgments), computational (one rule makes a prediction the other can't), or something else, but it has to be concrete enough that someone could run it.

3. What the results would mean (~250 words). Say in advance what outcome would favor the Jeffrey side and what outcome would favor the strict-conditioning side. Also say what a messy or null result would do to the claim.

4. What the test can't settle (~200 words). Be explicit about what stays open even if the result is strong—for example whether the two sides were ever in real conflict, or whether "correct" is doing normative work that no observation can reach. End with a judgment call: is the dispute genuine, partly verbal, or both?
