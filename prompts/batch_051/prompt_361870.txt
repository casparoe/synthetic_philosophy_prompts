I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to get my head around something for a piece about how the replication crisis, huge team-science consortia, and machine-learning-driven discovery have changed what it means for a scientific field to be healthy. I keep coming back to Lakatos. As I understand him, his big move was to say the right unit of evaluation is not a single theory but a research programme: a hard core of background assumptions surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. A programme is progressive when changes to the belt keep generating new predictions that actually get confirmed; it's degenerating when the belt just gets tweeked after the fact to soak up data the programme didn't anticipate. He meant this as a way to be tougher than Popper but more realistic than Kuhn about how science actually works.

Here's the part that nags me. Lakatos's criterion seems to lean very hard on being able to tell a prediction the programme genuinely made in advance from an adjustment that just explains what was already on the table. But now preregistration is supposed to pin down the in-advance part, while open datasets and flexible machine-learning pipelines let a researcher, in effect, test thousands of theory-adjacent tweaks after seeing the data and then report the one that looks novel. And in a 2,000-author collaboration, the sequence of who-tweaked-what-when can be almost impossible to reconstruct. Does Lakatos's argument for judging research programmes survive these developments? Is the novel-prediction criterion dead, repairable, or mostly fine, and which premise actually takes the hit?

Please act as an expert philosopher of science. Before you give a verdict, brainstorm a few different precise meanings of novel prediction in a world with preregistration and after-the-fact model search, then tell me which of those meanings Lakatos's argument actually needed.
