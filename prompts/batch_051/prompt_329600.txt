I'm a CS PhD student working on reinforcement learning, mostly on model-based agents that have to act under real compute budgets and misspecified enviornment models. I've been reading Herbert Simon on satisficing and bounded rationality—especially the 1955 *A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice* and his Nobel lecture. The part of the Nobel lecture that stuck with me is the scissors analogy: rational behavior is shaped by the match between internal cognitive capacity and the structure of the task environment, not by either one alone. I keep feeling that this is just what the RL literature already says with value functions, function approximation, and early-stopping rules, but my economist friends say I'm steamrolling over the parts that dont fit.

What I want is a systematic translation: restate Simon's satisficing thesis—that agents search options until one meets an aspiration level, and that the aspiration level adjusts in response to how hard the search is—in RL vocabulary, using policies, Q-functions, model classes, rollout budgets, reward thresholds, termination conditions, and exploration bonuses. Be careful not to smuggle in RL assumptions like a known MDP, a stationary reward signal, or a fixed policy class. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Simon survive cleanly, which get distorted, and which have no expression at all. In particular, I want to know whether an aspiration level is best thought of as a value threshold, a reference point, a stopping rule, or something RL has no clean name for, and whether satisficing is just approximate dynamic programming under compute constraints or a more radical claim about behavior.
