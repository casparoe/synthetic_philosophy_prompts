I am taking an upper-level seminar on causal inference and the philosophy of causation, and we have spent several weeks on Pearl's causal hierarchy and the do-calculus. I can carry out the derivations, but I am still unsure about the conceptual layer underneath them, and I would appreciate a careful explanation of one issue. A structural causal model is usually introduced as something more than a joint probability distribution: it adds a causal graph and a set of functional relationships between each variable and its direct causes, together with exogenous background variables. What I want to understand is exactly what these extra elements are meant to capture about the world, and why that content cannot in general be recovered from, or reduced to, facts about association. In particular, could you explain what the modularity or autonomy of the structural equations amounts to, and why it is essential if the do-operator is to make sense as a representation of an intervention rather than as a disguised form of conditioning? And, relatedly, why are counterfactuals well defined only after the structural equations have been fixed, so that full knowledge of the observational and interventional distributions can still leave counterfactual questions underdetermined? I am less interested in the algebra of the adjustment formula than in a conceptual account of what structural causal models assume and why those assumptions are as strong as they are.
