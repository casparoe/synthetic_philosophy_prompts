A colleague in my philosophy of mind reading group gave me this argument, and I keep feeling the conclusion must be wrong, but I cannot find the slip. (1) An illusion is a representation that misleads its user about what is really there. (2) On the user-illusion view of consciousness, phenomenal consciousness is just such a representation: it seems to present a stream of private, ineffable qualitative experiences, but the brain contains nothing of that kind. (3) A representation can mislead a user only if the user is conscious of it; otherwise nobody is taken in. (4) So anyone taken in by the consciousness-illusion must already be conscious of the misleading representation. (5) Therefore the user-illusion view presupposes the very phenomenal consciousness it says does not exist, and is self-refuting. She concluded that illusionism about consciousness is not merely false but incoherent. I would rather keep the illusionist view alive than accept a five-line refutation, but I do not yet see what keeping it costs. Which premise should be rejected? Explain why it initially looked right, what the user-illusion view actually claims, and how a defender would redraw the target of the argument.
