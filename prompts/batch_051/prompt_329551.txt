I teach philosophy of mathematics at a small university, and I'm reworking the first week of the course into a kind of diagnostic unit. Every year I get students who, when I ask why the definition of a group singles out associativity, identity, and inverses rather than some other package, either say it's just a convention or say that's what groups really are—and neither is a position so much as a shrug. I want a decision tree (say six to nine nodes, four to six leaves) that sorts a student from concrete, answerable questions about particular definitions to a named view about what makes a mathematical definition the right one. Please build the nodes around cases the students already know: why the open-set definition of continuity is now treated as the right notion in topology even though the epsilon-delta version is what most students meet first; why groups rather than semigroups, monoids, or quasigroups became the central algebraic object; why 1 is not prime; why the empty set is a subset of every set.

The leaves I'd like at minimum are: a realism view on which good definitions track mathematical kinds that are already there; a fruitfulness view on which definitions are chosen for the theorems they make elegant and general; a conventionalist view on which definitions are path-dependent artifacts; and a deflationary view on which there's no such thing as a right definition, only a definition that is useful for the problem at hand. Build the tree from the questions and the allowed combinations of answers first, then locate the named views in that space, rather than writing questions backward from the labels. Write each node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and after the tree note any answer combinations that have no occupant and whether that's because their incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't actually separate. Give me the instrument itself, starting directly with the first node, no preface or commentary.
