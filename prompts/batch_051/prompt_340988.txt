So I've been rereading the old skeptical chestnuts and one of them suddenly snapped onto something modern, and I want someone to stress-test the analogy. You know the problem of the criterion: to separate true beliefs from false ones you need a criterion, but you can't justify any criterion without already useing some of your beliefs or another criterion, so you land in a circle, a regress, or an arbitrary stop. Textbook stuff. But I can't stop seeing the replication crisis in psychology and the social sciences as this exact structure running live. The field lost confidence in its old de facto criterion—roughly, peer review plus a significant p-value—and tried to build a better one out of preregistration, larger samples, multi-lab replications, registered reports. Except the new criterion had to be validated using the same kind of empirical machinery that had just been revealed as leaky, and the actual arguments people were having (this replication failed because it wasn't faithful, the original effect had hidden moderators, the replicators were p-hacking in reverse) each presuppose a standard for deciding which study counts as the good one, while that standard is exactly what's in dispute.

What I'd like is an analysis of the replication crisis through that lens, in the style of a long blog comment—say 700 words or so. I don't need the history recapped; I know the rough beats (the precognition flap, the big multi-lab reproducibility projects, the p-curve fights, the preregistration shift). What I want is three things. First, a careful mapping of the circularity: who is playing the role of the searcher for a criterion, where exactly the bootstrapping happens, which moves in the debate are just restatements of the circle, and whether the problem of the criterion actually recommends any side in these disputes or is compatible with all of them. Second, an argument about whether the reform movement genuinely escapes the problem or only relocates it—and whether a Humean story (nature refuses to let us sit in the skeptical moment, so we carry on with induction anyway under a new banner) is the right description of what the field actually did. Third, run it the other way: does this episode make the problem of the criterion look like a devastating insight, an academic toy, or a decent description of ordinary methodological bootstrapping? Where does the lens stop being useful? I care more about the chain of reasoning than about the verdict.
