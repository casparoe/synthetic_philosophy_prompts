Two mates of mine, Sofia and Dan, got into it at dinner over a project Sofia's cousin works on: a language-nest programme for a language with about forty fluent speakers left, all over seventy, in a region where every child now grows up speaking the regional creole. The project pays the remaining speakers to come into the nursery and talk to the toddlers all day. Dan thinks it's a poor use of donor funds; Sofia thinks he's being philistine. The argument went roughly like this.

(1) Dan: A language is a tool, not a patient. If the last speakers prefer their grandchildren to be fluent in a language with better jobs attached, then the language dying isn't a harm to them — it's their choice. You can't turn people into living monuments.

(2) Sofia: That pretends the choice happens in a vacuum. Every family faces incentives to switch: schooling, work, land registration. So the shift is like a fishery being overfished by fifty boats each acting sensibly: the result is a loss none of them would have chosen — the language goes, and with it the oral histories, the medicinal plant knowledge, the kinship terms, a whole way of carving up the world.

(3) Dan: Now you're running two arguments together. One is instrumental: the language stores useful knowledge. Fine, record it, translate it, archive it. The other is 'different grammars make different worlds' — but the strong version of that idea is contested, and the weak version is preserved by the archive. You still haven't shown any loss that can't be put in a database.

(4) Sofia: A database preserves a language about as well as a field guide preserves a rainforest. A language lives in use — in teasing, in a grandmother correcting your word order, in singing the chorus wrong on purpose. And the community isn't asking for documentation; they're asking for the nest. The last speakers want to die knowing someone will still be scolded in their language.

(5) Dan: Nobody's saying don't feel sad. I'm saying we can't fund every language nest, and if the test for worth saving is 'the speakers want it', then every language passes and you have no way to choose. The same money could keep a clinic open or vaccinate children. Sadness doesn't do triage.

(6) Sofia: Triage only says be selective, not that this one is below the line. And your clinic comparison assumes value comes in a single measurable currency; by that logic we'd never save a small church, a dialect poem, or anything that doesn't scale. If the villagers want the nest and a donor wants to fund it, there's no principle that says the clinic always wins.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say who is right about which specific point, flag where each move overreaches, and say what both of them are missing (for instance the gap between instrumental, intrinsic, and constitutive value, or between a community freely abandoning a language and being pushed out of it by asymmetric incentives)? Then finish with a single line \answer{...} containing one of 'Dan', 'Sofia' or 'split', whichever is the fairest bottom line.
