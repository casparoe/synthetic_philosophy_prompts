In his Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Ludwig Wittgenstein advances two positions that, on their face, seem difficult to hold together. In proposition 6.522 he maintains that there is indeed what cannot be put into words, that it shows itself, and that this is the mystical. Yet in proposition 6.53 he specifies the correct method of philosophy as follows: one should say nothing except what can be said, namely the propositions of natural science, and whenever another person tries to say something metaphysical, one should demonstrate to them that they have failed to give a meaning to certain signs in their propositions. He concedes that this method would be unsatisfying to the other person, but insists that it would be the only strictly correct method.

The first passage appears to assert that there is something beyond all saying, and even to characterize it in positive terms; the second appears to rule out as meaningless any attempt to speak of anything beyond what can be said, which would seem to include the very assertion made in 6.522. Can the two be reconciled without making one of them a mere rhetorical gesture? Is the distinction between saying and showing enough to do the work, or does it merely relocate the problem? Does the later passage require treating the earlier one as itself a piece of the ladder to be thrown away? Present the strongest readings on each side, state what each reading must concede, and give a verdict on which reading requires the least special pleading.
