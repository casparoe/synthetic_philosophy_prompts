Ok here's a view I've basically signed on to and I want to know how exposed it is. I accept Jeffrey conditionalization as the right norm for updating on uncertain evidence: when you don't learn some proposition outright but just shift your credences over a partition (say, you look at a cloth in bad light and move to 0.7 that it's blue rather than green), you should set your new credences by averaging your old conditional-on-each-element credences weighted by the new element credences, while holding those conditionals fixed — the rigidity assumption. Tell me what would count against this as a normative account of ideal rationality, not the psychological point that real people dont do it. I'm thinking of: cases where the rigidity assumption itself fails because the experience bears directly on the conditional probabilities; the fact that successive Jeffrey updates don't commute, so the order of uncertain evidence matters in a way it maybe shouldn't; the result depending on which partition you choose; and the deeper worry that uncertain evidence isn't well modeled as a shift over a fixed partition at all, since the evidence itself (the glance, the bad lighting) is doing work that no partition element captures. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed Jeffrey conditionalizer would concede as counting against the view rather than writing off as features, and be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability and whether that's actually a problem for a normative rule like this.
