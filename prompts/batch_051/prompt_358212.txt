Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about assembly theory — the idea that an object's complexity should be measured by the minimal number of joining steps needed to construct it from an elementary set of parts, where you're allowed to reuse subassemblies once you've built them. My friend's view was that this finally gives an objective, observer-independent notion of complexity: a molecule or an artifact is complex if its assembly index is high, and that isn't a matter of taste or of which compression program you happened to choose. I kept insisting I'd seen this construction before.

To me it looks structurally almost identical to the minimal size of a straight-line program over a finite alphabet — the thing from grammar-based data compression and circuit complexity where you can define new variables by concatenating earlier ones, which is also basically the smallest context-free grammar generating the object (and if your operation is addition instead of concatenation, it's an addition chain). In both cases you're measuring how many layered join operations you need when you're allowed to cache and reuse intermediates, and both seem to inherit the same awkward features: the measure is relative to the primitive set and the allowed operations, and finding the minimum is in general hard to compute exactly. So I argued that "assembly index" is basically grammar-complexity with a physicsy name, or worse, a quantity that only looks objective because the choice of primitives gets left implicit.

My friend pushed back that assembly index is supposed to track something physical — the actual number of joining steps in a real construction history, not just the shortest description in any old programming language — and that the interesting claims are empirical anyway (high assembly index as a signature of life, distinguishing designed from random objects), so they'd survive even if the formalism is borrowed. I don't think he's obviously wrong, but I can't tell whether those replies are substantive or just restate the same construction while hiding its relativity to the primitives. So: is the parallel I noticed deep, or am I just pattern-matching between two things that both happen to involve building objects from parts? If it's real, what transfers from the computer science side — dependence on the primitive set, hardness of smallest-grammar problems, sensitivity to which operations you allow — and does any of that bite against assembly theory's claim to be an objective measure of complexity?
