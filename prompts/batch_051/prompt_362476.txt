I am an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on emergency powers in legal and political philosophy, and the readings and discussion have mostly centered on border closures, lockdowns, and other public health restrictions. After several weeks, I have ended up with what feels like a coherent set of commitments, but I am no longer sure the pieces all fit together, and I would like an audit of the position rather than a general explanation of the topic.

First, I take the rule of law's value in emergencies to be largely forward-looking: emergency frameworks should let citizens and officials know in advance, at least in broad outline, what a government is permitted to do, so I am suspicious of evaluations of state action that are wholly retrospective. Second, I also believe that many of the lockdown and travel measures imposed in the early phase of the pandemic were legitimate in the sense that they deserved public compliance, even though their legal basis was contested at the time and the statutes they relied on had never been tested in those circumstances. Third, I accept the practice of ex post legislative ratification as a proper way to bring such measures within the legal order after the fact; a legislature may confirm, after the emergency, restrictions that it did not authorize in advance. Fourth, I do not think that kind of ratification by itself settles legitimacy, because a government with a legislative majority could pass a blanket retroactive approval of its own abuses, and I want a court to be able to examine afterward whether the powers were used reasonably and without pretext. Fifth, I think courts should broadly defer to the executive while the acute phase of an emergency is still unfolding; it would be a mistake to require full judicial approval for each new restriction as it is imposed. Sixth, I think ordinary people should generally comply with emergency orders even when they suspect those orders are unlawful or disproportionate, while at the same time I think they retain the standing to challenge the orders in court and to criticize them publicly without being accused of undermining the public health response.

My question is whether these commitments jointly hold. Which of them pull against each other, ordered by severity, and what exactly would have to give in order to make the set consistent? Are there pairs that only appear to be in tension because they answer different questions—and if so, what distinction dissolves the apparent conflict? If the cheapest repair is to revise one commitment rather than abandon it entirely, which commitment should be revised, and what would the revised version have to say?
