I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I have been asked to draft one essay question for a take-home exam in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on distributive justice. The question should concern the disagreement between Rawls and Harsanyi over choice behind the veil of ignorance, and it should be framed so that a student cannot do well by summarizing maximin, expected utility, and the difference principle in sequence. In particular, the question should force students to locate the exact point at which the two arguments come apart: whether the disagreement is about rational choice under ignorance, about which information the veil admits, or about whether the original position is a formal decision problem at all.

Please write: (a) a single essay question with clear instructions for a 90-minute answer; (b) a model answer of roughly 600–700 words; and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 marks. The marking scheme should name five must-hit moves, with the marks each earns, and a one-sentence note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. At least two must-hit moves should go beyond textbook restatement — for example, explaining why Harsanyi's equal-probability model is not a neutral formalization of the veil but already encodes a substantive assumption about impersonality; or showing that Rawls's appeal to maximin is better understood as encoding constraints on the principles the parties may accept than as a general decision rule; or distinguishing maximin from minimax regret so precisely that the two cannot be treated as interchangeable. The model answer should present the arguments directly, without relying on citations, page references, or external literature.
