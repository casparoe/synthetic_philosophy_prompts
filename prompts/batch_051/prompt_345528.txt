Two colleagues in my philosophy department work on the emotions and have, for years, been arguing past each other. One holds a neo-Stoic judgment theory: an emotion just is an evaluative judgment, an assent to a proposition such as “I have been wronged,” so that grief is the judgment that something irreplaceable has been lost and fear is the judgment that danger is at hand. The other holds an embodied-appraisal theory: an emotion is a perception-like representation of a matter of well-being — danger, loss, offense — carried by patterned bodily changes, which can arise and be correct without the subject’s having assented to any proposition at all. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the statement they would jointly sign.

Write that document, not an essay about it. It should consist of: (1) a numbered list of claims about emotion that both can sign without strain, covering intentionality, appraisal, the role of bodily changes, the phenomenon of recalcitrant emotion, and what distinguishes emotion from mood, reflex, and dispassionate evaluation; (2) a table of residual disagreements in which each position is stated in a form its own proponent would accept, not a straw version; and (3) a list of genuine cruxes. For each crux — for example, the person who sincerely judges that flying is safe and yet feels fear, the question whether creatures without language or evaluative concepts can have emotions, and the question whether a wholly disembodied evaluative judgment could count as an emotion — specify the kind of case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on the point, and record each side’s prediction or commitment in advance. Conclude by saying which parts of their disagreement, if any, are merely verbal, and defend that verdict from the agreed claims rather than by declaring a false synthesis. Where they genuinely disagree, say so precisely.
