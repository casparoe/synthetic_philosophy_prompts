I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called "Probability and the Unknowable Future" — sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute meeting. We are about to meet the doomsday argument, and I have learned from teaching it before that students can follow the Bayes arithmetic while feeling nothing from the move that does all the work: the self-sampling assumption. They easily see that a low numbered ball drawn blind favors the smaller urn, but they don't feel the same pull when it's their own birth rank doing the drawing. I'd like a game that makes them occupy that inference before it is given a name.

Please design a runnable in-class activity for the first forty-five minutes that stages the self-sampling move under another, innocuous description: two possible "worlds," a scarce observation about where one stands in one of them, and an incentivized judgment, committed to before any jargon appears. The students should be made to update — and to stake tokens or points, not money — on the basis of nothing but their own position in a draw, so that they can later recognize that exact move as the engine of the doomsday argument. Include concrete rules, a materials list that uses nothing beyond paper (and perhaps envelopes or a container), a scoring or payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the game.

Then give me a twenty- to twenty-five-minute debrief that maps moments in the game onto the argument rather than lecturing from the top: the two worlds become competing hypotheses about total human population; the observed position becomes one's birth rank; the forced update becomes the likelihood shift of the doomsday argument. The debrief should end with the places a thoughtful student should resist — especially the reference class problem and the difference between being inside the draw and watching it from outside — and I'd like the students to leave able to say, in their own words, what the self-sampling assumption is, why it moves probability, and at least one place they would hesitate to apply it to the real human case. Finally, tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this, both mathematically and philosophically, and how you'd handle each; and be straightforward about which feature of the doomsday argument the activity cannot reproduce no matter how well it runs, and how you'd supply that missing piece without letting the session collapse back into a lecture.
