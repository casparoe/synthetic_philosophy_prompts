I keep noticing an asymmetry in how we judge people's moral worth. Two doctors both run free Saturday clinics for uninsured patients, same hours, same patients, same quality of care. Dr. A does it because he likes being seen as the doctor who runs free clinics; if no one ever found out, he'd probably stop. Dr. B does it anonymously; if you ask why, she says she wants to be the kind of person who does such things and she'd feel like a fraud if she quit. Most people will say A's action has less moral worth, maybe none, while B's seems to have plenty. But both motives are self-directed; B's is just aimed at her own character rather than her reputation. Is there a principle that lets wanting to be good count as acting for the right reasons while wanting to be seen as good does not? Or is the difference just that one motive embarrasses us and the other flatters us? Now push it on the hard version: suppose B becomes just as preoccupied with her own virtue as A is with his image—she keeps checking whether she's still the kind of person who'd do it, and she'd be quietly devastated to discover she isn't—while the patients get exactly the same benefit. I want the strongest possible justification for treating B's action as morally worthier, and then an honest verdict on whether it survives that version. If it doesn't survive, tell me what's really doing the work in the original asymmetry. While you're at it, separate what's actually settled about moral worth and acting for the right reasons from what's still genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't dress a contested view up as the consensus.
