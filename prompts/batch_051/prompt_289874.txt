I am a PhD student in cognitive science, and I have been trying to get clear about a move that now seems common in my field: extending the intentional stance to large language models. The stance, as Dennett originally defended it, says that we are justified in interpreting a system as an agent with beliefs and desires whenever doing so buys us predictive purchase, and that this justification does not require the system to possess those states literally in the way a human does. The standard presentations were steadied by examples such as chess-playing computers, thermostats, and animals: systems that are temporally extended, act in an environment, and can be predicted by asking what it would be rational for them to want and believe.

A transformer-based language model looks different in ways that seem to matter. During inference it is stateless between calls apart from the visible context; it selects no actions of its own; it does not gather evidence or update over time; and the objective it was trained on, predicting the next token, is a training target rather than a goal the system is disposed to pursue. Yet the same prediction-first reasoning appears to license saying that the model believes or expects things, and people working with these systems now speak that way freely.

I want to know whether the original argument survives this development, and if so in what form. Specifically, which premise does the transformer case put pressure on: the claim that predictive payoff is sufficient for belief-talk, the claim that full-fledged agency need not be present, or some other premise? Is the best repair a weaker thesis, such as that belief-talk about language models is legitimate only relative to a task specification and not as a claim about the model's standing states? Or does the case reveal that the argument was implicitly assuming something these systems lack? I am interested in the logic of the argument, not in the policy question of whether such attributions are useful or safe. Please answer in American English.
