From there I reasoned like this:

(1) The quality of life in a city is properly measured by the average well-being of its residents.
(2) If a policy lowers a city's average well-being, then it makes life in the city worse.
(3) Consider a development in which the newcomers are better off than they were where they lived before, but each newcomer is still below the city's old average for some years, so the city's average after they arrive is lower than before.
(4) Therefore the development makes life in the city worse, even if every single person involved—old resident and newcomer—is better off than they would have been without it.
(5) The same reasoning applies to having a larger family or a larger species: if the extra children or extra lives are good but below the current average, adding them makes the family or the species worse, not better.

Step (5) seems absurd to me, but I can't tell which earlier step is doing the damage. Is this argument valid?
