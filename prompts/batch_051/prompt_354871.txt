Take Jackson's Mary's room thought experiment as the base case: Mary has complete physical knowledge about human color vision but has never had a chromatic experience, and on first seeing red she seems to acquire knowledge she previously lacked. Now change one feature of the case. Mary's first chromatic experience is not red, and it is not produced by looking at a colored surface. It is produced by a neural interface that directly coactivates the red and green opponent channels, yielding a stable, uniform experience of reddish green — a forbidden color that, according to the opponent-process theory Mary knows perfectly, no ordinary viewing condition can cause. She knows in advance that this stimulation pattern is physically possible, and she knows its complete physical description; she has simply never undergone it.

Work through how the physicalist and the anti-physicalist should each analyze this variant, and say whether the knowledge argument becomes stronger, weaker, or structurally unchanged. Weigh two competing considerations. The physicalist might argue that the usual ability/acquaintance reply is more plausible here: if a color cannot be presented by any ordinary object, Mary's inability to anticipate it from textbook knowledge looks like a limit of her training, not a missing fact about the world. The anti-physicalist might argue that the variant is stronger: an experience that cannot be triangulated through any public color sample or color vocabulary is precisely one where no amount of physical or linguistic knowledge could do the job. Which side has the better case, and does substituting a forbidden color for red alter the structure of the original argument, or only its window dressing?
