For a philosophy of measurement seminar, I am writing a paper on whether assembly theory's assembly index — or any of its cousins — is really measuring complexity when it claims to be, rather than measuring some nearby quantity under the same name. Before I evaluate the candidates, I want the specification of the target: what must an adequate measure of the complexity of an individual object be able to do, and what must it refrain from doing, before we should accept it as a measure of how complex that object is?

Produce the list of requirements. The candidate notions I have in view include Shannon entropy (uncertainty of a probability distribution over a system's states), Kolmogorov complexity (length of the shortest program that outputs the object) and computable approximations to it such as Lempel–Ziv complexity, Bennett's logical depth (the computation time a near-minimal program needs to produce the object), Gell-Mann's effective complexity (the length of a compressed account of an entity's regularities, with incidental randomness set aside), statistical complexity (the amount of information about a system's past that must be retained in order to predict its future optimally), and the assembly index at the center of assembly theory (the fewest joining operations needed to build the object from a fixed stock of elementary building blocks, with already-assembled subparts reusable).

For each requirement, give the specific case, phenomenon, or formal result that forces it. Be concrete about the cases any adequate measure must classify correctly, or at least not perversely: a perfect crystal, a genuinely random string, a bacterium, a watch, the proof of a deep theorem, a sequence produced by natural selection versus the same sequence produced by chance. Be concrete about the distinctions it must respect: complexity versus randomness versus order versus information content; the complexity of an object versus the complexity of the process or history that produced it; intrinsic versus observer- or representation-relative properties; and complexity as one scalar versus complexity as a multidimensional profile. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable if the notion is to mean anything worth meaning, and which are parochial commitments of information theory, algorithmic complexity theory, or the physics of life.

Then state whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and specify exactly what has to be given up if so. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most work excluding the candidate measures now on offer, and compress the remaining candidates' standing against that requirement. A table is welcome where it can be kept compact.
