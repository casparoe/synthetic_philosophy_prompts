For a decision-theory reading group I have been asked to set down my philosophical profile on self-prediction so the other members can probe it for coherence. Here it is.

(1) I am an evidential decision theorist. Provided the predictor is sufficiently reliable, I one-box in Newcomb's problem, because my choice would be evidence about what has already been predicted.

(2) I accept a general probabilism about my own actions. Before I am in the business of deliberating about a choice, a proposition about my own future act, such as that I shall choose A next Tuesday, is an ordinary proposition about the world, and I may hold a precise credence in it.

(3) But I also hold that once I am actually deliberating about whether to choose A or B, I should not hold an unconditional credence in my choosing A or in my choosing B. To do so would be to regard the matter as already settled, whereas deliberation requires treating the alternatives as open. This is the grain of truth I find in the thought that one cannot simultaneously deliberate about an act and treat its performance as something to be predicted.

(4) I see no principled asymmetry between first- and third-person prediction. If I may assign credence 0.8 to a stranger's choosing A on the strength of evidence about her dispositions and situation, then the same kind of evidence about my own dispositions and situation should equally license my assigning credence 0.8 to my choosing A.

(5) I think the rationality of an act should not turn on when I first happen to consider the problem. A theory that prescribes one act before I have formed a self-prediction and another once I have done so is, to that extent, suspect.

I would like these views audited rather than merely restated. Go through them and say which pairs are genuinely in tension and which are only apparently so; rank the genuine tensions by severity, with a sentence for each explaining the rank; then identify the single least costly revision and what it gives up.
