I've been working through Quinn's self-torturer and would like an audit of the position I've settled into before I circulate it. The setup, for concreteness: a person can set a dial to any integer level from 0 to 1,000. At level 0 it delivers no shock; at level 1,000 it delivers continuous severe pain for the duration of the trial. Each one-level advance pays a fixed sum of money and increases the shock by an amount so small that adjacent levels are experientially indistinguishable. Offered a single choice at the outset — stay at level 0 with no payment, or jump directly to level 1,000 and receive the full cumulative payment — the person would refuse the jump, and would be right to refuse it. Yet when the same change is offered incrementally, one level at a time, every step presents itself as a good bargain, and he moves level by level into a state he would have declined as a single step.

Here are the commitments I currently hold:

(1) Transitivity. If outcome A is at least as choiceworthy as outcome B, and B is at least as choiceworthy as outcome C, then A is at least as choiceworthy as C. I treat this as a fixed point of practical rationality.

(2) Global refusal. In the one-shot choice, the agent is rationally required to refuse the direct jump from level 0 to level 1,000 with the full cumulative payment; the final state is worse than the initial state, and the offered sum does not make up for it.

(3) Stepwise acceptance. At each level k below 1,000, when offered the one-level advance for the fixed payment, the agent should accept. Adjacent levels are experientially indistinguishable and the payment is a pure benefit, so refusing would be giving up a good for no felt cost.

(4) Diachronic innocence. If an agent takes a finite sequence of steps, each of which is rationally required (or at least rationally permissible) when considered at the time, then the agent has not made a rational mistake in taking the whole sequence; a course of action assembled only from individually rational steps cannot itself be rationally criticizable.

(5) No sharp cutoff. There is no first setting n such that the advance from n−1 to n was already the one the agent should have refused. The pain ramps up in a sorites-like way, and it is a mistake to try to locate the precise point at which the increments become too much.

Do these commitments hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than giving an undifferentiated list. For any pair that merely appears to conflict, explain why the conflict dissolves. For the genuine tensions, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence, and what that revision would cost.
