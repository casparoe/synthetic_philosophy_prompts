I convene a university seminar on linguistic relativity that has been meeting for over a year, and our central argument has hardened into a standoff. We return to the same passages and divide into the same two camps; the same exchange recurs every meeting. The founding text under dispute is Edward Sapir's Language: An Introduction to the Study of Speech (1921). The contested passage from the opening chapter is:

"Speech is a human activity that varies without assignable limit as we pass from social group to social group, because it is a purely historical heritage of the group, the product of long-continued social usage. It varies as all creative effort varies—not as consciously, perhaps, but none the less as truly as do the religions, the beliefs, the customs, and the arts of different peoples."

One camp treats this as licensing the claim that different languages impose different cuts on experience: "without assignable limit" and the parallel with religions and arts mean the conceptual schemes of communities genuinely diverge. The other camp says the passage only denies that speech is biological like walking; it says nothing yet about whether thought is affected by the patterns of a particular language, and the variation could be superficial. Our disagreement is no longer about the truth of linguistic relativity but about what this passage can even be used to establish. I do not need a verdict on which camp is right. I want a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled: are the camps disagreeing about the referent of "varies," about the logical implications of the passage, about what counts as relevant evidence, or about a prior question such as whether "thought" is the sort of thing that can be shaped by a purely historical inheritance? My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that one camp is thinking of language as a system of available distinctions and the other as a set of observable verbal practices, and that two senses of "language" are being traded on. Tell me whether that diagnosis is sound, and, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, a reformulated question, or an acknowledgment that the argument is not resolvable because it is not a single argument.
