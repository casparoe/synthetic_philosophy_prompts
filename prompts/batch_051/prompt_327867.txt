Arendt's banality of evil thesis, as most people remember it from Eichmann in Jerusalem, rested pretty heavily on what she saw in the courtroom: a grey, cliché-spouting clerk who didn't seem monstrous so much as thoughtless, a man who seemed to have never really thought about what it was he was doing. The point as usually remembered is that enormous evil can be done by people who aren't frothing ideologues, just people who fail to think and lose themselves in logistics.

Suppose now the later historical work is right, that Eichmann was privately much more of a committed, fanatical antisemite than he let on in Jerusalem—boasting about his role, annoyed mainly that the job wasn't finished. Does the banality-of-evil claim actually depend on Eichmann himself having been thoughtless, or was that just the evidence Arendt happened to have in front of her? If Eichmann's banal courtroom persona was in part an act, is the thesis just dead, or does it survive as a claim about something else—the kind of institution where evil becomes routine, the way it can look banal from the outside, the many actual clerks around him?

What I'm after is which premise the new evidence bites on. If you think the thesis survives, say what 'banality' still means once Eichmann himself is no longer a clean example of it. If you think it dies, say what the salvageable point is, if any, and what it should be called instead.
