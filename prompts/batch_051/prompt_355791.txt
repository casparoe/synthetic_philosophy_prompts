You are a philosopher of science specialising in anthropic reasoning, Bayesian inference and the Fermi problem. I have been turning over an argument that purports to show that the Great Filter lies ahead of us rather than behind us. The conclusion feels oddly compelling, but I suspect the argument is doing something sly that I cannot quite localise. Please evaluate it step by step, and do not merely tell me whether you like the conclusion.

The argument runs as follows. I am willing to grant the empirical premises (1)–(3) for the sake of discussion; my unease is downstream of them.

(1) The Milky Way is roughly thirteen billion years old and contains billions of rocky planets in habitable zones, many of them billions of years older than the Earth.
(2) A technological civilisation that builds self-replicating probes, or that spreads from star to star at even a modest fraction of the speed of light, could cross the entire galaxy in the order of tens of millions of years — a small fraction of the time available.
(3) We observe no convincing trace of any such spread: no probe in the Solar System, no astro-engineering, no transmission that settles the question.
(4) If the decisive bottleneck were one of the stages we have already passed — the origin of life, complex single cells, multicellularity, or tool-using intelligence — then, over billions of potential starting points, at least some lineages should have squeezed through and gone on to fill the galaxy anyway; near-total silence would then be surprising.
(5) The silence is therefore best explained by a bottleneck in the stage we have not yet passed: the transition from an ordinary technological civilisation to a durable, expanding, galaxy-spanning one.
(6) We are an ordinary technological civilisation that has not yet made that transition.
(7) Therefore the step most likely to stop us lies ahead of us, and the Great Filter is in front of us rather than behind us.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, defend it against the strongest objections you can think of. If it is not, identify exactly which step fails — or which move between steps fails — and explain why even the most plausible reading of that step does not rescue the inference. Be explicit about what the observed silence is and is not evidence for: the joint improbability of the whole sequence, or the conditional improbability of the final step. Finally, say whether some weakened version of the conclusion can be salvaged, and what additional evidence would be needed to locate the filter.

Think through this carefully before settling on a verdict, and write your answer in British English.
