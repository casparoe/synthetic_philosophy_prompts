I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but over the past few years I've been trying to make my everyday ethical beliefs cohere on one subject: how an individual should think about small contributions to very large collective harms, such as factory farming, climate change, and elections. The recurring problem is what I later learned is called the inefficacy objection, and my commitments have accumulated piecemeal rather than forming a system. I've reached the point where I can't tell whether the whole set type-checks. I'm going to write my current commitments down as a fixed list and ask you to audit it the way I might audit a codebase: identify the constraints I've inadvertently imposed on myself, locate places where two of my stated beliefs cannot both be true, distinguish real conflicts from conflicts that dissolve once the terms are pinned down, and tell me which single change would remove the most inconsistency at the lowest cost. Please don't lay out the options and leave me to choose; take a definite position on each point and defend it.

Here is the list.

(1) For consumer and environmental choices whose effects are diffuse, I accept an expected-marginal-effect test: whether I should do X or refrain from X is settled, at bottom, by whether my own choice can be expected to change the amount of harm in the world, even by a small probability of a small amount.

(2) Buying factory-farmed chicken is wrong.

(3) Applying (1) to (2), my single purchase of factory-farmed chicken has effectively zero expected effect on the number of chickens raised and killed; supply chains are stochastic, and my individual demand signal is lost in noise.

(4) Therefore (2) must be explained differently. The explanation I actually believe is something like a participation principle: when a practice is seriously wrong, there is real reason not to pay into it, consume its products, or knowingly make myself a participant, even when my individual participation changes nothing at the margin.

(5) I also think I should vote even in a safe-seat national election, where the chance my vote decides the outcome is negligible. Here too I don't think the reason is causal leverage but something about taking part in the practice of citizenship on its own terms, so (4) and (5) feel like the same kind of ground.

(6) Yet I'm suspicious of Kantian generalization. The question what if everyone did that strikes me as a bad test for these problems: it treats my choice as though it controlled everyone else's choices, and it seems to confuse average with marginal effects. I don't want my verdicts in (2), (4), or (5) to rest on that test.

(7) I hold a parity principle: if two choices match on every feature I'm willing to count as morally relevant, then I should judge them alike; I won't allow special cases merely because an act feels central to my identity or because the stakes are uncomfortable.

(8) Flying to Europe for a one-week holiday strikes me as wrong for me, given the climate. But I can't honestly say I know whether this is because (a) the marginal expected harm is large enough to pass the test in (1) once I use a social-cost-of-carbon or threshold-risk calculation, because (b) the participation principle in (4) also covers buying an airline ticket, or because (c) I'm smuggling in some separate environmental principle. I'd rather not run (8) through a different code path from (2) if (a) is really just a numerical bridge between them.

Please audit this set. Which of these commitments are actually in tension, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; for each, identify what would have to be given up and what the cheapest revision costs. Also tell me whether the scoping of (1) to consumer and environmental choices is doing principled work or is just an ad hoc carve-out to keep voting in (5) safe. Finally, tell me which single revision you would make to clean up the most.
