I have been trying to make sense of a legal practice that strikes me as inconsistent, and I would like someone to work through it with me.

My city has an ordinance, passed in 1951, making it an offense to operate a fortune-telling business. It was enforced for a few years and then, by all accounts, ignored for seventy years; the police do not cite anyone under it, and several people openly advertise palm readings. A few years ago a city councilmember came across the ordinance and asked the police to start enforcing it. When the first charge reached the court, the judge dismissed it. The judge did not say the ordinance was unconstitutional, or badly drafted, or superseded by a later law. The judge's entire reason was that the law had gone unused for so long that invoking it now would be unfair and arbitrary.

I expected the city to appeal or the council to repeal the ordinance. Instead, the same judge heard a different case that year involving another old ordinance, a noise rule from 1962 that had also been only sporadically enforced, and refused to excuse the violation. The judge said, in substance, that a statute is whatever the legislature enacted, that it stays law until the legislature repeals it, and that neither the police nor the courts get to cancel a law by neglecting to enforce it. Later, the council did pass a new regulation covering fortune-telling, which suggested that everyone involved still felt the old law had some kind of standing.

I have since learned there is a name for this, something like desuetude, but I have never studied law or philosophy. What I want to understand is not the legal test a court would apply, but what picture of law the two decisions taken together presuppose. A law seems to be treated both as a standing command from the past, untouched by whether anyone obeys or enforces it, and as something whose authority can quietly lapse through disuse. What view of what makes something law, and of why it binds people, would make the judge's pair of decisions coherent rather than contradictory? What does each decision quietly rule out? And if one took the reasoning in the fortune-telling case seriously, that a law's force depends partly on ongoing use and settled expectations, what would have to change in the way we think about the rule of law, about legislative supremacy, and about the worry that officials should not be able to nullify statutes they dislike by ignoring them?

Finally, please indicate what you are least confident about in the answer: where the reasoning could most easily fail, and what kind of consideration, about enactment, enforcement, fair notice, or something else, would be most likely to change your conclusion. Please keep the entire response to no more than 900 words.
