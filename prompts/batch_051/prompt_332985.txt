As a software engineer with no background in philosophy, I keep running into a puzzle in my work on signup and settings flows. People in my field routinely treat a default as acceptable and a requirement as objectionable, even when the two are engineered to produce the same result. A team will pre-select the option that lets the company collect and sell user data and bury the opt-out several screens deep, and they will describe this as sensible defaults, good product design, or simply giving users a choice. The same team will say it is coercive and paternalistic for a product to make that data sharing a hard condition of using the service. Both practices steer almost everyone to the same outcome, and both work because almost no one changes a default or reads the settings. Is there a principle that actually justifies treating these two cases differently? The obvious candidate is that a default preserves a genuine choice, but I am not sure that survives the case where the default is deliberately made sticky, through hidden settings, added friction, or wording that discourages changing it, so that the choice is preserved in name only. I would like the strongest principle you can construct for drawing a defensible line between acceptable steering and unacceptable compulsion, along with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or partly abandoned.
