Last week I had one of those arguments that leaves you less sure of your own position than when you started. A friend and I debated whether a good human life requires novelty, variety, and at least the possibility of adventure, or whether those are merely instrumental goods that a hedonistic or desire-based theory of well-being can absorb without residue. He defended the instrumental view: if someone were genuinely content with forty years of the same meals, the same walks, the same conversations, and no surprises, then there would be nothing prudentially missing from her life, and any unease we felt about that life would simply be evidence that the pleasure had worn thin. I resisted, and kept reaching for cases: the contented person who has quietly stopped growing; the person whose days are objectively varied but who experiences them as an undifferentiated blur; the thrill-seeker whose appetite for the new is compulsive and self-damaging; the middle-aged parent who trades adventure for security and seems to gain rather than lose. But I could not state cleanly what a theory of well-being would have to get right in order to honor these cases without turning novelty into a fetish.

What I want is the desiderata, not the verdict. Write the specification that any adequate theory of prudential value would have to meet if it is to handle novelty, variety, adventure, and the fear of a boring life. State each requirement in a way that makes clear which cases it is supposed to get right and which distinctions it cannot collapse — for example, between felt and objective variety, between novelty that is good for a person and novelty that merely stimulates, between needing variation and needing growth, and between the value of novelty as such and its value for the story a person can tell about her life. For each requirement, give the consideration behind it and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Then make explicit which pairs of requirements are in tension and might not be jointly satisfiable, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive theories. If the correct position is that novelty has no independent standing in a theory of well-being, that should be argued as a conclusion about the desiderata rather than presupposed; if it does have independent standing, the desiderata should make clear exactly where its edge is.
