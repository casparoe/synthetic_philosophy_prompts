I have a friend who has spent a fair amount of time thinking about what gratitude actually demands, and over the years I have collected a set of verdicts she holds firmly about particular cases. They look to me like the output of a coherent policy, but I suspect more than one policy fits the evidence, and I want the policy reconstructed rather than simply summarised.

Her earlier verdicts:

1. The burning car. A stranger pulls you out of a burning car and is seriously injured in doing so. She says you owe the stranger substantial gratitude — plausibly a standing debt of a real kind.

2. The lucky delay. A distracted driver runs a red light ahead of you, forcing you to brake and wait while the junction clears; because you are delayed for two minutes, you are not at the next junction when a lorry runs the red light there. She says you owe the driver nothing. Relief or gladness is appropriate, but luck is not a benefactor, and there is no gratitude debt to anyone here.

3. The easy merge. A driver sees you signalling and slows slightly to let you into their lane, losing barely a moment of their time. She says gratitude is owed, though only a small amount; the smallness of the cost makes the debt small, not absent.

4. The late warning. A colleague discovers that the lift is faulty and leaves you a voicemail warning you not to take it; the message reaches you only after you have already climbed the stairs, so nothing about your day changes. She says you still owe the colleague gratitude for the attempt, because the failed outcome does not cancel what the attempt itself calls for.

5. The sewers. Your street is not flooded because of a sewer system built 150 years ago by engineers and ratepayers who never had you or your street in mind. She says no gratitude to anyone in particular is owed. You can be glad to live now rather than then, and you can approve of the scheme in a diffuse way, but approval or gladness is not gratitude to a benefactor.

New cases:

A. Healing energy. Your cousin, on hearing that you are unwell, spends a week earnestly trying to send you healing energy and visualising your recovery, with complete sincerity and some inconvenience to herself; you do not believe this does anything, though you believe her goodwill is real. Does she think you owe the cousin gratitude?

B. The anonymous grant. A wealthy stranger works through a list of people in financial difficulty, is moved by the details of your file, and anonymously clears your debts without ever learning your name and without wanting any contact with you. Does she think you owe the stranger gratitude?

C. The dead aunt. A great-aunt you never met leaves you a sizeable inheritance; the accompanying note, written while she was alive, says she kept track of you from a distance and wanted you to be provided for. Does she think you owe her gratitude?

D. The deleted account. A close friend, sincerely convinced that your social-media use is ruining your life, quietly deletes your account for what they regard as your own good; you are furious, and you regard the act as a net harm rather than a benefit. Does she think you owe the friend gratitude?

I am less interested in the bare predictions than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, and which cases most cleanly tell those rules apart. Please give me, in British English:

1. Her predicted verdict on each of A–D, with your best guess as to the reasoning she would give.

2. At least three rules that fit all five earlier verdicts and yield different predictions on at least one of the new cases. State each rule precisely enough that it could be applied mechanically to further cases.

3. Your best guess as to which rule she is most likely running in fact, and the reasons for preferring it over the others.

4. A further case of your own invention that would most cleanly discriminate between the leading candidate rules, and what you would expect her to say under each.
