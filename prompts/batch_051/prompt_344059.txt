I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or game theory. We're building a bot for a two-player game, and we use iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies to prune the bot's action space before running a costly search. The table below gives the bot's payoffs: rows are the bot's actions, columns are the opponent's possible responses, and higher numbers are better.

|       | X | Y | Z |
|-------|---|---|---|
| A     | 5 | 0 | 1 |
| B     | 0 | 4 | 0 |
| C     | 1 | 5 | 4 |

Our solver produces the following argument:

(1) A rational expected-utility maximizer never chooses a strategy that is strictly dominated by another pure strategy.

(2) The same principle applies when the dominating alternative is a mixed strategy: a randomization over the bot's other pure strategies that yields strictly higher expected payoff against every possible response of the opponent.

(3) In this game, A is strictly dominated by the mixed strategy 0.5B + 0.5C. The solver's justification is that this mixture yields 0.5 against X, 4.5 against Y, and 2 against Z, so its worst-case payoff (0.5) beats A's worst-case payoff (0). Therefore A can be discarded.

(4) Once A is discarded, what remains is the 2×3 game with the bot's actions B and C against the opponent's X, Y, and Z.

(5) In that reduced game, B is strictly dominated by C, since C beats B against X (1 vs. 0), Y (5 vs. 4), and Z (4 vs. 0). Therefore B can be discarded.

(6) Therefore C is the only rational action for the bot in this situation, and we should hard-code C.

The lead designer swears C is correct, and I can see that B is just worse than C. But step (3) feels like it is smuggling something in too quickly, and I don't want to ship a pruner that is doing hidden work. Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails first, and why? If it is valid, where does my suspicion about step (3) go wrong? As part of your answer, tell me explicitly what would change your verdict (which payoff pattern, definitional reading, or extra assumption would make the argument go through) and where your own analysis is most likely to fail.
