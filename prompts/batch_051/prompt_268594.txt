I have been reading Donald Davidson’s essay *What Metaphors Mean*, and I am struggling to see how his position relates to the conceptual-metaphor programme associated with Lakoff and Johnson in *Metaphors We Live By*. In the essay, Davidson insists that metaphors mean only what their words literally mean, and that what we are tempted to call a metaphor’s special content is really an effect it has on its hearers: when Romeo says that Juliet is the sun, the line does not carry a coded paraphrase; it simply prompts us to notice things. Davidson seems to locate metaphor entirely on the side of use, not of meaning. Lakoff and Johnson, by contrast, treat systematic metaphor as evidence of underlying conceptual mappings—argument as war, time as motion, and so on—which are cognitively real, structured, and partly constitutive of how we understand abstract domains. On their view, if someone says that time races or that time crawls, there is something systematic to be explained, and that explanation seems to be a matter of content rather than mere strategic effect.

My difficulty is not so much with either view taken alone, but with what exactly the disagreement amounts to.
