I've been calling this "setup virtues" and I want to know if its real or if I've just rediscovered something with a sillier name. Since I got promoted into managing my old coworkers, I've watched the same person be decisive in a morning standup and then spend an hour in a meeting not pushing back on a plan they told me they thought was wrong. The situationist critique of virtue ethics always rang true to me — people don't seem to have the kind of stable cross-situational traits Aristotle wants. But I also can't shake that some people are more honest or fair than others over a year, even if not in any given 20-minute slice.

So my theory is: a virtue isnt a disposition to do the right thing when the moment arrives. It's a disposition to arrange things so the moment doesn't arrive as a temptation. Honesty is keeping the books so there's no quiet way to fudge them; it's not taking the job, not having the conversation with the one person who will go along with it, putting yourself around people who will call you out. Courage is scheduling the hard conversation early in the morning before you have a chance to weasel out of it. The virtue is in the setup, not the showdown.

I know this smells like situation selection from personality psychology, and like the local-traits response to situationism, and maybe like Aristotle's line about the temperate person not even wanting the wrong pleasures. But I can't tell whether I'm adding anything or just describing "self-control plus luck" with extra steps.

Give me an honest assessment. What's the closest thing already out there, what does my account get right, and where does it break? Specifically: which virtues can't be rebuilt as setup virtues, and does that failure kill the theory or just mark its limit? Answer in American English please.
