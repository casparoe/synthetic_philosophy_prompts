My university is floating a new consent policy and I want a walkthrough of how it would actually play out, not whether it sounds good. Here's the mechanism. Students who want to hook up in a dorm must first open a campus app, tap off a menu of the acts they're agreeing to (kissing, touching over clothes, sex, whatever), and the other person confirms thier own list. A logged yes expires after twenty minutes unless both partners tap 'still yes' to renew it. Either person can hit revoke at any instant and the other's phone buzzes immediately. The log stays encrypted and is only decrypted if someone files an invalidity complaint later, at which point a panel of two trained students and one staff member reviews the timestamps and interviews both parties. The panel can void consent on three grounds: deception, coercion, or incapacity—and 'deception' is defined to cover any lie about something the complainant subjectively considered material, even if the other person couldn't have known it mattered.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. What does the existence of the log do to who initiates, who over-discloses, who routes around the app, and what people say to each other before and after? Where does it get gamed first: by the person who treats a 'still yes' tap every twenty minutes as proof of enthusiasm, by someone who says nothing during an act they privately dislike and then files a complaint, by the accused who claims the revoke buzz came to late? Does the encrypted-log-plus-panel design actually fix he-said-she-said, or does it just move the fight to what counts as a material lie and what counts as coercion? And can its failure modes be patched—shorter renewal windows, a rule that a later revoke automatically triggers an investigation, a ban on citing the renewal taps as evidence of enthusiasm—without turning the whole thing into something worse than what it replaced?
