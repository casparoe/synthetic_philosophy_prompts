Read Peter Shaffer's Amadeus — the stage play or the film — through Rawls's distinction between envy and resentment and his account of why envy can sometimes be excusable. The popular description of Salieri is that he simply envies Mozart, but much of the work is organized around Salieri's sense of having been wronged: he believes he struck a bargain with God, honored his side of it, and was defrauded, and he treats his campaign against Mozart as a response to that fraud. I want a reading that takes both the work and the philosophy seriously: determine whether Salieri's passion is Rawlsian envy, Rawlsian resentment, or something that shifts between the two over the course of the story; identify the scenes that most sharpen the distinction (for instance, the framing scenes with the priest, or the moments when Salieri studies Mozart's manuscripts and sees a divine hand) and the scenes that resist or complicate it; and say honestly where Rawls's categories run out and the work shows something the theory does not capture. Does the work ultimately support Rawls's view that envy, unlike resentment, is not a moral feeling but can be excusable under certain conditions, or does it present Salieri's envy as more morally intelligible than Rawls allows?
