I've been trying to work out what I think about a cluster of cases where the only question seems to be what one person owes another in attitude, not just in action. My gut verdicts:

1. A nurse gives a difficult elderly patient flawless care but privately finds him revolting and vents about him to coworkers, never letting it show to him. I still think she wrongs him.

2. A stranger holds the door for me and says "after you," while privately despising me for no reason. I don't feel wronged; there was nothing beyond the door he owed me.

3. A daughter cares for her aging mother, does every chore, but the resentment shows in sighs and clipped answers, and the mother says she'd rather have no visits. I think the daughter is failing her in a way that matters, and it isn't just a failure of tact.

4. A friend writes a warm thank-you note for a gift she actually hates. I think that's fine; hiding an unflattering opinion there seems generous rather than wronging.

5. A politician publicly apologizes for a racist remark. The wording is perfect, but he means none of it and still thinks the remark was true; he's only doing it to keep his donors. I think he wrongs the people he insulted, maybe worse than before.

Cases 1 and 2 look nearly alike to me (hidden contempt behind a correct surface), and so do 4 and 5 (warm words with a cold interior), but my gut splits both pairs.

The one I keep going back and forth on: a doctor who privately believes her uninsured patients are failures who brought it on themselves, but gives them exactly the same meticulous care and never lets it show. Does she wrong them?

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? State it as precisely as you can, say what it predicts about the doctor and about new cases I havent thought of, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't accomodate without something ad hoc.
