We did this thing at math club where everyone had to say what bet they'd take in some made-up marble-lottery situations, and I noticed my own answers make no sense when you line them up. I want to know if there's one rule that gets all my gut choices, or if I have to drop some of them.

Here are the situations (you win money for drawing the right marble, and I care about the money, not the colors).

There's a big jar with 90 marbles. 30 are red. The other 60 are black or yellow, but nobody told me the mix — could be 60 black and 0 yellow, 0 black and 60 yellow, or anything between. I get to pick one bet:

Bet A: win $20 if I draw red.
Bet B: win $20 if I draw black.

I take A easily. Red I have a real number for; black feels like betting blind.

Same jar, new choices:

Bet C: win $20 if I draw red or yellow.
Bet D: win $20 if I draw black or yellow.

I take D, also easily. That's 60 marbles for sure. Red-or-yellow might only be 30.

Then I realized: in the first choice I'm acting like red is more likely than black. In the second I'm acting like red-or-yellow is less likely than black-or-yellow. But red-or-yellow is just red plus yellow, and black-or-yellow is just black plus yellow. I added the same yellow to both sides and then flipped. That feels really hard to defend, but both original choices still feel right.

Some other calls I'd make:

- There are two jars. Jar A has exactly 50 red and 50 black. Jar B has 100 marbles, red or black, and I have zero info about the mix. If I have to bet on red, I'll happily take Jar A but not Jar B. But if I have to pick red vs black inside Jar B, I don't care — they look symmetric. So I'm indifferent inside both jars, but across the jars I have a strong preference. That seems like it shouldn't happen.

- If Jar A pays $20 on a win and Jar B pays $30, I still take Jar A. If Jar B pays $50, I take Jar B. So I'm not never betting on the unknown jar; I just need the extra payoff to be big enough. My friend says that means I secretly think the unknown jar is rigged to have fewer reds, but it wasn't — a random machine filled it.

- Someone offers to let me pay $1 to draw from Jar B and win $10 if it's red. Gut says no. For Jar A with the same $10 payoff, I'd probably pay up to $3 or so. I realize those aren't the same question, but the only difference is that I don't know the odds, and that's doing a huge amount of work.

My best guess at a principle is: avoid bets where I can't say the odds, unless the unknown bet pays enough extra. But that feels like a rule about my fear, not about the bets themselves. Is there a sharper principle that captures all of this? Should I be assigning probabilities to the unknown jar anyway? And what would that principle say about a case where the mix in the unknown jar was already fixed before I bet, versus a case where the mix gets decided by a coin flip after I bet — does that change anything? Wich of my gut choices has to go if I cant keep all of them?
