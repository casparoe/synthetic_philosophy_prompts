I have been arguing, on and off, with a friend about conscription, and I would like your actual credence rather than a survey of positions. We are both broadly consequentialist-leaning, but we split sharply on whether a military draft is a relic or a defensible default. The case we keep returning to is deliberately stylized: a wealthy liberal democracy whose security policy is armed neutrality. It maintains armed forces for deterrence and territorial defense, foreswears expeditionary operations, and holds no alliance commitments that could pull it into distant conflicts. One can see rough echoes of this in several European states, past and present, but I do not want the answer to turn on their current institutional details. The proposition I want your credence in is this: for such a state today, a broad-based conscription-and-reserve system is morally preferable, all things considered, to an all-volunteer professional force of roughly equivalent defensive capacity. By "morally preferable" I mean preferable from an impartial standpoint that takes seriously the fair distribution of the risk of death, the state's relationship to its citizens, and the effect of the force structure on whether and how military force actually gets used, not merely cheaper or more traditional. I want a number, not "it depends on the details"; my friend is around 0.8 and I am around 0.3.

More important than the number is the decomposition. Please break the question into its main sub-questions and assign each a rough probability: (a) whether an all-volunteer force of equivalent capacity inevitably amounts to an economic draft, recruiting the poor through pay that functions, in effect, as a coercive offer; (b) whether bearing the risk of military death is the kind of burden that should be distributed by lot or universal service rather than left to labor-market self-selection; (c) whether a conscript reserve makes armed neutrality more credible in deterrent terms, or instead makes escalation and mobilization too easy; (d) whether conscription is in itself a rights-violating form of forced labor even when the state is purely defensive; and (e) whether a large professional corps or a broad conscript reserve poses the greater risk to democratic control over the use of force. Then show how these sub-questions combine into your overall credence, identify which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and say what empirical finding or argument would move that sub-question the most. Finally, if my friend is at 0.8 and I am at 0.3, where do you think our disagreement most likely actually lives?
