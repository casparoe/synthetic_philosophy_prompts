I keep seeing historians talk as if climate, germs, and volcanoes are historical actors — a climate shift that "did" something, a pathogen that "reshaped" society, a river that "dictated" where a city could grow. I'd like a working definition of a historical actor: a thing that can genuinely be said to act in history, rather than just sitting there as a background condition or being a cause in a thinner, mechanical sense.

Here's my test set. I'm only confident about the endpoints: the general is clearly an actor, the freak storm is clearly not. The rest I genuinely don't know, though I've got hunches I can't quite defend.

- A general who decides, on his own authority, to retreat at a decisive battle.
- A freak storm that scatters an invasion fleet right as its about to land.
- The bacterium Yersinia pestis during the Black Death.
- Mount Vesuvius in the destruction of Pompeii.
- The Little Ice Age as a factor in late medieval and early modern harvest failures and witch panics.
- The Nile in shaping the rhythm and location of Egyptian agriculture.
- The printing press in the spread of the Reformation.
- The English East India Company in the conquest of India.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and tell me which verdicts come out least secure. Then tell me what hangs on this: if the definition counts climate and pathogens as actors, are we already sliding into environmental determinism? Or can you call something a historical actor without saying it steers or determines history?
