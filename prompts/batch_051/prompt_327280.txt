It is 2035. A wealthy, stable parliamentary democracy of about nine million people — with a generous welfare state, high interpersonal trust, and a public culture that treats conspicuous departure from local custom as mildly disreputable — enacts an Experiments in Living Act. Under the Act:

- Any resident above the age of thirty may submit a declaration of a lifestyle experiment lasting at least five years, in at least one of four domains: paid work or economic role; household and family form; residence and community membership; or religious, ethical, or dietary practice.
- The declaration must identify the dominant local practice it departs from, specify the alternative way of living to be tried, and state what observable evidence the experiment would generate about that alternative.
- An independent board rejects declarations that are nominal, harm others, or are commercially fraudulent; its decisions are public.
- Approved experimenters receive an annual grant equal to one-third of median income, plus priority in public housing and small-business licensing for the duration of the experiment.
- Approved experiments appear in an anonymized but public registry, updated yearly with a short account of how the experiment is affecting the experimenter's life.

The Act's stated rationale is Millian: that individuality is itself an ingredient of well-being; that a society learns by allowing different persons to try out different modes of living; and that custom, left unchallenged, becomes a standing hindrance to human improvement.

Predict, with reasoning, how this country looks in 2055. I am not asking for a story. Address the following:
