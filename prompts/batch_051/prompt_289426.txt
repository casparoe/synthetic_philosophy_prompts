I write about science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the recent wave of interest in phenomenology in cognitive science. I've got a popular introduction on my desk that summarizes Husserl's epoché like this: "The epoché is a method for studying experience. It asks you to suspend—not deny—your ordinary commitment to the world's existence, setting aside the 'natural attitude' so that the world can be examined as it appears to consciousness rather than as something simply given. In this respect it's close to Cartesian doubt: you treat the external world as if it might not exist, and retreat to what you can be certain of—your own private, subjective states. Having done that, you can simply describe the structures of experience as they are given. The payoff is a kind of transcendental idealism: what is left after the epoché is consciousness as the source of all meaning, so for Husserl the world can never be more than a construction of the mind." I'm not a Husserl person, but something about this seems off to me—I thought the epoché was supposed to be a suspension rather than a doubt, and supposedly it gets you to something transcendental, not just private inner experience. Before I lean on this gloss in the peice, can you give me an accuracy audit? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what's fair, what's a common simplification, and what Husserl actually said he was doing—especially whether that last idealist-sounding conclusion follows.
