Analyze South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission as a test case for the main philosophical accounts of what a state may do about punishment after a transition from state-sponsored atrocity. I know the history; what I want is the theory. The arrangement was this: a new democratic state offered individual perpetrators amnesty from prosecution on condition of full public disclosure of politically motivated acts, with the act judged against a political-objective test and a proportionality requirement, while those denied amnesty remained liable to ordinary prosecution. Take three positions — desert-based retributivism, consequentialism about punishment (deterrence and social stability), and restorative justice — and, for each, work through what it implies about the TRC. In particular: (1) Did the successor state have the standing to waive punishment, or does a victim's claim to see an offender punished belong to the victim in a way that cannot be transferred to, or cancelled by, a new government? (2) Can full disclosure, public confrontation, and the residual threat of prosecution for those who fail the conditions count as accountability, or are they merely the price of impunity? (3) Which features of the TRC does each position explain naturally, and where must each misdescribe or ignore the episode? Finally, turn the lens around and say what the case does to the theories: does it confirm one of them, expose a gap they all share, or show that the standard categories — punishment, impunity, forgiveness, amnesty — are too coarse to capture what transitional justice actually does?
