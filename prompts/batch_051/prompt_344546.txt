Act as a philosopher of language who has thought a lot about the concept of understanding and about machine translation — take the old Hofstadter objection to statistical translation as background, and don't waste space on quotes or citations.

I recently started pre-screening machine-translated asylum interviews at a legal aid office; I'm the one deciding which sentences are safe for an attorney to rely on. Ive realized I can't actually say what I'm claiming when I tell a colleague the engine "doesn't really understand" the Spanish. So I need a working definition of understanding a sentence — not of translation quality, but of what it is for a person or system to understand a sentence in the course of handling it.

Here are the cases. (1) A professional court interpreter translates an idiomatic line, then explains what it presupposed and why a literal version would have misled the jury. (2) An old rule-based system replaces each Spanish word with its single most frequent English dictionary entry; it gets very simple sentences sort of right and turns figurative language into word salad. (3) A person with no Spanish has memorized 50,000 exact Spanish–English sentence pairs; for anything on the list she returns a fine English equivalent, and for anything slightly new she is helpless. (4) A translation-memory tool of the kind professional translators use pulls an exact human translation out of a subtitle corpus; the output is normally flawless. (5) A modern neural machine translation system handles news articles and hospital discharge instructions well, but silently picks the wrong reading when a sentence's meaning depends on a bit of common-sense knowledge, and it never flags the ambiguity. (6) A monolingual English speaker sits in a Chinese-room setup running a complete but mechanical rulebook that maps written Spanish to written English, without knowing what any of the Spanish means. (7) A large language model translates a French pun by producing an English sentence and adding a footnote that explains the original had a pun, even though it has never seen or heard the things the words refer to.

I know (1) has to count as understanding and (2) can't; my vertigo is mostly about (3)–(7), so don't give me a definition that only fixes the clean cases. Start by brainstorming at least three candidate definitions and say how one of the messy cases kills each one. Then give your best defintion, run it through every case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. End by telling me which of your final verdicts is the least secure and why.
