A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether technical systems can be political in themselves, and by the end we were fairly sure we had been talking past one another in at least three different places. Rather than go in circles again, we want to reframe the argument as an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement that a committed design-politics theorist and a committed neutral-instrument theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The design-politics position should be stated as the view that at least some technical systems have political properties built into their arrangements, defaults, and constraints, such that the system itself shapes who can act, pass, coordinate, be seen, or be held to account, in ways that do not simply reduce to what any particular user or operator later decides to do. The neutral-instrument position should be stated as the view that artifacts as such are politically inert: their constraints acquire political significance only through human practices, assignments of meaning, choices about deployment, and decisions about enforcement, maintenance, and exception-making.

The deliverable should not be a defeat of one side by the other. Please produce the statement such a collaboration would converge on. I would like it to have two parts.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. It should cover the cases the two sides spent the evening on: an automated benefits-eligibility system that encodes criteria its own caseworkers cannot readily inspect or override; a road network whose clearances and interchanges make some journeys straightforward and others impractically costly; and a public seating structure that permits resting but reliably deters sleeping. The agreed claims should be non-vacuous, should dispose of disagreements that were merely verbal, and should clarify terminology such as political in themselves, imposed by the artifact, and neutral with respect to use.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state a single live sub-question; give the precise formulation each side would sign, with each side free to word its own position in the way it considers most accurate; identify the crux or cruxes beneath that disagreement; and, where possible, specify the case, intervention, comparison, or test that both sides agree would genuinely bear on the question, with each side recording its prediction in advance. The tests should be ones both sides could accept as diagnostic rather than rigged, and they should not presuppose that one side is already right.

The statement should in particular separate two issues we kept collapsing into each other: first, whether a designed constraint can be political before any particular act of interpretation or enforcement occurs, and second, whether asserting that it can is a way of letting powerful human actors evade responsibility. We also disagreed about whether introducing a new alternative, such as a parallel transit route or an accessible alternative to the seating structure, changes the political character of the original artifact or only changes the character of the larger system. The final text should be one both parties could put their names to without feeling that the wording was slanted, and each residual disagreement should be made exact rather than softened into ambiguity.
