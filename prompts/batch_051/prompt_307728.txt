You are an expert in the philosophy of language who has thought extensively about metaphor. Think carefully, and before you settle on a verdict, briefly articulate the strongest version of each position in this dispute rather than the easiest version to knock down.

Last week I witnessed a long argument between two friends, Elena and Marcus, about whether a metaphor has a meaning beyond its literal content. I have not been able to decide who is right, so I am reporting their exchange as faithfully as I can. Please adjudicate it point by point: say who is right about which specific claim, where each of them overreaches or mischaracterizes the other, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.

Elena began with an example. If someone says that a project is at a crossroads, she said, the words themselves say something about roads and crossings, and nothing more. Taken literally, the sentence is false, or at least not about the project. What the sentence does is cause the listener to notice that the project has reached a point where it could go in different directions. But that noticing is an effect the sentence has on us, not a further thing the sentence itself says.

Marcus replied that this cannot be the whole story, because the effect is not random. The image of a journey brings with it a whole organized set of correspondences: the people involved are travelers, the goal is a destination, difficulties are roadblocks, and the possible choices are forks in the road. If a metaphor were just a prompt to free-associate, he argued, we could not explain why that structure is so predictable. The sentence carries that structure, and carrying that structure is what metaphorical meaning is.

Elena answered that predictability only shows the comparison is familiar. Marcus, she said, was confusing what the words mean with the background knowledge people bring to understanding them. A road sign saying stop causes drivers to brake, but the braking is not part of the sign's meaning; the sign means stop. In the same way, the journey mapping is something we already know, not something the sentence itself asserts.

Marcus said the road-sign case was not analogous. When we speak of looking forward to Friday, of a deadline approaching, of having fallen behind schedule, there is no fully literal way in which our concept of time is already fixed and then given a decorative costume. The movement metaphor is doing the conceptual work. So it will not do to call it merely knowledge we bring; it is the way the thought is built.

Elena then conceded that the mapping may do psychological work, but insisted that showing how people think about time is not the same as saying what a sentence says. If saying that the project is at a crossroads is neither true nor false of the project, then it has no content in the sense of something asserted; what it has is a reliable effect on the mind. Calling that effect meaning jumbles two different questions, one about the sentence and one about the thinker.

Marcus said that this reply simply assumes that the only real meaning is literal, true-or-false meaning. Once you require a metaphor to issue in a clean statement that can be true or false, he argued, you have ruled it out in advance. The set of correspondences is not outside the words. A word like deadline carries the travel frame along with it every time it is used, and a theory that treats all of that as non-meaning is the theory missing the phenomenon.

Elena answered that deadline is a dead metaphor, worn into an ordinary word, and that dead metaphors can be handled as ordinary vocabulary. The interesting case, she said, is the live one. Saying that the project is at a crossroads works because the unusual wording makes the listener notice a likeness between a stage of a project and a road crossing. That works more like sarcasm or hinting: the speaker gets the listener to work something out, but the sentence itself carries only its plain meaning plus an expectation that the listener will draw the comparison.

Marcus answered that sarcasm is a poor parallel because a metaphor does not hint at a single implied opposite; it imports an entire frame, and the details of that frame are part of what a competent listener gains. He added that the line between dead and live metaphors will not hold still. Ordinary speech is saturated with half-dead expressions such as moving on, being stuck, or getting somewhere, and any account that needs a sharp cut between literal and metaphorical meaning will have to carve through language people use every day.

That was roughly where they stopped. Please give me a careful, point-by-point adjudication of the argument as it actually unfolded.
