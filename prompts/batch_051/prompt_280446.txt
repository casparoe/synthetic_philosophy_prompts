I cover philosophy and the sciences for a general-interest ideas magazine, and I am drafting a piece about an internal strain in Schopenhauer's mature system that is usually passed over too quickly. In the second book of The World as Will and Representation he appears to maintain that the will is the blind, restlessly striving inner nature of everything and that the intellect is only an instrument of the will, so that striving and dissatisfaction are built into existence itself. In the third and fourth books, however, he appears to maintain that disinterested aesthetic contemplation can quiet the will and that ascetic self-denial can abolish it in the individual. The two strands seem to conflict: the first says nothing in a person stands outside the will, while the second seems to require something, namely the intellect, to stand outside the will and turn against it. Are the strands genuinely incompatible, or can they be reconciled, and if so on what reading and at what cost to his pessimism? Give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: four candidate reconciliations of the apparent conflict, followed by a fifth point giving your verdict on which reading is the most honest and why. Use British English throughout.
