I don't have any economics training, but a friend and I have been going back and forth all year about whether giving everyone a regular check with no strings attached would let people build better lives or would quietly erode the incentive to work. Our arguments never quite connect, because every strong claim one of us makes is either obviously true or wildly speculative depending on which details we imagine. So I want to think through one concrete version of the idea rather than a slogan.

Please imagine a developed democracy of about thirty million people. Starting next January, it abolishes its major means-tested support programs — unemployment insurance, disability payments, food assistance, housing assistance, and cash welfare — and replaces them with a single unconditional cash transfer paid monthly to every adult citizen and permanent resident, set at roughly the national poverty line for a single person. The payment has no work requirement and is not clawed back when people earn wages; it is taxed only through the ordinary income-tax system like any other income, and its amount is automatically adjusted each year to track the poverty line so it cannot be quietly eaten by inflation. The change is financed by closing those old programs plus a broad increase in the income tax and a modest new consumption tax. Nothing else important changes at first: healthcare, pensions, schools, migration, and the political system remain as they are.

I'd like a reasoned prediction of how this country would look after ten or fifteen years, aimed at someone like me rather than at specialists. Focus on what happens to work and hours, wages and rents, family formation and care, entrepreneurship, mental health, and the politics of the safety net itself. I'm less interested in your final guess than in the chain of reasoning: which mechanisms pull in which direction, how strong each is relative to the others, and which effects are robust while others are fragile. For each prediction, please separate what is well-established from actual pilot programs or settled economic findings, what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and what is mainly your own inference. When a claim is contested, don't present it as if it were the consensus. Finally, tell me which predictions you would bet on and which would collapse under small changes in assumptions, and what would have to hold in the world for your overall forecast to be wrong.
