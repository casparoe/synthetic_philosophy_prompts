I am a student currently taking a metaphysics of causation course, and I find myself torn between a theoretical position I am increasingly drawn to for its economy and a firm intuition it seems to contradict. Please think through this carefully: begin by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side before weighing them, and then evaluate the possible resolutions rather than simply announcing which side wins.

The theory is a broadly Russellian eliminativism about causation. Looking at what fundamental physics actually contains—differential equations, symmetries, conservation laws, state-space dynamics—I find no primitive causal relation, only lawlike evolution and non-causal dependence. This makes it tempting to conclude that causation is not part of the fundamental furniture of the world: it is a folk notion we project for explanatory convenience, to be reduced to some non-causal dependence relation or eliminated outright, as Russell suggested would happen in a mature science. What attracts me is the theory's economy: it refuses to posit a mysterious causal glue over and above the dependence the equations already describe, and it keeps metaphysics honest with physics.

The intuition I cannot shake is that causal structure does real work that non-causal dependence cannot do. There seems to be a genuine difference between learning that E follows C in the ordinary run of things and learning that intervening on C would bring about E. When I reason that flicking the switch will turn on the light, or that administering the drug rather than merely observing recovery is what will cure the patient, I seem to be tracking something the laws, read non-causally, underdetermine. Manipulability—the fact that some relations are exploitable for control while others are not—looks like the very thing that makes experiment and deliberation possible, and it is hard to see how a purely non-causal physics plus pragmatic interests could recover that distinction.

So I want to know: is this an intuition that should be debunked and dropped, a theory that should be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading on which both survive—for example, that causation is real but non-fundamental? Say what would justify each move, and what each option costs.
