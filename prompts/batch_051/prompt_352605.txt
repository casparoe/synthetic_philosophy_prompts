I just got out of a long argument with a friend about whether a translated sacred text can be inspired, and he sent me this from Augustine, City of God, Book 18, chapter 43:

"For the same Spirit who was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the seventy men when they translated them, so that assuredly they could also say something else, just as if the prophet himself had said both, because it would be the same Spirit who said both; and could say the same thing differently, so that, although the words were not the same, yet the same meaning should shine forth to those of good understanding; and could omit or add something, so that even by this it might be shown that there was in that work not human bondage, which the translator owed to the words, but rather divine power, which filled and ruled the mind of the translator."

His summary of it was that Augustine is saying the seventy translators were individually possessed by the same Spirit that had spoken through the prophets, so every place where the Greek Septuagint differs from the Hebrew — an addition, an omission, a different turn of phrase — is itself an extra prophecy, which makes the Greek the better text; from that he concluded that translating from the Hebrew the way Jerome did is a step backwards, and that a translation is only inspired when it follows the Greek. I think he's overreading but I dont know enough to say exactly where, since Augustine really is saying something pretty strong about the Seventy. Could you walk through the passage and tell me which parts of his summary the text actually supports, which parts he's stretched or added, and whether the conclusion about Jerome follows from anything Augustine says there?
