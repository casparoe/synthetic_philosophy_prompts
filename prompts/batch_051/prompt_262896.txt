Consider two ordinary judgments that sit badly together. When a couple who wants a child has one, most of us treat this as a good thing that has happened — good for the parents, but also a good addition to the world. A city that gains residents is said to be thriving; the extra people are not treated as neutral. Yet if we zoom out, many of the same people will not say that a world of eight billion people living good lives is better than an otherwise identical world of seven billion people living lives that are just as good on average, even though the larger world contains more of the very thing — well-being — that seemed to make each added life a good. And almost nobody thinks we should aim to bring about as many happy lives as possible. The added life is welcomed up close and refused at a distance. That looks like an asymmetry in ordinary moral thought: additional happy lives count as improvements in a family or a city, but not in the population as a whole.

I want to know whether any principle actually justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is an inconsistency we should simply own up to. Please give me the strongest candidate principle you can find — averaging value across lives, caring only about people who already exist or who will exist regardless, something about relationships or narrative making some lives count and others not — and test it against the cases that matter: a couple deciding whether to have a first child; the same couple deciding whether to have a fifth child; the death of an existing child set beside the non-existence of a merely possible child; and a case in which the additional lives are very numerous but barely worth living. Then deliver a verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, revise it, or abandon it, and what is the cost of that verdict?

I would also like you to say how confident you are in what you claim, and to single out the point you are least sure of. Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
