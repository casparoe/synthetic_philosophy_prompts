My mother died in the spring, and since then my young daughter has watched Pixar's Coco more times than I can count while I have been reading the Zhuangzi in the evenings. The two keep colliding in my thoughts. I would like you to read Coco through the Zhuangzi's view of death, and to be honest with me about how far that lens reaches.

The collision, as I experience it, is this. Coco runs on a moral economy of remembrance: the dead persist in the Land of the Dead only so long as someone among the living remembers them, and a second, permanent death comes with being forgotten. The film's premise is that what the dead most need from the living is to be kept in being — Héctor begins to dissolve as Coco's memory of him fades, and the ending restores him by returning his photograph to the family ofrenda. The Zhuangzi, as I read it, moves in nearly the opposite direction: death is not annihilation but one more transformation in the great turning of things, and the sage does not cling to a separate self that must outlast its season. The passages I keep returning to are the story in which Zhuangzi drums on a tub after his wife dies and answers Hui Shi by describing her passage through transformation after transformation, and the dream dialogue with a skull, in which the dead man will not trade the rest of death back for the toils of life and asks whether his former clinging to life was not a delusion. And yet Coco also has its moments of release: Héctor's near-dissolution is met by the film with something close to calm, and what resolves the story — so it seems to me — is not chiefly that Héctor is rescued from oblivion but that the living and the dead finally see one another plainly and abandon the old concealments.

I would like a close reading that does justice to both sides. Where does the film dramatize the very attachment the Zhuangzi diagnoses — attachment to a persisting self, to being remembered, to warding off a final dispersal? Where does the film, maybe against its own metaphysics, say something consonant with the Zhuangzi's acceptance of transformation? And where does it resist the reading, so that an honest interpreter would have to say that Coco is not, in the end, a Zhuangzian film? I do not want the film reduced to an allegory or the philosophy flattened into a single take-away; I want to see both where the lens illuminates and where it breaks.

Because I am using this to think through my own grief rather than to win an argument, please also tell me what would change your reading. Which scene, read differently, or which contested interpretation of the Zhuangzi's view of death, would lead you to a different verdict — and where is your reading most likely to fail?
