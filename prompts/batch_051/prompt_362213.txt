Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group, Ana and Ben, have been arguing for weeks about whether the nonreductive physicalist has any good answer to the causal exclusion argument, and neither will grant the other a point. They agree on the background. Physical causal closure: every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. Nonreductive physicalism: mental properties are realized by physical properties and are not identical with them. Exclusion: an effect cannot have more than one sufficient cause unless it is a case of genuine overdetermination, as when two bullets strike a target at once. Put together, these three claims seem to imply that a mental event's causing a bodily movement overdetermines that movement, since the movement already has a complete physical cause.

Ben says the nonreductive physicalist should accept overdetermination and then show that it is not the objectionable kind. Overdetermination by two bullets is problematic because the causes are independent and each would have sufficed; but the mental and the physical are not independent, since one realizes the other. He compares them to a clay statue and the lump of clay. The statue and the lump do not jointly dent the cushion; they are the same thing under two aspects. The mental cause and its neural realizer are like that, so there is only one cause, not two.

Ana replies that Ben has just made her point. If the mental stands to the physical as the statue stands to the clay, then the mental is not a distinct cause at all; it is a description of the thing that did the causing. Either the mental event is identical with the physical event, which gives up nonreductive physicalism, or it contributes no causal work of its own, which is epiphenomenalism under a polite name. Ben has not described a third option.

Ben answers that distinctness of property does not require distinctness of cause, and he offers the determinable–determinate relation. A ball's being scarlet and its being red are distinct properties — scarlet is a determinate of the determinable red — but the scarlet ball does not overdetermine the red detector's response. Its being scarlet and its being red belong to one causal story. Likewise, the neural realizer is the determinate and the mental property is the determinable, so they do not compete.

Ana says this analogy misfires. In the scarlet ball case, there is one causal transaction, and being-red is simply a less specific way of describing the cause that did the work. That is causation at a higher level of description, not a higher-level cause. The exclusion argument never denied that we can describe the solitary physical cause in coarser terms; it denied that the mental property does work over and above the physical one. On Ben's analogy, the mental is that coarser description.

Ben's final reply is an appeal to proportionality. A cause should be neither too specific nor too general for its effect. The ball's being scarlet is too specific for the red detector's response: the detector responds to the ball's redness, not its exact shade, so being-red is the better-proportioned candidate for the cause. By the same standard, a mental property is often better proportioned to a bodily action than any single neural realizer, so the mental is not simply a redundant description; it is sometimes the better candidate for being the cause.

Ana answers that proportionality selects which description of a cause is the more explanatory one; it does not select among competing causal engines. The determinate does the implementing, and the determinable does the explaining, and these are answers to different questions. Ben still has not shown how a mental property that is distinct from its physical realizer also contributes causal work.

I was asked to referee and genuinely could not. Please give a point-by-point adjudication: identify the separate claims they are disputing, say who is right about which ones as actually argued, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. End by saying who is more right overall.
