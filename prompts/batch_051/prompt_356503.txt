I am a novelist, and for my current project I am writing a character who has adopted a strict form of freeganism: she will only consume animal products that would otherwise be wasted — meat retrieved from a supermarket dumpster, a deer struck by a car, eggs from rescued hens, leather from a coat left out for collection. In trying to work out how she would defend this practice to another character, I have found myself persuaded by a view I have come to think of as the no-difference principle. On this view, the wrongness of using animal products lies entirely in the difference one's choice makes to future animal suffering, chiefly by creating or sustaining economic demand. A discarded piece of meat is already dead and already outside the chain of demand; eating it adds no suffering and shifts no incentive. The principle has the virtues I usually want in a theory: it is simple, it is unified, and it yields a definite verdict in every case, which is precisely why I keep reaching for it.

But I also have a firm and persistent intuition that something is still wrong when she eats the body of a sentient creature under the most harmless possible circumstances. It feels less like a calculation about consequences than like a question of what relationship to animals the act expresses and cultivates, and of whether an animal's body is the kind of thing a person should be willing to treat as food at all. The intuition is strongest with the discarded supermarket steak and noticeably weaker with roadkill, and I cannot yet explain that gradient. Someone in my writing group keeps telling me this is just the residue of a culture that treats animal flesh with a special taboo, not an insight into anything real. I do not find that obviously true.

I would like the strongest version of both sides, and a negotiated verdict rather than a flat choice. Specifically: does the no-difference principle exhaust what can be said about the ethics of using animal byproducts, or is the intuition tracking something a purely causal account leaves out? Under what conditions would each move be justified — accepting the intuition and amending or abandoning the principle, explaining the intuition away and retaining the principle, or showing that on a more careful reading the two do not really conflict? And what should I make of the fact that the intuition varies by case in a pattern I cannot articulate?
