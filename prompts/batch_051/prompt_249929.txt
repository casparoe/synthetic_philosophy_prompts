I am a novelist planning a book that follows one family in central Italy across many generations, from the last years of the Roman Empire to the twelfth century. The earliest characters say they speak Latin, though their everyday speech already diverges from the classical authors they copy; the latest say they speak the common tongue of their region—what later centuries will call Italian—and treat Latin as a separate, learned language. I want a pivotal scene in which a character becomes aware that what he speaks is no longer, in some decisive way, the language of his ancestors. But I suspect the scene assumes a boundary that may never have existed. Was there ever a generation in which Latin became Italian, or only an unbroken chain of speakers, none of whom crossed a threshold? If there was no threshold, how would a thoughtful character of, say, the eleventh century truthfully describe his own language, and how can I let him speak it without making him a mouthpiece for modern categories? Is my desire for a single scene of crossing a novelist's illusion that I should resist?
