I've been teaching a logic of ordinary language seminar, and a student cornered me after class to ask why I keep hedging—"that one's not true, but I wouldn't call it false either." I realized I've never actually checked wether the cases where I hedge add up to one phenomenon or several. So I wrote out my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to help me reverse-engineer whatever principle I'm apparently running on. I'm less interested in labels for their own sake than in whether there's one principle that gets all of these, which verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it says about cases I haven't thought of.

My verdicts, roughly in order of how settled I feel:

1. "Amir is bald," said of a man with a full head of hair: false.
2. "Amir is bald," said of a man who is obviously bald: true.
3. "Amir is bald," said of a man right in the middle of the borderline: neither true nor false. It's not that I don't know his hair count; there's just no fact about the matter.
4. Yet "Either Amir is bald or he isn't," said of that same borderline man: true. I see that 3 and 4 look inconsistent; I'm reporting the gut, not defending it yet.
5. "There will be a naval battle here next Thursday," said when nothing about the present settles it: neither true nor false, not merely unpredictable.
6. "The present king of Germany is bald": false, not gappy.
7. "The number two is green": false, not gappy, even though it's a category mistake.
8. "Sherlock Holmes lived on Baker Street": true in some story-relative way; but "Sherlock Holmes had a scar above his left ankle," which the stories neither mention nor rule out: neither true nor false—or maybe just not true. This one shakes my confidence.
9. "This very sentence is false": I want to say neither true nor false, but I could be talked into both.
10. "It rained on this exact spot on May 3, 40,000 years ago": true or false, full stop, even though no one will ever know which. No gap here.
11. "The average American family has 2.4 children": true if read as a claim about the mean, but in ordinary conversation something in me wants to call it gappy. I may just be confusing two readings; tell me if this case doesn't belong on the list.
12. "If it rains tomorrow, the picnic will be moved indoors," said when it won't rain tomorrow: I flip-flop. Sometimes it feels true, sometimes gappy.

My working hunch, which I'm happy for you to wound, is one principle: a statement is neither true nor false when there's no fact of the matter, as opposed to merely no way for us to know. But that seems to explain too little (6 and 7 look like they could go either way on it) and to sit badly with 4.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Which ones does it force me to give up? And what does it say about a case I left off on purpose: a man who is right at the edge between "clearly bald" and "borderline"—a borderline case of a borderline case? Finally, if the honest diagnosis is that I'm running several different principles and fooling myself with a single word like "gap," then tell me how the cases should be seperated.
