I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so be patient with me. I've ended up as the person on my team who decides whether old code and config can be deleted, mostly because I was around for a disaster a couple of years ago: we removed a scheduled job that looked unused, and six months later an auditor asked why the regulatory report for one particular overseas market was never generated. It turned out the job did that and only that, and nobody had thought to ask. Since then I've been running a strict rule, and I want to know whether it's actually defensible or just trauma with a principled-sounding name.

The rule: nobody may delete, rewrite, or switch off any part of the system unless they can first state, in a short paragraph, what the thing was originally for, and either (a) why that purpose is now handled elsewhere or no longer exists, or (b) why the risk of removal is low enough that we can afford to be wrong. If you can't say why the fence might be there, you don't get to take it down. I only later found out that this is basically Chesterton's fence, and maybe the Burkean idea that institutions can encode knowledge that no single person holds, but I hadn't read any of that when I made the rule. It just felt obviously right.

My collegue, who is more of a "delete it and see what breaks" engineer, says my rule is status quo bias with a nice coat of paint. His points: we can't explain most of what we'd want to delete, reconstructing original intent is expensive and unreliable, and the correct way to find out whether dead code matters is to remove it behind a flag and watch metrics, or run it in shadow mode. He also says the rule is unfalsifiable in practice, because by the time something reaches me the reason is normally unknown, so the thing just stays there forever and the codebase rots.

So here's what I want from you. Please take the role of a professional philosopher specialising in the epistemology of institutions or conservatism, and think carefully rather than giving me the usual pop-philosophy answer. Is my rule defensible, or does it collapse into a bias once you have version control, feature flags, staging, and metrics? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the good part (not breaking things that exist for reasons nobody currently remembers) without turning into "never delete anything"? And from the inside, how would I tell whether I'm being prudent or just scared of another audit finding? Start by brainstorming the strongest case for his position, then work out where it breaks, if it does.
