I am a retired chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been reading moral philosophy, especially the literature on collective responsibility and complicity. Something I keep noticing, with the eye of someone who spent decades inside large technical organizations, is how carefully institutions are engineered so that no single node in the chain—no person, no committee, no payment—can be said to cause the harm. The philosophy I have been reading is preoccupied with whether responsibility requires making a causal difference, and with the distinction between causing a wrong and merely enabling it, ratifying it, benefiting from it, or joining a collective project within which it occurs. I would like to see this tension between institutional design and moral complicity turned into sustained satire.

Please write a deadpan internal compliance memorandum, of about 650 words, from the Office of Supply-Chain Integrity of a large multinational (invent the company) to its regional managers, explaining how to restructure a proposed mining contract in an unstable jurisdiction so that the company may proceed while remaining "complicity-neutral." The memorandum should be dry, euphemistic, and procedurally plausible, with a header, date, and distribution list. It should have philosophical teeth: the author should take for granted, without ever naming it, a purely causal account of complicity, and should therefore recommend arrangements such as paying into a sovereign wealth fund rather than to a military unit, licensing through a state-owned intermediary with full legal personality, outsourcing site security to an autonomous contractor with a no-onward-dissemination clause, distributing the relevant knowledge across departments so that no single signatory can be shown to have known the salient facts, and recording whistleblower concerns in an annex without escalating them. A reader who knows the complicity literature should recognize that each recommendation is precisely the kind of move those arguments condemn—that responsibility can travel through shared intention, ratification, and benefit even when no causal contribution can be isolated, and that engineered deniability is itself a form of complicity—while a reader who knows none of this should still find it darkly funny and legible. Include at least one footnote from the company's Ethics and Risk Alignment subgroup that unwittingly states one of the standard philosophical positions as corporate policy.
