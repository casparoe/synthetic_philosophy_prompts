In the essay “Of Money” in his *Political Discourses* (1752), David Hume writes:

“If we consider any one kingdom by itself, it is evident, that the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence; since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money, and a crown in Harry VII.'s time served the same purpose as a pound does at present. It is only the public which draws any advantage from the greater plenty of money; and that only in its wars and negociations with foreign states.”

Take sixteenth-century Spain as a case study for this passage. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these: between roughly 1530 and 1650, the Spanish Crown brought enormous quantities of silver from Potosí, Zacatecas, and elsewhere in the Americas to Seville; the Spanish price level rose severalfold; wages, rents, and state salaries adjusted unevenly; the Crown used the silver to fund wars in the Low Countries, Italy, and Germany, and to service debts to Genoese and German bankers; Spanish goods became expensive relative to English and Dutch goods; and Spain’s domestic cloth industry contracted while imports rose. 

Analyze this episode through the quoted passage. What would Hume’s distinction between the private and the public advantages of money lead us to expect for Spain, and where does the episode confirm, complicate, or fall outside that distinction? Work through the following questions in particular. If, as Hume says, the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence within a single kingdom because prices are proportioned to it, was the Spanish price revolution simply the adjustment he describes, or did the silver do real and lasting damage before and through that adjustment? Does the fact that Spain was a public actor engaged in wars and foreign negotiations rescue the claim that the public alone gains from plentiful money, and if so, what does that imply about the domestic economy that paid for that public advantage? Finally, what does the Spanish case reveal about the limits of Hume’s clean separation between a domestically neutral money and a merely public benefit?
