I'm a novelist, which is probably why my best friend asked me to give the toast at her wedding instead of one of her philosophy colleagues. She's a moral philosopher who works on supererogation—the class of acts that are good but not required—and she's been obsessed for years with a weird effect: the moment you start praising someone as saintly, or say that some kindness was the least she could do, you quietly turn the gift into a tax. She actually believes this, and she's told me, half-joking, that she wants a toast with no talk of love being a duty and no line about how she and her partner earned each other. The room will be half philosophers who will catch every slip into obligation language and half family who just want to feel warm and a little teary. I've spent the past year trying to write a novel about a woman who keeps doing quietly extreme things for people, and I keep hitting the same wall: how do you praise that kind of character without flattening her into a moral category? So this toast is also, honestly, me trying to solve a craft problem in public.

Write me a five-minute wedding toast built around the distinction between what we owe each other and what goes beyond owing. The supererogation idea should do real structural work—it should determine what the speech can and cannot say, not just decorate it. I want it to land for both halves of the room, and I want it to read as affection rather then a lecture. After the speech, include a short plain-English note for me: say where the toast takes liberties with the actual philosophy or leans on a contested reading of supererogation, and tell me which part of the toast you'd rewrite if you came to think that praising an act as beyond duty really does turn it into a requirement—that's the place where your reasoning feels most likely to fail.
