My friend and I just spent hours arguing about the Hart–Fuller debate and neither of us moved an inch. He takes the positivist line: legal validity is one question and moral goodness another, so the Nazi statutes were law — wicked law, but law. I take the Fuller-ish line: a regime that rules by secret retroactive decrees and demands the impossible has stopped doing what law does, so calling its edicts 'law' is already a moral judgment in disguise. We agree on basically every fact about the grudge informer cases and even on the moral revulsion, so the word 'law' is doing all the disputed work. The literature has had the same shape since the 1950s, so I don't want a verdict on who's right; I want a diagnosis of the impasse. Is it verbal, substantive (are moral criteria built into the concept of law or not), or are Hart and Fuller just answering different questions, one descriptive-explanatory and the other interpretive and justificatory? And what would actually count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, a better-posed question, a test about how officials actually use the concept, or a reason to think it's permanently blocked? My suspicion is that 'law' keeps getting used in a neutral classificatory sense and a legitimating sense at the same time, but I'm not sure that's not just renaming the problem.
