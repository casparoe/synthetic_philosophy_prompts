My colleague in philosophical logic has a long-running practice of assigning each sentence one of three values: T (true), F (false), or N (neither true nor false). She has, however, so far recorded verdicts only on unembedded sentences, never on compounds, so her views about how N behaves inside negations, conjunctions, and disjunctions are still unknown. Her record:

- 'The whale is a fish' — F.
- 'There is no present king of Ruritania' — T.
- 'The present king of Ruritania is bald' — N.
- 'There will be a storm tomorrow', said in circumstances where the future is not yet settled — N.
- 'Ada is tall', said of a borderline case for 'tall' — N.
- 'The number seven is hungry' — N.

She must now rule on:

'There will be a storm tomorrow, or there won't be a storm tomorrow.'

I want your prediction of the verdict she should give if she is to remain consistent with her record, and the reasoning behind it. In particular: identify the most plausible rules that fit her recorded verdicts, say what each rule predicts for the new sentence, explain which rule you think best represents her and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly decide between the rules. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please answer in British English.
