In American contract law, whether someone has assented is settled largely by an objective test: courts ask what a reasonable person in the other party's position would infer from the parties' words and conduct, not what the party privately intended. The objective test does its hardest work in contracts of adhesion. When a user clicks 'I agree' to terms they have never read, a court will usually treat the click as a manifestation of assent and bind them anyway. I think I see a structural parallel between this and a familiar split in epistemology. Internalists about epistemic justification hold that a belief is justified only if the believer has some kind of access to the grounds that justify it; externalists hold that a belief can be justified by facts entirely outside the believer's ken, such as the reliability of the process that produced it. My parallel is this: clickwrap agreement is 'externalist agreement.' The clicker is counted as having agreed not because of any occurrent mental state of agreement, but because of a publicly observable indicator that reliably marks agreement in ordinary cases. Is this parallel deep, or is it a loose analogy or a confusion? State the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify at least one point where the two cases come apart. In particular, if the contract case shows that externalism can impose a binding normative status that the agent would disavow when shown the reasoning, is that a reason to worry about epistemic externalism — or does contract law break the analogy before that conclusion can be drawn?
