Hey — in my philosophy of law class we've been going over desuetude, and theres one thing that keeps bugging me. The standard American position is that a statute stays valid no matter how long its gone unenforced; a court can suddenly dust off a 19th-century law against public swearing or whatever. A few legal systems treat long-unenforced statutes as dead letters instead. I get that part as a disagreement about separation of powers and democracy.

What throws me is that the same legal system is totally comfortable with non-use (sort of) extinguishing private rights. If someone openly farms my land for 20 years and I do nothing, adverse possession can cost me the title. If I stall on a lawsuit for years and the other side relies on the delay, laches can bar my claim. But if the state ignores a statute for 80 years, my settled expectation that it's a dead law counts for basically nothing. I realize adverse possession isn't just my passivity — someone else had to possess, and laches needs reliance plus prejudice. So maybe the asymmetry isn't as sloppy as it first looks. But then what's the principle that explains why another person's use and reliance matter when the right is private, but not when the right is the state's? What view of law, or of what makes a rule actually binding, does that dominant practice presuppose? Is it coherent, and what would have to change in how courts handle old statutes and stale claims if we replaced that view consistently in either direction?
