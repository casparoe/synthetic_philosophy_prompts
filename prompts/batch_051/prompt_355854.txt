Write the joint statement that a committed doxasticist about delusions and a committed anti-doxasticist about delusions would co-sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the two-factor theory of monothematic delusions (Capgras, Cotard) as the shared focus. Take doxasticism to be the view that delusions are beliefs: the delusional content is genuinely endorsed and represented in the patient's belief system. Take anti-doxasticism to be the view that delusions are not beliefs but some other state — e.g., an imagining the patient mistakes for a belief, a passively held narrative, or an avowal that does not report endorsement. Both sides accept, as a working framework, the broad two-factor structure: a first factor supplies an anomalous experience (in Capgras, the loss of felt familiarity with a close relative), and a second factor is a deficit in how candidate explanations of that experience are generated, evaluated, or monitored.

The document should have two parts. Part 1: a numbered section of agreed claims about the two-factor theory and the clinical facts, each substantive but acceptable to both sides without reservation. Part 2: a disagreements table. For each row, give: the crux in neutral wording; the doxasticist's own formulation of their side; the anti-doxasticist's own formulation of their side; and a prespecified observation, test, or case both sides accept as bearing on the crux, plus each side's prediction and the outcome that side would count as a concession. The table must include at least these cruxes: whether the patient's assertion of the delusional content should be read as endorsement or as something weaker, such as reporting an imagining; how much inferential integration with the patient's other conduct a state must display to count as a belief, and how much integration delusions actually display; and how double bookkeeping — asserting, e.g., that a spouse has been replaced while continuing to interact with the 'impostor' in mostly ordinary ways — is best explained. Add at least one further crux built around a borderline case, such as a patient who produces the delusional claim under some probes but not others, or whose conviction in it fluctuates. For that borderline case, specify a concrete probe (e.g., a low-stakes wager on the person's identity, an approach-avoidance measure in a real-world interaction, or a psychophysiological measure of familiarity response), and have each side state in advance what it predicts and what result would weaken its view. End by identifying any part of the disagreement the two sides conclude is merely verbal, and say how it was restated as a substantive claim or discarded.
