I spent a long career in atmospheric modelling, so I'm comfortable with systems where what you emit today has its consequences a hundred years out; the philosophy came later, after I retired. Lately I've been stuck on the old climate-economics fight between Nicholas Stern and William Nordhaus over how hard we should work now to protect people who don't exist yet. The bit that bothers me is pure time preference. As I understand it, Stern set the pure rate near zero — around 0.1% a year — on the grounds that the date a person is born isn't a morally relevant fact, while Nordhaus used something like 1.5% a year, partly to respect the impatience that market interest rates seem to display. That one parameter, together with the other assumptions, pushed them to very different places: Stern concluded the right response was an urgent, front-loaded push to cut emissions, while the DICE model had the optimal path as a slow ramp with a modest carbon price at first.

Take that episode and analyze it through the lens of pure time preference, please, but do it carefully. Lay out what pure time preference actually is and how it sits inside the Ramsey formula (as I understand it, the social discount rate comes out as the pure rate plus an elasticity coefficient times the growth rate of consumption). Then identify the players — not just Stern and Nordhaus, but present decision-makers and the future people whose utility gets discounted — and say what the concept says each side faced and should have done: a high pure rate makes future utility count for less solely because of when it occurs, while a near-zero rate treats a life in 2150 as morally equal to a life now. But I want you to be suspicious of the mapping. I keep suspecting that what gets labelled a disagreement about pure time preference is really a disagreement about how much future growth will make our descendants richer, about how risk-averse to be toward catastrophic tail outcomes, and about wether market interest rates can tell you anything about how much present people should count future people. Finally, run the lesson both ways: what does the theory of pure time preference explain about the episode, what does it misdescribe or leave silent, and what does the episode show about whether 'pure time preference' is a cleanly seperable ingredient in policy, or just the drawer where other disagreements get left?
