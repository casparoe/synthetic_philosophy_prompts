I've spent the last few months reporting on how historians argue about their own discipline, and there's an old fight I keep tripping over that seems to have gone absolutely nowhere in decades, and I cant tell whether that's because it's deep or because it's badly posed.

On one side you've got the people who say the serious object of history is power: states, institutions, wars, diplomacy, ruling classes, the decisions made at the top. Their line, roughly, is that you can't understand anything that happens to ordinary people unless you first understand the structures that constrain them, and those structures are made and operated by elites. So starting with the everyday life of a peasant or a factory worker is, at best, colour; it doesn't explain anything.

On the other side you've got the history-from-below and microhistory crowd, who say that version of history is just the self-importance of the powerful written into the discipline. Their view is that the big structures are themselves produced and sustained by millions of ordinary choices, and that you only see how power actually works by looking at it from the receiving end, in a particular village, a particular trial, a particular household. Scale down, they say, and you notice things the archives of the state literally cannot show you.

And here's what's driving me mad: as far as I can tell, neither side denies the other side's facts. Everyone agrees states existed and elites made decisions; everyone agrees ordinary people had lives and some kind of agency. Yet the argument doesn't move. The same methodological jabs get recycled, the same kind of examples get thrown at each other, and people just retreat to their own departments.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement about what causes historical change, and it isn't a verbal one either; it's a disagreement about what history is for — explanation versus something more like recognition or redress. But I'm not confident. It could equally be a hidden empirical bet about where the causal action is, or a disagreement about which scales of analysis count as explanation at all.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict, engage with what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, a different question, a kind of evidence, or just an admission that the two projects shouldn't have been expected to converge in the first place.

Oh, and if you could answer in British English, that would be great — my editor's a stickler for it.
