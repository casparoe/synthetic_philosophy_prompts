I am giving a talk next month to our university's environmental policy working group, and I want to be ready for the question period. The group is made up mostly of economists and political scientists who work on common-pool resources, with a few ecologists, and in past meetings they have tended to treat Hardin's tragedy of the commons as a straightforward prisoner's dilemma and to cite Ostrom's design principles as the empirical discovery that refutes Hardin. My thesis is that this framing is doubly misleading. Hardin's original model is best understood as a description of an open-access regime rather than a genuinely governed common-property regime, and Ostrom's work does not so much overturn the logic of the tragedy as specify the institutional conditions under which that logic can be avoided. Treating all shared resources as a single tragedy of the commons, I will argue, conflates two different kinds of problem with two different remedies and leaves public-goods analyses with the wrong variable set.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections I should actually expect from this audience, not a generic list: ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually put them, with a concise reply to each and a note on where my reply is soft. Include the objection I should most hope nobody raises, and be explicit about what I should concede up front rather than defend. Assume the audience is technically sophisticated about public goods and collective action but impatient with purely conceptual tidying that does not change an intervention or a model. Please write the entire response in continuous prose, as in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
