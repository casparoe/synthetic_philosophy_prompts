I manage the quarterly independence fund at a small nonprofit that works with adults who've had acquired brain injuries. Each round we have enough for maybe seven or eight substantial awards across twenty-ish clients; everyone else gets a small standard package, and the board keeps saying "distribute fairly, not equally" without telling us what that means. The awards are things like vehicle modifications, communication devices, physiotherapy blocks, job coaching, home adaptations, or respite care for a family caregiver. I keep finding that every intuitive rule breaks on real cases. Two clients with the same diagnosis can need very different amounts of money, so equal resource shares feel wrong. Two clients with identical functional levels can be completely different in how happy they are, so targeting reported welfare seems to reward the cheerful and miss the quietly desperate. And a lot of our clients won't ask for things they'd clearly benefit from unless a case manager pushes them, so revealed preference is a mess.

I remember reading about the equality-of-what debate — welfare vs. resources vs. capabilities — and I want to turn it into something my staff can actually use. I don't need a summary of the debate, I need a procedure. Write me a checklist or decision tree that a case manager with no philosophy background can run in under an hour per client, to place each client in a funding tier and give the board a defensible explanation for the tier. The output has to survive a board meeting of non-philosophers asking "why did she get more than him." At each step, say which of the three framings is doing the work, what to do when they give different answers, and where you've just made a judgment call dressed up as a rule. Then run the procedure on two or three invented cases — at least one where it gives an answer my gut says is wrong — and say what the checklist can't handle but the board will still fight about.
