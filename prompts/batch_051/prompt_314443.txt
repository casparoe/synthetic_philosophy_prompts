I sit on my university's research ethics board, and a computational neuroscience group has submitted a protocol that's split us down the middle. They've built what they call a high-fidelity digital mouse: a simulation of a mouse brain at cellular and synaptic resolution, running on a cluster, with realistic sensory inputs and a little virtual body to move around in. The protocol is to run fifty thousand instances across five mouse lines, subject half of them to a battery of genuinely nasty-sounding manipulations—simulated nerve injury, simulated maternal separation, a simulated footshock conditioning paradigm—and then terminate all the instances when data collection is done. One camp on the board keeps saying this is just differential equations, no more a moral patient than a weather forecast getting rained on; the other camp says the moral issue is the causal structure, not the silicon it happens to run on, and if there is something it is like to be that mouse then simulated pain is pain.

I've been asked to draft the board's decision memo for next weeks meeting. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a concrete time frame and conditions the lab could actually comply with. It also needs to lay out criteria we can use for future protocols like this, and it has to engage seriously with whichever side we end up ruling against rather than strawmanning them. The extra wrinkle is that we have no independent way to test whether the sim is conscious in the first place, and some of the "pain" manipulations look like nociceptive circuit activity that may not amount to suffering at all—a question that keeps recuring every time we try to write the policy. My own view isn't settled, so the memo has to make the strongest case rather than back into a conclusion I already hold.
