While reading a generally reliable book on the philosophy of science, I came across this summary of the problem of old evidence:

“Standard Bayesian confirmation theory says that a piece of evidence supports a theory only if it makes that theory more probable. But evidence that was already known before the theory was proposed — such as the anomalous precession of Mercury’s perihelion at the time general relativity appeared — already has probability one, so it cannot raise the probability of anything. Bayesians therefore have to say that evidence known in advance did not support the new theory, even though scientists plainly took it to do exactly that. The usual repair is to pretend the evidence had not yet been observed and ask how likely it would have been if the theory were false; but this move makes confirmation depend on a fictional, historically arbitrary judgment, so the embarrassment remains.”

I know the underlying problem is genuine, but the way the paragraph runs from the technical point to the final dismissal feels too quick to me. I’d like an accuracy audit of this passage. Separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a distortion or overstatement. Where the summary slides from a real limitation of the formalism to a stronger claim about what Bayesians “have to say,” identify the slide. Then evaluate the closing thought: does the charge of historical arbitrariness follow from the accurate parts, and how would a careful Bayesian epistemologist respond to it? Be explicit about which claims you accept, which you reject, and why.
