A scheme I keep turning over. Take a small country that does armed neutrality — Switzerland, Sweden or Finland, roughly — and has been moving back toward conscription. Rather than plain universal service, it runs 'service or pay': everyone is conscripted at 18 for around 11 months, but you can buy your way out by paying the full cost of employing one professional soldier for those same 11 months — salary, training, equipment, pension, the lot. The buyout money is ring-fenced, and by law each buyout has to fund one additional professional soldier, not disappear into general revenue. Career soliders are recruited openly, while conscripts who don't buy out fill the reserve and civil-defence roles the professionals are too few for. Walk through what this does over, say, twenty years. Who ends up serving and who ends up paying? Does the force become more professional or actually less? Is the fee just a class filter with a fig leaf, and does that matter more or less in a country whose defence depends on the whole population being willing to fight? What gets gamed first — medical exemptions, inflated cost-per-soldier figures, overstaffing the professional cadre, or something else? Does ring-fencing change the incentives at all, or is it only moral cover? And where does the thing break first in peacetime, and then in a actual mobilisation? Answer in British English.
