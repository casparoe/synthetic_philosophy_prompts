I write about tech and the strange afterlife of old media, and I keep hitting the same puzzle in different costumes: who should be allowed to do what with a creative work when the owner is gone, dead, or just unfindable? My gut calls are below.

A novel from 1972, author dead, publisher long gone, out of print for decades. A library digitizes it and puts it online. Fine.

A 1998 game whose studio went bankrupt and no one seems to hold the rights. A fan writes a patch so it runs on modern machines and gives the patch away. Fine.

Same situation, but a company bundles the patched game and sells it. Not fine — though I can't say why that changes it, apart from money.

A photographer dies without heirs; her negatives turn up at an estate sale; the buyer sells prints commercialy. Not fine.

An online game gets shut down and fans run a private server. Fine.

A company bought a defunct software firm's assets years ago, does nothing with the code, but sends takedown notices to an abandonware archive. Ignoring those is not fine, even though the code is dead in every practical sense.

A living author went silent thirty years ago and can't be found; someone translates her out-of-print book and sells it. Not fine.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the case I keep flipping on — a museum puts on a paid exhibition of an unfindable living painter's work without permission? If some of my verdicts have to go, which ones, and what would dropping them show about the principle I should accept?
