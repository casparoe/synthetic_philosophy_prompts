I keep turning over the explore/exploit framework from reinforcement learning, and it struck me that Mill's experiments in living in On Liberty looks like an explore/exploit policy written in moral vocabulary. Map ways of life onto arms of a multi-armed bandit: the prevailing conventions are the known-best arm, the eccentrics trying unorthodox ways of living are pulls of untested arms, and aggregate social welfare is the cumulative reward. Read this way, Mill's argument that individuality generates data about how to live and that conformity leaves society stuck in a local optimum is just the claim that a society that never explores has zero information gain and can't improve. Individuality is exploration; conformity is pure exploitation.

I can't tell whether this is a genuine identity of structure or me imposing a loss function on a view that does not have one. If it is deep, results should transfer both ways. A bandit would have something specific to say about how much eccentricity a society should permit: exploration should be high early and decay over time, it should be directed at the most promising arms (UCB-style), and a deviant way of life that keeps yielding low reward should be dropped quickly. Mill sounds like he wants variation kept alive permanently, and his harm principle blocks society from suppressing unprofitable experiments even when the data already looks bad: that resembles a side constraint rather than regret minimisation. So the parallel makes at least one prediction that seems to fail. What would have to be true of both sides for the transfer to work, and where exactly does the analogy come appart? For instance, arms in a bandit are pre-defined and stationary, but ways of life are invented by the experimenters, and trying them changes both the experimenter and the society that observes them; how bad is that disanalogy? Is there anything that does transfer cleanly, or is this just the usual thing of pattern-matching every trade-off onto explore/exploit?
