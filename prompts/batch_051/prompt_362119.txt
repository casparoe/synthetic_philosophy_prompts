I keep hearing the same analogy used to argue that deference to authority can itself be a virtue, and I want someone to pressure-test it. A friend of mine puts it this way: a subordinate in a functional chain of command—a soldier on an order, a lab assistant in the Milgram setup—is like a hammer in a carpenter's hand. The hammer doesn't pick the nail, doesn't aim, doesn't decide how hard to swing; it just lets the hand do the work. So when a legitimate authority gives the order, he says, the moral weight of the act travels upward to the one who decided, and the subordinate's virtue is exactly to be a dependable instrument rather than a second-guesser. I suspect the analogy is doing the real argument, not just illustrating it. Can you audit it? Give me the correspondence table (carpenter, hammer, nail, skill, swing, the crooked shelf—whatever the right columns are), then the points where the mapping breaks. Be specific about which features of the hammer get quietly imported into the subordinate (no foresight, no judgment, no capacity to refuse, no aims of its own) and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion that moral agency moves up the chain. Does the deference-as-a-virtue conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If its a bad analogy, what's a better one?
