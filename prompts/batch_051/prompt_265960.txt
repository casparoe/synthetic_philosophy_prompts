I retired a few years ago from a career in climate science, where I spent my working life building models that fed into decisions affecting other people and whose internal behaviour could still surprise their own designers. Since then I have been reading philosophy of technology, and I keep returning to the debate over responsibility gaps for autonomous systems. Call one camp the gap realists: they hold that as systems learn and act without direct human specification of each output, we encounter genuine responsibility gaps—cases in which a harm occurs but no person or institution is fully answerable for it, not merely because evidence is missing, but because the justificatory link between any human decision and the outcome has become too attenuated to ground blame. Call the other camp the responsibility redistributionists: they hold that this is a diagnostic artifact, and that responsibility never vanishes; it must instead be redistributed across designers, deployers, auditors, regulators, and perhaps organisations as moral agents. On their view, the absence of a single convenient defendant is not the absence of responsible agency.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who has written on moral responsibility and the ethics of autonomous systems. Before you draft, spend some time brainstorming the strongest version of each camp's position and the weakest point each camp would privately concede; you may set this out in a short preliminary section marked as brainstorming, but it should not replace the final document. Then draft the document that an adversarial collaboration between the gap realists and the responsibility redistributionists should have produced. Structure it as follows: a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, about how learning systems behave, what responsibility requires, and where current institutions struggle, substantive enough not to be vacuous; then a disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view, with a note wherever a disagreement turns out to be at least partly verbal; then a section identifying the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and finally, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance and a statement of what result would make each side revise.

Use as a running example a last-mile delivery robot whose collision-avoidance policy, a learned mapping from sensor inputs to steering actions, is retrained across its fleet through a stream of decentralized updates that no single engineer reviews, and which strikes a pedestrian in a way no engineer anticipated. Two tentative hypotheses of mine, which you should test and refine rather than accept, are that one crux is whether forward-looking answerability (who must explain, repair, and adjust practice after a harm) can substitute for backward-looking attribution (who deserves blame or punishment); and that another crux is whether the practical opacity of a learned policy differs in kind from the unpredictability of a human employee, or only in degree. If you conclude that part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why; if an apparently verbal disagreement masks a substantive one, say that instead.
