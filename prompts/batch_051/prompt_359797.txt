For a project in my government class, I have been reconstructing the death penalty votes of one state supreme court justice, and I am trying to predict how she will vote in an upcoming case. Her previous votes seem to involve retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing an innocent person, but I cannot tell which consideration actually drives her, and I suspect more than one principle could fit her record.

Her earlier cases are these. In State v. Becker, an inmate already serving life without parole killed a guard during an escape attempt; guilt was established by security video and the testimony of several officers. She voted to uphold the death sentence and wrote separately that for a person already permanently removed from free society, death is the only additional penalty the law has left. In State v. Okafor, the defendant was paid to kill a business owner and had planned the killing over several weeks, leaving a clear record of the arrangement, and his guilt was not seriously contested. She voted to uphold the death sentence, emphasizing that the murder was calculated in advance and carried out for money. In State v. Ruiz, the prosecution's case rested on a single eyewitness identification with little physical corroboration. She voted to set aside the death sentence, writing that an irreversible punishment cannot rest on a meaningful chance that the wrong person might be executed. In State v. Perry, a nineteen-year-old with no prior record shot a friend during a heated argument. The shooting was captured on video, so guilt was not in question, but she voted to set aside the death sentence, writing that although the killing was intentional, it was the product of a sudden argument rather than calculated planning.

The upcoming case is State v. Voss. The defendant abducted a stranger, held the victim for two days, and inflicted prolonged suffering before the killing. He acted alone and not for material gain. Guilt is documented by a recording and by his confession. At sentencing, the defense and prosecution experts broadly agreed that he understood the wrongfulness of his conduct but was driven by a compulsive sadism that did not involve the weighing of penalties; the state argued that this made him more dangerous, not less culpable.

I need to write a prediction of what consistency requires her to do in Voss, but different principles seem to fit her earlier votes equally well. Please help me answer these questions. First, what principle best captures her four earlier votes, and what is at least one other plausible principle that captures them just as well? Second, how would each of those principles classify Voss, and what is your best prediction of her vote? Third, which earlier case is the closest analog to Voss, and which way does it pull? Fourth, what further capital case, if I could add one to her record, would most cleanly settle which principle she is actually running?
