I'm TA-ing an intro political philosophy course, and the prof's short-answer question is giving me trouble to grade. The question asks students to interpret a specific passage from Rousseau. It goes like this:

Rousseau writes in *The Social Contract*, Book II, Chapter 2: “Sovereignty, for the same reason as makes it inalienable, is indivisible; for will either is, or is not, general; it is the will either of the body of the people, or only of a part of it. In the first case, the will, when declared, is an act of Sovereignty and constitutes law: in the second, it is merely a particular will, or act of magistracy—at the most a decree.”

The question then asks: Does this passage help us decide whether a populist leader who says they are enacting “the will of the people” actually has the general will on their side? Why or why not?

One student's answer was:

“Rousseau distinguishes the general will from the will of all. The will of all is just private interests added together, while the general will aims at the common good and applies equally to everyone. This passage shows that the general will is expressed only when the whole people, not just a faction, makes a decision. Since the general will is always right, any vote of the full citizen assembly that follows proper sovereign procedures is by definition the general will. So if a populist leader holds a national referendum and the majority votes yes, the result is the general will, and the leader is carrying it out. People who oppose that are just insisting on their own private will against the people’s will.”

I'm torn. I think the student is right about the general will vs. will of all distinction, but I have a nagging feeling they're sliding into identifying the general will with majority votes in a way Rousseau wouldn't accept, and maybe misusing the “always right” line. Could you grade the answer out of 10 and give me the specific comments I'd put in the margins, point by point? I'd rather not just write “mostly right but somewhat sloppy” without being able to say exactly where it goes wrong.
