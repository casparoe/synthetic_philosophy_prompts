I have been trying to figure out whether a private reporting rule I follow is actually good epistemic practice or just a professional tic dressed up as rigor. As a science journalist, when I cover stories with a well-defined statistical base, I have no trouble with numbers: a clinical trial, a poll, a weather model, an actuarial table. In those cases I will happily write that an effect is significant at a given level or that a model assigns a 40 percent chance of rain. But when I cover one-off, non-repeatable questions, I refuse to put a probability on them. I mean questions like whether a particular AI lab will produce transformative systems within a decade, whether a specific climate tipping point will be crossed on a given timeline, whether a pending piece of regulation will actually change industry behavior once it is implemented, or whether a country will follow through on a security guarantee. For those, I use graded but non-numerical language: "plausible," "not established," "more likely than not," "we simply do not know." My rule is that numerical probabilities belong only to cases where I can identify a stable reference class or a model with known error behavior, and that where no such thing exists, no number is better than a misleading one.

The rule feels principled to me because it tracks something like Keynes's contrast between risk and uncertainty. On that picture, risk is the kind of situation that admits of numerical probability, while uncertainty is the kind that does not, and treating the second as if it were the first produces a false precision that no amount of statistical hygiene can repair. But an editor I respect says I am using Keynes as an excuse for evasiveness. His objection is that Keynes did not treat uncertainty as the end of reasoning. He insists that evidence can still be better or worse, that the weight of an argument matters independently of whether its conclusion can be priced, and that reasonable people must act and advise on the basis of their best judgment even when they cannot compute the odds. On his view, my refusal to quantify, even as a range or an imprecise probability, is a way of avoiding the question of how strongly I actually believe a claim. He adds that my verbal labels are themselves ambiguous, that readers parse "plausible" in wildly different ways, and that when the stakes are high I owe them something more explicit than a dignified shrug.

I want a serious assessment of this habit, not a defense of false precision and not a demand that every hunch be converted into a decimal. Is it defensible to refuse numerical probabilities outside stable reference classes, or is that simply a way for a journalist to feel rigorous while dodging the hard question? What would a Keynesian in the spirit of the view, rather than a scholarly reconstruction, say about my policy, and where would the policy break down? Are there conditions under which I should quantify anyway, for example with intervals, odds, or degrees of confidence, and are there cases where the "no number" rule is doing genuine work?
