I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and I am working on an essay about a puzzle that strikes me as under-treated: G. E. Moore's hand-waving proof that there are external things is widely treated as valid, yet just as widely treated as having made no real dent in the skeptical problem. The proof is worth taking seriously precisely because that double reaction is so stable: it feels irrefutable and beside the point at once. I want my essay to locate the source of that gap, and I do not want to fall back on the usual line that Moore simply missed the skeptic's point.

Before I assess the proof itself, I want the prior deliverable: the desiderata. Write what any adequate proof of an external world — or, more broadly, any adequate answer to external-world skepticism — must actually accomplish, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The list should include, but need not be limited to, the following candidates: whether the premises must be more certain than the conclusion; whether the argument must avoid begging the question against the skeptic, and what begging the question amounts to in that setting; whether the proof must persuade, or only be sound; whether it must explain why the gesture seems too easy, as if centuries of difficulty could be dissolved by a one-line inference; and whether it must respect ordinary epistemic principles such as closure under known entailment (if you know one thing and know it entails a second, you are in a position to know the second) rather than quietly revising them. For each item, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp — the Moorean common-sense camp, the contextualist, the closure-revising camp, and so on — and flag any two or more desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied. Then give a brief scorecard for Moore's proof against the list, mainly to show the list does real work. Please keep the reply under 700 words; a table is acceptable.
