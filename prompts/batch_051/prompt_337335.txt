I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on judgment under uncertainty and applied epistemology. The final exam is a two-hour in-class essay worth 20 marks. The course has covered base-rate neglect, the planning fallacy, the inside/outside view distinction, reference class forecasting, and philosophical work on epistemic modesty and deference. In past years, students could recite the distinction but could not handle a case where a base rate is relevant yet the case is unusual: some treated the base rate as decisive, others dismissed it on the first disanalogy they noticed. I want an exam question that makes both shortcuts fail.

The question should be built around this invented case. A research consortium is deciding whether to fund a three-year, $4 million project to take a working solid-state battery prototype to manufacturing. The team's inside plan forecasts launch in 18 months, itemizes forty engineering milestones, and the team has hit the last seven. The consortium's database of 58 previous hardware commercialization projects shows that 11% shipped within three years; among those that shipped, the median cost overrun was 140%; and the most common failure point was the prototype-to-manufacturing transition. Nearly all of those previous teams also had detailed plans and had hit early milestones. The team argues the database's reference class is misleading because most of those projects never had a working prototype that passed third-party testing, as theirs has.

Please write (i) the essay question, framed so students must propose and defend a principled rule for combining the base rate with the team's case-specific information, not merely report the inside/outside distinction; (ii) a model answer of about 800 words that sets out the strongest version of each side, then defends a specific weighting, discussing the reference-class objection, whether the team's inside details are diagnostic conditional on the base rate, and what epistemic modesty does and does not require in this case; and (iii) a marking scheme out of 20, organized around five or six must-hit moves, with the marks each earns and a sentence on the characteristic mistake each move catches—including the two shortcuts described above.
