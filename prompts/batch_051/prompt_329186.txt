I'm giving a colloquium in a fortnight at a linguistics department that's mostly generative morphosyntacticians, along with one historical linguist who still thinks Neogrammarian sound change is exceptionless and one psycholinguist whose whole career is built on dual-route models of past-tense processing. My talk defends the claim that the regular/irregular split in morphology is not a real architectural divide: so-called exceptions—suppletion like go/went, umlaut plurals, strong-verb ablaut—are just the tail end of the same rule system, where a rule's conditioning context has narrowed to a single lexeme, and the 'default' rule is simply the one with the broadest context. I've got a one-paragraph argument sketch I'll summarise at the start of the talk, so use it as the target: I start from an else/where-style competition among rules (the more specific rule wins, the general rule applies elsewhere), then I claim historical sound change is exactly the machine that manufactures the gradient—it turns transparent decomposable morphology into stem alternations by applying exceptionlessly across contexts, and with enough lexical fallout you get suppletion. The last step is that the psycholinguistic evidence for two routes (frequency effects, double dissociations, regular inflection of nonce forms) can be re-read as the high-type-frequency end of a single system rather than evidence for a separate rule component.
