I have been working through the dichotomy of control as taught in the modern Stoicism revival — the division between what is eph' hēmin ("up to us") and what is not, where serenity is supposed to come from concerning oneself only with the former. The standard statement I was taught divides things this way: up to us are judgment, choice, and intention; not up to us are the body, health, property, reputation, and what other people do. But when I sort concrete cases by feel, my verdicts do not fall neatly on either side.

Here are my verdicts. (1) Whether an unkind thought flashes through my mind when I see someone is not up to me; whether I take it up, dwell on it, or act on it is up to me. (2) After I have locked the door and checked the handle three times, my belief that the door is locked is not something I can reverse at will; I would say even my assent to that belief is not up to me in the moment, although I can choose to stop checking. (3) Whether I improve at the piano over six months is, I would say, substantially up to me through how I practice, not merely the practicing itself; yet injury, plateaus, and limits of talent can intervene. (4) When someone insults me, the first flush of anger is not up to me; whether I continue to ruminate and let it become a settled state is up to me. (5) In addiction, the craving is not up to me, and the decision not to act on it can feel dramatically less voluntary at 3 a.m. than in the afternoon; I resist saying it is either simply up to me or simply automatic. (6) What others think of me is not up to me; acting so as to deserve a good reputation is, at least in the attempt, up to me. (7) That I acquired some conviction in childhood was not up to me; whether I scrutinize it now is up to me.

My working principle is that what is up to me is a second-order act — endorsing, refusing, or suspending an impression, and choosing to act on an endorsed impression — while the arrival of impressions, first-order content of perception or thought, and downstream results are never up to me. That principle fits (1), (4), and (7), but it seems to misclassify (2), where there is no distinct second-order act yet the first-order assent does not feel voluntary, (3), where a downstream result feels more up to me than the principle allows, and (5), where the second-order act is present but compromised. I would like you to treat these verdicts as data. Is there a single precise principle of the "up to us" that captures most of them, and if some must be overturned, which and why? State the principle precisely, say which cases motivate each clause, test it on cases I have not listed (a spontaneous aesthetic preference, an implicit bias, a decision made under duress, and a failure of attention), and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
