Suppose a country enacts an Apology Registry Act to govern official state apologies for historical wrongs. Under the Act, any apology issued by the state for a historical wrong must, within three years of being issued, be accepted by a majority vote of a descendant assembly elected by voters who self-identify as descendants of the wronged group, with eligibility for that electorate certified by an independent registrar using genealogical evidence and evidence of community membership. If the assembly accepts, the apology is entered on a permanent public register, and courts must thereafter treat the facts acknowledged in the apology as established in any later proceeding against the state. If the assembly rejects, the apology is expunged and the state may not issue a materially similar apology for fifteen years. The issuing government must also table, within the same three years, a concrete remedial measure — a compensation fund, land return, institutional reform, or something equivalent — with first-year funding of at least one percent of the annual justice budget. Walk through how this institution would actually behave: which wrongs governments would choose to apologize for, how governments and their lawyers would draft the texts, how descendant groups would organize and splinter around the vote, and what forms of gaming or capture would arise on both the state side and the claimant side. Then identify the worst failure modes, say what the Act does to the distinction between symbolic acknowledgment and material remedy, and assess which failures can be fixed by amendment without gutting the registry's underlying purpose.
