I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've lately been reading the classics in philosophy—Theseus's ship, the sorites paradox, all that. It got me thinking about something from my other hobby, maintaining old open-source code. Suppose a program was originally released under a strong copyleft license, say the GPL. A developer wants to get out from under the license, so he divides the program into forty modules. Every month he replaces one module with a freshly written one that passes the same tests, and he does it clean-room style: he writes from a spec of what the module should do, never looking at the original code. Each individual replacement is small enough that nobody would call the result a new program—you'd just say the program got a new module. After forty months, not a single original line remains.

Here's the question. The developer claims that, somewhere along the way, the program stopped being a derivative work of the GPL original and became an independent work, so the copyleft obligations no longer apply. At what point, if ever, does that happen? Is the finished, fully replaced program still a derivative work? If you think it crossed a line somewhere in the middle, where was the line, and what made that particular replacement different from the one before it? Does it matter weather he replaces modules in dependency order (low-level utilities first) versus jumping around? And does the ship of Theseus actually help here, or is program identity under copyright a different kind of problem from the one the ship is meant to illustrate?
