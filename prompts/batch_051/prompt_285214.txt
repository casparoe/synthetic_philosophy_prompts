For some time I have been developing a small theory of dreaming, and I would welcome an honest assessment from someone who knows the philosophical literature better than I do. My trade is fiction, and the theory grew out of an observation about revision. When I heavily revise a rough draft — merging two characters, moving a scene, changing an outcome — I eventually lose all access to what the draft first said; I remember only the strongest surviving version, and I mistake that memory for a memory of the original. I have come to suspect that dreaming is the same kind of process. I call the view "revision without an original." Its central claim is this: we do not undergo dreams while asleep. The sleeping brain produces only unstructured fragments — images, affects, stray dialogue — with no narrative coherence. The "dream" we report in the morning is a fast, largely unnoticed act of waking or half-waking editing, in which the mind imposes plot, motive, and sequence onto those fragments, much as a novelist shapes notes into a scene. Because the edit is all that remains in memory, we attribute it to the sleep and conclude that we had a dream. I am aware of a tradition that denies that dreams are experiences we have during sleep, and another that holds there is no fact of the matter about a dream's real content. What I want to know is whether my view is merely a version of one of those, a genuinely distinct third option, or an unstable hybrid. Please assess it charitably but candidly: where does it break, and where, if anywhere, does it improve on the alternatives?
