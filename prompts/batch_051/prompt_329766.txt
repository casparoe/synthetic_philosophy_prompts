I direct continuing education for a mid-sized hospital system and own the process that signs clinicians off as competent to perform new protocols — sepsis pathways, behavioral de-escalation, medication reconciliation — without supervision. We run several hundred sign-offs a year: a wrong sign-off can harm patients, and a wrong refusal triggers remediation and grievances. Our current process is a written test plus a scripted simulation, but we regularly find staff who score well and then fail to do any of it on the floor under load — and, when debriefed, they can state exactly what they should have done. I've been reading about Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action: roughly, that someone doesn't truly know a thing unless they act on it, and that a failure to act shows the knowledge was never really there. Write a sign-off procedure for my clinical educators that treats this doctrine as a working assessment rule. The educators have no philosophical training, each sign-off has about thirty minutes of observation and debrief, and the procedure's output is a recommendation to our credentialing committee. The procedure should say, in order: what evidence can count as knowing a protocol on this view, what a sufficient answer to each check looks like, where the procedure stops and refuses to sign someone off, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is a judgment about readiness rather than a checkable fact. Flag the distinctions that must be kept rather than flattening the doctrine into "just watch behavior instead of testing," and flag the judgments that cannot be proceduralized. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a nurse who passes every written sepsis-pathway check and narrates the steps correctly after a simulation, but did not start the bundle in simulation until prompted; (2) a physician who follows the de-escalation protocol whenever observed or audited but bypasses it otherwise, while writing unusually good reflections about when the protocol shouldn't apply; (3) a clinician who cannot explain the rationale for a protocol in debrief but, across many observations, performs it correctly and appropriately in varying conditions. Make sure at least one case comes out in a way the committee would resist. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do.
