I got roped into translating a short French poem for my cousin's wedding because I apparently 'did French at school', and I've also just gone down the Hofstadter rabbit hole—the book where he tries dozens of English versions of that tiny Marot poem about the rose, trying to keep the form. I won't quote the poem, don't worry. Somewhere between trying to keep the rhyme scheme, the metre, and the actual sense, a friend convinced me of the following, and now I can't find the exit.

(1) What a text says is fixed by the meanings of its words in their arrangement.
(2) A faithful translation says the same thing as the original.
(3) Keeping a rhyme scheme and metre in the target language forces you, at least somewhere in the poem, to use words whose literal meanings differ from the original's words—you can't use the same words in the same order, and the formal constraints keep pushing you into synonyms, added bits, or shifted images.
(4) Therefore any verse translation that preserves the poem's form also changes what the text says.
(5) So no verse translation that preserves form can be faithful.
(6) But that surely proves too much: it would make verse translation an art of necessary unfaithfulness, and by the same reasoning even a prose paraphrase in the same language would change what the text says whenever it changed a word.

I'm inclinded to keep (2) and (3), and suspect the trouble is really in (1) or (4), but I can't see what rejecting either would cost me. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? And please answer in British English.
