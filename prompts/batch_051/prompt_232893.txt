A colleague who studies skilled action makes the following claim. When an experienced outfielder catches a high ball, they are not computing where the ball will land, nor holding any representation of its future landing point. Instead, the fielder keeps a low-dimensional optical variable roughly fixed — for instance, running so that the ball appears to rise at a constant rate in their visual field — and holds that variable steady until the ball reaches their hands. On this picture, the predictive structure is not stored in the player's head; it is spread across the player's movement and the continuously available optical information. The two accounts can produce identical running paths.
