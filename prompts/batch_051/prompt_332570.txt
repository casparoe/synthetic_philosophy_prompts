For years I have used a version of the simulation argument as a brake on longtermist fanaticism, and I want to check whether the brake survives a change in an assumption it may have been quietly relying on. The brake runs roughly as follows. Longtermist expected-value reasoning treats the number of future moral patients as effectively astronomical and treats the far future as a real, influenceable object of action. But the simulation argument says we should assign non-negligible probability to the hypothesis that our world is an ancestor simulation. If we are simulated, the future we are trying to steer is not the base-reality future: it may be truncated when the simulators end the run, its far reaches may never be computed at all, and whatever happens in base reality is outside our causal reach. So the enormous expected values that attach to shaping the long-run future should be discounted for model uncertainty about which futures are eligible, which are reachable, and which will simply be switched off, and the fanatical conclusion should lose its grip.

I have begun to suspect, though, that the brake depends on treating a simulated future as a lesser future — on the idea that moving probability mass from biological descendants to simulated minds shrinks what is at stake. Consider the changed assumption, now widely taken seriously in discussions of whole-brain emulation and digital minds, that a sufficiently detailed simulation of a mind is itself a moral patient and counts the same as a biological one. On that assumption a civilization running ancestor simulations is not cancelling the far future; it is manufacturing one. And if simulated civilizations can themselves eventually run simulations, the total stock of morally relevant far-future lives across the nested hierarchy could be vastly larger than the single base-reality population that ordinary longtermism counts. The sign would then threaten to flip: rather than dampening fanaticism about the far future, the simulation argument would supercharge it by multiplying the number of lives the fanatic is obliged to count.

My question is whether the original brake survives this changed assumption, and if so in what form. Which step in the brake actually fails when simulated minds count: the claim that our leverage over nested simulated futures is negligible, the claim that simulated lives are less real or less eligible, or the claim that simulation uncertainty should lower rather than raise the expected number of relevant far-future patients? Or does the brake still go through because our own branch's termination risk dominates the expectation, or because our actions now cannot be expected to propagate across levels of the hierarchy? I am interested in what happens to the argument under this assumption, not in whether the resulting conclusion is congenial.
