I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a longform piece about a disagreement in moral philosophy that has outlived decades of counterexamples: the debate over trolley problems and the Doctrine of Double Effect. Rather than recapitulate the usual exchanges, I want to show readers what a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two main readings could produce, with the residual disagreement made exact. Please draft that document.

The two positions are the Permissibility Reading and the Appraisal Reading. The Permissibility Reading holds that the distinction between harm an agent intends and harm an agent merely foresees can itself make the difference between a permissible and an impermissible act. The Appraisal Reading holds that the distinction is real and morally weighty but does not alter permissibility; it bears on retrospective blame, censure, and the meaning or significance of the action.

Anchor the document in the standard pair of cases: the bystander who diverts a trolley from five workers onto a side track where one worker is standing, and the bystander who stops the trolley by pushing one person from a footbridge into its path. You may add one or two variants only if both camps would agree they bear on a stated crux.

The deliverable has two parts. First, an Agreed claims section: exactly five numbered claims, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks, each substantive enough that neither side is merely signing a truism. They should include claims about the two cases themselves, not only claims about the debate. Second, a Disagreements table with exactly five rows and these columns: the issue; the Permissibility Reading's exact wording; the Appraisal Reading's exact wording; the crux, stated as a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the test, case, or argument both sides accept as bearing on the crux; and each side's prediction registered in advance. The wording columns must be ones each side would accept for its own view, with no slanted verbs and no strawmen.

Make the cruxes more specific than the original disagreement. At least one crux should concern the closeness problem, understood as the difficulty of keeping the intended/foreseen distinction stable once a side effect can be described more or less tightly as part of the action itself; at least one should concern a variant in which the person killed is being used as a means rather than merely being in the way. Where one side predicts a mixed result rather than a clean win, have it say so.

Below the table, add a brief note outside the document: state how confident you are that each side would actually sign its column, and identify the single clause or test you are least sure about.
