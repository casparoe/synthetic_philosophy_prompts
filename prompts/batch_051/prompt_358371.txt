So I'm a PhD student in philosophy of social science, and I keep coming back to Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism that there can't be laws of history — not just that Marx in particular got it wrong, but that the whole project of making large-scale historical predictions are methodologically broken. I'd like you to run that lens over one real episode: the collapse of the Soviet bloc from the fall of the Berlin Wall through the dissolution of the USSR in 1991. I know the standard story — planned economies stagnating, Gorbachev's reforms spinning out of control, nationalist movements, the wall coming down. Don't rehash that. What I want is the careful two-way mapping. First, what would a Popperian have been entitled to say, before 1989, about the Soviet project as an attempt to steer history according to a supposed law of development towards communism? Second, which parts of the actual collapse does the Popperian framework genuinely explain — for instance the way predictions and expectations fed back into behavior (what Popper called the Oedipus effect), or the failure of holistic planning — and which parts does it have nothing to say about, because they're about economics, institutions, nationalism, or just contingency? Third, run the same lens over the aftermath: people immediately drew a new historicist lesson from the collapse, the end-of-history triumphalism of the 1990s — the Fukuyama-style claim that liberal democracy had won for good. Is that move self-undermining from a Popperian point of view? And finally, does the episode actually confirm Popper's anti-historicism, or does it just confirm that one particular historicist prediction failed? I'm most interested in where the lens has to be bent or goes silent — a theory that explains too much here should make me suspicious.
