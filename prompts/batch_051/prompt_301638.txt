Before I retired I spent most of my working life in physical chemistry, and I have lately become troubled by a habit I once treated as a virtue: treating statistical-mechanical probabilities as objective features of the world rather than as bookkeeping for our ignorance. The principle one meets again and again here is the Principal Principle, understood roughly as the requirement that a rational agent's credence in a proposition, conditional on its objective chance and on admissible evidence, equal that chance. I am after a number, not an exposition.

Consider a gas prepared in a low-entropy macrostate in a sealed container. Its microdynamics are deterministic: the exact microstate at the moment of preparation fixes the entire subsequent trajectory. Statistical mechanics nevertheless assigns a well-defined probability p to the system's being in macrostate M one minute later, obtained by a coarse-grained measure over the microstates compatible with the observed macrostate. Now let P be the following proposition:

P: In a deterministic world, at least some such coarse-grained probabilities are objective chances in the sense required by the Principal Principle, so that a rational agent who knows the observed macrostate, the dynamics, and the measure — but not the exact microstate — is rationally required to assign credence p to the system's being in M, and this requirement flows from the nature of chance rather than from a merely pragmatic rule.

Give me your current credence in P. If you think P needs sharpening, give the minimal sharpening and estimate the sharpened proposition instead, noting what changed. Then decompose your estimate into the sub-questions that carry the weight: whether determinism is compatible with non-trivial objective chance at all; whether a coarse-graining or typicality measure can single out p non-arbitrarily rather than by an arbitrary choice among rival measures; whether the Principal Principle is the correct articulation of the norm connecting chance and rational credence, or needs amendment in just these cases; and whether the fact that finer information about the microstate would rationally compel moving from p to a determinate 0 or 1 shows that p was never an objective chance. Assign a rough probability and a line of justification to each, say how you combine them, and then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would most move that sub-question. For my part I sit near 0.4, moved mainly by doubts about the measure; I want to know whether that is where the stress in the view actually lies.
