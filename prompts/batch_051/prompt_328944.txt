I recently moved from a product-focused ML team into an interpretability group, and I keep running into the same frustration that drew me here: our models are right more often than they are legible, and most of our usual training signals suppress the difference rather than exposing it. A senior researcher on the team has proposed a concrete scheme, and I have been asked to write the internal analysis of it before we consider piloting it. We would train a main model on a large corpus of questions with known answers. For each item, the model produces a chain of thought followed by a final answer. Separately, a reader — either a much smaller model or a rotating pool of human auditors — sees the question and the chain of thought but not the final answer, and must assign a probability that the main model's final answer will be correct. The reader's reported probabilities are scored with a proper scoring rule against the actual correctness of the main model's final answer, and we track how much the reader's predictive accuracy improves over a no-chain baseline. So far this is a familiar side evaluation. The twist is the training signal for the main model: it is rewarded only for the improvement in the reader's ability to predict whether its final answer is correct, and not at all for whether that answer is actually right.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a moral position to evaluate. The motivating claim is that this forces legibility in the precise sense that matters: a separate observer could look at the reasoning and tell when the model is about to fail, even on questions the observer could not answer alone. I want to understand how the scheme would actually behave under optimization pressure. What would it incentivize on easy, hard, and ambiguous items, and does it genuinely train the model to reveal its own uncertainty, or does it create an incentive to choose final answers that are easy for the reader to classify — including knowingly wrong, hedged, or abstention-like answers whose incorrectness is easy to advertise? How would a learned reader system be gamed differently from a pool of human auditors, and where does each version fail first as the task distribution shifts or the main model improves? Finally, suppose the first version of this goes badly in ways we did not predict: which failure modes would be structural to the idea rather than incidental to its implementation, and which could be repaired without abandoning the core insight?
