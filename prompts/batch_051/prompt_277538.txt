I'm a PhD student in demography, and there's a two-faced pattern in how the people around me think about population that I've never managed to get a straight answer about. On the one hand, we treat average welfare as if it were the whole story: countries are compared by GDP per capita, a city is described as better off when average incomes rise, and if a country's population grew while total output stayed flat we'd say it had gotten worse, because the extra people diluted the average. The added people themselves count for nothing, or as a liability. On the other hand, the same people describe below-replacement fertility in Japan, Italy, of South Korea as a slow-motion catastrophe, and they support baby bonuses, parental leave, childcare subsidies and pro-natalist tax policy to keep the birth rate up. If additional people just dilute the average, why is it a crisis when fewer are being born? And if additional people are good in themselves, why do we reach for per-capita measures that ignore them?

The inconsistency gets worse when you look at conservation. A lot of the same people who'd never say a city is better just because it's bigger will tell you that a species going from a few hundred individuals to a few thousand is unambiguously good news—not because the individual animals are better off, but just because there are more of them, full stop. And in private life everyone still seems to think a growing family or a growing town is a good thing worth celebrating, even when the growth doesn't raise anyone's average wellbeing.

I'm not looking for reading recommendations or citations. I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value here: what, exactly, does this set of practices presuppose about whether extra people (or animals) make the world better? Could a purely average-based view justify the pro-natalist policies without smuggling in the idea that extra people are good in themselves—say, because babies are needed to pay for pensions or care for an aging population? Could a total-based view make peace with the near-universal use of per-capita measures, perhaps by treating the average as a proxy for individual welfare rather than a theory of value? And is the whole pattern philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival views would license these practices and the real confusion is our own? I keep being told I'm overthinking it, but it seems to me that we move fluidly between two cost-benefit logics that are not obviously compatible.
