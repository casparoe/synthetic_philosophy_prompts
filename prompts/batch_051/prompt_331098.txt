Health systems commonly assess the value of a medical intervention by its cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). The QALY embeds a specific arithmetic of value: a year of life is assigned a quality weight anchored at 0 for death and 1 for full health, and the two numbers are multiplied; one year at a weight of 0.5 counts as exactly half a year of full health. Interventions are then ranked by how many QALYs they add, and the gains of different people are simply summed, with no weighting by recipient. At the same time, many of the systems that use this calculus make irregular exceptions—for identifiable dying patients, or for especially severe or rare conditions—accepting costs per QALY far above what they would normally allow.

What theory of how values combine does this practice presuppose? Specifically, which of the following does the QALY framework actually commit its users to, and which does it merely fit well with? (1) Within a single life, quality and duration combine multiplicatively rather than additively or lexically. (2) Across persons, value is strictly additive, so no QALY is worth more than any other no matter who receives it. (3) Quality and duration are fully commensurable, so some sufficiently large amount of mild benefit can always outweigh a given severe harm, with no lexical priority for avoiding the worst states. Reconstruct the implicit axiology as precisely as you can, separating what the use of the QALY strictly presupposes from what it is merely naturally paired with, and explain how the exceptions bear on the question: do they show that the practice is not committed to a single view after all, or are they concessions layered on top of, and perhaps in tension with, the underlying arithmetic? Finally, indicate what the practice would have to change under a lexical view on which no quantity of mild well-being could outweigh a particular severe harm, or under a view on which distribution matters as well as aggregate QALYs.
