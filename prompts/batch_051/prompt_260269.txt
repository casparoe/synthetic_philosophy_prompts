I've been thinking about how philosophers adjudicate conflicts between general moral or political principles and intuitions about particular cases, and the procedure looks to me like a statistician cleaning a dataset. Principles play the role of a fitted model; case intuitions play the role of data; the preference for simple, general principles plays the role of a regularization penalty. When a recalcitrant case conflicts with an otherwise powerful principle, philosophers either discount the case as distorted, complicate the principle to accommodate it, or add an auxiliary hypothesis that dissolves the conflict — that is, outlier rejection, refitting, or adding model terms. There is no held-out test set, and much of what separates philosophers seems to be a difference in rejection thresholds and in the weight assigned to simplicity. I want to know whether this is a genuine structural identity or an attractive but shallow analogy. Please make the mapping precise; say what would have to be true of case intuitions and principles for results from statistical model selection or robust statistics to transfer; and give at least one prediction the analogy makes that turns out false or misleading.
