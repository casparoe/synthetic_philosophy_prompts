You are a moral philosopher who specializes in translating distributive ethics into operational rules for health policy. I am a lead analyst at a public health technology-assessment agency. Every month I receive roughly a dozen proposed interventions—screenings, treatments, prevention programs—but we can fund only some, and the stakes are high: rejected interventions mean preventable death and disability for identifiable patient groups. The interventions differ in the size of the health benefit they would produce and in how badly off their intended beneficiaries are at baseline, in terms of current health, life expectancy, and household income. My team currently ranks nearly everything by cost per quality-adjusted life-year and is uneasy about it, but our final recommendations must be defensible to a non-philosopher board in a one-page summary, and no analyst on the team has formal training in moral philosophy or welfare economics.

Your task is to write a reusable step-by-step procedure—call it a working prioritarianism checklist—that operationalizes the idea that a benefit of a given size matters more, morally, the worse off its recipients are. It must be usable by one analyst in under half a day per batch, without fitting a social welfare function or doing original modeling. Before giving the procedure, brainstorm briefly which elements of prioritarianism can survive translation into a half-day checklist and which cannot.

The procedure must explicitly handle all of the following:

1. The preliminary screens an analyst runs before applying any priority weights, such as effectiveness, cost, and evidence credibility.

2. A concrete, defensible way to assign priority weights to recipients, rather than an unexplained table of multipliers.

3. The leveling-down objection and the boundary it marks: first, a rule stating that pure leveling down—making a better-off group worse off without benefiting anyone—is never chosen; second, a test for distinguishing that objectionable case from a permissible trade-off in which a better-off group loses some benefit so that a worse-off group gains substantially more, together with a rule for when such a trade-off is acceptable.

4. How to handle interventions whose beneficiaries span different levels of disadvantage, or that impose costs partly on better-off groups, for instance through taxation, so that the checklist does not silently assume a clean separation between better-off and worse-off.

5. A short list of hard cases the checklist will handle poorly, an honest marking of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as computation, and a statement of what the checklist cannot do—especially where prioritarianism itself stops giving answers and an institutional or political decision must take over.

Then apply the procedure to these three compressed cases:

- Intervention A: adds 2 quality-adjusted life-years to people whose baseline health and income are already good, at a cost of $20,000 per quality-adjusted life-year.
- Intervention B: adds 0.8 quality-adjusted life-years to people with severe congenital disabilities and low income, at a cost of $80,000 per quality-adjusted life-year.
- Intervention C: would slightly worsen outcomes for a middle group in order to substantially improve outcomes for a worse-off group.

State what the procedure recommends in each case, identify any recommendation that a pure cost-per-quality-adjusted-life-year ranking would oppose, and explain why the divergence is or is not justified. If the procedure cannot decide a case on the information given, say exactly what further information it would need rather than guessing.
