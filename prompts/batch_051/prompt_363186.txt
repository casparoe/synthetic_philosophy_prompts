In one 45-minute session I need to get them to see a distinction that both gets called monism: the view that there is exactly one thing (Parmenides, Spinoza on a common reading) versus the view that there are plenty of things but exactly one fundamental thing, namely the whole, with ordinary objects depending on it (priority monism, roughly Schaffer). I don't want you to explain these views to me; I want a plan for how I should teach that distinction. The big thing I want them to grasp is that the interesting fight is over what's fundamental, not over how many things exist.

So: what has to be in place first, and what does each later piece depend on? Pick one concrete running example that can carry the whole thing — I'd reach for a table and its parts, but that seems to stack the deck toward 'parts are fundamental, wholes depend on parts,' which is exactly the intuition priority monism is trying to question, so if not the table, what, and what features must the example have and lack? Which misconceptions should I expect and where in the sequence do I head each one off — I'm already betting on 'priority monism says tables and chairs arn't real,' 'fundamental means smallest,' and 'this is just holism or emergence again.' And give me the one check-for-understanding question I can ask at the end that would seperate someone who's got the dependence-running-from-part-to-whole idea from someone who has just learned the labels. What should I openly refuse to cover at this level (formal mereology? gunky worlds? the exegesis of whether Spinoza was actually an existence monist)? Be definite about all of it: choose one sequence, one example, one end-of-session question, and own the choices with reasons rather than giving me a menu.
