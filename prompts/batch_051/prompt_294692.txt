I co-chair the student conduct board at a small liberal-arts college, and I need a decision memo for a case that's split us right down the middle. All first-years take a required course called Design and Computation, and the course runs in a persistent online building environment—a shared virtual campus that's always on, where students prototype structures that have to actually work: load-bearing walls, circulation, sightlines, and so on. One student spent most of the term building an elaborate virtual house for her final review; it had custom interior logic and weeks of iteration in it. Two nights before the review, a classmate used a permissions exploit to delete the entire build. Her only backup was seven days old, so the most developed version of the design is gone. She's asking us to treat this as the code's prohibition on damaging or destroying the property of another—a clause we've only ever applied to physical objects and money. The classmate says that's a category error: a virtual house is not a thing, it's a pattern of bits that can in principle be rebuilt, so the actual offense, if any, is misuse of the system, not destruction of property. Half the committee thinks he's right and that finding property damage here would drag every in-world prank into conduct proceedings; the other half thinks the object was real property in every way that mattered to her work and the loss is real enough that the code should cover it. Write the memo the board can act on: a bottom line (violation or no violation, and if violation, what sanction, given our range from formal warning up to transcript notation), how we should read 'property' going forward, the reasoning we could defend if either side appeals, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the position you recommend against.
