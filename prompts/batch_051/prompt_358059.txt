Two engineers on my team got into a long argument in a design meeting about how to build the "ethics layer" for a home-care robot we're prototyping. The robot has to make decisions in seconds — like whether to wake a sleeping patient to give a scheduled medication, or how much to say about a worrying test result before a doctor is present — and we've been going back and forth about whether that layer should be a fixed set of rules with a priority ranking, or something more deliberative. I jotted down the exchange afterwards because I couldn't decide who was making more sense.

(1) Ana argued for explicit rules with a priority ordering: encode principles like "don't cause preventable harm," "respect the patient's stated wishes," "don't deceive," and so on, then give them a fixed precedence so the robot always has an answer. She said any ethical theory does this anyway, just in prose; we'd be forced to make the ordering explicit, which is a feature, not a bug. (2) Marcus pushed back that the real disagreement has never been about what the rules say but about how you break ties between them — when not deceiving conflicts with not causing distress, say. Once you put a priority ordering on top, he said, the entire moral content moves into the ordering, and that ordering is just a preference. You've built a little utilitarian calculus in disguise and labeled the output "ethical." (3) Ana replied that humans also have an ordering; they've just absorbed it from education and experience until it feels natural. We have to pick some default, so we're not dodging the philosophy, we're forcing it out into the open. And for genuinely hard cases, the robot can flag the conflict and defer to a human caregiver. (4) Marcus answered that deferral is exactly the kind of thing that fails in deployment: the bad cases are the ones where there is no human there in time, and anyway "defer to whoever set the priorities" means the robot inherits one engineer's biases and then gets to wear the halo of "ethical AI," even though it's priorities are just somebody's gut calls. (5) Ana said the alternative — training on thousands of human feedback ratings — hides the same biases in a way no one can audit, whereas a rule hierarchy can at least be inspected, debated, and changed rule by rule. (6) Marcus said auditability isn't the same as being right. A bad priority ordering is transparently bad; that doesn't make the robot's action moral. Maybe the layer should be adversarial instead: have the machine run several ethical perspectives against each other before acting. (7) Ana said that either the perspectives converge, in which case the adversarial machinery was pointless and a plain rule would have done, or they don't, and something has to settle the conflict — which is a priority ordering again, just one level up and far harder to inspect. Then the meeting ran out of time.

I genuinely can't tell whether Marcus landed his main point or Ana successfully absorbed it. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate: say who is right about what, where each one overreaches, and what the disagreement is really about underneath the engineering talk.
