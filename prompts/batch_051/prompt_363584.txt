I've been getting into philosophy of history and I've ended up with a view that makes my old lab instincts itch: there's no principled way of seperating pseudohistory from real history. Atlantis, ancient aliens, Fomenko's claim that ancient chronology is mostly a medieval fabrication — those get called fringe, but when I look at the actual inferences, it looks like the same game as mainstream ancient history: you decide which ambiguous evidence to take seriously, fit it to a prior narrative, and treat the absence of evidence as itself informative. The difference seems to be institutional respectability, not method. In the lab I'd have said a hypothesis earns its keep by risking failure. So tell me what would count against my view. What evidence or arguments would show that pseudohistory really is doing something different in kind? I want cases where fringe programmes died from their own results rather than from gatekeeping, predictions the mainstream made that fringe frameworks couldn't have made, and any formal features — testability, consilience, falsifiability, whatever — that cleanly mark the divide. Sort them by how decisive they'd be. And be honest: have I defined 'principled line' so strictly that the view is unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a problem for this kind of view?
