I teach an undergraduate seminar on the intellectual history of democratic socialism, and one session is built around Albert Einstein's 1949 essay, Why Socialism?, published in the first issue of Monthly Review. The students can reliably restate its main commitments: on Einstein's account, capitalism concentrates private capital into an oligarchy whose power cannot be checked even by democratic politics, wastes labor through unrestrained competition, and leaves individuals exposed to avoidable unemployment and insecurity; the remedy he endorses is a socialist economy in which the means of production are owned by society, production is planned to meet community needs rather than private profit, every person is guaranteed a livelihood, and democratic institutions remain strong enough to protect individuals from the bureaucratic power that a planned economy could otherwise produce. The students are much less good at doing anything with these commitments once the conversation moves past 1955.

I would like a handout for the next session that models the kind of thinking the course is trying to teach: an extrapolation rather than a summary. Predict, with justification, how Einstein would most plausibly have judged two developments that postdate him. First, the modern movement for a universal basic income, increasingly defended on the ground that automation will eliminate the need for wage labor and that a guaranteed income would give people genuine freedom. Second, the rise of platform cooperatives and other worker-owned firms in digitally mediated, algorithmically managed sectors, proposed as a repair to gig-economy precarity that stops short of full state ownership. For each development, reason from the specific commitments identified above, not from a generic socialist position; where those commitments pull in different directions, say so, and explain which pull you take to be stronger and why. Finish by naming one claim in the essay that your two predictions jointly put under pressure, if any.
