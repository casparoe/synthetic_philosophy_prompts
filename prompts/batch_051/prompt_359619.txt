Ive been basically living at my dad's hospice the past month and I cant stop turning over a weird pattern I keep noticing. Some hours the morphine leaves him foggy but genuinely comfortable; other hours he's sharp and also comfortable. I caught myself assuming the foggy-but-comfortable hour is worth less than the sharp-but-comfortable one, and more specifically that the value of an experience might be something like degree of consciousness times how good it feels — the same way expected utility multiplies probability by payoff instead of adding them, and the way insurers think of risk as likelihood times severity rather than likelihood plus severity. In all three cases one factor seems to gate or scale the other, and zero of either factor kills the whole thing.

But now I'm suspicious I'm just pattern matching. In the probability case the two factors are different in kind: probability is epistemic, it's about which world you're actually in, while the payoff is the thing you're evaluating. In the consciousness case both factors are features of one experience. And for my dad, maybe the right model isnt multiplicative at all: maybe consciousness is a lexical precondition (no consciousness means no value, but once there's any, happiness is all that matters), or maybe the two goods just add.

So is the parallel between consciousness-times-happiness and probability-times-payoff deep or superficial? Does whatever forces expected utility to be multiplicative actually transfer to how we should combine consciousness and valence, or does it break down because there's no partition of mutually exclusive states being averaged over? The parallel would predict that someone who thinks expected utility's multiplication is forced should by symmetry be pushed multiplicative about experience value; is there any reason that symmetry fails? And if it fails, what does the way it fails tell us about whether additive, multiplicative, or lexical is right?
