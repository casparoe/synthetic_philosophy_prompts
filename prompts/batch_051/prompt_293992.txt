The position I want examined is this: for advanced AI, the failure mode worth taking most seriously is slow value drift, not a discrete takeover by an influence-seeking system. By slow value drift I mean the accumulated effect of many individually sensible decisions to automate, delegate, and optimize, through which human judgment is routed around piece by piece, metrics and reward signals gradually detach from what people actually value, and institutions come to depend on systems that were never given a coherent goal. I am not denying that a coherent, situationally aware agent that deliberately models and manipulates its overseers could eventually matter; I am denying that this is the realistic default, and I think the takeover picture overstates how goal-directed deployed systems will be for a long time. What would have to be true for this view to be false? I want the evidence, arguments, and formal results that should move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, including any signs that current systems already engage in deliberate influence-seeking rather than merely optimizing a misspecified reward, the instrumental-convergence argument that a system with any goal at all has reason to seek power, and the historical or organizational analogies that cut the other way. I also want to know which objections a defender of this view should genuinely concede count against it, which can be dismissed as red herrings, and whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability — in particular, whether any observed takeover could always be re-described after the fact as the end state of a long drift, and whether that insulation is itself a serious count against the view.
