I am a high school junior preparing an essay for a national writing competition on law and technology, and I would like detailed comments on my draft. The essay argues that the orphan works problem and the abandoned software problem should not be treated as two instances of the same category, and that abandoned software calls for a different default than orphan books or photographs. The draft is below. Please comment mainly on the reasoning and accuracy, not the prose: whether the central distinction does real argumentative work, where the essay overclaims, and which parts rest on contested normative premises rather than settled background.

Draft:

Orphan works are usually discussed as one problem: a copyrighted work's owner cannot be found, so would-be users cannot get permission, and the work sits unused. The standard responses involve limited liability, registries, or collective licensing. I think that framing lumps together two situations that need different treatment.

The first is the inert work: a book, a photograph, a film. Once fixed, such a work does not become less usable merely because time passes. If someone copies the work while the owner is missing and the owner later reappears, the main harm is a delayed or unpaid license, and that can be repaired, more or less, by a fee afterwards. The work is not being destroyed by waiting; it is being withheld. Here a licensing or damages-limiting solution makes sense, because it allows use while preserving the owner's eventual economic claim.

The second is infrastructural software: operating systems, firmware, emulators, file-format converters, abandoned code libraries. Unmaintained software does not just sit there; it decays. Hardware changes, formats drift, dependencies break, and vulnerabilities accumulate. When a program's owner cannot be found, the loss is not merely forgone access but an ongoing loss of function. The loss is also largely irreversible: a program that could have been preserved, patched, or emulated five years ago often cannot be reconstructed after institutions and users have migrated to replacements. Waiting for licensing is therefore a form of gradual deletion.

The normative conclusion I draw is that abandonment should relax exclusivity only for the infrastructural cases. For inert works, the system can be fixed by making permission and damages predictable. For abandoned software, I propose what I call a stewardship default. After a defined period and documented attempts to locate the owner, a library, archive, researcher, or user could legally preserve, patch for security and interoperability, emulate, and study the software without facing infringement liability or statutory damages for those specific acts. The original owner would keep the right to exploit the work commercially and to stop bad-faith uses, such as reselling the code as a competing product. The person invoking the default would bear the burden of showing that the use was conservational or noncommercial and that a genuine search for the owner had been made.

The principle underneath is that the variable that matters is not the owner's absence alone. It is the combination of three things: the owner's absence, the work's dependence on continued maintenance, and the irreversibility of the harm caused by leaving it untouched. An abandoned novel can be stored and licensed later; a maintained program already has an owner who can consent. Only at the intersection of absence, decay, and irreversibility does ordinary enforcement buy society almost nothing while producing a loss the owner's later reappearance cannot undo.

The strongest objection I can see is the incentive one: if going quiet costs a developer the exclusive right to permit or refuse preservation, developers might abandon less successful products sooner than they otherwise would. But the proposal keeps the commercial exploitation right, which is where the main financial return from software lies. A developer who is still trying to profit gains nothing from allowing the product to rot into someone else's nonprofit stewardship; the default would pressure developers to either maintain a product or release it deliberately rather than drifting into silence. A second objection is that users should back up and migrate earlier. But that asks the public to absorb irreversible cultural and technical loss in order to protect a rightholder who, by definition, is not present to benefit from that protection.
