Here's an asymmetry in legal practice I can't stop thinking about. In plenty of jurisdictions, courts are totally happy saying a corporation intended to defraud people, knowingly filed false statements, or acted recklessly — they build criminal convictions on those mental-state predicates without blinking. They'll reconstruct the firm's intent from emails, meeting minutes, and bonus structures, and none of that is treated as weird. But if you ask whether the same corporation felt remorse after the fraud, or panicked when the subpoenas landed, or whether the compliance department was depressed all quarter, the same lawyers suddenly get all philosophical and say that's a category mistake becuase a corporation doesn't have a mind. So mental states get imputed when they matter for guilt, and denied when they'd matter for mitigation or for moral responsibility in any thicker sense. Both are psychological predicates; neither is literally true of a charter and some bylaws.

I want the strongest principle that actually draws this line. It can't just be that intentions are functional states and emotions are phenomenal states, because legal and financial practice is full of apparently affective corporate talk: a firm is described as optimistic in its earnings calls, having a strong risk appetite, being emboldened by a ruling. Are those all just metaphors? If they're metaphors, why isn't saying the firm intended the fraud also a metaphor? And if the real line is just that the law imputes whatever attitude is useful for liability, contracting, or standing and refuses the moment it would count toward mitigation, then is corporate mens rea itself just a convenient legal fiction? I'm asking whether we should be fine with that fiction — law isn't psychology, and the fiction might be useful — or whether we should stop pretending firms intend things at all and punish them only through strict liability or regulatory-style penalties.
