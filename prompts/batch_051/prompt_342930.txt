So my brother has spent the last two years building a wooden sailboat in the backyard. He's not a carpenter, he's an accountant, and he's basically learned as he went, which means he's redone whole sections more than once when they came out wrong. He has no interest in actually sailing it. Once it's finished and he's convinced it's seaworthy, he wants to donate it to the local youth sailing club and go back to his desk.

I cant get past this. To me the only thing that made all those weekends and the back pain and the re-sanding worthwhile was ending up with a boat he'd use. If the club gets the boat, fine, but they could have gotten a boat from a kit. All the extra struggle he put in didn't make the boat any better than a kit would, so the struggle itself didn't buy anything. I told him he'd have been better off writing a check and keeping his Saturdays.

He shot back with something like: 'If it had been easy, it wouldn't have been worth doing. I wanted the hard part.' That sounds like rationalizing to me, but here's what I can't shake: when he asked if I'd really swap two years of building for a kit boat in the driveway and those Saturdays on the couch, I couldn't honestly say yes. So I don't fully buy my own line.

I guess what I'm asking is whether there's a coherent story where the difficulty itself was what made the project worthwhile, rather than just the price he paid for a boat. Or is my side right that effort is just a cost? Feel free to tell me the question's badly set up, or that we're both confused in different ways — I'd rather hear that than a forced verdict. And if parts of this are just genuinely contested or come down to temperament, say so instead of pretending its all settled.
