I've gone down a rabbit hole on game theory lately and ended up at this thing called program equilibrium, and it kind of convinced me of a bigger idea. The setup is the normal one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: two rational players both defect, because defecting is better for you no matter what the other person does. But suppose each player has to submit a computer program instead, and before the game each program gets to read the other program's source code. Now a program can say: I'll cooperate if and only if the other program's code shows it will cooperate against me. If both programs do that, mutual cooperation becomes stable — no repeated play, no reputation, nobody coming in afterward to punish defectors. I was arguing with my friend in coding club about this, and the bigger lesson I've been carrying around is that transparency of your decision procedure can replace trust. If an agent's actual rules are open to inspection in principle, you don't need a history with someone or a promise about the future to get pro-social behavior; you just need legibility. I've been applying it to real stuff, like thinking companies should be forced to publish their pricing or content-moderation algorithms, because mutual visibility would let them cooperate instead of racing to the bottom. But my friend says I'm definately over-reading one toy model, and honestly I can't tell if he's right or just annoyed that I beat him at Rock Paper Scissors with a script.

So treat my view as the claim that the program-equilibrium mechanism generalizes: transparent, inspectable decision procedures are enough to make cooperation rational among self-interested agents, and trust, reputation, and external enforcement are in principle dispensable. What would have to be true for this view to be false or overblown? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, not just a pile of objections. I'd personally be most persuaded by a real-world case where making decision procedures more transparent made two parties more cutthroat rather than more cooperative, or some formal result showing the toy case can't survive small changes. If the strongest objection is just that self-referential programs are light-years away from actual people and firms, say whether that's decisively against me or just a shrug.
