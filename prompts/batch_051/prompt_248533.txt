Consider the following position, which I am inclined to accept. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, in its standard form, runs as follows. If each individual's preferences over lotteries on social states satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms, and the preferences of a social observer over the same lotteries also satisfy those axioms, and the observer respects unanimous preferences — if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, she is indifferent between them, and if every individual weakly prefers one lottery to another, she weakly prefers it as well — then the observer's utility function must be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, with non-negative weights. On the natural reading, this appears to prove that the only rational social welfare function is weighted utilitarianism; and if one adds Harsanyi's impartiality condition, under which the observer assigns equal probability to being in each person's position, the weights equalise and one obtains utilitarianism proper. I am therefore tempted to say that prioritarianism, egalitarianism and their relatives are not merely rival moral views but violations of rationality or of respect for unanimous preference, and that utilitarianism is forced on any rational impartial spectator.

I want you to stress-test this as a piece of moral theory, not merely as a piece of mathematics. Tell me what would have to be true for the position to be wrong, or for its force to drain away. Work through the assumptions one by one — the expected-utility axioms as applied to the social observer, the Pareto condition as applied to preferences over lotteries, the interpretation of the von Neumann–Morgenstern utility numbers as welfare, and the impartiality step — and say, for each, what the world or our evaluative practice would have to look like for that assumption to fail, which failures are live possibilities rather than merely logical ones, and which would be decisive rather than merely awkward. I would also like to know what a defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what its critics say. If the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by adjusting an interpretation or re-scaling utilities — say so, and say whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. I suspect I would be most moved by a demonstration that the theorem's additive form is an artefact of imposing the independence axiom on the social observer rather than a discovery about rational social choice, but do not let that suspicion shape your assessment.
