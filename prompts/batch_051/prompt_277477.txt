I am a novelist writing a book in which much hinges on whether a jury, and eventually a reader, will say that a character brought about a death intentionally. I have been reading experimental philosophy, and I have come to think the ordinary word is less settled than it sounds. What I want is a working definition of the word intentionally—or of the phrase did it intentionally—that classifies the following cases correctly. I list them from the clearest to the most vexing.

1. A man plans for weeks, points a rifle at a rival, and pulls the trigger; the rival dies. Here, surely, he killed the rival intentionally.
2. A man trips on a torn carpet and stumbles into a child, knocking the child into the road. Here, surely, he did not knock the child into the road intentionally.
3. A company chairman approves a program he has been told will increase profits but also damage the environment. Asked about the damage, he says the environment is a matter of complete indifference to him and that his only concern is profit. The program goes ahead, and the damage occurs.
4. The same chairman approves a program he has been told will increase profits but also improve the environment. Asked about the improvement, he says the environment is a matter of complete indifference to him and that his only concern is profit. The program goes ahead, and the improvement occurs.

Experimental work I have read suggests a persistent asymmetry: people are markedly more willing to say that in case 3 the chairman damaged the environment intentionally than they are to say that in case 4 he improved it intentionally, even though his foresight, his indifference, and his causal role appear to be the same. A definition that cannot capture or explain that asymmetry seems to me insufficient.

5. A soldier throws a grenade into a bunker to save his platoon, knowing that a civilian may be sheltering there; the civilian is killed. I can imagine readers going either way on whether he killed the civilian intentionally.
6. A doctor gives a dying patient a large dose of morphine to relieve pain, knowing it will also depress the patient's breathing; the patient dies sooner. Again, I can imagine disagreement about whether the death was brought about intentionally.
7. An archer who has never hit a target fires at a distant bullseye, wanting to hit it, and by sheer luck strikes it exactly. I am unsure whether he hit the bullseye intentionally, or merely succeeded at what he meant to do.

I should add that I half-suspect the word is doing two jobs at once—one descriptive and one evaluative—and that cases 3 and 4 pull apart because of blame and credit rather than because of any feature of the action itself. If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which of the seven cases move between them. In any event, run your definition or definitions through all seven cases, and then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and how you would revise if that verdict had to be reversed.
