I am a novelist, currently plotting a book in which a community must decide how to allocate flood defences without knowing the probability of various flood levels. While trying to make the characters argue well rather than simply represent caricatures, I realised I have a cluster of commitments in the philosophy of decision-making, and I should like an audit of whether they hang together before I build the novel around them. I have been reading Frank Knight's *Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit*; his contrast between risk, where objective probabilities can be assigned, and uncertainty, where they cannot, has become load-bearing for me.

Here are the commitments I find myself holding:

(a) Under genuine Knightian uncertainty, maximin is the correct rule for public policy: choose the option whose worst-case outcome is least bad.

(b) Because the original position is a choice under deep uncertainty, Rawls is correct to use maximin there, and Harsanyi's expected-utility argument must fail, since it relies on probability assignments and interpersonal utility comparisons that would not be available behind the veil.

(c) In my own personal decisions under uncertainty, minimax regret is more rational than maximin, because maximin seems to overcompensate by planning everything around the absolute worst case, whereas minimax regret respects how a choice would feel after the fact.

(d) A decision rule should be invariant under re-description: splitting an option into sub-cases, adding an irrelevant option, re-labelling the options, or re-framing the same structural choice as individual rather than collective should not by itself change the verdict.

(e) For boundedly rational agents like us, satisficing is generally more rational than optimising, because search and deliberation are costly, and choosing the first option that meets a reasonable aspiration threshold is often the correct procedure.

(f) The choice of decision rule should not simply track whether the decision-maker is an individual or a collective. If the underlying choice structure is the same, then the same rule should apply in both cases unless there is a principled institutional reason for divergence.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in decision theory and distributive justice.
