I've spent a lot of time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and there is a parallel I keep noticing that I can't settle on my own.

The no-free-lunch theorems in optimization and the problem of induction seem to me to be making the same underlying point from different directions. As I understand them, the NFL results say that averaged over all possible objective functions, no optimization or learning algorithm outperforms any other: every gain an algorithm makes on one class of problems is exactly paid for by a matching loss on another. A learner can beat chance only if it is already shaped to the structure of the particular problems it will actually face. The problem of induction is the point that no amount of observed regularity can force the unobserved to resemble the observed; any inference from past cases to future ones must presuppose some regularity or uniformity, and that presupposition cannot itself be justified from the data without circularity.

The parallel I keep seeing is that both locate the entire burden of success in an assumption the data cannot purchase. In the optimization case, the assumption is the algorithm's inductive bias or prior, which helps only if it happens to align with the distribution of problems the world actually throws at it. In the induction case, the assumption is that nature is uniform, or at least regular enough that observed patterns will continue. Both seem to say: there is no leverage without a bet on the structure of the world, and the bet is not earned by the evidence. And in rationalist discussion both get used in the same rhetorical way—the NFL results against the dream of a universal optimizer, the problem of induction against the dream of a purely empirical, assumption-free method.

But I suspect the analogy may be doing more work than it can support. The NFL theorem is a formal statement about expected performance under a specific kind of averaging over all possible functions, and its force depends on how natural that averaging is. The problem of induction is not a claim about average-case performance at all, but about whether a particular inference can be justified at all. These are not obviously the same. A machine-learning practitioner could reply to NFL by saying we do not care about the uniform average over all functions, because the real world occupies a tiny, structured corner of function space and our priors track that structure; a skeptic could reply that the belief that the world occupies that corner is exactly the belief the problem of induction says cannot be earned from the data. I cannot tell whether that exchange makes the analogy deeper or marks where it breaks.
