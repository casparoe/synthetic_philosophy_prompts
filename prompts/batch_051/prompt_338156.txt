I have been trying to sort out my actual views about small risks, because I suspect I have never really faced the question whether my everyday driving is worth it. I drive a few miles to work and run errands without a second thought, and I will push a little over the speed limit on an empty highway to save ten minutes, but I would never ride a motorcycle even if it halved my commute, and I will not let my child cycle to school along a busy road — and when I stop to think, I cannot point to a difference in the size or likelihood of the harm that would justify how differently those three feel. I will happily take a weekend flight across the country for a friend's party, and I eat runny eggs and raw-milk cheese without demanding a risk calculation. Yet I declined the side-airbag package on our car, which would have removed a small but real annual fatality risk for nine hundred dollars, even though I suspect the risk I declined to buy down was at least as large as several risks I routinely ignore because they are familiar or come wrapped as food. I also find that my willingness to accept a risk of death for money does not scale: I would take a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of dying for ten thousand dollars without much unease, but I would refuse a two-in-a-hundred chance for two hundred thousand dollars, and I cannot tell whether that is a rational response to the difference between a negligible and a substantial risk, or something less defensible.

My working theory is that I treat a risk as acceptable when it is familiar, voluntary, spread across many small exposures, and not vividly imaginable, while I refuse risks that are unfamiliar, one-off, or borne by people close to me; but that theory predicts I should regard my own ordinary driving as suspect, and in fact I do not. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures most of these verdicts. In particular, does a micromort-style framework — converting small fatality risks into expected costs and weighing them against the time, money, and pleasure gained — recover my pattern, or is the real work being done by something like dread, familiarity, control, the difference between risks I choose for myself and risks I choose for others, or the difference between a recurring background risk and a single vivid gamble? State the principle precisely enough to apply it to a new case, apply it to the case I keep returning to — whether my ordinary daily driving is worth it on the terms I actually seem to endorse — and apply it to a case I have not listed: taking a light aircraft to a remote island on holiday, which I would probably refuse even though the numbers may be no worse than flights I take without thinking. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts has to go if no coherent principle can hold them all, and whether I should revise my driving or revise my theory.
