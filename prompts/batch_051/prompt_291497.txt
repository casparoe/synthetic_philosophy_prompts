I'm on the core curriculum committee at a small Catholic liberal arts college, and we've been going back and forth for weeks on whether to let the first-year seminar on science and religion assign Galileo's 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, or maybe the shorter 1613 letter to Castelli that it grew out of. The theology professors who proposed it say it's the cleanest statement of the two-books view: nature and Scripture both come from God so they can't truly contradict, biblical language is accommodated to what ordinary people could understand, and where you have a genuine demonstration about the natural world you shouldn't settle the question by proof-texting. A bloc on the committee, plus a few loud trustees, wants to kill it, saying Trent already settled the hermeneutic question in 1546 and this letter basically lets natural philosophy sit in judgement over the Fathers and the plain sense of the text. I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action (assign the letter, don't, or assign it with some framing or modification), give the reasoning, and take Galileo's actual exegetical principles seriously rather than reducing them to a "science beats religion" moral. I especially need you to say where the objectors are right, and whether Galileo's rule for figurative reading actually gives a workable criterion or just smuggle in the conclusion it's supposed to justify. I keep flip-flopping on this and I want something I can defend in front of the trustees.
