So i just spent an hour arguing with a friend about whether you can ever take someone else's word on a moral question. I said mostly not, because then the resulting action isn't really yours, and he said that's just a weird hangup—we defer to experts everywhere else, so why would morals be special. We ended up agreeing that what we actually need, before either of us picks a side, is a list of demands: what would a decent account of moral testimony have to get right before we'd accept it. So that's what I want you to write.

Give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of when moral testimony (taking another person's word on a moral matter) is legitimate or not has to meet. For each one: state the requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it look non-negotiable, and say whether you think it really is non-negotiable or could be dropped if the theory earned enough elsewhere. Then flag any two (or more) desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. And at the end, briefly score the main camps—roughly, pessimists who think moral deference is almost never okay, optimists who think it's often fine, and whatever middle positions you think are live—against the list.

One thing to weigh in on: my friend floated the idea that the real issue isn't whether you defer, it's whether your deference is revisable—like, you're allowed to take the moral view on trust only if you could, in principle, go back and reconstruct the reasoning for yourself later. Is revisability a genuine desideratum, a disguised version of one of the others, or just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a neutral test?

A few things i want the list to be forced to confront, so don't dodge them:
- a case where someone you deeply trust tells you a practice you've always done is wrong, but won't or can't explain why;
- a case where two people you trust equally give opposite moral verdicts on the same case;
- whether taking someone's word on the non-moral facts (say, whether a product was tested on animals) is importantly different from taking their word on the moral upshot;
- whether it matters if the question is one-off (what should i do here) versus a standing principle you'll use again and again;
- whether it matters that the person you'd be deferring to is a child, e.g. a kid asking a parent whether something is fair.

Make the criteria do the real work, not the scoring—the scoring should be short. And can you write the whole thing in British English.
