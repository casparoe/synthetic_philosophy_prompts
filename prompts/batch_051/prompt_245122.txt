I've spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums that I've picked up the habit of trying to reverse-engineer the actual decision rule behind someone's case-by-case judgments, rather than taking their stated principle at face value. I have a colleague, a comparative psychologist who reviews animal-cognition manuscripts, who invokes Morgan's canon constantly. I take the canon to mean, roughly, that we should not interpret an animal's behavior as the product of a higher psychological process when a lower one would account for it. I'd rather not ask her directly, because when I do she just recites the canon; I want the gears-level version of the rule she is actually running.

Here are the rulings I have collected:

1. A rat presses a lever for sucrose. After the sucrose is paired with illness, the rat presses far less, even though pressing still delivers the now-aversive outcome. She ruled: attribute goal-directed action, because a pure habit account is inadequate where a habit would persist through devaluation.

2. A pigeon pecks a keylight on an interval schedule; devaluing the food does not reduce pecking. She ruled: attribute a stimulus–response habit, not goal-directed action.

3. A scrub jay that cached food while a competitor watched later re-caches in new locations when alone; jays that cached in private do not. She ruled: do not attribute an understanding of what the competitor can see or know; attribute a lower-level rule tracking whether it was observed while caching.

4. An octopus watches a conspecific open a jar to get food, then opens a novel jar of a different design on its first attempt. She ruled: attribute observational learning, because stimulus enhancement plus individual trial-and-error is inadequate where there was no trial and error.

5. A crow that has never encountered wire fails to retrieve a bucket from a tube with a straight wire, then spontaneously bends the wire into a hook and retrieves the bucket, and later bends novel wires of different gauges. She ruled: attribute some causal or means–end understanding, because accidental bending plus reinforcement cannot explain the one-trial spontaneous bend.

6. A bumblebee learns to pull a string to get sucrose, and untrained bees acquire the skill after watching a trained bee; the behavior spreads through the colony. She ruled: withhold any attribution of culture or imitation, because associative learning plus social facilitation is adequate.

Next week she must rule on this case: a Goffin's cockatoo, in its first session with a five-step lockbox (remove a pin, slide a bolt, turn a wheel, lift a latch, open a lid), solves it in a single continuous bout after a long period of inspecting the box, with no prior training on the individual steps. The same bird then immediately solves a rearranged box in which the steps must be performed in a different order.

I want you to tell me: which candidate rules fit all six earlier rulings (I suspect there are at least two, and that they are different versions of Morgan's canon); what each candidate rule predicts for the cockatoo case; which verdict you would bet on if you had to; and what further case or piece of evidence would most cleanly discriminate between the remaining rules. I care more about the structure of the rules and where they diverge than about which label she would use.
