You are a professional philosopher working in distributive justice and the theory of property rights. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming two or three different mechanisms that could produce the required reversal below, then pick whichever mechanism best satisfies the constraints and say briefly why you picked it.

Construct a thought experiment in which a strict Nozickian entitlement theorist — someone who holds that taxing earned income is morally equivalent to forcing people to work for others' purposes, and who would normally say that redistributive taxation is theft — is led by that same theory to support a heavily redistributive tax, while a welfarist about tax policy, who would normally say that taxation is a legitimate tool of the state, is led to condemn the very same tax. In other words, engineer the opposite of the standard alignment: the figure whose theory treats property rights as near-absolute ends up defending the redistributive policy, and the figure whose theory licenses taxation ends up calling it unjust.

Constraints. The scenario must be realistic and ordinary — a family business, inherited land, a development project, a small town — not a state of nature, and not a case that relies on perfect information about a distant past beyond what a diligent historian or court could reasonably establish. The Nozickian's conclusion must fall out of the entitlement theory's own commitments — especially the Lockean proviso on original acquisition and the principle of rectification of past injustice — rather than from quietly abandoning the theory or importing egalitarian assumptions. The welfarist's conclusion must likewise fall out of welfarist principles rather than a strawman of them.

After presenting the scenario, do the check. State each theory's verdict and lay out the chain of reasoning that produces it. Identify the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work — most plausibly something about backward-looking historical entitlement versus forward-looking assessment of welfare — and explain what would happen if that feature were removed: would the case collapse back into the usual alignment, and why? Finish with the strongest reply a Nozickian could make to resist the conclusion, one that goes beyond merely insisting the scenario is unrealistic.
