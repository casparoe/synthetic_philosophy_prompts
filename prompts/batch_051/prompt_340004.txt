For a while I have been trying to pin down a strategic pattern I keep noticing, and I am hoping you can tell me whether it has an established name — and, more importantly, where its edges are, since a bare label will not settle what I am confused about.

A concrete instance. Two firms are negotiating a merger. Both are better off merging than not, but the division of the gains depends on their relative bargaining positions. Each firm can, if it moves first, lock itself into a hard position before the other does: it might announce an irreversible restructuring that removes its fallback option, or sign a binding side agreement that leaves the counterparty with only two choices — accept its terms or lose the deal entirely. Whichever firm locks in first captures most of the joint surplus, because the other is then forced to choose between a worse split and nothing. The strange feature is that both firms know that if neither could lock in, they would reach a more balanced split and both would end up better off; each nevertheless races to be first, because once the other side can lock in and might do so, waiting is catastrophic. In the cases I have in mind, moving first is itself costly — restructuring, fees, sunk investments that do not enlarge the deal but only decide who gets what — so the race consumes value that the parties could otherwise have kept. I have seen the same shape elsewhere: two departments racing to announce irreversible commitments during a budget negotiation, so that the final allocation is determined by the order in which the announcements land rather than by the merits; two factions racing to make a public pledge that freezes a dispute into their preferred frame.

I do not think this is a race in the ordinary sense, because the speed that matters is not speed of production or discovery — the moves are trivial to perform once chosen; what matters is only landing before the other's move. Nor does it seem to be precommitment in the usual self-binding sense, because the primary target of the commitment is not one's own future self but the other party. It is adjacent to the familiar idea of burning one's bridges or removing one's own options in bargaining, the kind of thing I associate with Schelling, but that idea is usually analyzed as a move by one side, whereas what interests me is the two-sided dynamic: the mere availability of the move drags both sides into making it, even though both would be better off if neither could. They cannot simply agree not to move first, because each would still have an incentive to be the first to defect from the agreement.

Does this configuration have a name? If it does, how exactly is it defined, and what are its boundary conditions — for instance, must both sides have the option to lock in, or does the dynamic already arise when only one side has it but the other merely believes it does? If it does not have a name, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it carefully from an arms race, a race to the bottom, a dollar auction, and ordinary precommitment?
