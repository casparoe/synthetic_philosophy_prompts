I am a novelist planning a work of speculative fiction built around the question of whether a social insect colony can have a single mind. The touchstone for me is the thought experiment Douglas Hofstadter stages in the "Ant Fugue" dialogue of Gödel, Escher, Bach: the ant colony Aunt Hillary, treated as a candidate self — a consciousness whose parts are not conscious. I want a short story that makes a reader feel the force of that possibility rather than understand it as a philosophical thesis.

Write a 2,000–3,000-word piece of literary fiction in close third person with no human protagonist; a human observer is permitted only if she stays outside the story's epistemic center. The narration should move between the scale of individual ants — local cues, pheromone trails, brood care, alarm signals — and the scale of the colony itself, so that colony-level events such as a migration, an epidemic, a war with a neighboring colony, or the founding of a new nest read as decisions, moods, panics, or preferences of one spread-out being. Then, at least once, let the deflationary view have its full say: the thought that all of this is just many small automatons running local rules, and that "mind" is a story we impose on the whole. The story must not settle which is right. Where the two readings conflict — when an ant seems to choose, when the colony seems to suffer — the prose should keep both interpretations equally available, so that the reader feels the genuine vertigo of the question.

Do not let the story become allegory or lecture; let the structure of the narration do the philosophical work. After the story, add a single final line in the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence statement of the philosophical crux the story is built around.
