Here's a puzzle I keep tripping over in my ethics seminar. I want a working definition of gratitude — the normatively loaded attitude, not just the warm feeling — that gets the following cases right. (1) A friend fronted you rent money in a crisis and you now feel you owe them something real. (2) A colleague goes well out of their way to help you, but only because they're quietly angling for your support in a department vote. (3) An anonymous donor paid for surgery you couldn't afford, and you'll never be able to find or thank them. (4) You learn, years later, that your parents turned down a much better job in another city so you could stay in your school district, before you were old enough to notice. (5) You walk away unharmed from a natural disaster and find yourself grateful to the universe, though there is clearly no one to thank. (6) A mentor's brutal criticism, which stung at the time, turns out to be the reason you finished your dissertation. I'm confident that (1) and (3) are gratitude and that (2) is not, and I'm genuinly unsure about (4), (5), and (6). I especially want to know whether your definition requires a benefactor who intended to benefit you, and how it handles gratitude toward luck, God, the dead, or the past. Propose the definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it gives an answer you'd want to resist or revise.
