I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of language seminar—18 students, 80-minute sessions—and we've just hit Grice. Every year the Cooperative Principle and the maxims strike them as a list of obvious etiquette tips, and my lecture about the difference between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by uttering it doesn't land as anything more than a taxonomy. I want to replace my opening lecture next week with an in-class game that makes them do the work themselves. Design a runnable activity for the full 80 minutes in which each round a speaker is given a target content that they cannot state directly (something socially delicate or face-threatening, so literal assertion would be dispreferred) and must get a hearer to recover it by openly flouting one of the four maxims, not covertly violating it. There should be a referee or a small judging panel, a scoring system that rewards hearers for recovering the intended implicature and punishes speakers for accidental entailments or presuppositions that smuggle the content in literally, and then a cancellation phase where the speaker adds a follow-up that cancels the implicature (or discovers they can't without contradicting themselves). I want the notions that implicature is calculable from literal content plus the assumption of cooperation, and that it is cancellable, to emerge from how the rounds actually play out, not from any upfront framing.

Afterward, write the debrief I'd run: it should map concrete moments from the game onto the Cooperative Principle, the maxims, flouting versus violating, and particularized versus generalized implicature, and it should honestly flag what will predictably go wrong—scoring disputes, speakers accidentally presupposing the forbidden content, hearers leaning on shared background that the speaker never actually implicated, or everyone just using tone of voice and calling it manner. Also tell me which feature of Grice's view the game cannot reproduce; my guess is that its the point that the derivation of an implicature is a rational reconstruction of what a hearer could work out, not a description of real-time processing, but make the argument yourself. No pre-lecture, and don't let the word 'Grice' come up until the debrief.
