I'm in a formal epistemology course this term, and we've just finished two weeks on Aumann's agreement theorem. In the textbook version, the result says roughly this: if two agents have the same prior probability distribution over a state space, each updates by conditionalizing on the information supplied by their own partition of that space, and their posterior probabilities for some target proposition become common knowledge between them, then those posterior probabilities cannot differ. The slogan form is that rational Bayesians with a common prior cannot agree to disagree. My professor presented this as a deep constraint on rational disagreement, not just an idle formalism.

What's bothering me is that the world the theorem seems aimed at looks less and less like the world I'm in. Aumann's model is expressly comfortable with the two agents having different information partitions; that's not the problem. What it rules out is different priors. But different priors now feel like the design rather than the glitch: two assistant models fine-tuned on different corpora start with systematically different expectations long before today's evidence arrives, and a personalized recommender system doesn't merely partition a shared space of possibilities differently; it effectively installs a different sense of which possibilities are live and how likely they are. I'm tempted to conclude the common-prior assumption is carrying all the weight and the theorem survives only as the near-tautology that agents who start from the same prior and update flawlessly cannot end up with common-knowledge disagreement — but I don't trust that conclusion because the result keeps getting invoked as if it told us something about real, stubborn disagreement.
