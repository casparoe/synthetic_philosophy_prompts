I'm writing a novel whose protagonist, after a stint reading pop philosophy, decides to run her entire life on a strict hedonism: the only thing that makes a day go well is pleasure, and the only thing that makes it go badly is pain. She keeps a daily ledger where she has to decide which of her experiences counted as pleasures, and how much each counted, and she lets the ledger make both small and large decisions for her. The stakes in the book are that she treats the ledger as authorative — if a day shows no pleasures, she takes that as proof the day was worthless and starts cutting similar days out of her life. I want her to be a smart true believer, not a parody, so I need a real decision procedure she could follow rather than just "whatever feels good."

The problem is her experiences don't look like each other at all: finishing a difficult novel, a hot shower, the relief when a lost bill turns up, succeeding out of spite to prove an ex wrong, a horror film she couldn't look away from, folding laundry, the feeling after a run she hated for the first mile. I gather philosophers disagree about whether these have anything in common besides the word "pleasure." So: write me the checklist or decision procedure she runs when an experience goes into the ledger. It should be something a smart layperson can actually execute in a few minutes, with the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and clear places where it stops with a no. It also needs to say how she should score or weight the experience once it counts, since she has to compare a shower against finishing the novel somehow.

Make the procedure actually handle these cases, because these are the ones that'll come up in scenes: relief (is the ending of a pain itself a pleasure?), anticipation and memory (does looking forward to something count now?), pleasures she would disavow (the spite thing), and chosen experiences that feel bad while they're happening (the horror film, the run). Also say what the procedure should do when her own testimony conflicts with it — when she says she enjoyed something the checklist wants to reject, or the other way around.

Then run the procedure on three of her days: (1) an afternoon where she forces herself to finish the novel and is proud but also hollow, (2) an evening of small vengeful wins that leave her too wired to sleep, and (3) a Sunday where she does nothing at all and feels calm but empty. At least one of these should come out in a way she would not like, and say so.

Last thing: I know this is contested ground. Tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire, which step would change if the pleasures-are-a-distinctive-felt-quality view is right rather than the pleasure-is-liking-or-enjoying view, and what argument or evidence would make you rebuild the procedure in a different direction altogether. I'd rather know where the ground under her is sand than have a tidy system that's quietly wrong.
