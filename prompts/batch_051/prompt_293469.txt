Two of my housemates got into a long argument after we watched a documentary on weather forecasting, and I still can't referee it. Liam (physics) said determinism means the present state fixes all later states, so if weather is deterministic it is in principle predictable; chaos is just an epistemic nuisance about our not knowing the initial conditions to infinite precision. Karin (philosophy of science) replied that the phrase 'infinite precision' is doing all the work and names something no actual predictor could have, so 'predictable in principle' isn't a kind of predictability at all — it's a story about an impossible observer. For any finite predictor, sensitive dependence puts a hard horizon on forecasts, and beyond that horizon the system is unpredictable in the only sense that could matter to us.

Liam came back that her standard makes the word 'predictable' useless: even a boring pendulum has growing error if you only know its speed to ten decimal places, so by her lights nothing is predictable. The 'in principle' was meant to distinguish systems where the uncertainty is in us from genuinely stochastic systems. Karin said that distinction is exactly what she doubts, because from the inside — the only place a predictor can stand — the two situations look the same: you have a finite data series, you estimate the state, you run the model, and the forecast blows up. She also said there's no clean line between measurement error and model error in real cases, so 'the same state plus a tiny error' is already an idealisation. Liam answered that model error is irrelevant: chaos is a fact about how the true state evolves under the true dynamics, not about bad models of it. Karin replied that for systems we actually care about you never have the true state or the true dynamics, so the only meaningful question is whether our whole modelling package keeps working, and in a chaotic regime it stops working sooner than the 'in principle' story admits.

They asked me who was right, and I genuinely couldn't separate the threads. Could you go through this move by move and say who is right about what, where each is overclaiming, and what distinction would have kept the argument from looping? I'd especially like to know whether 'predictable in principle' is defensible or just a muddle, and whether any of this bears on determinism itself. Please write the answer in British English.
