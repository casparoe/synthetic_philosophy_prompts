Rawls's argument for the difference principle goes something like this: behind the veil of ignorance you don't know your place in society, your natural endowments, or your conception of the good, so you'd hedge and pick principles that let inequalities stand only when they make the worst-off as well off as possible. A lot of the intuitive work is done by the idea that the distribution of natural talent is just a natural lottery—it isn't chosen by anyone and isn't produced by the basic structure, so the fair move is to let the talented benefit only if the least advantaged share in the gains.

Now suppose a cheap, safe germline enhancement technology becomes available. Rich families buy it for their kids; a couple generations later, most of what looks like natural talent is actually downstream of parental income. Its not a natural lottery anymore, or at least its a lottery whose tickets are sold by the basic structure. Does Rawls's argument for the difference principle survive this change? Give me the bottom line first—survives, needs repair, or dead—then say which premise the technology actually undercuts, whether that premise was load-bearing for the argument or just doing rhetorical work, and whether the principle can be patched up (for instance by treating access to enhancement as a primary good, or by treating the talent market as part of the basic structure) instead of collapsing into something much more egalitarian.
