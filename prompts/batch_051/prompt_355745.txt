So my wife and I are nonreligious, and we're raising our two kids (6 and 9) without any religion. My parents are very devout — not in a scary way, they're warm and generous, but faith is the center of their world and they genuinely worry about the kids' souls. We've settled on a policy for family visits: the kids know we dont believe, but they're told that at Grandma and Grandpa's house we don't argue about God, and if they're asked directly (like, 'Do you believe in Jesus?') they should say something like 'I'm not sure yet' or 'we don't talk about that much at our house.' We never make them say prayers they don't want to, but they sit quietly through grace and say amen with everyone. They know it's a bit of a performance, and their cousins probably assume they're just normal Christian kids.

I'm genuinely torn about this. At home we tell the kids honesty is a core value: that they should always tell us the truth and that we'll always tell them the truth. But this policy seems to train them to be evasive, or maybe to outright lie, with the people they love most outside our house. On the other hand, we're protecting a relationship with grandparents and a big extended family, and maybe teaching them that you can love people without making every difference into a confrontation.

So my question is: what theory of honesty, or of belonging, does this practice actually presuppose? Does it only make sense if we're already committed to something like 'religious belief is a private matter' or 'family closeness can survive on shared presence and ritual rather than shared belief'? Or is the practice compatible with several different theories, and we havent actually committed to one by doing it? Is there an inconsistency in what we're doing, and if we replaced the presupposition with a stricter one — say, that honesty requires full disclosure even when it costs you — what exactly would we have to change about how we handle these visits?
