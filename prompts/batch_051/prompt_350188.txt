Last night a friend and I got into a long arguement about a logo redesign. The new logo's 'a' was so stylized that half the people in the room read it as an 'o' with a tail, and my friend insisted it wasn't really an 'a' anymore. I said that can't be right, because 'a' looks totally different in Times New Roman (the double-story form) than in handwriting or Comic Sans (the circle-with-a-tail form), and we still call both 'a'. He said those are just different acceptable ways of writing the same letter, whereas the logo crossed some line. We never managed to say what the line was.

So I'm after a working definition of what makes a written mark an 'a' — the letter itself, not the sound or the word 'a' — that correctly handles these cases: the Times New Roman double-story 'a'; the single-story 'a' in Comic Sans and most handwriting; an uppercase 'A'; an italic 'a'; a child's mirror-image 'a'; the Cyrillic letter 'а', which is pixel-for-pixel identical to a Latin 'a' but belongs to a different alphabet; the Greek alpha 'α'; a blackletter 'a' that modern readers often misread; a scribbled note where the 'a' is indistinguishable from a 'u' but the writer meant it as an 'a'; and a logo mark that the company calls an 'a' but almost nobody reads as one. Run your definition against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then tell me which of your verdicts is the least secure. Please answer in American English.
