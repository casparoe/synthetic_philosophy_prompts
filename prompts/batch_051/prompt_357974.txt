For the past few years I have been broadly convinced by the rationalist case for geometric rationality, by which I mean the Kelly/log-wealth view that a gamble should be evaluated by the expected logarithm of wealth rather than by its expected monetary value. The argument has exactly the systematic virtues I usually find decisive: if one repeatedly stakes a fixed fraction of wealth on a favorable bet, expected-value maximization pushes that fraction toward one and therefore almost surely drives wealth to zero, because ruin is absorbing and what survives is what compounds. The quantity that actually governs a realized path is the geometric growth rate, and maximizing that means maximizing expected log wealth. From this the view generalizes into a claim about rationality itself: expected-value maximization is not a neutral gold standard but a mistake that optimizes an arithmetic mean across parallel worlds, dominated by rare jackpot paths one will almost surely not occupy, and much of what is called risk aversion is really this multiplicative correction in disguise. If my preferences over money look linear, the theory says, that is only because I am inspecting them locally, where log wealth and wealth approximately coincide.

And yet I have a firm intuition that will not go away, and it is about one-shot bets. Suppose a friend offers me exactly one flip of a fair coin — heads, I win $110; tails, I lose $100 — and suppose I can comfortably absorb the loss, with no repeat, no relevant compounding, and no later gamble depending on how this one lands. Here there is no long run over which a time average and an ensemble average can diverge; there is simply one draw. The intuition says: take the bet, and more generally let expected monetary value be the criterion for a single non-ruinous gamble. Declining a positive-EV bet leaves money on the table, and letting the stake's size relative to my bankroll dictate whether I accept feels like importing a concave utility function under the cover of a theorem. If my utility genuinely is linear over the relevant range of wealth, what could be irrational about maximizing expected value when the bet happens only once?

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for example, one in which Kelly betting is just expected utility maximization with logarithmic utility of wealth, a permissible but not mandatory utility function, and geometric rationality is a theorem about long-run growth rather than a rival to expected utility theory. Does the one-shot case show that the general theory overclaims, or does the theory's debunking story about expected value apply even where there is no repetition? Under what conditions would it be rational to abandon the intuition, and under what conditions would the theory have to retreat to repeated, ergodic settings?
