After a long argument with a friend about whether the Bell-test results are what actually killed local hidden-variable theories, I was left wanting a worked-out answer to the following counterfactual. Suppose that from the first serious experimental tests of Bell's inequalities in the 1970s onward, every attempt—including the later generations of tests designed to close the known loopholes—had found results consistent with the local-realist bound and no statistically significant violation. The quantum formalism remains exactly as it is in our world, so it continues to predict violations for those same entangled states: experiment and theory now contradict one another. Give me a reasoned prediction of how physics and the philosophy of quantum mechanics would have developed in that world. I am less interested in whether things would have gone one way or the other than in the chain of reasoning: what happens to the major interpretations of quantum mechanics; how the EPR argument and the notion of nonlocality would be remembered and taught; which results tied to Bell's theorem remain significant if the theorem is sound but nature obeys local realism; and how physicists would most plausibly respond to a mathematically intact theory that makes wrong predictions. Be explicit about which links in the prediction are robust and which are guesses, and say what would change your mind—which consideration, if it turned out to be false, would most undermine your overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
