I keep noticing a slide in arguments about what's possible, and I don't have a word for it. The clearest example: someone says "I can conceive of a world where the physical facts are all the same but the colors we see are swapped—where what I call red looks to me the way green looks to you." They use this to argue that color experience isn't fixed by the physical facts. When you object that merely imagining it doesn't show it's really possible, they take a step back: "I'm only saying it's conceivable, which is a fact about my mind, not about the world." Fine. But a few paragraphs later they're drawing conclusions as if the imagined world were a genuine possibility—maybe using it to refute physicalism or to say which truths are necessary. The concession only lasts until it would cost them something. It's not exactly motte-and-bailey, because the two readings are both respectable and the speaker isn't hiding in either one; it's more that the word "possible" gets asked to do two different jobs, and the speaker switches jobs on a schedule that suspiciously favors their argument. Is there a name for this? The closest I know is "equivocation," but that makes it sound like a simple mistake, and the people doing it often know the distinction perfectly well. If there's no established term, what would you call it, and how would you separate the innocent version (using conceivable scenarios as heuristics or probes) from the bad version (using the same scenario as a premise about the world)? I'm after the carving, not just a label, so boundary cases matter.
