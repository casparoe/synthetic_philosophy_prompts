I am a retired experimentalist — most of my working life was spent in a laboratory where a measurement either settled a question or it did not — and I have recently begun reading seriously in the philosophy of artificial intelligence. After working through McCarthy and Hayes’s 1969 paper Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence, I find the frame problem more slippery than most philosophical discussions admit. As I understand their diagnosis, they distinguish three grades of adequacy for a representation of change — metaphysical, epistemological, and heuristic adequacy — the first requiring that the representation track how the world actually behaves, the second requiring that an agent could acquire it from experience, and the third requiring that it support efficient inference about what to do next. The frame problem arises because a formal account of action appears to require, for every action and every property, a statement of whether that property persists or changes across the action, and the resulting statements multiply without ever illuminating the underlying regularities. I am not yet interested in surveying the standard responses. First I want the specification of what an adequate solution to the frame problem must deliver. Write the desiderata: the cases it must get right, including the ones that sound trivial but are not; the distinctions it must respect, such as between change and persistence or between what a system knows and what it can actually use in time; the results from logic or computer science it cannot contradict; and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then identify which single requirement does the most to exclude the candidate approaches currently on offer.
