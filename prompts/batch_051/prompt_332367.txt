During a recent graduate seminar on practical reason, I was struck by how little the internalism/externalism debate has moved in the decades since Bernard Williams put the question sharply. The internalist, roughly, says that a consideration can be a reason for an agent only if it is connected to something in that agent's existing motivational set, at least by a sound deliberative route from where the agent actually is. The externalist says there can be reasons that apply to an agent regardless of whether the agent could be moved by them from their actual starting points. Williams's Owen Wingrave case is treated by both sides as a kind of fixed point: the young man who has no motivation to join the army, despite his family's insistence, is said by the internalist to have no reason to join, while the externalist insists he has a reason nonetheless. The case has been picked over endlessly, and the standard moves—deliberative routes, motivations under improved conditions, the normativity of reason-ascriptions—are all on the table. Yet the debate seems not to be converging, and I suspect the participants are not disagreeing about a single proposition. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. Is it a verbal dispute about the word reason itself? Is it a disagreement about which cases count as data? Is it two different projects—one semantic, one first-order normative—being run under one heading? Or is there a hidden empirical or psychological claim about what human beings can be brought to care about that neither side has isolated? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question, or something else? Please do not survey the literature; I want the disagreement itself diagnosed, with the Owen Wingrave case used as a concrete test of your account. Keep the answer to no more than 600 words.
