Ive been poking at the game Nomic lately (you know, the one where changing the rules is itself a legal move) and I've ended up with a distinction I need checked. I call them "closed" and "open" self-amendment. Closed self-amendment is when a system changes its rules through a procedure that stays fixed while the change happens, like a legislature voting under a constitution that isn't itself on the table. Open self-amendment is when the procedure for changing rules is itself up for revision, like Nomic, where the rules that govern changing the rules can be changed using those same rules.

My claim is that open self-amendment can't actually go all the way down. At any given moment there's always some rule about how to change rules that's being treated as fixed—not because the rules say it's fixed, but because the people using the system have to stand somewhere. I've been calling that the "procedural floor." An example: suppose the rule is "any rule, including this one, can be amended by a two-thirds vote." The first time you amend that rule, you're using two-thirds as the floor. If the amendment lowers the threshold to a simple majority, you didn't use the new threshold to change itself; you used the old floor one last time and then stepped onto a new one. So even the most reflexive-looking amendment is really a sequence of closed steps with a floor that shifts between them.

What I want to know is wether this distinction actually does any work. Is it already a known thing (just tell me the gist, no citations)? Is my "floor" actually a floor, or is that the spot where the theory quietly assumes the thing it's trying to explain? And where does it break: give me the strongest kind of case I'm missing—maybe a system that really does amend itself all the way down, or a reason the paradoxes of self-amendment are logic problems after all, not floor problems.
