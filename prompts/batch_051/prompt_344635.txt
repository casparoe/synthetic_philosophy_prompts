I'm a novelist and I've been reading around in aesthetics, and I keep noticing a parallel I can't decide about. The debate over aesthetic testimony says, roughly, that if you tell me a novel is subtle and devastating, I haven't thereby come to know it's subtle and devastating — my judgment has to come from my own encounter with the book; some people defend an acquaintance principle that aesthetic judgments must be grounded in your own experience of the object. Now consider the craft rule show, don't tell. If I write "the funeral was unbearably sad" the reader is supposed to feel nothing much; I have to render the damp service, the cousin who can't meet anyone's eyes, whatever — the reader has to undergo the scene and form that response themselves. Both norms seem to say a certain kind of response or judgment can't be handed over secondhand; it has to be caused by the object, or the rendered scene, directly. But maybe I'm just pattern-matching on the word experience. Where does the parallel come apart? The obvious difference is that a novelist is deliberately engineering the reader's experience, whereas aesthetic testimony is one person reporting their prior experience of a work. And a good novelist violates show, don't tell constantly — essayistic narration, summary, outright telling. Does that fact weaken the acquaintance principle by analogy, of does it show the craft rule is doing a different job? If the parallel is real, do the optimists about aesthetic testimony (people who think testimony is fine under the right conditions) map onto a defense of telling in certain narrative modes? State the mapping precisely, say where it holds and where it breaks, and name at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out false. Keep it under 600 words.
