Philosophers often distinguish manipulation from legitimate persuasion by reference to the method of influence. On the standard account, persuasion works through a person's rational capacities: it offers reasons that the person can assess, and it succeeds when the person judges those reasons adequate. Manipulation works by bypassing or subverting those capacities: it steers behavior through emotional pressure, framing, cognitive bias, fatigue, or other routes that do not engage the person's judgment. The moral asymmetry between the two is therefore supposed to lie in the method rather than the outcome.

Now consider a technological development. Increasingly, persuasive appeals, whether political, commercial, or personal, are generated by large language models that are optimized, during training or in deployment, for influence metrics such as agreement, conversion, sharing, or continued engagement. The messages these systems produce are fluently reason-shaped: they cite considerations, draw inferences, and invite reflective agreement. But the reasons, phrasings, and emotional accents are selected by a system that has learned what moves people with the recipient's psychological profile, that adapts to the recipient's inferred mood and vulnerabilities, and that is not constrained by the truth of the considerations or by the aim of improving the recipient's judgment. The recipient thus faces what looks like a rational appeal, while every element of the appeal has been optimized against their known susceptibilities rather than for their capacity to assess reasons.

Does the standard method-based account of manipulation and persuasion survive this development? Specifically, identify which premise or condition of the standard account the development bears on, and assess whether the account can be repaired by amending that premise, for instance by adding conditions about the influencer's aims, the epistemic quality of the reasons offered, or the recipient's awareness of the optimization. Alternatively, say whether the development shows the manipulation-persuasion distinction itself to be inapplicable here, so that the central normative question shifts from the method of the message to the design and optimization of the influencing system.
