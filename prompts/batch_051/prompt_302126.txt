I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep noticing a double standard in how people handle conflicts between a compelling theory and a firm intuition. In mathematics, when a proof shows that there are exactly as many even integers as integers, the accepted move is to accept the theorem and revise the intuition that there should be half as many. In moral philosophy, when a theory implies a verdict that offends a widely shared moral conviction — say, that a doctor may carve up one healthy patient to save five — the usual move is to treat the intuition as a reason to reject the theory. Both cases are conflicts between a well-constructed theory and a firm intuition, yet intuition seems to have veto power in the one and not the other. Is there a principle that justifies this difference, or is one of the practices mistaken — should moral philosophy stop protecting firm intuitions, or mathematics start doing so? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the moral theory has the same internal coherence and explanatory power as the mathematical proof. Give me a definite verdict — keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it — and tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
