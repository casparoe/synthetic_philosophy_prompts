In a course on medieval ethics, we have been working with a deliberately simplified version of Abelard's position on moral culpability, stated as a checklist rather than a historical thesis. On this checklist, a sin occurs exactly when: (1) the agent believes the contemplated act to be forbidden; (2) the agent consents to the act, where consent means the formed will that the act be done — not mere inclination, enjoyment of the fantasy, or failure to resist a stray impulse; and (3) consent, once formed, contains the whole sin, so performing the deed neither increases nor decreases the guilt. Call this the consent-only checklist.

Test it against two edge cases. First: a person has been taught from childhood that eating a particular fruit is a serious sin, and they fully believe this. One day they eat the fruit deliberately as an act of contempt for the law they take themselves to be violating, though no one is harmed and, let us stipulate for the sake of one of the sub-questions, the act is not in fact forbidden. Second: a person who has long resented an enemy one evening forms the considered will to send an anonymous, life-ruining letter the next morning; during the night they die of a stroke, without having sent the letter, without having harmed the enemy, and without having revoked the will. There is, in the checklist's sense, a completed consent but no deed and no further mental event.

Please work through each case criterion by criterion and say whether it is a sin under the consent-only checklist, identifying where the checklist yields a determinate verdict and where it falls silent. Then consider whether either case is a counterexample to the checklist. In doing so, evaluate at least these proposed amendments: adding a requirement that the act actually be wrong, and not merely believed to be wrong; adding a requirement that consent persist for some time, survive an opportunity for revocation, or otherwise reach the deed; and allowing the deed itself, or the harm it causes, to add an independent increment of guilt. Conclude by either defending the consent-only checklist as it stands or stating precisely how you would amend it and why the amended checklist handles the cases better.
