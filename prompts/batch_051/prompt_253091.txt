I spent my career in statistical physics, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophical literature on reduction and emergence. A structural resemblance keeps bothering me, and I would like to know whether it is deep or whether I am merely pattern-matching on the word "derivable."

In my old field, the canonical example of a successful reduction is the derivation of macroscopic thermodynamics—the ideal gas law, say—from the statistical mechanics of molecules. But the derivation only goes through cleanly in a limit: infinitely many particles, infinite volume, fixed density, or, for transport laws, infinitely long times. Real systems are finite, so the higher-level law is never literally derived from the microtheory; it is recovered as the leading term in an expansion, with corrections that vanish as the limit is approached. Physicists do not call hydrodynamics or thermodynamics "emergent" in any metaphysical sense. They call the reduction approximate and scale-dependent, and they go on to compute the corrections.

The philosophical debate seems to me to have the same shape. Reductionists want the higher-level theory to be derivable, in principle, from the lower-level theory plus bridge laws. Emergentists point out that the higher-level behavior is not so derivable—different micro-arrangements realize the same macrostate, and the macro-law holds only under idealizations that real systems never satisfy. Each side then draws a metaphysical conclusion: either the higher level is "nothing but" the lower, or it is something over and above it. But the physicist's third option—reduction as a controlled limit with corrections—looks like neither of those conclusions. It says the macro-level is not identical to the micro-level, but it is also not something over and above it; it is the asymptotic behavior of the micro-level under a specified limiting operation.

My question is whether this third option maps onto the philosophical debate or merely changes the subject. Is the physicist's notion of reduction-as-limit the same relation the philosophers are arguing about, or is it a different relation wearing the same name? If the parallel is deep, what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer? And to test it from the other direction: what does the parallel predict that seems false on the philosophical side—for instance, that "emergence" should come in degrees measured by the size of the finite-size corrections, and that strong emergence would correspond to the failure of the relevant limit to exist at all?
