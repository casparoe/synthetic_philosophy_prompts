I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I'm currently writing a dissertation chapter on promissory obligation. My supervisor refuses to state her view outright—her line is that cases come first and theory later—so I have been reconstructing her position from the verdicts she gives in conversation. I now have five cases on which I'm confident of her judgments. Duress: you promise to lend a colleague money only because he has threatened to reveal something damaging unless you do; she says no obligation arises. Play-acting: you say "I promise" as a line in an improv scene, and everyone present, including the person addressed, understands it as part of the performance; she says no promise was made. Release: you promise to return a borrowed book, and before the due date the owner tells you he releases you and no longer wants it back; she says the obligation ends. Forgotten promise: you promise to repay a loan to a colleague who later forgets both the loan and the promise, has made no plans around the money, and will not think about it again, though he adds that he would still be glad to be repaid; she says you remain bound. Death of the promisee: you promise to visit a sick friend in hospital on Sunday, and he dies on Friday; no one else is affected one way or the other by whether you go. She says the promise no longer binds.

The case I want to ask her about, but have not yet had the chance, is this. You promise your elderly aunt that you will spend Sunday afternoon with her. On Friday she has a severe stroke and is left in a persistent vegetative state from which, according to her physicians, she will not recover. The nursing home staff are meeting all her physical needs, and no one else—family included—has any stake in whether you appear. Would my supervisor say the promise still binds? I want more than a verdict: I want the structure behind it. Please lay out the candidate principles that fit her five recorded judgments, say what each principle predicts about the aunt case, identify which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog, and say whether the earlier record genuinely determines her verdict or underdetermines it. I have been circulating among a bare reliance view, an interest-based or assurance view, a directed-duty view, and a conventional-practice view. If the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, tell me honestly, and propose a further case—as clean and mundane as mine—that would best separate whatever principles remain in the running.
