I've been retired a few years and have been reading my way through philosophy of language, and I keep tripping over generics — bare-plural sentences like "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria." Forty years of field biology left me reaching for exactly these sentences, and I realize I still don't know wether they're true or just useful. So I'd like a working definition of generic truth: what makes a sentence of the form "Ks are F" true, or assertible, when it plainly isn't a universal. Test it against these, with my own off-the-cuff verdicts:

- "ducks lay eggs" — I'm sure this is true, even though only females lay and only in season.
- "mosquitoes carry malaria" — I'm also sure it's true, even though almost all mosquito species never do.
- "hyenas are scavengers" — I'm sure this is false, or at least misleading; spotted hyenas mostly kill their own food.
- "sharks attack people" — I want to call it false, but I'd have put it in a feild guide's "behavior" note myself.
- "E. coli causes food poisoning" — I want to call it true, but most strains are harmless gut flora.
- "bees make honey" — torn. Only a handful of the ~20,000 bee species make honey, but it sure feels like it characterizes bees more than "sharks attack people" characterizes sharks.
- "bats carry rabies" — torn. A tiny fraction of bats carry it, and public-health guidelines are fine with the sentence.
- "chickens can't fly" — torn between false (I've watched them clear a fence) and true (compared to birds generally).

Propose a definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it gives a verdict you'd want to override.
