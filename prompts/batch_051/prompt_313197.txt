A friend and I spent last evening arguing about whether a government can create a convention by decree, and we kept coming back to one test case: Sweden's 1967 switch from driving on the left to driving on the right. I want a careful Lewisian analysis of that episode, running in both directions between the theory and the case. Take the relevant features of a convention, roughly as I understand Lewis, to be a regularity in a recurrent situation that nearly everyone follows; a nearly universal preference to follow that regularity on the condition that enough others follow it; a workable alternative that nearly everyone would have preferred under the corresponding condition; and common knowledge of those features. Then work through the pre-1967 left-hand practice, the legal reversal itself, and the post-1967 right-hand practice, saying for each stage which of those features it had and which it lacked, with attention to people's expectations and common knowledge before, during, and after the change. Be strict about separating what the legal act itself could accomplish from what it had to borrow from expectations that did not yet exist, and then say what the episode shows about the theory: a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it was never built to handle. Conclude by resolving our argument: is my friend right that a decree can itself make a convention, am I right that it can at most put in place the conditions from which a convention may form, or does Sweden's case split the difference? Write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a careful letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
