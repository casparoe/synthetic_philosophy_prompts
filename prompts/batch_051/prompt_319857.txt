I serve on the Public Integrity Board of a mid-sized city. The city attorney is asking us to approve a proposed settlement of a whistleblower suit. The plaintiff, a former code-enforcement inspector, alleges she was fired after refusing to soften a report about corrosion in a publicly owned parking structure. Her lawyer has made two settlement offers: $160,000 with the terms public, or $185,000 with a mutual non-disclosure clause covering both the amount and the underlying allegations. She says the extra $25,000 is what she would charge to give up her ability to speak publicly; the city's anti-retaliation ordinance already protects her from blacklisting. City counsel recommends the higher figure with the non-disclosure clause, noting that the inspection records themselves remain publicly available and that a confidential settlement now is likely cheaper than continued litigation.

The board is split over what the public's right to know is. One member treats it as a trump: it overrides the city's policy goals of saving money, ending the dispute quietly, and avoiding bad press, though it could yield to a genuine competing right, such as a real threat to an individual's safety. A second treats it as a side constraint: the city may not weigh the public's interest in disclosure against its own goals at all, because the right functions not as a goal to be maximized but as a prohibition on treating public oversight as an item with a price; it yields only when another side constraint is unavoidably at stake. A third treats it as an instrument: the phrase does no independent work, and the board should compare the expected consequences of a confidential and a public settlement, including the precedent each sets for future officials.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It must recommend one course of action—approve the non-disclosure clause, reject it, or approve only under specified conditions—state any conditions precisely, take a side among these three views of the right, and give the strongest argument against your recommendation an honest run before explaining why you nevertheless recommend it. The board will act on this document, so the bottom line must be unambiguous.
