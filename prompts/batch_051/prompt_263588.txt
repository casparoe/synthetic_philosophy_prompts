In our economics class we played the dollar auction and now I can't stop thinking about it. The setup is: a £20 note is sold to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder also has to pay their last bid and gets nothing, and bids go up in £1s. My teacher said a rational person gets dragged way past £20 because quitting always looks worse than one more bid. He said it explains the Vietnam war and bidding wars and somthing like that. I tried to write the argument out properly and I can't find the hole in it:

(1) Suppose you're second-highest at £(n-1), and the other person is winning at £n. If you quit now, you lose £(n-1) for certain.
(2) If you raise to £(n+1) and the other person then quits, you win the £20, so your net is 20 - (n+1) = 19 - n.
(3) But 19 - n is always bigger than -(n-1), which is 1 - n, no matter how big n gets. So at any point, raising is better than quitting.
(4) The other player will do the same calculation whenever they're second-highest, so they'll raise instead of quitting too.
(5) Therefore, once two people have started bidding, neither will ever quit, and the bids will go past £20 to whatever they can actually pay.

That seems mad. Is the argument valid, and if not, which step exactly fails and what should that step say instead? If it is valid, are two rational people with £100 each actually doomed to keep bidding to the cap over a £20 note? If knowing each other's cap matters, does that break the proof, and which step does it mess with? And if there is a flaw, does the real-world point about escalation and wars still go through even if this proof doesn't?
