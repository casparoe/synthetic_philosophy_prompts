I am one of three partners in a small but growing software firm, and we are currently redrafting our internal code of conduct and the incentive scheme by which we pay and promote our staff. The process has surfaced a long-running disagreement between my co-founders: one insists that everyone is, in the end, out for themselves, and that the firm should be designed on that assumption; the other insists that many of our best people act from genuine concern for the firm and for one another, and that treating everything as self-interest would corrode exactly that. I have found myself reading around the distinction between psychological egoism and ethical egoism, and I have been stopped short by two further ideas. The first is the thought that ethical egoism may be a self-effacing theory: someone who accepts it might have self-interested reason not to cultivate explicitly egoistic motives in herself, perhaps even to keep the theory quiet or to hope that other people never adopt it. The second is the thought that if psychological egoism were true, a great deal of our ordinary praise and blame for selfishness would need to be reinterpreted, softened, or abandoned.

Please describe the most important implications of these ideas for our task: how we should design incentives, how we should talk to staff about the firm's values and about self-interest, and how we should regard colleagues who describe their own motives as other-regarding. I do not want a survey of possible positions; I want you to take a definite stance on each implication you draw out and to give the reasons for those stances. In particular, please say whether psychological egoism, even if true, would justify building the firm around it; whether it would be self-defeating for us to frame the firm's code in openly ethically egoistic terms; and whether the self-effacingness worry should make us less confident that our incentive scheme needs to assume ubiquitous selfishness.
