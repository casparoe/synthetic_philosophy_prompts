I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a module on AI safety and philosophy of technology. We have been reading Stuart Armstrong and Benjamin Levinstein's paper "Low Impact Artificial Intelligences". They write: "The first challenge is, of course, to actually define low impact. Any action (or inaction) has repercussions that percolate through the future light-cone, changing things subtly but irreversibly. It is hard to capture the intuitive human idea of ‘a small change’."

This passage made me wonder about a notion I have been toying with. I call it "counterfactual fog". The idea is that rather than trying to identify low impact directly, one could require that after the AI acts, the future remains hard for an ideal predictor to settle for longer than a baseline. More precisely, define the fog of an action as the length of time, comparing two worlds, one where the action occurs and one where the AI simply idles, during which the best available future-predictor cannot reliably register a divergence between the two worlds beyond some small epsilon. A low-impact action is then one whose fog is not significantly shorter than the fog produced by idling itself. This would, supposedly, allow small useful acts like manufacturing a few paperclips while blocking the shutdown of the internet, because the latter would make the divergence obvious quickly. But I worry it merely measures detectability rather than impact, and that a clever AI could do enormous harm inside a chaotic system where the effects are statistically invisible. Also, if the baseline world with an idling AI diverges anyway, the measure could license almost anything.

Could you honestly assess this proposal? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: the nearest existing idea, the strongest objection, the second strongest objection, whether any objection is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and what would need to be added to distinguish the proposal from that existing idea.
