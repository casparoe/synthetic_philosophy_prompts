I've been arguing with a friend about whether the Stoic case against emotions still works now that we know how much emotional processing isnt a judgment at all. My friend is basically a romantic: she thinks the feelings are themselves a kind of value-perception, not noise. The Stoic argument, as I understand it, goes: (1) an emotion, a passion, is just an act of assent to an impression that something good or bad is present; (2) most such assents are false, because they treat things like reputation, money, health, and death as genuinely good or bad when only virtue and vice are; (3) so the wise person withholds that assent and is thereby free of the disturbance. Hence the famous apatheia: the passions are noise, not part of the good life. I know the Stoics already allowed for tiny involuntary first movements — a flinch, tears, a startle — that even the sage feels but doesn't endorse. The argument is meant to apply to the full-blooded passions, the ones that actually disturb you.

But it looks to me like modern emotion research attacks step (1) directly. We have pretty solid evidence that alot of emotional response is subpersonal: people show conditioned fear responses to masked faces they report not seeing, stress hormones rise before any conscious evaluation, and animals and infants have something very much like fear or anger without the conceptual equipment to assent to propositions about good and bad. If that's right, then a big chunk of what we call emotion is not an assented-to judgment, so the advice to just withhold assent couldn't dislodge it.

Does the Stoic argument survive this, maybe by restricting emotion to the cognitive layer and calling the rest first movements? Or does that repair concede the whole game, so that the Stoic therapy only works on a small, voluntary slice of our emotional life while the rest really is noise (or signal?) we can't reason away? I want a verdict on whether the argument is dead, repairable, or actually fine as stated, and which premise the science actually bears on. Also tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what new evidence or consideration would flip your verdict the other way. Keep it under 600 words.
