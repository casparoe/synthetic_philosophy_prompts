Write a deadpan Swiftian modest proposal in the form of a policy pamphlet of roughly 700 words, in the voice of an earnest public official, advocating that all beliefs be brought under a government-run evidence-licensing regime. The proposal should recommend penalties scaled to how easily a belief spreads: small fines for idle under-evidenced beliefs, escalating to 'doxastic quarantine' for under-evidenced beliefs likely to shape other people's conduct. The target should be Cliffordian evidentialism, the position associated with W. K. Clifford that any belief adopted on insufficient evidence is morally blameworthy because beliefs guide action and spread through a community. The piece should be a fair reductio of that position rather than a caricature of any person. Its humor should come from exaggerating real features of the James–Clifford debate: the attempt to treat every mental commitment as a matter of moral certification, the impossibility of auditing belief before the evidence is complete, and the Jamesian point that a blanket rule against believing beyond the evidence is itself a passional preference for avoiding error over gaining truth. Keep the irony deadpan and legible, without winking or breaking character, and make the piece funny to someone who knows the debate while remaining intelligible to someone who does not. Do not use citations, footnotes, or scholarly name-dropping inside the piece. After the piece, on its own final line, write \answer{...} enclosing a short label for the position being satirized.
