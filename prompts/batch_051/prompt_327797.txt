I'm a retired physicist — spent thirty years in precision measurement, so the vocabulary I actually think in is protocols, repeated runs, calibration, systematic versus random error, sampling distributions, fitting parameters, confidence intervals, that sort of thing. I've only recently gotten into philosophy of probability, and I keep noticing that the three standard positions — frequentism, subjective degree of belief (Bayesianism), and propensity — all feel like they're trying to say something about what "probability" is, but each one seems to pick out a different thing in my old lab language.

What I'd like is a systematic restatement. Take the debate among frequentists, subjectivists, and propensity theorists and translate it into the vocabulary of an experimentalist: measurement protocols, ensembles of runs, error budgets, models with free parameters, updating fits as data comes in. For each position, tell me what probability would correspond to in that language — a limiting relative frequency of a protocol, a rational degree of belief that gets updated the way I'd update a fit, a physical property of the setup itself. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones you had to distort or fudge, and which parts of the philosophical debate simply have no counterpart in how an experimentalist talks.

I'm especially interested in single-case probabilities, because that's where I get stuck, and I find it suprising how much can turn on them. A fair die having chance 1/6 of coming up six on the next throw: in my world I can only make sense of that as a statement about what a protocol would produce over many throws, or about my own uncertainty. But the propensity view seems to want to say it's a fact about this very throw, written into the setup somehow. That sounds to me like mistaking an artifact of how the experiment is organized for a property of nature. But maybe the translation will show I'm the one smuggling in the assumption. I'd like your honest verdict on the residue: is my operational vocabulary missing something real, or do some of these philosophical positions get shakier when you force them into operational terms? Treat the stuff that doesn't survive as the interesting part, not as a bug.

And go easy on me with the phil jargon — I'm fluent in the physics but a genuine novice over here.
