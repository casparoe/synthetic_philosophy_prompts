Ive been reading around in population ethics and health economics and I keep noticing something I dont have a word for. People combine values in at least three different ways, and almost nobody seems to notice that theyre switching between them, sometimes in the same report. Sometimes they just add: ten mild headaches are worse than one horrible migraine, or enough small benefits to enough people outweigh a single large loss, and that feels so obvious nobody defends it. Sometimes they multiply: a policy that removes half the risk of death counts as half the value of eliminating the risk entirely, and in health econ a QALY is literally a number of life years times a quality weight, then added up across patients. Sometimes the rule is neither of those but closer to a veto: no quantity of money, however enormous, is treated as comparable to bulldozing one particular burial ground, and that dimension just wins before the other numbers are even consulted. And then theres the animal welfare case where people quietly multiply something like degree of consciousness by the intensity of pleasure or suffering, so a trillion barely-conscious insects with mild discomfort becomes comparable to one severely suffering mammal just by choosing the multipliers.

The thing Im actually after is not which of these rules is the right one. Its the second-order pattern: the combining rule gets chosen silently, and the choice of rule seems to do a lot of the moral work before anyone has stated a principle. Does that pattern have its own name, and if yes, how is it different from plain old aggregation or tradeoffs? If it has no established name, what would you call it, and can you lay out a clean taxonomy of the combining rules people actually fall back on, including the edge cases where a rule is additive along one dimension but a veto along another? Im less interested in a label than in getting the carving right, so a bare name with a dictionary definition wouldnt satisfy me.
