After retiring from a research career in the physical sciences, I have been reading about bounded rationality, and I find myself tangled in an argument that seems to reduce the whole idea to ordinary optimization. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot locate the mistake.

(1) Satisficing, as Simon describes it, consists in setting an aspiration level and accepting the first option that meets or exceeds it, rather than searching out and comparing all options.

(2) In a standard sequential search problem—the distribution of possible options known, each additional search carrying a known cost, and the agent maximizing expected value net of search cost—the optimal stopping rule has just this form: keep searching until an option's value passes a reservation level, then stop.

(3) Any particular act of satisficing can therefore be described as if the agent were following the optimal stopping rule for some suitable assignment of beliefs about the distribution and the cost of further search.

(4) If such a redescription is always available, satisficing is not an alternative to optimization; it is simply optimization with search costs made explicit.

(5) The distinction between satisficing and optimizing thereby collapses, and bounded rationality becomes either an empirical claim about search costs or an empty label for behavior that maximizes under some description or other.

(6) But this conclusion proves too much. Simon's starting point was that real agents often lack the distribution and the cost function and could not compute the optimal reservation level even if they had them; choosing the aspiration level is itself a judgment made under ignorance. A chemist accepting the first catalyst whose yield clears a threshold is not thereby solving an optimal stopping problem.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (6), but I want to know what I am committed to if I do.
