I was reading Keynes's A Treatise on Probability and he puts the principle of indifference roughly like this: if the evidence gives you no known reason to expect one alternative over another, then you should assign them equal probability. Fine, but my roommate says Bertrand's paradox shows exactly why that idea is broken. His argument went like this:

(1) Suppose a chord of a circle is drawn at random. The principle of indifference says that any natural way of dividing up the possible cases should give the same probability for a given event, since all the divided cases are equally good candidates.

(2) One natural way to pick a chord is to pick its distance from the center along a fixed radius and then draw the chord perpendicular to that radius. The chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle exactly when that distance is less than half the radius, so the probability is 1/2.

(3) Another natural way to pick a chord is to pick its two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference. Then the chord is longer than the triangle side exactly when the shorter arc between the endpoints is more than 120 degrees, which is one third of all possible arcs, so the probability is 1/3.

(4) Both methods split the possibilities into alternatives with no known reason to prefer one over another, so the principle of indifference gives both 1/2 and 1/3 for the same event.

(5) Therefore the principle of indifference is either ambiguous or false.

He thinks the mistake is in step 2 or 3, but I suspect the sneaky bit is actually step 4. Tell me which step fails and why, or whether the argument goes through. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
