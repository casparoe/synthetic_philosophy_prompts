I am working on how modern statistical genetics has changed the old debate about what heritability means, and I want a committed assessment of one specific argument. In his 1974 article The Analysis of Variance and the Analysis of Causes, R. C. Lewontin argues that the analysis of variance is not, by itself, an analysis of causes. His point is that a heritability estimate partitions phenotypic variation in a particular population with a particular environmental and genetic range; the resulting components describe the structure of variation in that population, not which genes causally produce which phenotypic differences or what an environmental intervention would do. He adds that variance components can be read causally only if one already has an independent causal model linking genes, environments, and phenotype.

The later development I want to test against this argument is within-family GWAS: sibling-difference and parent-offspring trio designs that use the random segregation of parental alleles to estimate direct genetic effects while controlling for family environment, ancestry, and assortative mating. Some researchers describe these estimates as more nearly causal—as measuring what carrying an allele does, rather than merely how much variation it accounts for in a population.

Does Lewontin's 1974 argument still apply to this development? Which specific premise does within-family GWAS put pressure on? Does his argument survive intact, survive in a repaired form, or has it been superseded for these direct-effect estimates—or is something else going on, such as within-family GWAS answering a different question from the one Lewontin warned about? I want a definite verdict and the reasoning behind it, not a balance of considerations. Also tell me which single subclaim your verdict depends on most and what evidence would change it.
