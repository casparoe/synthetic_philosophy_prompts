Suppose I am genuinely torn between two job offers and, while I am weighing them, I try to assign a probability to the proposition that I will accept the Chicago offer. The following argument suggests that this is not merely difficult but incoherent.

(1) While I am deciding, whether I will take the Chicago offer is not settled; the point of deciding is precisely to settle it.

(2) Assigning a probability to the proposition that I will take the Chicago offer treats that proposition as an estimable fact about how things stand with me, to be read off evidence in the way one reads off the probability of rain.

(3) But the same proposition cannot be, for the same agent at the same time, both an open question to be settled by choice and an estimable fact about how things already stand with her.

(4) Therefore, a person engaged in deciding cannot coherently adopt any probability for the proposition that she will take one option rather than the other.

(5) Consequently, the ordinary remark, made in the middle of deliberation, that one is likely to take the Chicago offer is not a harmless report of uncertainty; it is either a sign that one has already stopped deliberating or a confusion of the practical standpoint with the theoretical one.

I have a strong suspicion the argument overreaches, but I cannot locate the fault. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, explain whether the failure lies in an outright false premise or in an equivocation between two senses of a word, and say what a corrected version of the conclusion would be — in particular, whether the reasoning supports only the weaker claim that certainty (probability 0 or 1) is incompatible with genuine deliberation, or whether it should instead leave room for standing credences about one's own choices.
