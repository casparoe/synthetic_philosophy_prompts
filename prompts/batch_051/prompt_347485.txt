I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, writing on the ethics of small commitments—promises made in passing to arrive at an agreed time, to return comments by a stated day, or to take on some minor departmental burden. In my department these commitments are broken, or kept late, often enough that people quietly stop relying on them, and the existing norm of a brief apology and no further consequence seems to produce a steady background of resentment without changing behavior.

A colleague has proposed replacing that norm with a voluntary bonding scheme. Whenever a member makes a commitment to another member, they may post a bond of an amount they choose into a shared account before the promised act falls due; the amount is visible to the department. If the commitment is fulfilled within an agreed grace period, the bond returns in full. If it is fulfilled late, the bond is split evenly between the other party and a departmental reliability fund used for common goods such as the seminar coffee budget. If it is not fulfilled at all, the whole bond goes to the other party. Unbonded commitments may still be made, but the scheme carries a companion norm: members are to treat unbonded commitments as mere statements of intent, and grievances about their breach are not to be pressed.

I would like you to treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a moral proposal to endorse or reject. What would it incentivize among promisors, among those deciding how much reliance to place on a commitment, and among third parties observing the size of bonds? How would it be gamed, and would the companion norm survive that gaming? What second-order effects would it have on trust, on people's willingness to enter small commitments at all, and on the moral psychology of keeping them? Finally, are the worst failure modes correctable by adjusting the scheme's parameters—the forfeiture rules, the grace periods, the publicity of bond amounts—or do they indicate something deeper about the limits of pricing trust?
