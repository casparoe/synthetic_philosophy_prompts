I'm writing about the role of the eternal recurrence in Nietzsche's ethics, and I've found that the key notion is usually left vague: what it is to genuinely will or affirm one's life in the face of the thought that one will live exactly this life again, endlessly. Propose a working definition of genuine willing of the eternal recurrence, as distinct from merely accepting it, endorsing it on intellectual grounds, or experiencing a passing feeling of satisfaction, and test it against the following cases:

1. A person says yes to reliving his life exactly as it was because he believes the universe is deterministic and he has no alternative, but he would gladly choose a different life if he could.
2. A person says yes out of a sense of duty, though the thought of reliving his life leaves him cold.
3. A person says yes to everything in his life except one particular hour.
4. A person says yes only after silently editing out the worst parts of his past.
5. A person says yes because his life has been unusually pleasant and he wants more of the same.
6. A person says yes but insists that the belief would make no difference to how he lives or what he does.
7. A person says yes through gritted teeth, out of defiance, while still hating most of what happened.
8. A person says yes on the condition that he would remember nothing between recurrences.
9. A person says yes only because he loves one person in his life and wants that again, while resenting everything else.
10. A person never entertains the thought, but already lives, decides, and forms projects as though each moment would recur.

I am fairly confident that (1) and (4) are not genuine willing, and I'm genuinely unsure about (7), (8), and (10). State the definition, apply it to each case, identify the places where it yields a surprising or unacceptable verdict, and then use it to say what the thought experiment is actually testing for.
