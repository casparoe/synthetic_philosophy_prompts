I'm on the board of a small tenants union, seven of us, and we have to pick one of six demands to put to the landlord next month: rent freeze, boiler repair, a cap on late fees, longer notice periods, bike storage, subletting rights. We've been burned before by the fact that the winner flips depending on whether we vote A against B first or B against C first, so someone proposed the following rule. Each board member submits a full ranking of the six demands, plus a 'fervor' number from 0 to 10 for each demand, meaning how much they'd personally fight for it. The chair then draws the obvious left-right axis — moderate to confrontational — and throws out any ballot whose ranking can't be made single-peaked on that axis. The surviving ballots are combined into a Borda count, with each member's fervor scores used as weights. Anyone whose ballot gets thrown out also looses their vote in any tie-break.

Walk through how this actually plays out. What does it incentivize when members report rankings and fervor numbers? Who gains influence, which kinds of demands tend to win, and where does the rule get gamed first? Is throwing out non-single-peaked ballots a fix for cycling or does it just push the problem somewhere else? And if the goal is to stop the cycling without making the chair a dictator, what would you change?
