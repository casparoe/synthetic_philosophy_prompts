Design a runnable classroom activity for an undergraduate critical-thinking seminar (18 students, 90 minutes) on the ethics of belief: the question whether doubt is a moral duty, and when the demand for still more evidence stops being a virtue and becomes an evasion. The goal is for participants to come away no longer treating the slogan "think critically" as an unambiguous good, but as a practical dilemma with living costs on both sides.

The activity should not be a lecture, and it should not be a free-form debate about whether skepticism is correct. It should be a structured exercise with roles, rules, explicit payoffs, and real but small stakes (for example, points that decide a small privilege, first pick of snacks, or relief from a minor chore). Participants should be forced under time pressure to decide how much evidence is enough before they will believe a claim, publicly assert it, or act as though it is true, and they should discover the distinctions among believing, asserting, and acting on a claim from their own behavior rather than from the facilitator's explanations. Most importantly, they should run into the underlying problem themselves before the philosophical vocabulary is introduced; the proper names and positions should appear only in the debrief, and the activity must work if participants know none of the relevant philosophy in advance.

Design the incentives so that both failure modes are genuinely painful. Sometimes a team that proceeds on weak evidence should cause visible damage to a shared goal, and sometimes a team that demands strong evidence should cause visible damage of a different kind, such as missing a warning, delaying a decision that others urgently needed, or treating a forced choice as if it could be indefinitely deferred. Build in a third temptation as well: the rules should occasionally let participants use insufficient evidence as an excuse to avoid a costly or embarrassing commitment, so that they can discover that doubt itself can be self-serving rather than honest.

Include a concrete timeline for the 90 minutes: brief framing without revealing the target, role assignment, the exercise itself with enough rounds to expose the tradeoffs, and a substantial debrief. The debrief should map particular moments in the exercise onto the relevant philosophical positions stated simply: the requirement not to believe on insufficient evidence; the reply that refusing to commit in a forced, urgent choice is itself a risky act rather than a neutral exercise of caution; and the idea that how much evidence is enough depends asymmetrically on what it would cost to be wrong in each direction. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run (for example, teams gaming the points, a dominant participant flattening the tension, or the exercise collapsing into debate) and how the facilitator should handle each. Be frank about which feature of the ethics of belief the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator will have to supply in words afterward. Do not assign pre-readings or include a reading list. Finally, include a short note stating how confident you are in the proposed design and singling out the one design choice you are least sure will survive contact with a real classroom, with a sentence on why.
