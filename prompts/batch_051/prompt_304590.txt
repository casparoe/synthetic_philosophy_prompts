I'm a program officer at a mid-sized family foundation that gives about $2m a year to anti-poverty work, mostly in one state. Each quarter I go through 25 or 30 proposals and shortlist ten for the board, and the board keeps splitting along the same line: some members want us to fund stuff with clear, countable outcomes (a food pantry expanding its hours, a job training cohort, rental assistance for 40 families), and others want us to fund "systems change" (advocacy to change state benefits rules, campaigns to reform zoning or school funding formulas, base-building and civic engagement) where the theory is you move the default for tens of thousands of people but you can barely measure your own contribution. I suspect this is really a fight about how much uncertainty we're willing to buy, not a clean strategic disagreement.

Write me a reusable decision procedure for sorting proposals along that direct-service vs. systems-change spectrum, something I can run in two or three hours per proposal before the board sees it. My biggest worry is that "systems change" has become a label anything can hide inside, while the safe direct stuff can quietly prop up the problem it treats. So the procedure should operationalize at least these ideas without making me read systems theory: leverage points (intervening where a small push changes rules or feedback loops for many people), unintended effects (including a service program letting a bad system off the hook), and attribution (how much we need to know our grant actually caused the outcome). It should say, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where the answer depends on values or risk appetite and has to be handed to a person, and what the procedure cannot decide no matter how well I run it.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals. At least one result should make a board full of "just fund the measurable stuff" people uncomfortable, and at least one should have the procedure say no to a proposal that sounds impressively systemic but isnt:

- $40k to expand a food pantry currently serving 300 families.
- $75k over two years to a coalition pressing the state to adopt automatic renewal for SNAP and Medicaid so eligible people stop falling off the rolls.
- $60k to a community organizing group whose stated theory is that a multi-year campaign to change local zoning to allow corner stores in food deserts will take four to five years and has no measurable milestone in year one.

Finally, tell me where the procedure will predictably misfire — which kinds of proposal it would reject when it shouldn't, and which kinds it would wave through when it shouldn't — so I know where to put the manual override.
