I’m a policy analyst for a regional transportation authority, and lack of a consistent screen is driving me up the wall. We get proposals every few weeks to shift some service or asset out of ordinary public management or private contracting into worker cooperatives, social ownership, profit-sharing employee ownership, that kind of thing. The board wants a screen before they spend real time on one, but I’ve got maybe a day per proposal, no staff, and people on both sides who will accuse me of smuggling in conclusions. I keep coming back to Mill’s late Chapters on Socialism (1879). Early in it he writes: “So far as their power reaches, or may hereafter reach, the laws of property have to depend for support upon considerations of a public nature, upon the estimate made of their conduciveness to the general welfare, and not upon motives of a mere personal character operating on the minds of those who have control over the Government.” That looks to me like the principle worth operationalizing: for any change to how property is held or controlled, the justification has to be general welfare, not the personal or class interest of the people who benefit directly. Can you turn that passage into a reusable decision procedure I can run on a proposal? I’d like it as a checklist with questions in order, an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Please don’t just summarize Mill; translate him into an actual screen. Then apply the procedure to two cases: (1) a proposal to convert our city’s bus service into a worker-owned cooperative with an exclusive franchise, and (2) a proposal to convert the city’s parking utility into an employee-owned profit-sharing company. Make sure at least one application ends in an answer the proposers won’t like, and say explicitly where Mill’s own later socialism would push back on or go beyond the procedure, since I dont want to pretend this is his whole view. At the very end, put \answer{...} containing just the name of the test in your procedure that is best at catching proposals that really only serve the personal or class interest of the proposers.
