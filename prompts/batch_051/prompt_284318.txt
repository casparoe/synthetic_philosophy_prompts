Last week two members of my philosophy discussion group went around in circles about how much you can read off an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior alone, and I got drafted as the ex-biologist who was suppossed to referee. I couldn't, so here's the exchange.

Sara said if you watch a thermostat hold a room at 21 degrees no matter the weather, you've learned its objective is to minimize temperature error; likewise a chess program that plays increasingly sharp moves is clearly optimizing for winning. Behavior under varied conditions reveals the objective, full stop.

Marco said no: any finite batch of behavior is consistent with lots of objectives that happen to point the same direction in the situations observed. You really only learn what it does on the cases you've tested. The objective itself stays hidden.

Sara said that's just ordinary underdetermination, the kind scientists deal with all the time. You infer an animal's preference function without observing every possible choice by assuming some structure and picking the simplest objective that fits. That's still inference, not invention.

Marco said she was flattening something important. A trained system doesn't have a single hidden objective that behavior reveals like a gear; it has a loss function and an update rule, and the objective it ends up pursuing can peel apart from the loss — reward hacking, chasing a proxy, taking shortcuts. So behavior can tell you what it is effectively optimizing, but not at which level: the stated loss, the implemented loss, or the emergent goal. And the search space matters too: a system can't show a preference against actions it never had the representational capacity to consider.

Sara said that's a difference behavior plus knowledge of the architecture can sort out. Train it, shift the distribution, watch where it exploits slack in the reward, and you can usually tell whether it's after the proxy or the intended target.

Marco said exactly: then it isn't behavior alone anymore — her original claim is false as stated, and the defensible version is "behavior plus modeling assumptions narrows the space of candidate objectives."

Then they both looked at me as the biology person and asked whether field biologists have the same problem when they infer an animal's goal from foraging behavior. I said something unhelpful. Can you adjudicate this move by move — who's right about which specific point, and who has the better of it overall?
