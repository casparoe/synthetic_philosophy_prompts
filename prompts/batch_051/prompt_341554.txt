I run a small philosophy discussion group and I want a "which view of moral testimony do you actually hold?" quiz for the session where we read about testimony and moral knowledge. Make it 9 questions. Each question should be a concrete case — someone defers to a friend, a doctor, an AI that's weirdly good at moral prediction, a partner in a relationship, a hiring committee — with two or three answer options, phrased so you're answering by what you would actually think or do, never by labels. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these leaves: full optimism (moral testimony is just testimony, nothing special about it); strong pessimism (it can't even give you moral knowledge); moderate pessimism or the understanding view (it can give knowledge but not the understanding that makes someone a full moral agent); and some version of the responsibility/autonomy view (the problem isn't epistemic, it's that you're outsourcing something you have to do yourself). If there's a fifth coherent combination — like fine in emergencies but corrosive as a standing policy — give it its own leaf, or mark it unnamed if no one really defends it. I especially want two axes to come apart: whether the respondent thinks testimony transmits moral knowledge at all, and whether the defect they feel is about knowledge, about understanding, or about agency. Include at least two questions that compare a moral case with an ordinary empirical case (mushroom identification, say) so we can tell whether the discomfort really is specific to the moral case. Flag any leaves the questions can't separate — I suspect strong pessimism and the responsibility view will blur, because someone who says you can't get moral knowledge from others often also says you owe it to yourself to think it through, but those are different claims. Also note which answer combinations are logically possible but unoccupied, and say whether you think thats because nobody bothered, the view is unappealing, or its incoherent.
