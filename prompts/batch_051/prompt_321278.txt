A couple of people in my reading group — the kind that keeps arguing about decision theory after the actual book discussion ends — got into it the other night about a view one of them has started calling tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and suffering matters, and that positive pleasure is, at best, a neutral extra rather than something with value of its own. I took rough notes because I genuinely couldn't tell who was wining. Here's roughly how it went.

Sasha: "The view looks self-refuting. If only the absence of craving matters, then your desire to be free of craving is itself a craving. You're pulling the rug out from under your own feet. And if caring is a form of craving, I don't see why the rest of us should care about other people's suffering on your account."

Ravi: "That conflates first-order wants with the second-order aim of not having them. I can want to get a splinter out without craving splinterlessness the way an addict craves a fix. Working toward the absence of craving is like taking an antibiotic for an infection; the treatment isn't the disease."

Sasha: "That line lets anyone launder an obsession. 'I don't crave X, I just soberly recognize that X is correct.' What independent mark distinguishes the anti-craving aim from any other preference? If there isn't one, then your view is just one preference among many, and the claim that only absence-of-craving matters is doing no work."

Ravi: "The marker is urgency. Suffering and craving have a demanding, must-remove quality that pleasures don't. Nobody who is free of pain is urgently missing a bit of extra joy, but everyone with a bad itch wants it gone. That's why negative states get special status and positive ones don't."

Sasha: "Sure, that supports an asymmetry: other things equal, removing pain is often more pressing than adding pleasure. But you need the much stronger claim that pleasure has no value at all once suffering is absent. A no-suffering world with lots of joy isn't obviously just as good as a no-suffering world with none; people pay money for that difference. Your urgency point doesn't get you to zero weight."

Ravi: "But 'people pay money for that difference' is exactly the kind of preference tranquilism says not to trust. The preference for extra joy is itself a craving. You can't use the craving to justify itself."

Sasha: "Now you've shifted. Earlier the view was that craving is intrinsically bad along with suffering. Now the argument is that the preference for pleasure is a craving and therefore suspect. Not every preference is a craving, unless you define craving so broadly that any positive valuation counts — and if you do, you've defined your conclusion into existence. If instead your claim is empirical, that pleasure reliably causes clinging and later suffering, then tranquilism is a psychological bet, not a thesis about what matters, and it leaves open that a pleasure that doesn't cause craving could be valuable."

Ravi: "I'll own the empirical part. In practice, pleasure is usually the bait that leads back into craving, so a life organized around it undercuts itself. The part where a craving-free pleasure would be valuable only sounds like a refutation because we're still treating pleasure as a good by default."

Sasha: "But that means the only thing left supporting the view is the instrumental worry that pleasure causes craving. If some pleasure didn't, you'd have no principled reason to deny its value. Either you count it and tranquilism is false, or you don't and you're no longer arguing from anything except a stipulation."

I can't tell whether Ravi's last move is a retreat that changes the subject or a legitimate clarification of what "matters" was supposed to mean all along, and whether Sasha's final dilemma is as decisive as it sounded in the room. Could you go through the whole exchange and adjudicate move by move: who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing? Then tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict, and say which sub-judgment you're least sure of.
