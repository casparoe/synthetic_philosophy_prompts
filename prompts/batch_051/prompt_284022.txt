I'm on a university committee the provost set up to turn teaching quality into something the budget office can read off a spreadsheet. The current proposal ties 4% of each department's next budget increment to year-over-year improvement on an index made of three components: average student evaluation scores, retention in the major from first year to second, and average time to degree. Faculty Senate is convinced departments will game it by inflating grades, watering down courses, and quietly steering struggling students out of the major so the retention number looks clean. Our committee is split in ways I didn't expect. The statistician says the entire scheme is just Goodhart's law in action — once the index becomes the target, its correlation with actual teaching quality starts to drift — so we should refuse to put real money on a single composite metric. The economist says that's too quick: process measures such as whether a syllabus includes active learning are even easier to satisfy without changing anything, so an outcome-based index is noisy and gameable but at least not pure theater. A third member keeps insisting we're running together two different problems: a metric can slowly lose validity as people optimize it, and people can also game the reporting itself without changing the underlying outcome; she thinks the first calls for keeping several coarse measures and avoiding over-optimization, while the second calls for audits and low-stakes uses, but she hasn't offered a concrete proposal.

I'm the one drafting the short memo for Friday. It needs an actionable bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with safeguards — and if the answer is safeguards, two or three specific design changes, each tied to the gaming route it blocks. It also has to take seriously the provost's strongest argument, which is that every accountability system can be gamed, so the fact that this one can be gamed is not by itself a reason to do nothing. What should the memo say, and which of the committee's worries is being overblown?
