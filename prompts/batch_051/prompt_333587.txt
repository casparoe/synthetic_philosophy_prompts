I teach a first-year undergraduate course in the philosophy of science. In two weeks I am giving a single eighty-minute lecture under the title "Why determinism does not get you predictability." The students can follow a one-line recurrence and have met the idea of a state space, but they have no background in dynamics. I want the lecture to establish one precise claim: a system can be fully deterministic and yet effectively unpredictable from finite-precision knowledge of its initial state, and that failure of prediction is not a failure of determinism. I want the lecture built around a single system, the doubling map T(x) = 2x mod 1 on [0,1), which, read in binary, simply deletes the leading digit (the usual expansion ambiguities aside). Plan the lecture: which distinctions must be secured before that claim can be stated without hand-waving, and which can be deferred; the order in which the map's features should be introduced so that sensitive dependence on initial conditions appears as the forced consequence of the binary shift rather than a stipulation; the misunderstandings students will bring or form about determinism, prediction, chaos, and measurement precision, and at which point in the sequence each should be met; the respects in which the doubling map might actively mislead them about chaos in the physical world and therefore must be flagged, and what should be candidly set aside as beyond this level rather than silently skipped; and a closing five-minute check that would tell me whether a student owns the determinism/predictability distinction or has only learned to say that chaos is not randomness. Give me also your judgment on whether the doubling map can carry the whole lecture by itself or needs one short contrasting example, and where that example would belong.
