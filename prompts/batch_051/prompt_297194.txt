We're doing a unit on decision under ignorance in my rational choice class, and we connected it to the Rawls vs. Harsanyi argument about the original position. I've landed on a bunch of commitments and I honestly can't tell whether they cohere or whether I'm just patchworking. Here they are:

(1) In a one-shot decision where you genuinely have no probabilities over the states, maximin is the right rule. If it feels too conservative, that's a feature, not a bug.

(2) But in a one-shot decision where one state is some catastrophic outcome with a tiny but nonzero probability, maximin seems wrong to me — it makes you spend your life preparing for the apocalypse instead of taking decent options that would actually matter. There I lean toward minimax regret: pick the option that minimizes the most you could kick yourself later, where regret is how much better you could have done in that same state.

(3) For decisions that repeat a lot — an insurance policy you renew, or a rule applied across a whole population — I think you should just maximize expected value using your best rough probabilities, even if they're shaky, because over enough repetitions the randomness washes out and maximin is way too expensive.

(4) I also think a sane decision rule shouldn't let the menu mess with the verdict: adding an option that the rule itself would never pick shoudn't flip the ranking among the options you already had.

(5) On the original position itself, I'm with Rawls against Harsanyi: the veil of ignorance isn't a lottery, so you can't assign each person an equal chance of being anyone and then maximize expected utility. Maximin is the right rule behind the veil for that reason.

So does this hang together? I want an audit, not a lecture: where are the real tensions, ordered by severity, which ones only look like tensions, and what's the cheapest fix (and what would it cost)?
