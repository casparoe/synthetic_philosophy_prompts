I'm in a reading group working through On Liberty, and we've been trying to get Mill's harm principle precise enough to actually use rather than just gesture at. The version I've been carrying around is roughly: coercive restriction of speech is justified only when it's done to prevent a non-trivial setback to another person's interests—body, property, security, ability to go about their life—when that setback is a reasonably foreseeable result of the speech, when what's at stake is more than just people being offended or disgusted by the content, and when no less-restrictive response like counterspeech, blocking, or time-place-manner rules would be enough. Stated that way it feels tidy, but then I hit a case I can't settle.

The case: a few dozen neighbours in a small town form a "community safety" group aimed at one family they've decided is suspicious. They never threaten, trespass, or speak to the family. They run a public website that logs the family's comings and goings in real time, posts photos of them through their windows taken from the sidewalk, lists the children's school bus times, and has an "observations" section where members write things like "the kids didn't wave back, interesting." Nothing said is false; most of it is questions or neutral description, and the site's rules explicitly say not to contact or approach the family. A year in, the family has sold their house at a loss, pulled their kids out of school, and one parent is on anxiety medication. Is this speech that the harm principle permits us to restrict? I'd like you to go through my four criteria one by one and say where the case lands. Where my criteria don't decide it, tell me what a repair or a fifth criterion would have to say, and whether the case shows my harm/offense line is doing less work than I think. Please write it all as continuous prose, like an email—no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
