I'm taking a course on philosophical issues in the law of evidence, and I keep coming back to the problem of naked statistical evidence. I have reconstructed an argument for finding liability or guilt on bare statistical evidence, and I want to know whether the argument is valid; if it is not, I want to know exactly which step fails and what the failure amounts to.

The two cases that matter are these.

Blue Bus. A plaintiff is injured by a bus whose driver leaves the scene. The only evidence bearing on which bus was involved is that 80 percent of the buses in the city are operated by the Blue Bus Company and the remaining 20 percent by the Red Bus Company. No witness observed the colour of the bus. The plaintiff sues the Blue Bus Company, and the standard is preponderance of the evidence.

Gatecrasher, modified. A spectator is charged with entering an event without paying. The prosecution proves that 999 of the 1,000 attendees entered without paying, and that the defendant was selected for prosecution by a genuinely random process from the full attendance list. The standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt. I have deliberately changed the usual numbers, which in the standard version sit near or below one half, so that the statistical probability is unambiguously high.

Here is the argument.

(1) A fact-finder is justified in returning a verdict against a party just in case the fact-finder's rational credence that the party performed the act meets or exceeds the applicable standard of proof, understood as a probability threshold: above .50 for preponderance, and for proof beyond a reasonable doubt a threshold substantially higher than .50 but still below .999.

(2) Where the total evidence puts the defendant in a reference class in which a known proportion F has the incriminating property, and the evidence supplies no further information about whether this particular defendant has it, the fact-finder's rational credence should simply be F.

(3) In Blue Bus, F is .80; in modified Gatecrasher, F is .999.

(4) Both credences satisfy the thresholds in (1).

(5) Therefore the Blue Bus Company is liable, and the gatecrasher should be convicted.

That conclusion strikes nearly everyone in the class, including me, as unacceptable. My instructor's diagnosis is that the failure is in (1): he says a standard of proof is not just a threshold on posterior credence, because a verdict must rest on evidence suitably connected to this defendant rather than on the defendant's mere membership in a class. I cannot tell whether that really refutes (1), rather than importing a causal or explanatory requirement into the word justified, and I also cannot tell whether the actual defect might instead lie in (2), the direct inference from base rates, or in (3), the implicit choice of reference class. So my question is narrow: is the argument valid as stated, and if not, which step fails first? What would a corrected version of that step have to look like if it is to retain what is attractive about the threshold picture while making sense of the law's resistance to naked statistical evidence? Or, if the argument is actually sound, what exactly is the law's resistance a mistake about?
