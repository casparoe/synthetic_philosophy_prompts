I teach an undergrad seminar on design, incentives, and unintended consequences—about 18 students, 75 minutes. Most are philosophy or public policy majors; a few have seen game theory but nobody has formal econ. Last week we read Goodhart's original paper on monetary policy, the one where he says roughly that once an observed statistical regularity is used as a policy target, it tends to break down under the pressure of being targeted. I don't have the exact wording in front of me, but that's the idea.

I want a runnable in-class game that makes that collapse happen to them, not just hear it from me. The game should put students in charge of producing something under a measurable proxy—the score goes up, and the incentive structure quietly lures them into gaming the proxy so that the real underlying thing stagnates or gets worse. The moment of recognition should come from their own choices, before we name Goodhart or specification gaming. Give me exact rules, simple materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (points, snacks, privileges, whatever), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game to Goodhart's law and specification gaming. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—e.g., students not gaming hard enough because they know they're being watched, or gaming so hard the activity turns into a farce—and how I should handle each. The debrief should also say which feature of Goodhart's law this game can't easily reproduce and I need to supply myself.
