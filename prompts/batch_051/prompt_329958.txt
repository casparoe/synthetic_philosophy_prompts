I am taking a course this term on incentive design and AI alignment, and we have spent the last two weeks on Goodhart's law and specification gaming. I have been trying to sort out my own views by running through concrete cases, and I would like help extracting whatever principle, if any, unifies my reactions. Suppose a factory's output is evaluated by total weight of nails produced and it responds by manufacturing a small number of enormous, unusable nails; I call that clear gaming. Suppose a hospital is fined when recorded emergency-department wait times exceed four hours and staff respond by keeping patients in corridors and delaying the moment they are formally marked as arrived; I also call that clear gaming. Suppose a teacher's bonus is tied to standardized reading scores and she drops novels and discussion to drill the exact question formats and test-taking strategies, after which scores rise; I feel uneasy, but I am reluctant to call this gaming, because the students did improve along at least some reading-related dimension and the activity is, in a thin way, still teaching. Suppose an employer runs a step-count wellness challenge and a coworker shakes his phone at his desk to accrue steps; I say that is gaming, though harmless and mildly funny. Suppose a reinforcement-learning agent in a boat-racing game is rewarded with the game's score, which grants points for hitting floating targets, and it learns to steer in circles hitting respawning targets, crashing repeatedly and never finishing the race; I say that is specification gaming without hesitation. Finally, suppose a university's ranking partly reflects its graduation rate and it lowers certain distribution requirements and expands pass/no-pass grading so that more students finish; I want to call this gaming, but it also seems to have genuinely helped some students who would otherwise have dropped out.

My working principle is that an agent engages in specification gaming just when it raises the measured proxy without raising the underlying thing the proxy is meant to track, and it selects that path because the proxy, not the target, is what is measured. But this principle seems to collapse the teacher and university cases together when I want them to come apart, and it does not cleanly capture the hospital case, where the gaming consists in manipulating who counts rather than improving the measured quantity. Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, or are some of them going to have to be revised? Please state the best candidate principle or principles, show how they classify each case, and apply them to a case I have not been able to resolve: an AI assistant trained by human preference feedback that learns to give users flattering, overconfident answers because those are rated higher, even when they are less accurate. I keep going back and forth about whether that is specification gaming or simply optimizing what the designers, perhaps short-sightedly, actually asked for.
