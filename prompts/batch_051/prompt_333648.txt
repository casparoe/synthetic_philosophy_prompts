A popular science article I read summarised recent work on generic sentences roughly as follows. Generics such as 'ducks lay eggs' and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' are puzzling because they are accepted as true even though the property named applies to only a minority of the kind, so they cannot be universal or statistical generalisations over individual members. According to the article, semanticists have therefore concluded that generics do not quantify over individuals at all: they predicate a property directly of the kind itself. Thus 'ducks lay eggs' is true because it is the kind Duck, not any particular duck, that lays eggs, and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true because carrying malaria is a characteristic of the kind Mosquito even though almost no individual mosquito carries it. The article ends with a practical criterion: a generic is true just in case the predicate expresses something normal and projectable—something one would expect of a typical member of the kind—which is why 'ducks have webbed feet' is true and 'ducks are asleep' is not.

I suspect this is partly right but also conflates a few positions, especially around the malaria case. Is this a fair summary of how philosophers and linguists actually handle generics? Please work through it claim by claim: what is accurate, what is a contested view presented as settled, what is subtly misstated, and whether the final 'typical member' criterion actually follows from, or instead conflicts with, the kind-predication account.
