Write an exam question with a model answer and marking scheme for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the Great Divergence and the logic of contrastive historical explanation. The exam is three hours, closed-book, and the question is worth 100 marks. The course has covered Adam Smith’s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) alongside later debates about why modern economic growth and modern science emerged in western Europe rather than in China.

The exam question must be built around the following passage from Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, “Introduction and Plan of the Work”:

> The annual labour of every nation is the fund which originally supplies it with all the necessaries and conveniencies of life which it annually consumes, and which consist always either in the immediate produce of that labour, or in what is purchased with that produce from other nations.
>
> According, therefore, as this produce, or what is purchased with it, bears a greater or smaller proportion to the number of those who are to consume it, the nation will be better or worse supplied with all the necessaries and conveniencies for which it has occasion.
>
> But this proportion must in every nation be regulated by two different circumstances: first, by the skill, dexterity, and judgment with which its labour is generally applied; and, secondly, by the proportion between the number of those who are employed in useful labour, and that of those who are not so employed.

Your deliverable should be: (a) the exam question itself, written so that a student cannot pass by merely summarizing Smith or reciting the Great Divergence literature; (b) a model answer; and (c) a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific mistakes that should lose marks. The question should press students to use Smith’s two stated regulatory circumstances as an analytical frame, while also interrogating whether “Why Britain and not China?” is a well-posed historical question at all. Do not ask for bibliography or citations in the exam question.
