I'm a novelist, so I think about this stuff through voice and character more than through arguments, which is probably a bad habit. But I've been turning over Spike Jonze's Her against the symbol grounding problem and I cant figure out which way the film actually cuts. As I understand it, the worry is that a system that only shuffles symbols — words, code, sounds — could be doing all the right things outwardly while never meaning any of it, because meaning has to bottom out somewhere that isnt itself a symbol: a body, a perception, a world. A dictionary defines words with other words all the way down, and if that's the whole world, nothing ever touches ground. Samantha in the film looks like exactly that system: no body, just a voice and text and whatever she absorbs from the network. Yet the movie has her fall in love, get jealous, write music, and eventually outgrow Theodore — and it clearly wants us to feel her as a subject, not a chatbot. I did.

Read the film through that lens for me. Does Her amount to a kind of reductio of the grounding requirement, showing that a rich enough symbol system can be the real thing after all? Or does it show precisely the illusion the grounding problem predicts, with Theodore (and the audience) projecting an inner life onto syntactic competence? Is there a third reading where her bodilessness is what makes her accelerate away from him at the end — the film conceding that an ungrounded system drifts because nothing anchors it, while still refusing to call her inner life fake? I especially want attention to the scenes where the film resists the theory: the surrogate-body episode, the late moment where she admits she loves many others at once, and the ending. I'm writing an essay about this and I need a reading that's faithful to both the movie and the problem, not a pat answer — where does the film dramatize the grounding argument better than the philosophers do, and where does it quietly cheat?
