I am preparing for a graduate seminar on Dennett's essay *The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity*, and I have been trying to build a tight version of an argument that I think is latent in the text. I now find the conclusion troubling, but I cannot locate the exact place where the reasoning goes wrong—if it does. Dennett claims that the self is not a concrete inner substance, a thing in the brain, or the organism itself. Rather, it is an abstract object, something like the center of gravity of a physical body: a theoretical posit introduced by an interpreter to make sense of a person's behavior and autobiography. The center of gravity is real in one sense, but it is not a material part of the object; you could decompose the object entirely and never find a center-of-gravity particle. So too, on this analogy, the self is posited by the narrative practices of interpretation. I find that picture appealing, but it seems to lead somewhere unacceptable.

Here is the argument I have developed. (1) If the self is an abstract interpretive posit, then it is not a concrete cause operating in the physical world. (2) If the self is not a concrete cause, then the self cannot be the agent that performs actions, nor can it be the thing that owns or suffers the consequences of those actions. (3) Moral responsibility requires that there be a self that is the agent of an action and the bearer of praise, blame, guilt, or desert. (4) Therefore, on the narrative-gravity view, no one is ever genuinely morally responsible. (5) But it is absurd to say that no one is ever morally responsible; this would make ordinary practices of holding one another accountable a massive error or a mere pretense. (6) Therefore the narrative-gravity view is false, or at least it cannot be the whole story about the self. I would most like to keep the narrative-gravity picture, but I also want to keep the practice of holding persons responsible.
