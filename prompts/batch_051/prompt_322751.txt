I'm working on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, and I keep getting tangled in my own gut reactions to cases about spectacles. Here are my verdicts, mostly off the top of my head.

(1) A small town puts on a community play about a wronged widow who forgives; everyone knows the actors, the audience is local, and they talk afterwards. I think that's fine, maybe even good.

(2) A big city theater shows a tragedy that makes everyone cry, and afterward people say their morally improved, but nothing in their behavior changes. I don't want to count that as moral improvement at all.

(3) A town square dance where the line between spectators and participants dissolves: people start out watching some soldiers dance, then everyone joins in, and no one is just a spectator. I find this genuinely valuable, more valuable than any of the theater cases.

(4) A satirical one-man show mocking a local politician; the audience just sits and laughs, then leaves. I have a mild negative reaction, like it's not terrible but it's not doing what the square dance does.

(5) An immersive theater piece where the audience is required to participate, but the whole thing is scripted and designed. I want to say it's better than (2) and (4) but still not as good as (3), though I can't say exactly why.

(6) Watching a morally ambiguous prestige TV series alone and discussing it online with strangers, not neighbors. I don't strongly condemn it, but I feel it's different in kind from the square dance, not just degree.

The pattern I half-see is something about activity vs. passivity, and whether the emotion gets discharged in the spectacle itself. But then (1) is still a stage with actors and an audience, so that's the problem. Is there a principled account that gets all or most of these, and which of my verdicts would it force me to drop? My hunch is that the Rousseauian line would reject even (1), but I want to see whether my own gut really commits me to that.
