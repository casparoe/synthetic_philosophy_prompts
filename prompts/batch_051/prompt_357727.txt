I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility — about sixteen students, one eighty-minute session. Next week we turn to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I don't want to start by lecturing the regress at them, because students typically hear it as a clever lawyer's trick and shrug it off. I want them to run into the structure on their own, before I name it.

Please design a runnable in-class activity (game, structured role-play, or staged deliberation) that gets participants to feel the pull of the Basic Argument. The core idea I want the activity to embody is this: if being responsible for what you do requires being responsible for the self that acts, then you'd need to be responsible for how you came to be that self; but any attempt to discharge that by an act of self-creation or radical choice presupposes a chooser who already has standards, values, or dispositions to choose by — and so the demand only repeats one step further back. The recognition should happen in what participants try to do and fail to do, not in what I explain afterward.

Give me specific rules, materials (ideally nothing more than paper or index cards, or a simple shared document), a grouping scheme, a timeline that fills the eighty minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the steps of the Basic Argument. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, participants treating their starting values as brute givens, or jumping ahead to a free-will-versus-determinism debate — and how I should handle those failures. Be candid about the feature of the argument an in-person activity cannot reproduce (I suspect it is the necessity of the regress, as opposed to the mere felt difficulty of self-grounding) and what I'll need to supply myself. I'd like a committed design: make definite choices for this room, with reasons, rather than offering me a menu of possible activities.
