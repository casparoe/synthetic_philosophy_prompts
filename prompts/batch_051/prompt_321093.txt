I keep coming back to the thought that the word "nihilism" is doing too much work, because it gathers three denials that look structurally identical to me and I can't tell if the resemblance is deep or just a trick of the word. Mereological nihilism says there are no chairs, only particles arranged chair-wise. Moral error theory says no action is actually right or wrong, only actions that elicit certain attitudes in creatures like us. Existential nihilism says there is no meaning of life, only meanings people project onto things. The rhythm is the same in all three: deny the commonsense item, then hand back nearly everything in paraphrase — the chair is still there to sit on, the cruelty is still condemned, the project still absorbs you. My question is whether this is one argument form (the item does no work beyond the arrangement/attitude/projection, so it doesn't exist) or three different arguments that happen to share a name. If it's one form, does an objection to one of the three transfer to the others, and what would have to be true for the transfer to be legitimate? If it isn't one form, where exactly does the mapping break? And does the paraphrase consolation get thinner as you move from chairs to morality to meaning, or is it equally adequate in all three? Please also name one thing the deep-parallel reading predicts that comes out false in at least one of the three areas.
