I'm leading a discussion on The Great Gatsby for a reading group, and I've gone down a rabbit hole in the philosophy of love that I only half-remember from college. The live distinction, as I understand it (and please correct me if I'm mangling it), is between appraisal views and bestowal views of love. On an appraisal view, love responds to value already there in the beloved—her character, her beauty, whatever makes her lovable—and that value is what justifies the love. On a bestowal view, the order runs the other way: the lover confers value on the beloved, so that being loved is what makes her valuable (to the lover, and maybe in a stronger sense). I know Frankfurt, Velleman, and Kolodny get filed somewhere around this fight, but I dont need a literature review; I'd rather you just use the distinction as a lens.

I want you to play the role of a philosopher who reads fiction seriously, and to think carefully before committing. Read Gatsby's love for Daisy through this appraisal/bestowal frame. Is Gatsby best described as someone who bestows a value on Daisy that she doesn't independently have—the green light, the dream he built before he'd even re-met her as an adult, the whole self he invented as a boy—or is there a real appraisal underneath, something in her voice, her beauty, her careful social poise, that the novel treats as a genuine (if compromised) reason for loving her? Nick is the obvious contrast: he sees Daisy's carelessness and the Buchanans' wreckage clearly, and yet he also finds something admirable in Gatsby's sheer hopefulness. Does the novel conclude that bestowal is just self-deception dressed up as love, or does it suggest that all love involves a fair amount of bestowal and Gatsby's version fails for other reasons? Before giving your verdict, sketch the two or three most plausible readings and then say which one the text most supports. And dont force it: if the novel ends up resisting the appraisal/bestowal framing altogether and points somewhere more interesting, say that too, and tell me what philosophical point the book adds that a paper on love's reasons might miss.
