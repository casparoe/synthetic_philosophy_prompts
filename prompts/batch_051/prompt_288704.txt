Two people in my political philosophy seminar got into a long argument about the Rawls line that envy is a vice but that it can still be excusable, and I honestly still don't know who won. I'll reconstruct the moves as faithfully as I can.

(1) M said envy is just a vice. It isn't a judgment that an arrangement is unjust; it's wanting what someone else has, and it can be set off by perfectly fair differences, so the fact that an inequality provokes envy tells you nothing about whether the inequality is just.

(2) K replied that this is exactly what the excusable-envy idea is meant to complicate. On K's reading of Rawls, envy becomes excusable when the gap between classes is so large that the worse-off can't maintain their self-respect. In those cases the envy is a symptom of an inequality attacking what Rawls calls a primary good, so it isn't simply a flaw in the envious person.

(3) M came back: that only survives by smuggling in resentment under envy's name. Resentment is the moralized response to an injustice; envy is a competitive desire for the other's good. An arrangement can cause a vice without the vice counting as evidence about the arrangement, and it certainly doesn't make the vice good.

(4) K said that distinction is too clean. Most people don't feel one discrete emotion and then the other; the same experience can aim at the rich person and mean both that this is unfair and that I want it. And if you required someone to already know the arrangement was unjust before you counted their feeling as resentment, the feeling couldn't be doing any evidential work — which was the whole point of treating it as a signal.

(5) M said: fine, so at most envy is a fallible heuristic. But then it tracks injustice sometimes and misfires other times, which everyone admitted. The real work is done by the independent theory of justice that sorts the cases, so envy adds nothing.

(6) K said that sets an impossible standard. The independent theory has to be applied by someone, and felt responses like envy are part of what makes features of a situation salient enough to test in the first place. A theory of justice that runs without any such signals never actually gets applied to the world.

Then say where the argument as a whole actually turns — including whether K's use of Rawls is fair and whether M's envy/resentment distinction holds up. I want a point-by-point verdict, not a general essay.
