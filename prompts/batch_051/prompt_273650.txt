Give me a working definition of what it is for two surface forms of English to be the same word in the lexeme sense — the sense in which dictionary headwords group cat and cats together but not cat and catty. I am not asking about orthographic words or phonological words. I want the definition to do the boundary work that the slogan "inflection keeps you inside one word, derivation makes a new word" only gestures at. Take as your starting point that inflectional morphology (plural, tense, agreement) produces forms of one lexeme, while derivational morphology (agentive -er, nominalizing -tion, adjectival -al) produces a distinct lexeme. But the usual diagnostics for that contrast — syntactic obligatoriness, semantic transparency and compositionality, full productivity, whether the process changes lexical category, whether the base and output can themselves feed further derivation — do not always line up, and I want your definition to say what happens when they pull apart.

Then run your definition against each of the following pairs or sets. For each, give a verdict — same lexeme or distinct lexemes — and identify the feature of your definition that fixes the verdict. If your definition returns no determinate verdict, say so and state exactly what further information it treats as decisive:

- cat / cats
- child / children
- run / runs / ran / running
- go / went / gone
- good / better / best
- do (the verb, as in do the dishes) / don't (as in I don't know)
- teach / teacher
- destroy / destruction
- nation / national
- run (the verb meaning to move quickly on foot) / run (the noun meaning an act of running)
- mouse (the rodent) / mouse (a computer pointing device)
- bank (the side of a river) / bank (a financial institution)
- kick the bucket (in the idiom meaning to die) / bucket (the ordinary noun)
- get up (leave bed) / get over (recover from) / get by (manage) / get (obtain)

Finally, state which of your verdicts is least secure, and say what additional fact about the case would most pressure it.
