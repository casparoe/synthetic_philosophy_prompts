I recently became the patient representative on the data and safety monitoring board of a randomized trial comparing standard open surgery with a newer, less invasive procedure for early-stage cancer, a role I accepted in part because a relative had been through a similar trial and I wanted to understand what patients were being asked to accept. The primary endpoint was days alive and out of hospital at ninety days. At the first planned interim analysis, the less invasive procedure showed a clinically meaningful-looking advantage, but the difference had not crossed the pre-specified efficacy boundary, so the formal recommendation was to continue enrollment. That recommendation is what started the argument.

Elena argued that continuing to randomize now violated the trial's basic ethical condition. That condition, she said, need not mean perfect indifference between the arms, which would be psychologically impossible, but it does mean the accumulating evidence must not yet favor one of them, and here it did. To keep assigning people to open surgery while the trial's own data point away from it, she said, is to treat current patients as instruments for a cleaner answer that will benefit only future patients. Marcus replied that this standard of personal uncertainty is too strict to be usable: investigators nearly always suspect one arm is better, and that is often why they run the trial. The right standard, he said, is clinical equipoise, understood as honest professional disagreement among expert clinicians about which arm is better. That plainly still obtains, since practice has not changed, reasonable clinicians still disagree, and the signal is far from the pre-specified boundary. Elena answered that community disagreement is only as good as its basis: experts can remain divided for reasons unrelated to the current evidence—loyalty to the standard procedure, caution, career investment, or inertia—and a division that is real but not rationally grounded cannot by itself justify giving patients the treatment the data now suggest is worse. Marcus said her objection cut both ways. If poorly grounded disagreement is unreliable, so is the private leaning of a single board member at an interim look, and a trend this weak may well reverse; that is exactly why the boundary was set in advance. If the evidence is too weak to change practice, it is too weak to establish that one arm is inferior, so the trial remains ethically clean. Elena replied that this conflates two questions. Regulators and clinicians should indeed refuse to change practice on a weak signal, because changing practice imposes costs on everyone; but a person being randomized today is not a statistical policy question. We are choosing her treatment for her, and the level of evidence needed to justify enrolling someone in a randomization—genuine uncertainty about which arm is better for her—is not the same as the level needed to change medical practice.

I found myself unable to referee the exchange and said nothing at the meeting. Please adjudicate it point by point: for each move, say what is right, what is overstated, and where the real disagreement lies, then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
