Two people in a volunteer group I'm part of spent an hour in our Slack arguing about whether a decision last month was an instance of the unilateralist's curse, and I still don't know who had the better argument. Background: a few of us maintain a small open-source project, and one member, Priya, unilaterally published a half-finished "call for contributors" post to our public blog, linking a repo we'd all agreed wasn't ready for outside eyes. Around the same time, three or four other members admitted they'd each privately been weighing doing the exact same thing; everyone seems to have put the chance it would be net positive at maybe 60 or 65 percent, but the group had earlier agreed to hold off. The argument afterward was between two members who weren't Priya, and it went roughly like this.

The first said its a textbook unilateralist's curse: several people faced the same choice, each had a positive but noisy estimate, the group ex ante would have preferred that nobody act, and one person acting on private judgment produced the outcome the group would have voted against. He added that the fact she meant well is exactly the point, because the curse isn't driven by bad actors but by independent deciders each doing what locally looks reasonable.

The second replied that the curse is suppose to apply to cases where people can't coordinate or at least aren't overriding an explicit shared decision. Priya had a Slack channel, a stated group norm, and every opportunity to ask; calling it the curse just renames ordinary norm violation, and it makes the postmortem sound like a statistical problem when it was really one person deciding her own judgment outranked everyone else's.

The first pushed back that the curse doesn't require coordination to be impossible, only that each person is in practice deciding on their own estimate, and the fact that several people privately considered posting is exactly the setup. The group norm wasn't enforced by any mechanism, and the curse predicts that weak shared norms plus many deciders means someone eventually pulls the trigger. Priya wasn't defecting for private gain; she shared the group's aims and likely just drew a slightly more optimistic estimate from the same information.

The second answered that this is where the concept gets stretched until it covers everything. If someone has an explicit group agreement and overrides it while telling herself it's for the group, that's defection, not bad epistemic luck, even if the motives feel cooperative. She also pointed out that a blog post can be edited or unpublished in minutes, unlike the irreversible externalities the curse is usually invoked for, such as releasing code or contacting a journalist. What the group was actually angry about, she said, was that its decision procedure had been ignored, not that a bunch of symmetric agents each had a positive expected value.

Then say which of the two people has the better overall reading of whether this was the unilateralist's curse, and what you think both of them are missing about how the curse should apply in small, norm-governed, reversible settings like this one.
