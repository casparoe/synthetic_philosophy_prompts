I cover artificial intelligence and the philosophy of language, and a question I keep returning to is whether an older philosophical argument still holds up against the systems we now actually build. Take Davidson's radical interpretation argument. He imagined an interpreter confronting a wholly unfamiliar language with no dictionary and no bilingual informant, and he argued that the enterprise can succeed only if the interpreter assumes, as a constitutive constraint rather than a courtesy, that the speakers are by and large rational and mostly right about the world. Today, neural machine translation systems produce workable translations across many languages without attributing beliefs to anyone, without deciding whether any speaker is mostly right, and without applying anything that looks like a maxim of rational agreement. Does Davidson's argument survive this development? Pinpoint the premise that these systems put under genuine pressure. Is the right conclusion that the argument is untouched because machine translation is not interpretation but something thinner, or that the argument needs repair into a weaker but still important claim? And what, if anything, must such a system be said to assume for its output to be understanding rather than pattern-matching?
