I keep seeing a parallel between the familiar anti-moral-saint argument and reward hacking in ML, and I can't decide whether it's deep or superficial. Wolf's worry is that a person who maximizes moral goodness at every turn ends up unbalanced, and maybe not even good at the moral life; an RL agent that optimizes a single reward metric similarly produces degenerate behavior that scores high but isn't what the designers wanted, since the metric is only a proxy. But in the ML case the diagnosis is that the metric is a stand-in for a richer goal, whereas in the moral-saint case moral goodness is not a stand-in: it is one value among others, and maxing it crowds out humor, play, art, friendship. Is this the same structure about optimizing one evaluative dimension until it distorts everything else, or does the analogy break exactly because moral goodness isn't a proxy? What would have to be true of value for the ML-style degradation argument to transfer to ethics? And as a stress test: the analogy seems to predict that someone who maxed out a single non-moral excellence like musical skill would end up bad at it, which seems false — so where exactly does the parallel go wrong?
