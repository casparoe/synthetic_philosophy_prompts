I'm pretty sold on the anti-luminosity argument — the Williamson-style line that no non-trivial condition is luminous, where a condition is luminous iff whenever you're in it you're in a position to know you're in it. Run it on coldness. Take a long series of cases from clearly freezing to clearly not cold, each step too small to notice. If being cold were luminous, you could know you're cold in every cold case. But near the cutoff there's a case where, if you're cold, your felt evidence is basically indistinguishable from a neighboring case where you're not cold, so your belief that you're cold could to easily be false to count as knowledge. Repeating that slips you all the way down the series to the obviously-not-cold end, which is absurd. So luminosity fails, and the KK principle (knowing p puts you in a position to know you know p) goes with it.

But I can't shake the conviction that my own present pain is different. When I'm in pain, it seems impossible to be in pain and not be in a position to know I'm in pain; the pain just is what it feels like, so there's no gap between the state and my evidence for it. So at least some conscious states look genuinely luminous, and for those KK seems unshaken.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the pain intuition to be the thing I jettison — what would a debunking story of it look like, and would it have to deny that pain is self-presenting? And what would it take for the anti-luminosity argument to be the thing that gets restricted: is there a principled reason it works for 'cold' but not for pain, or does any carve-out just beg the question? Is there a reading on which both survive, like KK holding for a narrow class of self-presenting states while luminosity fails for everything graded the way cold is?
