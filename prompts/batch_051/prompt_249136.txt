People love to point to the gender-equality paradox — in countries that score higher on gender-equality measures, men and women seem to sort *more* into different fields, like engineering versus nursing, not less — and then conclude that those preferences are basically innate. I want to know whether the data can actually bear that weight. Design a test of the claim that the paradox shows gender-typical career preferences are mostly nature rather than culture. Be concrete: who would you study, what exactly would you measure (career choices, stated preferences, willingness to trade salary for flexibility, timing of when the preferences show up), and what pattern would count as real evidence for the 'innate' reading rather than for 'still cultural but sticky' — for instance, that equality indices mostly capture formal rights and not everyday norms, or that rich egalitarian countries let people express preferences that poor countries suppress via economic pressure. Then say what even a clean 'innate' result would and wouldn't settle: would it imply we shouldn't try to change gendered career gaps, or tell us anything about any particular person? And is there a way the nature/culture split is just badly posed here, becuase culture can shape preferences early enough that they feel innate?
