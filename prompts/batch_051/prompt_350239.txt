before I read another proposal about orphan works and abandonware, I want the actual spec: what does a theory of ownership-when-the-owner-is-gone have to get right? Let's say the purpose is a theory good enough for a small preservation project — someone digitizing out-of-print work and old software on a budget, not a rights-clearance office. Give me the desiderata list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the settled legal and economic facts it can't contradict, with the reason each one is a requirement. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference (preservationists vs. rightsholders vs. remixers), and tell me which pair or pairs, if any, can't be jointly satisfied.

Some things I suspect belong on the list, but I want you to justify them or tell me they're bogus: orphan status is a fact about whether the owner can be located, not about whether the work is still under copyright; a dead author with no traceable heirs is different from a dissolved company whose assets were never assigned, and both are different from a living author who just never answers permission requests; preserving a work is different from redistributing it, which is different from making derivatives; abandonware is a community term, not a legal status. The theory also has to stay consistent with copyright vesting automatically and lasting for decades with no renewal, and with moral rights persisting seperately in some systems. And it should make sense of the digital case, where copies are perfect and marginal cost is zero, and the software case, where all you might have is a compiled binary and no source.

The part I most want is the tension work. For instance: any scheme that lets an archive use an orphan work after a diligent search has to draw the search standard somewhere, and that line will either be weak enough to expropriate absent owners or strict enough to make most preservation illegal. Is that a real dilemma or can it be finessed? And is there a version of "use it or lose it" for abandoned software that doesn't gut the no-formality rule? Be honest about which requirements are doing the real work and which are just vibes. Finally, as you go, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present one side of a live fight as the consensus. A quick scorecard of the main positions at the end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their cases.
