Two friends of mine spent a long dinner arguing about the following case, and they asked me to referee. I couldn't decide, so I want your adjudication instead. The case: a mid-sized manufacturer sold a line of portable generators. Over several years, its own engineering reviews, warranty claims, and two outside consultants flagged a risk of fuel-line cracking; but no single decision-maker ignored a clear warning. Instead, the organization's standard practices—separate budgets for warranty payouts and product redesign, role-sliced approval chains, and bonus metrics that rewarded near-term margins—kept the problem from ever being acted on as one decision. Eventually a generator failed and killed someone. No individual employee intended the death, and no single manager saw the whole risk picture at one time. One friend says the company itself, not merely its employees, should be blamed.

Here is roughly how their argument went.

(1) He began: a company is not just a heap of individuals. It has an internal decision structure—its committees, approval rules, reporting lines, and metrics—through which choices are formed. The decision to keep selling the generator was made by that structure, so the company itself acted and can bear corporate-level blame.

(2) She replied: that describes how individual decisions are coordinated, but it does not add a mental agent. Only the humans inside the structure can intend, know, or be negligent. If no individual had the relevant belief or intent, then "the company decided" is a figure of speech, and blame must fall on the specific people who each overlooked what they should have noticed.

(3) He came back: that standard is perverse, because the more an organization spreads information across roles so that no one person holds the whole picture, the less blameworthy anyone becomes—so the worst-designed organizations become the most insulated. Institutions are blamed all the time for policies no single member wanted, and that is because their decision procedures themselves can be defective.

(4) She answered: the right response is precisely to stop treating diffusion as a shield—look harder at who designed the budgets and the metrics, and hold those individuals responsible. Blaming the company adds no extra agent. It only means fines paid by shareholders, higher prices for customers, and perhaps lost jobs for innocent employees, which is punishment of the wrong people.

(5) He answered: those costs are not arbitrary. Shareholders and customers benefit from the firm's activities and can exit or diversify. And the company persists even when the responsible managers leave: a firm can admit fault, promise a safety overhaul, and then break that promise years later under entirely new management. If we can hold it to its promises and its safety culture over time, then the persistence of blame is not a fiction.

(6) She responded: those are all just further ways of talking about changing groups of people and their incentives. Keeping the company as the object of blame is a pragmatic device for making sure someone pays, not evidence that a corporation has the kind of mental states that make blame true.

They each thought the other won the rhetoric but missed the point. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify what in each numbered claim is sound, what it gets wrong or overstates, and then say who is more right overall about whether the company itself can be blamed.
