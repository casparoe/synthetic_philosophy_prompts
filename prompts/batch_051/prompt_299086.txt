I'm giving a talk next month to a reading group that mixes machine learning researchers with a few formal epistemologists. The thesis I'm defending is that no-free-lunch theorems do not hand the skeptic a newly rigorous version of Hume's problem of induction. In the supervised-learning setting, what they actually establish is that no learner has lower expected error than any other when targets are averaged uniformly over all possible functions, and that is a constraint on how inductive bias must be distributed rather than a proof that any particular inductive method is unjustified. My positive claim is that induction is better understood as an underdetermination problem: the data never force a choice among priors, and no-free-lunch results are a vivid way of showing that a prior is never dispensable. The ML members will think in terms of generalization bounds and will dislike anything that sounds like special pleading for real-world datasets; the epistemologists will press on whether any prior can be justified without circularity. I expect the hardest questions from two directions. One is an ML researcher who thinks the theorem is essentially a mathematical restatement of Hume — no finite sample can warrant a generalization unless you already assume the world is regular, and that assumption is just what cannot be justified. The other is an epistemologist who thinks a universal prior over computable sequences, or some similar Bayesian recipe, already tells us which prior to use, so the no-free-lunch result is irrelevant rather than threatening. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually be raised, phrased the way each person would phrase them, with a short reply to each, an honest note on where each reply is soft, what I should concede up front and what I should hold, and the single weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
