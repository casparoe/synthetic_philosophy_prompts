I am a novelist, and I find myself genuinely drawn to pragmatist accounts of truth, though I suspect the attraction may be corrupting. The account I have in mind conjoins two thoughts. From James: that truth is not a static relation between a thought and a ready-made world, but a property beliefs acquire by working—by fitting experience, guiding action, and proving themselves reliable over time. From Peirce: that truth is not whatever momentarily satisfies an individual, but the opinion that an indefinitely continued community of inquiry would eventually settle upon. The theory's virtues are plain: it dissolves a mysterious correspondence, makes truth something we can test and recognise in practice, and explains why truth matters to us at all.

But I also have an intuition I cannot dislodge, and my trade keeps pressing it on me. A belief can be useful without being true, and true without being useful. Characters often are sustained by falsehoods that let them act well, and destroyed by accurate self-knowledge that arrives too late or serves no purpose. I think of this not only as material for fiction but as a fact about ordinary life. The divergence seems real, not merely a way of speaking. If that intuition stands, the Jamesian assimilation of truth to usefulness seems false; if the assimilation stands, the intuition must be a relic of a correspondence picture I have failed to inspect. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to go, what it would take for the theory to go, and whether there is a defensible pragmatist reading on which both survive—perhaps one that keeps truth and usefulness distinct but internally related. Please answer in British English.
