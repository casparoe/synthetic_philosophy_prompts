I'm helping run a reading group on whether democracy should be replaced or patched with something more epistemic, and one member keeps leaning on the Condorcet jury theorem. His version: if each voter is independently more likely than not to get a yes/no question right, then as the group gets large the majority is almost certain to be right — so we ought to restrict the franchise to the competent, or at least weight votes by competence. I want to push on the "independently" part and see how much of his argument actually survives.

Here's the variation I have in mind. Keep two options and a large group, and suppose each voter, thinking on their own, has a 0.6 chance of being right. Now add a common-error channel: with probability 0.2 a widely shared misleading narrative (a bad poll, a viral misreport) pushes everyone toward the wrong answer, so in that state each voter is only 0.4 likely to be right, independently; with probability 0.8 there's no shock and the original independant 0.6 setup applies. Walk me through what the jury theorem gives you now. Where exactly does the asymptotic gurantee break, and does majority rule still beat a single randomly chosen voter? Then do the same for the epistocratic move: what would a competence test or vote-weighting scheme have to assume to recover the guarantee, and does the common-error channel make it harder to tell who the competent voters were after the fact? I'm less interested in the generic pro/anti-epistocracy talking points than in what this one changed assumption does to the formal argument, and what the breakdown says about the theorem as an argument for epistocracy.
