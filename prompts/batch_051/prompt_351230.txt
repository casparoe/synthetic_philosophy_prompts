I'm a retired physical chemist who started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the community center last fall, and I keep finding that the instincts I spent forty years sharpening in the lab are almost useless for this stuff. Last week we read an essay defending hedonism about well-being, the view that the only thing good for a person in its own right is pleasure, and afterward two of the regulars, Beth and Stan, got into it in the parking lot. I listend to the whole thing and honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, which bugs me more than it should.

Beth started by saying she couldn't see what the pleasures have in common: the pleasure of a hot bath when you're chilled to the bone and the pleasure of finally seeing how a complicated mechanism fits together don't feel like the same kind of state at all, and if hedonists can't point to the shared ingredient, then "pleasure" is just a label pasted onto a mixed bag and the theory hasn't actually unified anything. Stan shot back that the common ingredient isn't a distinct sensation, it's an attitude: in both cases the person likes the experience, favors being in it, and wants it to continue for its own sake rather than for anything it produces. Beth replied that this move gives up the very thing that made hedonism attractive, namely that what's good is how things feel; if the common factor is just wanting or liking, hedonism becomes preference-satisfaction theory in a different hat, and then it has to count the tenth cigarette you mechanically crave as good even though you get nothing out of it, and besides, "enjoying" something just is taking pleasure in it, so the definition goes in a circle. Stan closed with the following: it isn't preference-satisfaction because he's restricting the attitude to a conscious pro-attitude toward an experience you're currently having, the craving smoker doesn't meet that, and the attitude can be defined functionally. When you're in the bath or working out how a mechanism fits together, you're in a state that makes you want the experience to continue and tends to make you seek more of it, so the functional signature, not a secret pleasant feeling, is the common thread.

Can you go through this exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches or gets sloppy, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd also like your overall take on which side is in better shape. Please just write this as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
