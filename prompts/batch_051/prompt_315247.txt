My friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about whether Marx's historical materialism is really defensible. At one point he summarized the position as follows: a society's economic base, roughly its technology together with the class relations that grow out of it, determines its superstructure—law, politics, religion, philosophy, art. Everything in the superstructure is at bottom a reflection of the economy, so the proper explanation of any legal, political, or intellectual development must trace it back to the economic interests of the relevant class. From that he concluded that ideas, legal forms, and political movements are never genuine causes in history, only effects, which is why serious Marxists do not try to explain social change by appealing to what people happened to believe. I kept objecting that this sounds crude and that Marx and Engels themselves seem to have said something subtler, but my friend insisted he was just stating the theory honestly. I would like a careful audit: which parts of his summary would a careful historical materialist accept, where is he subtly wrong or overstating, and does the last conclusion actually follow from the defensible core of the view?
