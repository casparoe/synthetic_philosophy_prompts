I just got out of a long argument with a friend about whether Franklin’s whole moral perfection project in the Autobiography is meant deadpan or is quietly ridiculing itself. She kept coming back to the bit near the end where Franklin says, after failing to keep to his thirteen virtues, that a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself to keep his friends in countenance. Her line is that this one sentence proves the whole project was social performance, not real moral ambition. I think there are several different things that sentence could be saying, and they don’t all support her. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it could be read as making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether the surrounding text—the actual recording of faults, the speckled axe bit, the claim that the attempt made him a better and happier man—actually supports it? Which reading does her use need, and is that reading consistent with the rest of the passage? Also flag any reading that would be imposing a modern irony-culture reading onto Franklin.
