Take the classic St. Petersburg game. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if it lands heads on the kth toss, the player receives 2^k dollars. The expected monetary value is infinite, yet people will typically pay only a few dollars to play.

Now change one thing about the standard finite-resources resolution of the paradox. That resolution usually imagines that the house has some known, finite maximum payout, so the game cannot actually pay the largest possible sums. Instead, suppose the house does have a finite cap, but the cap is itself random and unbounded in expectation. Specifically: before each play, an independent fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; let M be the number of tosses this takes, and set the house's maximum payout at 2^M dollars. The payoff coin is then tossed until it lands heads; let K be its number of tosses. The house therefore pays 2^min(K,M) dollars.

Work through the expected value of this modified game exactly, and explain what it shows about the finite-resources resolution. In particular: Is the modified game's expected value finite or infinite? How does it compare with the value the game would have under a known, fixed cap of some particular size — and what does that comparison tell us about how much of the work is done by the finiteness of the cap itself, as opposed to what the player knows about the cap or the shape of its distribution? Does the classic gap between expected value and what one should pay survive this variation, or does the variation dissolve the paradox — and does it dissolve it for the right reason? Say something, too, about whether the concave-utility resolution becomes redundant here or is still doing separate work.

Finally, tell me where your analysis is most likely to fail: which calculation you might be getting wrong, or which interpretation of the finite-resources move I should be careful about, and what would change your verdict if that alternative reading turned out to be correct.
