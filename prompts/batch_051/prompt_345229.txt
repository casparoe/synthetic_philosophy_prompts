Last night I got into a long argument with a friend who works as a photographer, and she convinced me of exactly one thing: my whole policy about believing photographs has never actually been examined. The policy is this. When I see a photograph of something, I default to believing the thing was really in front of the lens and looked that way, and I dont require a specific positive reason to trust the particular image the way I would for a painting or a verbal description. For a painting I have the opposite default: I treat it as evidence about the painter first and the scene second. She says this is backwards, since framing, lens choice, lighting, the moment selected, and post-processing are all as human as brushstrokes, and the camera's automaticity doesn't make the picture a window. But I think the automaticity does buy me something: a photo of a crowd usually tells me the crowd was actually there in a way a drawing of a crowd doesn't. Is my policy defensible, or is it the transparency metaphor doing my epistemology for me? If 'seeing through' is the wrong picture, what is the best restatement of the default — for instance, that photographs but not paintings carry a causal, spatial relation between scene and image, even when the photo is staged? Where does the default actually break down (AI-generated images, deepfakes, long exposures, composite astrophotography), and am I even entitled to a rule like this when I can't check provenance on most photos I see?
