I retired from a career in physical chemistry a few years ago and have recently begun reading the philosophical decision-theory literature with the seriousness it deserves. The coherence arguments for expected utility maximization have a particular hold on me. As I understand them, the representation theorems say that if my preferences over acts satisfy certain axioms — completeness, transitivity, and a suitable independence condition — then there is a utility function and a single subjective probability distribution such that I behave exactly as though I were maximizing expected utility relative to them. The Dutch-book and money-pump arguments add that violating these axioms does not merely make one's preferences aesthetically untidy; it exposes one to a sure loss or to a cycle of trades one would pay to be released from. The package has the economy I used to demand of a good model: a few conditions, a representation theorem, and a practical sanction for departing from the conditions.

Yet I keep stalling on completeness, and specifically on what it forces about belief. In my laboratory life — consider a first-in-class compound, a novel catalytic system, or a one-off decision about whether to remediate a contaminated site on sparse data — I never in good conscience had a single probability for the outcome. I had a range, and the width of that range tracked the thinness of the evidence; I would have regarded the instruction to collapse it to a point estimate as a demand to feign evidence I did not possess. The coherence argument, however, appears to require that a rational agent's preferences over arbitrary pairs of bets be complete, and the representation theorem then hands back a unique probability for every relevant event. I find myself pulled both ways: the systematic virtues of the theory make me want to accept its demands, while the intuition that a rational person may sometimes refuse to trade on a single number feels like a datum about rationality rather than a flaw in me. Which ought to yield, and how would I tell?
