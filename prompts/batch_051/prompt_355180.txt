Act as a professional philosopher of science whose specialty is the demarcation problem. Take your time: before you commit to an answer, sketch two or three candidate definitions and test them informally against the cases below, because I care about the reasoning and not just the final formula.

The question stopped being academic for me last month. My sister has a chronic inflammatory condition and, alongside her prescribed medication, has started paying for homeopathic consultations. When I called homeopathy pseudoscience, she asked me, perfectly reasonably, what the word means such that it covers her homeopath but not other medical practices she also has doubts about. I found that I could gesture at falsifiability and cherry-picked evidence, but I could not give a definition that survived her follow-up questions.

I want a working definition of pseudoscience that gets the following cases right. By "gets right," I mean it counts the first two as pseudoscience without gerrymandering, counts the third as science without gerrymandering, and gives a defensible answer either way for the rest:

1. Astrology. I take this to be a paradigm case of pseudoscience, and yet many of its claims are empirically testable and have in fact been disconfirmed.
2. Homeopathy as currently practiced. I take this to be pseudoscience too, even though it makes concrete efficacy claims that trials have repeatedly failed to confirm.
3. Germ theory and infectious-disease epidemiology. I take this to be a paradigm case of science.
4. String theory. Most physicists treat it as legitimate science even though its distinctive predictions are currently untestable.
5. Psychoanalysis. The classic Popperian example of pseudoscience, but contemporary analysts can point to testable hypotheses and some empirical work.
6. The cold fusion research program in the years after the 1989 announcement, when replications failed but a small community continued.
7. Climate modeling. It is scientifically serious, but it relies on parameterization and tuning in ways that are hard to falsify in isolation.

On the last four cases I do not have settled views; that is precisely the point.

Please do four things. First, propose the best definition you can and apply it to all seven cases. Second, flag any case where the definition gives the wrong result, no result, or a result you can defend only with strain. Third, revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict, whichever seems more honest. Fourth, tell me what the exercise shows about whether "pseudoscience" names one unified category or several distinct things. If you think the word does more than one job in ordinary use, say which job your definition captures and which it misses.
