A friend of mine who went from ML to the philosophy-ish side keeps sending me this argument, and I can't find the off-ramp. The claim is that "algorithmic fairness" as people actually use it is basically incoherent, and he runs it like this:

(1) For any real-world risk-scoring system that isn't perfectly accurate, there's an impossibility result: it can't simultaneously have calibration (same score, same probability of the outcome, regardless of group), equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative rates across groups.

(2) Each of those three, considered on its own, looks like the sort of thing fairness requires — or at least the sort of thing you'd need a reason to sacrifice.

(3) So every such system is guaranteed to violate some reasonable fairness standard.

(4) And nothing purely statistical tells you which of the three to give up; that decision is a value judgment about which group's errors matter more.

(5) So anyone who says they built a fair algorithm is either confused about what the impossibility result implies, or silently picking which group gets the extra false positives and which gets the extra false negatives.

(6) Therefore algorithmic fairness in practice is either an impossible standard or a euphemism for allocating error.

The conclusion (6) feels way too strong, but I can't find which step actually breaks. If forced, I'd most like to keep (1) and reject (6), but I don't know what that commits me too. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I want the step, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs.
