I'm giving a department colloquium next month and the room is the wrong room for a hand-wavy version of this paper. The audience is mostly comparative-politics people who know the Swiss and Californian ballot-initiative literature cold, plus two democratic theorists who are genuinely sympathetic to deliberative mini-publics, and one political historian who worked on the Leave side in 2016 and still thinks the referendum was fine and the implementation was the problem. My thesis is that the binary referendum doesn't merely risk a bad outcome; it creates a phantom mandate. A yes/no vote compresses at least three logically distinct judgments—that the status quo is unacceptable, that some specific direction of change is wanted, and that whatever implementation follows is authorized—into one mark, so the result systematically underdetermines what voters actually licensed. From that I defend the maybe-unfashionable conclusion that the fix is not more deliberation before the vote but restructuring the vote itself: two-stage or sequenced ballots where the first vote fixes direction (with a supermajority threshold where constitutional stakes require it) and a second, later vote ratifies a concrete implementing proposal drafted by the legislature in between.
