I'm giving a department colloquium next month to a university education school, defending the claim that many of students' systematic mathematical errors are best understood as theories rather than as missing pieces or bugs. My thesis is that characteristic errors — for instance, treating the equals sign as a command to compute rather than as a relation, or ordering decimals by string length — are generated by a small set of law-like rules, that students defend and elaborate these rules when challenged, and that new instruction is assimilated to them rather than replacing them; the implication I will press is that the right instructional response is conceptual change, not filling gaps. The audience is mathematics-education researchers, two developmental psychologists who work on conceptual change, and one prominent knowledge-in-pieces researcher who regards "theory" as exactly the wrong metaphor for these errors. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections I will actually get, in the vocabulary this audience uses, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; for each, a two-minute reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on where the reply is soft. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, flag which strengthened objection would be most likely to change my mind, and where my argument is most likely to fail.
