I have been thinking about the way states and international bodies spend public money on keeping particular cultural forms alive. They fund immersion schools and documentation projects for languages with only a handful of remaining speakers, subsidise crafts and festivals whose natural audiences have vanished, and maintain registers that give certain practices a protected status as intangible cultural heritage. The oddity is that the official justifications are almost always instrumental: heritage attracts visitors, language revitalisation supports the wellbeing and identity of communities, and cultural diversity is treated as a reservoir of knowledge and options for the future. Yet the pattern of expenditure does not track those justifications very closely. A language with two elderly speakers is treated as an urgent public good and a suitable object of serious investment, while a dialect, a local musical style, or a craft that still has practitioners but lacks institutional recognition is allowed to lapse without much comment. My suspicion is that the practice quietly presupposes that a culture can be wronged by its own disappearance, over and above the harms to its members, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine commitment or merely an unexamined manner of speaking.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the theory of value this pattern presupposes, rather than a survey of heritage policy. Does it commit the institutions involved to the view that languages, traditions, and art forms have value in themselves, over and above their value to the people who hold them or might come to hold them? Or can the entire practice be rebuilt on a person-centred footing, with future generations, non-members, and diffuse epistemic or aesthetic benefits doing all the work? I would also like to know whether the practice is internally coherent: which parts of it strictly presuppose an impersonal, intrinsic value of culture, which parts merely fit well with such a view, and which parts are philosophically underdetermined because several theories would license them. And, just as importantly, what would actually have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforward welfarist account on which only the interests of persons count—whether the funding patterns, the registers, and the priority given to endangered languages would survive, and if so in what form.
