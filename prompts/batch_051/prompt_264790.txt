I am a PhD student in philosophy of medicine, and I have been trying to get clear on where naturalistic and normative accounts of disease genuinely come apart. As I see it, the naturalist maintains that whether something is a disease is a purely biological matter: a condition is a disease when some part or process functions statistically below the level typical for individuals of the same species, age, and sex, regardless of whether anyone evaluates that condition as bad. The normativist maintains instead that disease judgments are irreducibly evaluative, tracking harm, disability, or some other disvalued departure from how a life is expected to go. I am not especially interested in the familiar test cases where an unambiguous functional failure is classified as a disease on both views. The problem occupying me is subtler. The naturalist's supposedly non-evaluative choice of a reference class can be quietly tailored to encode social evaluations, with the result that a condition counts as a disease only because an evaluative distinction has been smuggled in upstream.

I would like you to construct a concrete, worked example of that kind of failure. The example should involve an invented but medically plausible condition, detached from contested real-world diagnoses so that our classificatory intuitions do not simply track familiarity. It should show how a naturalist might individuate a reference class in a way that is presented as merely a biological refinement but actually tracks a social judgment about which bodies or lives set the standard of normal functioning. The condition should then satisfy the naturalist's own statistical criterion within that chosen reference class, while a normativist would plausibly say the classification depends on an evaluative choice of standard rather than on any fact about biological malfunction. Please spell out the case step by step, and explain why the naturalist cannot dismiss the normativist's diagnosis as merely conflating the concept of disease with the concept of a bad condition.
