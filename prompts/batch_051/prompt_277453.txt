I'm drafting a paper in the philosophy of language and need a thought experiment built to order. The paper's thesis is that indexicality is not a lexical category a language can eliminate by losing pronouns; it is a semantic function that survives by migrating into whatever grammatical machinery the language already has. I want the centerpiece case.

Construct a natural human language, L, with the following profile. L has no personal pronouns of any person, no demonstratives, no tense inflection, and no free-standing deictic adverbs such as those meaning now, here, today, tomorrow, or the like. Its only context-sensitive expressions are two bound morphemes. First, an egophoric suffix on finite verbs, of the conjunct/disjunct kind found in some natural languages: CONJUNCT marks a clause whose subject is the same individual as the speaker, and DISJUNCT marks a subject other than the speaker. The finite verb must bear one of these suffixes; there is no way to omit it. Second, a directional prefix on motion verbs, TOWARD and AWAY, interpreted relative to the speaker's current location. Everything else about L should be ordinary enough that normal human children could acquire it.

Now build an everyday two-person errand — a market trip, a hospital pickup, something equally mundane — in which a speaker A is in a state she would express in English roughly as I am the one who has to go to the market now, and can express it in L without any word resembling a pronoun. Engineer the case so that three frameworks give three different verdicts about A's state. A stripped-down possible-worlds semantics with only uncentered propositions and no character level treats the egophoric suffix as agreement and assigns A's state the same uncentered proposition as a purely descriptive belief about a person named A, so it predicts that the descriptive belief would move A in the same way. A Lewis-style centered-worlds semantics assigns A a self-ascriptive property and predicts the two states motivate differently. A Kaplan-style two-tier semantics says L is indexical after all, because the egophoric suffix has a character mapping context to content.

Then give the walkthrough. State the relevant utterance or thought and the context. Give the uncentered proposition, the centered content, and the character/content pair the three frameworks assign. Identify which feature of L's grammar does the work by running a deletion test: remove the egophoric suffix but leave the directional prefix, then remove the directional prefix but leave the egophoric suffix, and say in each case how the verdicts change. Finish by saying how a committed defender of uncentered possible-worlds content would attack the case, and how much of it would collapse back into a familiar Perry-style messy-shopper case involving an overt first-person pronoun.
