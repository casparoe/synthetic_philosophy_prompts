Longtime reader here. Something I keep chewing on: the law treats factual error and moral error wildly differently. Person A walks off with someone else's suitcase because he genuinely mistook it for his own; if the mistake was reasonable, a court is likely to let him off, since he never formed the relevant intent. Person B does something he genuinely isn't sure is wrong, or is even convinced is morally fine, but it happens to be illegal; the law basically says too bad, ignorance of the law is no excuse. So uncertainty about the facts can defeat blame, but uncertainty about the moral status of the act doesn't even get a hearing. What commitments about moral knowledge and culpability does this asymmetry presuppose? Is the legal system implicitly assuming that moral truth is available to everyone, or that moral error is itself blameworthy? How much of this is forced by the need for a workable legal system rather than any deep theory? And what would a practice that took moral uncertainty seriously — treating uncertainty between moral theories like uncertainty between empirical hypotheses — have to change about criminal blame, and would it collapse into letting everyone off?
