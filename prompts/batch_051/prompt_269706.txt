Please act as an expert philosopher of social science with a specialization in the historicism controversy and its Popperian critique. Think carefully, start by brainstorming the possible cruxes before drafting anything final, and then produce the document itself.

I am a PhD student in political philosophy. My department has a reading group on the philosophy of history, and two of its members — one a historicist, the other a Popperian — have been debating for a year whether there can be laws of history. They have now agreed to write an adversarial-collaboration statement, and I have been asked to draft it. The historicist holds that large-scale historical development exhibits laws or lawlike regularities that make long-range historical prediction possible in principle, for instance in the sequence of economic, political, or technological forms. The Popperian denies this: there are trends, tendencies, and local regularities, but history as a whole is a single non-repeatable sequence, and long-term social prediction is impossible in principle because the future growth of knowledge cannot be known in advance; the Popperian also suspects that part of the dispute about 'laws' is merely verbal.

Draft the document they could both sign. Format it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — what a law would have to be, whether there is a principled difference between trends and laws, what historical explanation does and does not commit one to, and what practical limits either position licenses. Second, a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, a formulation in neutral wording, then a formulation of each side's position that its own proponent would accept. Third, for each disagreement identify the crux — the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side — and, where possible, a case, type of evidence, or argument both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Throughout, flag which parts of the dispute are genuine and which are only verbal, and do not reduce either side to a caricature: the historicist need not be a fatalist, and the Popperian need not deny that social science discovers real regularities.
