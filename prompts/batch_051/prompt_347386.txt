Ok so my roommate took exactly one seminar on Heidegger and now acts like he's cracked the whole thing. His one-paragraph version goes like this: "Heidegger's insight is that most of the time we don't relate to things as objects at all — we just use them. The hammer disappears into the hammering. But when the tool breaks, it suddenly becomes visible as a physical thing: a lump of metal and wood with properties like mass and shape, sitting there in front of you. He calls everyday absorbed use 'readiness-to-hand,' and the broken-tool mode 'presence-at-hand,' and he thinks the second is when you finally see what a thing really is. The world, for him, is basically one big set of tools, and the only way it ever lights up for us is through failure — broken equipment, absence, and eventually anxiety. So the lesson is that we only truly understand things when they resist us, which is why he's so obsessed with breakdown."

I'm inclined to buy the first half — the hammer disappearing into the hammering — and maybe the part about the broken hammer revealing a physical object. But the claim that anxiety is the only place the world reveals itself sounds overblown, and the "one big set of tools" line seems deliberately uncharitable. Could you go through this summary and tell me where a careful Heidegger reader would say yes, fair, where they'd say that's a subtle misreading, and what the corrected version of the view actually is?
