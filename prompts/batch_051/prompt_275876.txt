Two students in my moral philosophy course found themselves deadlocked after a discussion section on Kant's Formula of Universal Law. They were arguing about a classmate who had downloaded a pirated PDF of a textbook he could not afford. Maya held that the act was wrong on Kantian grounds: the student's maxim was roughly 'I may take an author's work without paying whenever I do not want to pay,' and he could not consistently will that as a universal law, since a world in which everyone took works without paying would collapse the publishing practice on which his own act depends. Amir responded that this is the vacuous 'what if everyone did that?' move: universalizability asks whether one can will one's maxim as a universal law, not whether universal compliance would have bad consequences, and he can will the more carefully specified maxim 'I may download a textbook I cannot afford when my doing so makes no difference to the author,' because he can will that anyone in exactly that situation do the same. Maya replied that he was gerrymandering the maxim: if an agent can always append 'when it makes no difference,' the Formula of Universal Law condemns nothing and becomes empty. Amir answered that her original maxim was just as gerrymandered toward a guilty verdict, since it left out the features that actually mattered, and that Hare's distinction between universalizable prescriptions and the generalization argument shows the collapse-of-publishing consideration misses the point. Neither moved, and when a third classmate asked me to referee I could not sort it out. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, where each move overreaches, and what the two together are missing about how Kant's Formula of Universal Law, the generalization argument, and universal prescriptivism actually relate.
