I have been trying to think through voting paradoxes on my own, and I have arrived at a rule I call the Compact of Least Complaint. I do not have an academic background, so my terminology may be off; I care about the substance. I would like an honest assessment rather than encouragement.

The rule is as follows. Each voter submits a complete ranking of the candidates, with no ties. For any proposed final ranking, compare it with a voter's ranking pair by pair: if the proposed ranking orders two candidates in the opposite direction from the voter, that is one complaint for that voter. A voter's total complaint is the number of pairs on which they are contradicted. The first stage considers every possible final ranking and keeps only those for which the most-complaining voter complains as little as possible. The second stage keeps, among those, the rankings with the smallest total complaint across all voters. If several rankings remain, I treat them as equally legitimate rather than forcing a further tie-break.

An example: if a voter ranks A above B above C, and the proposed ranking is B above A above C, the voter has one complaint, on the pair A and B; the other pairs agree.

I believe the Compact treats all voters symmetrically, respects unanimity (if every voter ranks X above Y, no chosen ranking will put Y above X), and always returns a transitive ranking or a set of tied transitive rankings. Because it judges whole rankings at once rather than one pair at a time, I suspect it sidesteps Arrow's impossibility theorem, or at least sidesteps what is troubling about it. I can see that it deliberately lets a third candidate influence how a pair is handled, and my instinct is that this is a feature rather than a bug: fairness to whole rankings may require exactly that.

Please step into the role of a professional social choice theorist and think carefully before answering. Begin by restating the Compact in standard terms and identifying the closest existing proposals, since I may simply have reinvented something. Then give the strongest case for what the rule gets right, and after that the three or four most serious objections. For each objection, tell me whether it is only an uncomfortable trade-off any fair method might accept, or a fatal flaw in the underlying idea. If the Compact fails, say so plainly.

At the end, please state how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of, with a sentence about why.
