ok so in my English class we just finished 12 Angry Men and in my philosophy elective we've been doing peer disagreement, and I want to write my paper for the philosophy class as an actual reading of the film rather than just using it as an example. The basic debate is: when you find out someone with the same evidence and roughly the same smarts disagrees with you, conciliationism says you should lower your confidence a lot, and the steadfast view says you can rationally stay put if you've already weighed the evidence. In the movie, Juror 8 is the lone not-guilty vote against eleven people who are all convinced, and he doesn't have any special information or credentials, so on paper conciliationism says he should cave. But the movie clearly doesn't think he should, and the others flip one by one as he points out things they'd all missed — the old man's limp, the woman's glasses, the switchblade, the train noise. So I can't tell whether this is a vindication of steadfastness or secretly a conciliationist story where everyone updates when they get new arguments and not because of peer pressure.

Heres where I'm stuck, though. A not-guilty vote is technically a judgment that the prosecution didn't prove its case, not a claim that the kid is innocent, so maybe the jury isn't even the right kind of peer disagreement — they're not all evaluating the same proposition. And the film cheats by making the eleven's reasoning lazy and biased in ways the audience can see, which might mean Juror 8's so-called peers aren't epistemic peers at all. I'm wondering if the movie is dramatizing something the literature misses, like the difference between trusting your own first-order look at the evidence and trusting your read of eleven other people's looks, under time pressure, with a verdict on the line. I'd like comments on whether this reading is too neat, where the film resists the conciliationist/steadfast distinction, what the film adds to the debate that the arguments alone don't show, and what I should concede before I turn it in to a teacher who actually knows the epistemology.
