In a seminar on institutional facts, a fellow student dismissed the claim that some speech acts do things in the world rather than merely describing it by saying that "saying so doesn't make it so." I can see that the slogan is right about, for example, a weather forecast or a confident prediction: the assertion does not bring about the rain. But it seemed flatly wrong about a judge's verdict, a referee's call, an oral promise, or a marriage officer's pronouncement, each of which appears to change the normative situation by being uttered under the right conditions. I want to know exactly what precise claims this slogan could be making, from the trivial to the substantive to the false. For each reading, say (i) what class of speech act, if any, the claim is about; (ii) whether it is true, false, or true only when the utterance meets further conditions such as the speaker's standing, an applicable procedure, uptake by the right parties, or sincerity; and (iii) whether that reading would support using the slogan to dismiss performatives and declarations. Lay this out as a menu of disambiguations, and say where the slogan is doing illicit work by sliding from a platitude about describing to a mistaken conclusion about speech that changes what is the case. If the standard speech-act vocabulary helps pin down the readings — constative and performative utterances, illocutionary force, felicity conditions, declarations, institutional facts — use it.
