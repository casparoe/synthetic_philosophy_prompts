I've been developing a view I call the two-ledger theory of punishment, and I want an honest assessment. The claim is that "punishment" lumps together two acts with different justifications: the verdict-censure (authoritative public condemnation of a culpable wrong) and the hard treatment (the concrete burden imposed). I hold that censure is justified purely backward-looking — a culpable wrongdoer may be condemned because the condemnation is true and deserved — while hard treatment is justified purely forward-looking, by deterrence, incapacitation, and whatever makes shared norms credible. The familiar retributivism/consequentialism debate then dissolves: each side is right about one ledger and wrong to demand that its justification cover both. Test case: a wrongdoer who can never reoffend and whom no one would imitate. We should still censure him, but his hard treatment earns its keep only through further good. Assess this candidly: Is the censure/sanction split real or gerrymandered? Is the view already in the literature under another name? What does it get right, and where does it break — especially the worry that public censure is itself a burden, and that the amount of hard treatment partly constitutes what the censure says, so the two ledgers cannot stay cleanly apart?
