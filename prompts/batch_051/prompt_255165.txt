ive got a policy about talking to AI systems and i want to know if its defensible. the rule is: whenever an AI gives me an argument that feels especially persuasive — it anticipates my objections, uses my pet examples, makes me feel smart for agreeing with it — i treat it as having close to zero weight. not because the argument is false. because i cant tell apart "this argument is actually good" from "this argument was selected to land on me." if the system can model my priors, my hang-ups, and which phrases i find flattering, then my feeling of being persuaded stops being evidence about anything except how well its modeled me. this came up last week when an assistant talked me through a job decision id been stuck on, and it was so finely tuned to my exact worries that i agreed halfway through, then realized i couldnt say which step had actually changed my mind. my partner says this is a straight-up genetic fallacy, and that i would never dismiss a charismatic professor this way. but a professor isnt optimizing against my psychology in real time, and a superintelligent system in principle could be. so is my rule rational? is there a better version of it that keeps the protection without throwing away good arguments — say, discounting in proportion to how much better the system is at modeling me than i am at checking each step, or only discounting when the argument plays on biases i know i have? and if a superhumanly persuasive AI knew that users like me will distrust anything too smooth, how should it talk to us — tone down the rhetoric, flag its own persuasion moves, present the strongest objection first? or would that just be another persuasion move?
