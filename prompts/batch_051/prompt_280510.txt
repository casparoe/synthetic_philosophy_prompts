I am an undergraduate in a political theory course on religion and liberal democracy, and we have just started a unit on public reason. The running question in the course is when, if ever, religious considerations may legitimately figure in the justification of coercive law. In our last seminar, a classmate argued against a bill permitting assisted dying on the grounds that human life is a gift from God and that only God may take it. The instructor declined to engage with the argument and said, "religious reasons don't belong in politics," then moved the discussion along. The same sentence has surfaced before in the course: once in a debate about whether a legislator may invoke scripture in a parliamentary speech, and once in a debate about whether an ordinary citizen may vote according to a religious conscience. I suspect the instructor does not mean the crudest version of the slogan, but I am not sure which version was meant, and the ambiguity is beginning to matter because the sentence is being used to end discussions rather than advance them.

I want to bring to office hours a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making, so that I can ask which claim was actually doing the work. Could you lay out the distinct readings, separating claims about the content of a reason from claims about its source, its epistemic standing, or the identity of the person offering it, and separating claims about what a liberal state may enforce from claims about what a person may say in a seminar, in a campaign, on the floor of a legislature, or in the voting booth? For each reading, say whether it is fairly trivial, substantive but defensible, or radical and probably indefensible, and indicate which readings would actually justify declining to engage with a fellow citizen's argument in a university discussion as opposed to merely justifying a rule about how the state may justify law. Please phrase each reading as a proposition with truth-conditions, not as a label like "public reason liberal" or "secularist," so that I can test the readings against what the instructor actually said.
