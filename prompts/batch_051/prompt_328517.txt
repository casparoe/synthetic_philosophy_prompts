Consider the following widespread pattern of behavior, now so familiar in affluent societies that it is easy to miss: people treat a life as a collection of experiences that ought to be varied. They draw up bucket lists. They ask of a holiday or an evening out not only whether it was good but whether they have done it before, and they treat the mere newness of a restaurant, a city, or a novel as a reason for choosing it. They describe someone who eats the same lunch every day as stuck in a rut. Yet many of these same people will refuse to watch a film whose ending has been spoiled, on the grounds that the spoiler ruins the experience; they will pay a steep premium for a hotel room with a view they will actually look at for ten minutes; and they will plan an expensive trip around sights whose interest consists almost entirely in their being new.

Reconstruct the view of well-being that this practice presupposes, and assess whether the practice is philosophically coherent. In particular: does the practice presuppose that novelty is itself a constituent of well-being — one of the things that makes a life go well directly — or only an instrumental good, valuable because it keeps enjoyment from fading or holds off boredom? Can the practice, as it actually operates, distinguish these two claims, or is it consistent with both? What account of a boring life, and of why a boring life is bad, does the practice presuppose? Are there parts of the practice that point in opposite directions — for instance, a cult of novelty alongside an equal attachment to yearly rituals, familiar comforts, and repeat visits? Conclude by saying what would change in people's practical deliberation if the implicit theory were replaced with the rival view that novelty matters only as a means, and how much of the practice actually depends on the stronger claim that novelty is good in itself.
