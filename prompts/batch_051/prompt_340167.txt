I taught ecology before I retired, and I've spent this year reading about the shift from foraging to farming. I want to test a definition I've been using for whether a large-scale change counts as a mistake — I don't mean a bad outcome, I mean the kind of thing people look back on and say "we took a wrong turn." My working criteria are:

1. It made the people who lived through it worse off by their own standards — health, leisure, security, autonomy.
2. The costs were not forced by circumstances; there was a workable alternative, even if it was shrinking.
3. Something self-reinforcing locked it in regardless of welfare — population growth eating the surplus, sunk costs, neighbors emulating each other.

Now take the Neolithic in the fertile crescent. Early farmers got more calories per acre and storage against lean seasons, but the skeletal record shows shorter stature, enamel defects, and new infectious diseases, and most estimates say they worked longer hours than their foraging neighbors. Still, nobody sat down and chose farming; it was a thousand small decisions over centuries, and by the time the costs were visible there may have been no turning back, because populations had already grown past what foraging could carry.

Do my criteria classify the Agricultural Revolution as a mistake, taking each criterion in turn? Is there a criterion I should drop or restate because it does the wrong work on a case like this? And in the end: did we take a wrong turn, or is "mistake" just the wrong category for a process with no decision-maker?
