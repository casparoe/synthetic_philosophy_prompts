I need an argument audited. Consider the following decision problem, a version of what is called a counterfactual mugging.

A predictor Ω has already studied your decision procedure and formed a prediction about how you would answer this question: whether, if asked for $100, you would pay. Ω's predictions are not literally infallible, but assume for this problem that its prediction is perfectly correlated with the answer you actually give. Ω then flips a fair coin. If the coin lands heads, Ω asks you for the $100. If the coin lands tails, Ω does not ask you for anything; instead, Ω gives you $10,000 if and only if it predicted that you would pay in the heads case.

You are now asked for the $100, so you know the coin landed heads, and you know the whole setup.

Consider this argument that you ought to pay.

1. Ex ante, before the coin was flipped, there were two relevant strategies available to you: pay-if-asked and refuse-if-asked.
2. Pay-if-asked yields a net change of −$100 in the heads case and +$10,000 in the tails case, since Ω predicted that you would pay. Given the fair coin, its expected value is (−$100 + $10,000)/2 = $4,950.
3. Refuse-if-asked yields $0 in the heads case and $0 in the tails case, since Ω predicted that you would refuse. Its expected value is $0.
4. Therefore, ex ante, pay-if-asked is the strategy with the higher expected value.
5. Rationality requires you to choose the strategy with the higher expected value.
6. Since you are in the heads case, choosing pay-if-asked consists in paying the $100.
7. Therefore, rationality requires you to pay the $100.

A causal point—that paying now cannot cause Ω to have given you $10,000 in the tails case—seems too quick to me, because the argument never claims a causal connection. The work is all being done by the move from strategy evaluation to the act you perform in the branch you now observe. But I cannot tell whether some step smuggles in more than that, or whether the argument is in fact valid and the conclusion simply has to be accepted.

I want a precise audit. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, are all the premises true? If not, identify the first step that fails, or the step at which an equivocation or a scope shift is introduced; then give the strongest reading under which each premise is true and say whether the argument still goes through under that reading. Also say how confident you are in your assessment, and single out the point you are least sure about.
