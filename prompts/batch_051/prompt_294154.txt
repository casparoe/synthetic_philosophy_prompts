A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether a criminal law that is almost never enforced is still law in any meaningful sense, and I came away convinced that the easy answers on both sides are wrong. My friend insisted that a long-dormant statute is effectively repealed by settled practice and is therefore harmless; I suspected that a dormant statute remains a standing warrant for arbitrary enforcement, because one can never know when the state will choose to wake it up. I would like to think through this with a real case: the treatment of consensual sodomy in the United States between Bowers v. Hardwick in 1986 and Lawrence v. Texas in 2003.

Here are the salient facts as I understand them. In Bowers, the Supreme Court upheld a state law criminalizing consensual oral or anal sex, a type of law that was already rarely enforced against private adult conduct. By the time of Lawrence, the problem had taken an even sharper form: Texas had a statute that made certain consensual sexual acts criminal only when performed by two people of the same sex; the arrest grew out of a reported disturbance at a private Houston apartment; and the Supreme Court struck the statute down and overruled Bowers, without relying on any claim that the law had fallen into disuse.

I want an analysis of this episode through the lens of desuetude and the rule of law, with the traffic running in both directions. Concretely:

1. Reconstruct what a desuetude-based view should have said about the Texas statute while it sat on the books mostly unenforced. Should it have been treated as legally moribund—no longer available for prosecution except as an abuse—or does the fact that it was still occasionally invoked show that it was not truly desuetude but selective enforcement?

2. Identify what this lens explains about the period between Bowers and Lawrence: the gap between the statute’s text and ordinary practice, the sporadic and discriminatory pattern of enforcement, and the political difficulty of formal repeal. Then say where the lens misdescribes or goes silent.

3. Consider whether Lawrence is better read as showing that desuetude is insufficient on its own: even an underenforced criminal prohibition can inflict a real constitutional injury through the status it creates and the official stigma it licenses, quite apart from how often it is actually prosecuted. If so, does the case expose a limit in the desuetude critique, or does it extend it?

4. Finally, say what the episode shows about the underlying dispute from my argument with my friend. Does it vindicate the claim that dead-letter laws are harmless, the claim that they are a rule-of-law problem, or some third position—and what distinction would that third position require?

Keep the mapping precise: distinguish desuetude from mere underenforcement, spell out what counts as evidence that a law has lost its claim to guide conduct, and say clearly where the desuetude framework stops doing useful work.
