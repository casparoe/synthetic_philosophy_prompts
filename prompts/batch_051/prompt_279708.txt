I have been presenting a colleague of mine with cases in order to work out her implicit view about when, if ever, letting a sunk cost guide a decision is rational rather than a fallacy. Her rulings so far are these.

1. A woman who is disliking a film twenty minutes in, and who feels no particular regret about the ticket, is rational to walk out.
2. A general who continues an offensive that is now failing on the ground that withdrawing would make the soldiers already killed have died for nothing can be acting rationally.
3. A young man who drags himself to the gym several times a week only because he bought a nonrefundable annual membership can be acting rationally.
4. A firm that continues a failing product line solely because it spent fifty million on its development, where continuing secures no reputation, learning, or other forward-looking benefit and no felt aversion to waste is in play, is committing the fallacy.
5. A student who stays in a course he dislikes only because tuition is already paid, and who will gain nothing further from the term and feels no pang at the waste, should drop the course.

Now I have a new case for her. A couple has paid a large nonrefundable deposit on a wedding venue. They now believe that on forward-looking grounds alone the marriage would be a mistake, and there is no legal, reputational, or social penalty to cancelling; but they go through with the wedding anyway because cancelling would feel like throwing the deposit away.

Predict the verdict she would give on this new case. In doing so: state the rule or rules that fit all five of her earlier rulings; give your best guess about her verdict and the reasons for preferring one rule over the others; say which of the five earlier cases is the closest analog to the wedding deposit and why; and name a further case that would cleanly separate any competing rules that fit her earlier rulings. If those earlier rulings genuinely underdetermine her verdict, say what principle she would be committing to with each possible answer.
