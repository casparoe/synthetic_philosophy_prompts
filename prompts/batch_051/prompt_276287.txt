A textbook I'm reading summarizes Frank Ramsey's 1927 paper "Facts and Propositions" in the following way:

"Ramsey argued that there is no distinct philosophical question about what truth is. Once we say that a belief is true just in case it is successful or useful as a guide to action, we have said everything there is to say about truth. Truth is not a real property; it is just a word we use to endorse beliefs that work. That is why deflationists think every sentence containing 'true' can be replaced, without any loss, by a sentence that simply asserts the underlying belief itself."

I later looked at the paper, and I found a passage where Ramsey says, in effect, that once we have explained what it is for a belief to be true or false there is no further problem about the nature of truth, and that the appearance of such a further problem is a linguistic muddle. The textbook's paragraph doesn't match my understanding of that passage.

Is this a fair summary of Ramsey's deflationary view? Please go through it piece by piece and separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is a misrepresentation. In particular, does Ramsey's actual argument support the textbook's claim that deflationists are committed to saying 'true' can always be eliminated without loss? Please answer in American English.
