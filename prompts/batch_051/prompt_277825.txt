I retired a few years ago from a career in geochemistry and have been reading philosophy of physics with more patience than I could manage while I was still working. I am currently going through A. S. Eddington’s *The Nature of the Physical World* (1928), and I stopped at a sentence in the preface: “the progress of the principles of thermodynamics has wrought more gradual but no less profound change.” I take Eddington to be claiming that thermodynamics altered our conception of the physical world as much as relativity or quantum theory did, only less dramatically.

What puzzles me is how to reconcile that claim with what I have picked up from recent philosophy of physics. As I understand it, the fundamental dynamical laws are time-symmetric, and the thermodynamic arrow is supposed to be explained by a low-entropy initial condition rather than by making entropy increase a fundamental asymmetric law. If that is right, then the second law looks like a highly useful statistical regularity rather than a deep principle about the structure of the world. So I am unsure what Eddington’s “profound change” amounts to. Did he think the second law reveals something about time itself, or is his remark about the explanatory role of thermodynamics in the sciences rather than about fundamental physics? I suspect I am running together two senses of “law” or two senses of “fundamental,” but I cannot yet state the distinction cleanly.
