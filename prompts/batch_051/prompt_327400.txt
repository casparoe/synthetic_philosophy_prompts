Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* contains a passage in which he imagines a poor man's son, cursed with ambition, who admires the condition of the rich and spends his life pursuing wealth. After a great deal of labor, he discovers that the accommodations of the rich are not much better than those of a peasant, and that his pursuit has cost him the real pleasures he could have had. Smith then adds that this very deception is what rouses and keeps in continual motion the industry of mankind, and that it led people to cultivate the soil, build cities, and develop the arts and sciences.

I want to understand how Smith can hold both of those judgments at once. On one hand, the passage seems to condemn the pursuit of wealth as morally hollow and based on a delusion about what will make us happy. On the other, it credits that same delusion with the material and institutional achievements of commercial civilization. Is Smith's position better described as saying that commerce corrupts our moral sentiments while civilizing our outward conduct, or as saying that the market channels vanity into socially useful work without actually corrupting character? What exactly is the moral status of the deception, and does Smith leave room for a reader to approve of the system while withholding approval from the individual's motives?
