Over the last few years I've been on a university working group on transformative AI, and I've read enough of this literature to notice something odd. When people want to communicate why AI will matter, they reach for the same three historical cases over and over: nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution. It's not just that they cite them; they move between them in the same paragraph as if the lessons were interchangable. The nuclear analogy says the danger is a small number of actors, a sudden break, an arms race, and a catastrophic misuse or accident. The printing press analogy says the real story is the slow, diffuse crumbling of gatekeepers and a change in who gets to make meaning. The Industrial Revolution analogy says nobody at the time understood the upheaval, that the effects ran through labor, energy, and family structure over generations, and that there was no single decision point to fix. These strike me as very different pictures of what a transforming technology is and how its effects arrive.

So here's my question. If this practice of reaching for all three analogies is going to be more than impressive-sounding rhetoric, what philosophy of technology or historical change does it presuppose? Does it, for instance, presuppose that a technology has a stable internal logic that sets its social trajectory, so that we can read the future off of it? Do the three analogies presuppose compatible things, or does the practice only look coherent because its commitments are never written down? And what would actually have to change in how people discuss transformative AI if we rejected the presupposition you take to be doing the real work? I'm less interested in which analogy is the best and more in what the practice is quietly assuming. If the honest answer is that the practice doesn't force any single theory, and several philosophies of technology would permit it, say that too — but then tell me what would distinguish those philosophies in practice.
