I have been defending a form of selective scientific realism, and I would like to have the package audited for internal consistency before I send the draft to my committee. Here are the commitments, roughly as I hold them.

1. The no-miracles argument still carries weight with me: the best explanation for a mature theory's record of novel predictive success is that its central theoretical claims, including claims about unobservable entities and their properties, are approximately true.

2. I also take the pessimistic meta-induction seriously: the caloric theory of heat, phlogiston chemistry, and nineteenth-century ether optics all had significant empirical successes and were nonetheless abandoned. So I should not infer from present-day success that present-day theories as wholes are true.

3. I think selective realism is the right way to live with both of the above. When a successful theory is replaced, the parts that get discarded are mostly the parts that were idle or merely auxiliary; the parts that were actually doing the explanatory and predictive work tend to survive, often embedded in the successor theory. That is why the pessimistic induction is weaker than it looks.

4. I cash out the working-parts idea as indispensability: a part counts as a working part only if the novel predictions could not have been derived without it.

5. In practice, when I go back to the historical cases, I catch myself identifying the working parts by checking what did in fact survive into the successor theory. I do not think this makes (3) trivially true; I want to say it is a substantive empirical discovery that the parts that survived were the parts doing the work, and that things could have turned out otherwise.

6. But I remain an entity realist about the electron and the atom, not merely a structural realist. I do not believe only mathematical structure survives theory change; some of the entities themselves earn their keep.

The package feels stable as a whole, but I am too close to it. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, and which apparent tensions are only apparent? For the genuine tensions, rank them by how serious they are, and for each say what would have to be revised, what the cheapest revision costs, and whether it leaves the overall position something a selective realist could still want.
