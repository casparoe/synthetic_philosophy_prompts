I've been mucking around with something I call "the covenant's shadow." Here's the idea. When people contract out of the state of nature, they do it to escape the war of all against all. Hobbes is clear about that. In Leviathan, chapter 17, he argues that the only way to erect a commonwealth is to confer all power on one man or assembly, and he adds that covenants are just empty words unless there is a power to enforce them. Fair enough. But I've been thinking that the very act of setting up the sovereign creates a weird residue: the holdout. Suppose almost everyone agrees to lay down their right to all things, but one person, or one small group, refuses to covenant. Before the agreement, that refusal is unremarkable — everyone has the right to everything, so no one is specially wronged by someone not joining. But after the commonwealth exists, the holdout is suddenly in a worse position than they were in the state of nature, because now they face a unified power they never consented to. And yet the new sovereign can't punish the holdout for not consenting without already treating them as a subject, which is exactly what they deny being. So the covenant seems to generate its own shadow: a category of person whose non-consent becomes both the justification for the sovereign's force and a threat to its legitimacy. It's like a neighborhood where everyone agrees not to call the cops for minor disputes, and the one family that refuses to join keeps that option open. They benefit from the peace but everyone treats them as a wildcard, and the peace depends on keeping them in line even though they never signed on.

I want to know if this is a real hole in Hobbes's logic or just a way of saying that unanimous consent never actually happens and Hobbes knew that. Does the idea hold up under its strongest reconstruction? Has something like this been worked out before under another name? And where does the concept break down — what cases does it wrongly count as a problem? Please be candid: if it's just a metaphor, say so.
