I have been reading John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic, and there is a section that has been bothering me for weeks. As I understand him, Mill treats the absence of a counteracting cause as itself part of the causal story: a thing can contribute to an effect not by being present, but by being absent, when its presence would have prevented the effect. He calls something like this a negative condition, if I remember. That passage made me try to work out a distinction of my own, and I want to know whether it holds up.

I call the distinction upkeep causation versus intrusion causation. Intrusion causation is what we usually think of first: something new appears or changes and disrupts an otherwise stable arrangement. Upkeep causation is when an arrangement falls apart because some ongoing activity that had been holding it together stops being performed. My claim is that an omission is a cause only as upkeep causation, and only relative to a practice that was actually supposed to be continuing: a maintenance schedule, a standing role, a routine, or a promise. For example, if a caretaker stops watering a greenhouse and the plants die, the caretaker's omission caused their deaths because watering was part of the ongoing activity that kept the greenhouse system from degrading. But if I walk past that same greenhouse, never touch it, and the plants die for lack of water, my not watering them did not cause their deaths, even though my watering them would have prevented the deaths. The difference is that the caretaker's omission interrupts an upkeep practice, while mine is just an absent benefit.

I think this explains why we resist saying that every stranger who could have saved a drowning child caused the child's death, but we are willing to say that a lifeguard on duty caused it by not paying attention. It also predicts that there are no pure omission-causes in nature, apart from a system whose continuation has failed. A rusted bridge collapses because the maintenance crew did not replace a bolt, but the universe's not replacing the bolt is not a cause of the collapse; the omission becomes a cause only when the replacement was part of a going concern.

Is this a real distinction, or am I just restating the familiar idea that omissions require a duty? What does upkeep causation get right, and where does it break? In particular, does it make causation depend on human expectations in a way that makes it unfit for physical explanation? And has someone already worked out this exact idea under a better name? I want an honest assessment, including the strongest objection you can make.
