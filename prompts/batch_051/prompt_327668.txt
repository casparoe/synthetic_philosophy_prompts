You are a professional epistemologist who works on rational acceptance and on the difference between probabilistic and non-probabilistic evidence. I keep returning to an argument I sketched in lecture, trying to convince my epistemology students that the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are really the same problem in different clothes — so that any repair for one has to be a repair for the other. Their has to be a snag somewhere, though, and I'd rather find it before my students do. Go through the argument step by step and tell me exactly which step fails, or whether it actually goes through. I care more about the specific diagnosis than about a final yes/no.

(1) Consider a fair lottery with one million tickets and exactly one winner. For any particular ticket, the probability that it loses is 999,999/1,000,000.

(2) If the probability of a proposition on your evidence is 0.999999, then you are justified in believing that proposition.

(3) So, for each ticket, you are justified in believing that it will lose.

(4) If you are justified in believing P and justified in believing Q, then you are justified in believing P-and-Q.

(5) By applying (4) repeatedly, you are justified in believing that ticket 1 loses and ticket 2 loses and ... and ticket 1,000,000 loses.

(6) But you also know the lottery has exactly one winner, so you are justified in believing that not every ticket loses.

(7) Hence steps (1) through (6) would justify you in believing a contradiction, which is impossible; so at least one of (2) or (4) must be rejected.

(8) Now take an author who has just finished a long, painstaking book. For each sentence in the book, she has checked it against her notes and sources, and suppose her evidence makes it 0.999999 likely that any particular sentence is true.

(9) By the same reasoning as (2) through (5), she is justified in believing the conjunction of all the sentences in the book.

(10) But she also has strong inductive evidence, from having written earlier books, that any book this long contains at least one false sentence. So she is justified in believing that at least one sentence in the new book is false.

(11) Therefore the author is caught in the same contradiction as the lottery player, and whatever step fixes the lottery case must be exactly the step that fixes the preface case.

My real question is whether (11) is earned. Are the two cases genuinely parallel as constructed, or is the author's justification for the individual sentences a different kind of justification than the ticket-holder's? And notice that the author's reason in (10) looks like it might itself rely on a probabilistic rule of the very kind rejected in (7). Does that matter for the comparison? Please number your comments to match my steps and then give a final verdict on the argument as a whole, including whether the standard preface paradox would break at the same place as this simplified version.
