In my upper-level personal identity seminar we spent three weeks on Locke's Essay (Book II, Chapter XXVII) and Reid's "brave officer" objection, and the discussion has gotten weirdly stuck. We started with this line from Locke, early in the chapter: "That which has made the difficulty about this relation has been the little care and attention used in having precise notions of the things to which it is attributed." Everyone took it as a promise that once we distinguished the identity of a man from the identity of a person, the difficulty would dissolve. It did not.

The same two moves now repeat every session. One side says Reid's objection is basically logical: the retired general is the same person as the brave officer via one memory, the brave officer is the same person as the flogged boy via another memory, but the general does not remember being flogged, so Locke's criterion violates the transitivity of identity. On this view the memory criterion is just false for a relation that has to be transitive. The other side says Reid has only shown that "is person-identical with" cannot be defined as *direct* remembered connection. They claim the deeper Lockean idea is that personal identity is constituted by the unity of one consciousness, and that consciousness can extend itself by overlapping chains even when direct memory fails. On this view Reid is attacking a sloppy, empiricist cartoon of Locke.

I don't actually care who wins. I want to know what kind of disagreement this is. Are the two sides reading "the memory criterion" as two different claims, one direct and one chained? Are they disagreeing about whether personal identity must be transitive at all, or about what counts as a single consciousness, or about what Locke actually meant? Is this a substantive metaphysical fight, a verbal ambiguity, or an interpretive dispute? And given your diagnosis, what kind of distinction or reformulation would actually unstick it?
