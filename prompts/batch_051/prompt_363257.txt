Suppose I'm advising a small foundation that can fund, say, five philosophers for two years to work on complicity — specifically, the ways you can participate in wrongdoing without actually causing it. I'm less interested in a survey than in a defended ranking: which open problems should they go after first, and why? For each one, tell me why it's upstream of other questions, how tractable it is for people doing conceptual work rather than lawyers or social scientists, and what developments over the next couple years would make you move it up or down your list. I want the reasoning, not just a list.

The mess I keep circling around is this: a getaway driver whose car never gets used, an accountant who files routine paperwork for a fraudulent company, a person who buys from a sweatshop or keeps flying while knowing what aviation does to the climate, a voter who backs a candidate they know will do awful things, a bystander who says nothing. All of these feel like complicity, but the clean causal story runs out — in many of them the wrong would of happened anyway, or the contribution is too diffuse to trace. Some people think complicity needs a difference-making condition; others think you can be complicit just by being part of a joint enterprise, or by benefiting, or by authorizing or ratifying the wrong. I especially want you to lean into that last cluster — complicity as changing what's socially legitimate, or changing what the wrongdoer can take as permission, rather than changing what happens. That feels undertheorized compared to the causal stuff, but maybe I'm wrong that it's the thing to fund.

So: which questions about complicity-without-causation are worth two years of a philosopher's time, and which aren't? Rank them and defend the ranking. I'm asking because I want to spend this money well, not because I want an encyclopedia entry.
