I’ve been reporting on a few research fields where you see the same groups of researchers defending roughly the same core assumptions for decades, and I keep hearing people invoke Lakatos in arguments about whether a field is dying or just difficult. The usual line is that a Lakatosian research programme has a hard core that isn’t directly tested, wrapped in a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that get adjusted to absorb anomalies. A programme is progressive when those adjustments generate new predictions, some of which get confirmed; it degenerates when the adjustments are mostly after-the-fact accommodations of already known results. But I’ve found that in practice people use “degenerating research programme” more as an insult than as an actual diagnosis, and my sense is that Lakatos himself was more tolerant of temporary mess than the cliché version suggests.

Please give me the most important implications of Lakatos’s view for how a science journalist should assess a specific research agenda, lab, or field. I’m especially interested in whether it implies we should be more patient with programmes that are mostly dealing with anomalies, or more willing to call time on them; whether it gives any usable forward-looking criterion for telling when a programme has crossed from “stuck” to “degenerating,” or whether the judgement only works retrospectively; and what it implies about scientists who abandon a programme for a rival one that looks worse on short-run predictive success. Also, since I’d like to know how hard to lean on your answer: tell me how confident you are in your reading of Lakatos here, and single out the point you’re least sure of.
