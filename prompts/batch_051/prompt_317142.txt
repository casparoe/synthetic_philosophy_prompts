A colleague of mine maintains that the common-kind assumption in the philosophy of perception has a testable core, and that the argument from illusion—in the strong form that moves through a subjectively perfect hallucination—owes its force entirely to that core. As she formulates the testable claim: for any veridical perceptual experience, a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination with the same content could in principle be produced by replicating the very same internal neural state while the external object is absent, and because the two states are indistinguishable to the subject and identical in the brain, they are tokens of a single fundamental kind of mental event. The external object is, on this picture, merely a causal trigger for the state that does the experiential work. A disjunctivist rejects the central step: veridical perception is constitutively a relation to the mind-independent object itself, and no hallucination, however subjectively similar, is the same fundamental kind of state.

I am not asking whether the argument from illusion is valid or whether disjunctivism is true. I want to know whether this specific claim can be brought under experimental pressure. Design the strongest test you can. Give me the manipulation, the measurement, and the comparison between conditions, drawing only on methods available in current perceptual neuroscience—for example, psychophysics, fMRI, invasive electrophysiology, or direct cortical stimulation. Then tell me which outcome would favor the common-kind view and which would favor disjunctivism, and be precise about what even a clean result would fail to settle. In particular, address whether showing that the two conditions realize the same neural state in every measurable respect would refute disjunctivism, whether showing a reliable neural difference would refute the common-kind assumption, and whether the metaphysical residue of the disagreement survives either outcome.
