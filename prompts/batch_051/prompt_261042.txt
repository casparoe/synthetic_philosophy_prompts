The statue and the clay debate has always struck me as weirdly stuck, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. You know the setup: a lump of clay gets shaped into a statue. Before the shaping, there's clay and no statue. Squash the thing and the statue is destroyed but the clay survives, just as a lump again. So the clay and the statue seem to differ in their histories and in what would destroy them. But they also occupy exactly the same place, have exactly the same parts, same shape, same atoms. The pluralists (call them constitutionalists) say there are two things there that coincide: the statue is constituted by the clay but not identical to it. The monists say there's just one thing, the clay, and 'statue' is just a way of describing it while it's statue-shaped. The debate has run for decades and nobody seems to move; each side has a stock reply to the others moves. So my question is about the structure of the disagreement, not about who's right. Is this a substantive disagreement about how many objects exist on the pedestal, or is it verbal — a fight about what 'object' means or about how to count? Do the sides differ about the facts, about principles, or about a starting intuition neither one can argue for? I keep hearing that the pluralist is stuck with the grounding problem (if the statue and the clay share all their parts and arrangement, what makes them different, and where does the difference come from?), and I want to know if that's the real engine of the stalemate or just the pluralist's problem and not the debate's. Or is 'how many things are there' just an ill-posed question in this case, so the whole dispute is a mistake? My hunch, presented as a hunch, is that an awful lot of the argument is counting conventions dressed up in metaphysical costume, but I can't tell weather that's a real diagnosis or me being lazy. Give me your read on what would actually count as progress if your diagnosis is right — a principle for counting, a semantic distinction, a formal result, or just dropping the question — and say straight out if you think no progress should be expected. And when you answer, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of.
