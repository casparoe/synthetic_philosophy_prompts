Draft the joint statement that two linguists could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about whether grammatical categories are natural kinds. One is a category realist: she holds that at least some parts of speech and case categories—say noun and verb, or dative case—correspond to natural kinds, stable cross-linguistic clusters of morphosyntactic and semantic properties constrained by human cognition. The other is a category conventionalist: he holds that categories like these are language-particular descriptive conveniences, or at most family-resemblance clusters that typologists project onto languages, with no shared cross-linguistic essence. Anchor the statement in the concrete case of the category 'adjective.' Both sides must live with the fact that some languages appear to lack a distinct adjective class, and that in many languages adjective-like words split between noun-like and verb-like behavior. Structure the statement as follows, in order:

1. Agreed claims — a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including specific claims about the adjective facts and about what a single language can and cannot settle; roughly 200 words.
2. The exact disagreement — no more than 100 words, with each position phrased in wording its own side would accept.
3. Cruxes — two to four sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side; one sentence each.
4. Tests and predictions — for each crux, a case, corpus pattern, or psycholinguistic result both sides accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; format as a table with columns: Crux, Test, Realist predicts, Conventionalist predicts.

Do not include a bibliography or citations; make the document work through the case and the arguments alone.
