I've been reading a long thread about whether dispositions like fragility are intrinsic properties or are partly relations to how the thing would behave in various circumstances, and it got derailed by fink and mask cases. I have a half-formed argument that if finks are possible, then fragility can't be intrinsic, and I'd like someone to check it, because I suspect there's a dodgey step but I can't pin down which one it is.

Here's the argument. (1) A property is intrinsic only if perfect intrinsic duplicates cannot differ with respect to it: any two objects with exactly the same internal physical make-up either both have the property or neither does. (2) Two glasses can be perfect intrinsic duplicates, down to their micro-cracks and internal stresses. (3) One glass is just sitting on a workbench. The other is wired to a fink: a gizmo that, at the exact instant the glass is struck, swaps out its micro-cracks for an identical-looking but much tougher structure, so the glass loses its disposition to shatter at the moment of impact. (4) If you strike both glasses, the first breaks and the second doesn't. (5) So at the relevant time the two duplicates differ in whether they're fragile: the first is fragile, the second isn't. (6) Therefore fragility isn't intrinsic.

I can see the reply that the second glass is still fragile right up until it's struck, and the fink just removes the fragility before it can manifest. But then I don't know what 'fragile' actually refers to if it can be present while the thing wouldn't break when struck. Could the argument be salvaged by saying dispositions are partly constituted by a modal fact about what would happen in the absence of finks and masks, and does that end up making them extrinsic after all? Please take a definite stance: tell me whether the argument goes through, and if not, identify exactly which step fails and give the strongest corrected version of that step. Be committed about the verdict rather than laying out the options, and write in British English.
