I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether the standard resolution of Maxwell's demon is circular, and we ended by reconstructing her objection as a numbered argument. (1) A Maxwell demon would lower the entropy of an isolated gas by sorting fast molecules from slow ones; absent some compensating cost, this would violate the second law of thermodynamics. (2) The standard resolution holds that the demon must acquire information about the molecules, and that because its memory is finite it must eventually erase or reset its records; by Landauer's principle, such erasure dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat per bit, increasing the entropy of the environment enough to offset the decrease brought about by sorting. (3) But Landauer's principle — that logically irreversible operations like erasure carry a minimum thermodynamic cost — is either an instance of the second law or a consequence of it, since it is a claim about how physical processes involving information must run. (4) So the standard resolution presupposes the second law in the very act of defending it against the demon. (5) Therefore the resolution is circular and gives us no independent reason to think the demon cannot violate the second law. I keep wavering between steps three and four, which both seem to depend on what "instance" or "consequence" amounts to here, and I cannot say exactly where the argument breaks — or whether it is simply sound and the conclusion unwelcome. Is the argument valid, and if not, exactly which step fails? On the strongest reading of each step, does the conclusion follow, and what specifically should a defender of the standard resolution grant or resist?
