I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we've spent two weeks going in circles on the original vs. derived intentionality distinction. Searle's line, roughly, is that your beliefs and desires mean things in their own right (original intentionality), while a sentence in a book or a symbol in a computer only means something because we lend it meaning (derived intentionality). And he uses the Chinese Room to pump the intuition that no amount of symbol-shuffling can turn derived into original. But Dennett has this deflationary move I can't shake: he points to a humble vending machine that only accepts quarters, his old two-bitser example, and says that once we're willing to say the machine recognizes a quarter or wants a quarter in a loose derived sense, we've already granted that intentionality comes in degrees and can be built. Then he pushes it further: what makes the meaning in my head original rather than just derived from the slow, blind engineering of natural selection? Darwin ends up looking like a patient engineer, and my beliefs start to look no less borrowed than the machine's thirst for quarters — just a lot more complicated.

Please argue that the original/derived distinction can't do the work Searle needs it to do. I want your strongest version of the argument: once you grant that derived intentionality is real even if borrowed, the demand for an original source of meaning either regresses, bottoms out in mystery, or quietly collapses into complexity. Don't give me a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the reasoning. And be upfront about the costs — at the end, say what the view commits you to (does a thermostat have a sliver of intentionality? is there anything left of understanding that a machine couldn't in principle have?).
