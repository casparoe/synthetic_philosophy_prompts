I keep using a distinction in a paper I'm drafting and want it stress-tested before I put any weight on it. I call them "transcript honesty" and "affordance honesty." A system is transcript-honest if every assertion in the transcript is something a competent speaker would say is true, or at least not knowingly false. A system is affordance-honest if a reasonable user, given how the system presents itself—the confidence phrasing, hedging, formatting, what it refuses to mention, how it frames disagreement—wouldn't come away with a false belief or an overconfident one, even when every sentence in the transcript is true. My claim is that most of what gets called AI deception is really affordance dishonesty, and that the usual "honesty means not saying false things" definitions are still only tracking the first kind. Example: a health assistant that says "the evidence for this supplement is weak," then lists three solid clinical trials and twelve industry-funded junk studies with identical formatting and no signal of quality. Every sentence is true; the impression is false. Am I just reinventing "manipulation" or "bullshit" with extra steps? And does affordance honesty hold up as a property of a system, or does it collapse once "reasonable user" varies by context and person? Also, tell me how confident you are in what you claim and which point you're least sure of.
