Passage from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, Chapter 1, translated by W. D. Ross:

Again, it is from the same causes and by the same means that every virtue is both produced and destroyed, and similarly every art; for it is from playing the lyre that both good and bad lyre-players are produced. And the corresponding statement is true of builders and of all the rest; men will be good or bad builders as a result of building well or badly. For if this were not so, there would have been no need of a teacher, but all men would have been born good or bad at their craft. This, then, is the case with the virtues also; by doing the acts that we do in our transactions with other men we become just or unjust, and by doing the acts that we do in the presence of danger, and being habituated to feel fear or confidence, we become brave or cowardly.

Question:

Explain how this passage underwrites Aristotle’s account of the acquisition of moral character, and evaluate whether that account can survive the situationist critique. Your answer should state what the situationist critique claims, identify which part of Aristotle’s view it most threatens, and offer the strongest defense available to Aristotle.

Student answer:

Aristotle is arguing here that moral virtue is not innate, nor is it acquired by study alone. It is a matter of habituation: just as one becomes a lyre-player by playing the lyre, or a builder by building, one becomes just by acting justly, courageous by acting courageously, and so on. The training of habits, especially from youth, is central, and Aristotle points out that lawmakers rely on this fact when they try to make citizens good. The situationist critique, developed by John Doris and Gilbert Harman, challenges this picture empirically. Harman cites studies like the Princeton Theological Seminary experiment, in which students preparing to give a talk on the Good Samaritan helped a man in apparent distress at rates best predicted by how late they thought they were, rather than by their religious or moral beliefs. Milgram’s obedience experiments and bystander-effect studies make the same point: minor features of the immediate situation swamp the effects of broad character dispositions. Doris concludes from these results that there are no character traits at all, either global or local. Since Aristotle claims that people acquire stable virtues through repeated action, these experiments directly refute him: if a person fails to help once because she is in a hurry, that shows the stable trait was never really there, because a genuine Aristotelian habit would have been too strong for the situation to override. The best Aristotelian defense is to say that the experiments show what most people are like, whereas Aristotle is describing the rare phronimos, the person of practical wisdom whose virtue is not mere habitual reaction but an intellectual and motivational commitment. If virtue is rare enough, the failure of most experimental subjects leaves Aristotle’s ideal untouched.
