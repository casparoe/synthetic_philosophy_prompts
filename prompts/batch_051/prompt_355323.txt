I am enrolled in a course on moral philosophy, and we have been discussing Taurek's claim that the bare numbers do not count. The view appeals to me for structural reasons. If I must choose between saving A and saving B, I am weighing A's claim against B's claim; if one side now has five strangers, no single person's claim has been made larger or weightier by the addition, so the numbers seem to provide only one reason of the same kind repeated five times over rather than a single stronger reason. The theory treats persons as separate in a way that aggregative reasoning does not, and it gives a tidy account of why I need not simply maximize the number of lives saved.

At the same time, my strongest intuition in such cases is that of course I should save the five rather than the one, and that someone who saved the one would not merely have exercised a permission but would have failed to do what matters most. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended, and whether there is a reading of "the numbers count" on which both the separateness of persons and the five-over-one verdict survive.
