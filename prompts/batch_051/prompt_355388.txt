I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending a Bayesian version of the doomsday argument. His central commitments are, first, that we may treat our own position in the temporal sequence of human lives as if it were randomly sampled from the set of all humans who will ever have lived, and second, that finding ourselves around the hundred-billionth human should therefore push our credence toward smaller total populations and away from futures in which trillions of humans eventually live. He also holds that this shift survives the familiar worries that one cannot legitimately treat oneself as randomly sampled and that the conclusion depends on the prior one brings to the table.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for him, with a specific written follow-up for each, that go well beyond asking him to restate the argument. I would like the questions to pressure the view the way its most careful critics do. In particular, make sure the set includes at least one question on each of the following, with the follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give: (i) the self-sampling assumption itself — why the fact that we are alive now rather than among a much larger set of future people is evidence for anything at all; (ii) the reference-class problem — what makes the relevant reference class humans, observers, or something else, and whether that choice is doing the argumentative work in advance; (iii) the self-indication reply — that one's own existence is itself evidence for a large total class of observers — and how much of the doomsday shift it cancels; (iv) the objection that the argument's force is an artifact of a prior that already excludes large futures; (v) the practical upshot — whether the argument changes decisions about reducing extinction risk or instead predicts that such efforts will fail, and whether the distinction between doom soon and doom eventually matters for action; and (vi) what evidence or argument he would count as genuinely weakening the view. For at least three of these questions, the follow-up should be written so that it anticipates the specific defensive move the guest is most likely to make — such as insisting that the reference class is fixed by our scientific interests, that the conclusion survives a wide family of priors, or that the argument only lowers a distribution rather than predicting a date — and asks him to say what makes that move more than ad hoc.
