My reading group has spent a year arguing about whether rationality can be trained the way a martial art is trained. One camp—call them the trainability camp—holds that the core of good reasoning is a set of domain-general procedures: assigning numerical probabilities to predictions and keeping track of how they fare, noticing when one is rationalizing instead of reasoning, making Fermi estimates, separating the inside view from the outside view, and precommitting to decision criteria before seeing the evidence. They think these can be drilled in simplified, decontextualized exercises the way forms are drilled in a martial art, and that the drilled movements should later appear unbidden under the unstructured conditions of real decisions. The other camp—call them the context-bound camp—holds that reasoning is mostly content-bound: what looks like a generalized skill is really domain knowledge plus feedback in the target environment, and abstract drills produce at best near transfer to closely related puzzle-like tasks while leaving real-world judgment largely unchanged. Draft the joint statement this pair could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Begin with a numbered list of claims both camps accept—about human fallibility, about what existing training studies do and do not establish, about what would count as genuine transfer, and about any concession each side already grants the other. Then format the remaining disagreements as a table, with each difference stated precisely and neutrally, and with each camp's own position rendered in wording that camp would accept. Then list the cruxes: the sub-questions on which the dispute turns, such that a reliable answer would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete study, argument, or case both camps would agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. I suspect one crux is whether improvements on a trained exercise transfer to a novel high-stakes setting once the scaffolding of the exercise is removed, and another is whether the relevant improvements are procedural rather than a form of acquired content knowledge; use these only insofar as the two camps would accept them as cruxes. Finally, identify any part of the dispute that is merely verbal once key terms are pinned down, and say which terms would need to be defined for that to become clear.
