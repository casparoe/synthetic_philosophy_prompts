A few months ago, after a close friend's death made the question stop feeling hypothetical, I completed the paperwork and arranged the life-insurance funding for cryopreservation. Since then I have found myself defending a fairly specific view: that for someone with my values and financial situation, signing up is rational in expectation. I think of it as a low-probability, high-payoff bet on some future capacity to revive me — a modest annual cost now in exchange for a non-negligible chance at a very large amount of additional life later. I do not claim revival is likely; I claim the wager is good. The distinction matters to me, because nearly everyone I talk to attacks the probability, and I keep noticing that I can lower the probability substantially and the arithmetic still comes out ahead. That is starting to feel less like a strength of the argument and more like a warning sign about it.

I want to stress-test the position properly rather than defend it. What would actually count against the claim that this is a rational bet? Please sort the defeaters, from decisive to merely awkward, and tell me which of them a committed defender of the bet should concede genuinely count against it. I am especially interested in four regions. First, the revival and identity assumptions: what exactly has to be true about personal survival for the payoff to be attributable to me, and what evidence could show that it will not be. Second, the structure of the wager itself: whether reasoning of this "small cost, astronomically large payoff" form is vulnerable to a general objection about absurd high-payoff bets, and how one would tell a defensible long shot apart from a trick of the arithmetic. Third, the opportunity costs and the outside view — how this looks from a base-rate perspective, compared with other long-shot bets people actually make — whether the money and attention would be better directed at life-extension research, digital preservation, or ordinary present goods, and what would show that it would. Fourth, the institutional side: whether the relevant organizations and social conditions can plausibly persist long enough, and what would count as evidence that they cannot. Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether this position has been quietly insulated so that no conceivable outcome could refute it — and if so, whether that insulation is a fatal problem for a one-shot existential wager, or just the normal condition of this kind of bet.
