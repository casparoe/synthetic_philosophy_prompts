I teach an intermediate critical thinking seminar — about 18 undergrads, one 75-minute block, a lot of them think they already know what a good argument looks like. The next unit is on whether treating argumentation as a competitive sport (scoreboard, winner, points for moves, rankings) would make people reason better or just make them better at scoring. I don't want to lecture this: I want them to feel what a scoreboard does to there reasoning before we ever name the question. Design a runnable in-class game for that session. It should have two rounds that are otherwise identical in task, but one is scored like a sport and the other is deliberately unscored and collaborative, so the same students can feel what changes when the scoreboard arrives. The scored round should viscerally produce both the good side of competition (sharper objections, faster thinking, people actually prepare because they don't want to lose) and the pathology (people stop conceding clear points, defend assigned positions they don't actually hold, the scoring rubric starts to replace good reasons as the thing people optimize for). Give me the rules, materials, a point scheme that doesn't need real money, a 75-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the exact moments back to the question "would making argumentation a sport improve it?" Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce about real competitive argument: real stakes, rankings that outlast the session, audiences, money, and the fact that in actual sport the score is the point rather than just an exercise.
