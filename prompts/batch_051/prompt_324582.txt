A psychiatrist colleague has spent the past year arguing, in our philosophy of psychiatry seminar, that at least some monothematic delusions are not beliefs. Take Capgras: a patient will tell you, with apparent sincerity, that his wife has been replaced by an impostor, yet the patient will not act on the claim, will not draw even the obvious inferences it would ordinarily license, and often shows none of the affect that such a conviction would bring with it. On the two-factor account, one neuropsychological anomaly produces the impostor content, and a second deficit in belief evaluation keeps it from being rejected; my colleague reads the second factor as evidence that the delusion is not a belief at all, but something functionally closer to an imagining or a merely verbal endorsement. I suspect the doxastic/anti-doxastic dispute has run ahead of any agreed way of measuring belief. I want an honest answer to this: can the claim that a delusional report expresses a belief be brought under empirical test? Design the strongest test you can, specify which results would favor each side, and then say precisely what the test would and would not settle — in particular, whether the remaining disagreement would be empirical or merely a decision about how much of the functional profile of belief one insists on before granting the word.
