I spent most of my working life in the natural sciences, where one learned not to choose between competing models before writing down, independently of any model, the phenomena a model must reproduce. I have recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, and Bertrand Russell's "On Denoting" (1905) convinced me that I need the same kind of specification for the theory of definite descriptions before I read further in the secondary literature. The passage that convinced me is early in the article, where Russell writes:

"The subject of denoting is of very great importance, not only in logic and mathematics, but also in the theory of knowledge. For example, we know that the center of mass of the solar system at a definite instant is some definite point, and we can affirm a number of propositions about it; but we have no immediate acquaintance with this point, which is only known to us by description. The distinction between acquaintance and knowledge about is the distinction between the things we have presentations of, and the things we only reach by means of denoting phrases."

A little later he adds:

"All thinking has to start from acquaintance; but it succeeds in thinking about many things with which we have no acquaintance."

I read this as the claim that a phrase can reach something in thought even though no one has direct acquaintance with it, and that any adequate theory of denoting must explain how that is possible. I would like that treated as a requirement in the specification, and I would like to know whether it is as non-negotiable as Russell appears to think.

With that fixed purpose in mind — a theory adequate to definite descriptions as they occur in scientific and mathematical writing and in ordinary factual assertion, not one tailored mainly to conversational looseness — write the desiderata. For each requirement, state it explicitly, give the case that forces it (tying it to Russell's own examples where you can), and mark it as non-negotiable or as a demand peculiar to one philosophical camp. Cover at least the following: descriptions that denote nothing; the uniqueness carried by "the," and whether it is asserted or merely presupposed; true negative existentials of the form "the F does not exist"; scope ambiguities between a description and negation; the divergence between surface grammar and what is asserted; the possibility, illustrated in the quoted passage, of thinking and speaking about an object known only by description; and the behavior of descriptions inside reports of what someone believed or wanted to know. Then tell me which pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most to eliminate positions that would otherwise seem attractive. The deliverable is the list of requirements and their motivating cases; if you score Frege, Russell, or Strawson against the list, keep that to a short closing paragraph.
