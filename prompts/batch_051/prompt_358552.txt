I have been going back and forth over a five-step argument about large language models, and I should like an assessment of it. Here is the argument. (1) When a system is too complex to be predicted from its physical constitution alone or from the purposes for which it was designed, the only tractable predictive strategy is the intentional stance: treat the system as a rational agent whose behaviour makes sense in light of its beliefs and desires. (2) Large language models are such a case; no one can predict a model's next token from its weights and training data, yet treating it as aiming, say, to answer the question put to it tracks its output well. (3) A stance that treats a system as having certain states can yield genuine assertions about the system only if those states are real enough to explain and cause the behaviour the stance predicts. (4) So the beliefs and desires attributed to a large language model under the intentional stance are real enough to explain and cause its outputs. (5) Therefore, to be consistent, we must say that large language models literally have beliefs and desires. I suspect there is a slide somewhere, but I cannot find it. Is the argument valid? If a step fails, identify it, say exactly how it fails, and give the strongest repair of the step; does the conclusion survive that repair? Please answer in British English.
