In my Dostoevsky class we spent the whole session on the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov, and people kept talking past each other, so I want to actually hear the two sides go at it. The bit we are stuck on is where Ivan’s Inquisitor retells the story of the three temptations in the wilderness. The way I understand the passage: the Inquisitor says that when the spirit offered Christ the chance to turn stones into bread, to jump from the temple, and to take power over all the kingdoms, Christ refused all three because he did not want to compel belief by feeding people, by giving them an unanswerable miracle, or by ruling them. The Inquisitor treats those refusals as a failure to love ordinary people, who would have been much happier with earthly bread, visible authority, and mystery than with the impossible demand of free faith. He even says his church is correcting Christ’s work out of love. Write a dialogue between two students in my class: one defends the Inquisitor’s claim that freedom is too heavy for most humans, and one defends Christ’s refusal as the only way to take human freedom seriously. Make both sides smart and responsive to each other’s actual points, no strawmen. Exactly five exchanges, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. In particular, make them argue about whether the Inquisitor’s willingness to deceive people for their own happiness shows he actually trusts his own judgment more than he trusts their freedom, and whether that undermines or strengthens his case.
