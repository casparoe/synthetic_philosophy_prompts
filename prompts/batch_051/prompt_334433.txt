Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing in myself and everyone around me. People will insist that you really should vote—that staying home is a quiet failure of citizenship—even though there is no realistic elction where one vote flips the outcome, and in a safe seat not even that. But those same people, sometimes in the same conversation, will shrug off buying meat or taking a flight by saying one person's choice doesn't make any difference to the industry or to the climate. Both are cases where a single individual contributes something negligible to a gigantic aggregate. My best guess at a justification is that elections have a sharp threshold—the thing tips at exactly fifty percent plus one—while meat and emissions are smooth harms where each purchase just adds a tiny speck. But I can't see why that should matter: expected-value reasoning seems to treat the two cases the same, and if anything it treats the smooth cases as worse, because there the harm actually occurs bit by bit rather than being all-or-nothing. So is there a real principle here that lets you keep the voting-verdict and the meat-and-flight verdict at once, or is the asymmetry just a bias we live with and shouldn't pretend to justify? You're a philosopher who works on collective action and the inefficacy objection. Spend a while brainstorming candidate principles before you pick one, and then tell me which of my two ordinary judgments, if either, should survive.
