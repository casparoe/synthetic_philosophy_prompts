Two friends of mine recently spent an evening arguing over a thought experiment, and they never reached an agreement. The setup was this. Suppose a machine existed that could, given any painting, produce a duplicate that is physically indistinguishable from the original: the same pigment molecules arranged in the same way, the same canvas weave, the same fine network of cracks and wear, the same varnish, down to the last relevant detail. Assume that once the duplicate is made, no test available to science can tell the two canvases apart. The question was whether the duplicate would be just as good a work of art as the original, or in some way inferior.

Elena argued that the duplicate would be just as good. Two objects that are physically indistinguishable, she reasoned, cannot differ in any property that matters to someone who is not merely buying a name. If standing before the one and standing before the other give you the same composition, the same light, the same texture, and the same feeling, then preferring the original is snobbery about a label.

Ravi replied that a work of art is not identical with its material substrate. It is an achievement: this person made this thing at this time, with these intentions and under these constraints. The duplicate, however exact, is not that achievement, so it is a different work and a less valuable one.

Elena answered that the duplicate carries the same marks and therefore the same visible evidence of every decision the artist is supposed to have made. Unless we can see history with the naked eye, she said, the artist's decisions are not part of what anyone appreciates in front of the canvas. Ravi said we do see the history, in roughly the way we see the canvas as a painting rather than as a random pattern: we see it under a category, and the object's origin partly fixes the category. A forgery and a genuine painting, he said, may be perceptually indistinguishable and still be aesthetically different.

Elena granted the point for the sake of argument but said it proves too much. If newly learned facts about the past change the aesthetic object, then the work changes every time we learn who used to own it or whether the painter had a cold while working. What separates authorship from the rest of the biography? Ravi drew the line at creation. Authorship is partly constitutive of what the work is, he said, whereas ownership and the painter's moods are merely biographical facts that stand outside the work. The duplicate differs from the original precisely in how it came to be made.

Elena then proposed the museum test. Hang the duplicate without a label, she said, and every visitor has the same visual experience and the same response; the duplicate is therefore doing everything we ask a painting to do, and the only loss when the label is corrected is financial. Ravi replied that the visitors' experiences are not the same in content, because a viewer's appreciation is shaped by the history she reasonably attributes to the object. Learning that one saw a duplicate, he said, can rationally retrograde the experience, and not only for reasons of market value.

Elena pushed further. Suppose the machine had existed in the painter's own lifetime, and one of the two canvases came from the easel while the other came from the machine. Suppose also that no surviving record and no test can now determine which is which. Then Ravi's view makes the value of the better work depend on a fact that is in principle undetectable, and art appreciation becomes a kind of archaeology of hidden facts. Ravi answered that this is not a reductio: much of what we value depends on facts we cannot now recover, such as whether a letter was genuinely written by the person who signed it. Aesthetic value may outrun our evidence.

Elena concluded that Ravi had thereby admitted that the value difference is not in the canvas but arises entirely from relational facts about its history; the canvas itself is the same either way. Ravi accepted this. The work of art, he said, just is the canvas together with its history, so the two canvases are two different works even when they are physically identical. Elena replied that if that is so, then "the work" no longer picks out the physical object in front of her, and two indistinguishable canvases count as different works, which she finds too high a price.

I have not said what I think, but the exchange has stayed with me. Please go through the disagreement move by move and say, for each claim, whether it is sound, overstated, or question-begging, and who has the better of that particular step. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and whether the apparent disagreement dissolves once one distinguishes the physical object from the work, aesthetic value from market value, and appreciation from knowledge. Please write your answer in American English.
