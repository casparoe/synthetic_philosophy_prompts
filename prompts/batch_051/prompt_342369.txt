A friend and I just spent a long argument about whether a Maxwell's demon could run a perpetual-motion machine if it only recorded its measurements and never erased its notes; I would like a piece of sustained irony to settle the matter.

Write a deadpan manufacturer's safety-recall and compliance bulletin for a fictional "Demon™" temperature-difference engine whose controller (the demon) was shipped with firmware that let it accumulate measurement records indefinitely without resetting. The bulletin should be about 650 words, formatted with sections such as "Product description," "Reason for recall," "Affected units," and "Required remedy" (you may add sections), and written in the voice of a corporate or regulatory compliance office.

The humor should come from getting the physics right, not from winking. The bulletin should quietly take positions on: the entropy cost of erasing information (the kT ln 2 bound per bit); the point that acquiring information can in principle be done reversibly, so measurement alone is not where the second law reasserts itself; and the claim that an un-erased record merely relocates entropy into the demon's memory rather than making it disappear. It should be legible to someone who only knows the demon from a textbook, but a specialist should recognize that the satirical target is the practice of declaring the demon "impossible" without specifying what would have to be true to close a cycle. Do not attribute the jokes to named real researchers; invented agencies, models, and officials are fine.

After the bulletin, add a brief editorial note, not part of the parody, stating which part of the Landauer–Bennett reading the piece leans on and what experimental or theoretical result would make the satire unfair (for example, a credible demonstration of erasure below the Landauer bound, or a demon cycle that returns its memory to its initial state with no net dissipation). End, after the note, with exactly one sentence summarizing the satirical target on its own line beginning "Summary:".
