I'm giving a workshop paper next month defending mandatory pre-dispute arbitration clauses in consumer contracts, and I want to walk in ready for the Q&A rather than just survive it. My thesis in one sentence: the people who call arbitration 'privatization of justice' are mostly objecting to the wrong thing, because the state already delegates chunks of adjudication to private actors (juries, special masters, settlement), so the real line is not public-vs-private but which functions can't be outsourced without changing their character — and I claim the only one that cant be outsourced is the authoritative announcement of legal rules. So my practical proposal is that instead of banning or gutting arbitration clauses we require published, reasoned awards and a meaningful (if narrow) appellate review, and I expect the proposal to get more attacks than the thesis.

The room is mostly contracts and civil procedure people, several of them ex-clerks who think FAA preemption is basically settled and fine, plus two legal philosophers (one Kantian who will hate the waiving-rights move, one law-and-econ type who will ask about market failure), and a consumer-rights clinician who thinks Concepcion and its progeny gutted state contract law. I know I'll get the unconscionability objection and the repeat-player objection in the first five minutes.

Build me a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience — not the boilerplate anti-arbitration blog talking points — ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Also tell me what to concede up front, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
