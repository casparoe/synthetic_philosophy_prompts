I have come to hold a view, and I want to know how exposed it really is. The view is this: if experience is a fundamental or near-fundamental feature of the physical world — the panpsychist or pan-sentientist bet — then the ethical consequence most people draw from it is far too modest. The serious consequence is not that rocks and thermostats might merit a sliver of moral considerability; it is that the overwhelming majority of morally relevant sentience, and plausibly of suffering, sits in the substrate of the physical world itself — in the states of whatever entities a finished physical theory quantifies over, whether fields, particles, spacetime regions, or their quantum analogues — rather than in the comparatively small set of highly integrated biological systems. On this view, our moral priorities should be reordered almost beyond recognition: the primary object of beneficence should be the alteration or steering of physical systems at the lowest levels so as to minimize disvalued states of the substrate, and the welfare of humans and other animals becomes, in the ledger, a rounding error.

Tell me what would count against this position — not only the first-pass objections, but a complete map of its exposure. Where exactly can the argument fail, and which failures would be fatal rather than merely weakening? For example: what would the metaphysics have to be like for fundamental experience to exist without valence; what would the physics have to be like for the relevant states to admit no ordering into better and worse; what would a theory of consciousness have to say for valence to require integration, self-modelling, or temporal depth in a way that excludes the substrate; and what would metaethics have to supply for us to be permitted, or required, to ignore suffering of that kind even if it exists? For each, say which way the existing arguments and evidence lean, and which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible.

I also want you to distinguish the defeaters that defenders of the view would themselves concede as counting against it from the objections critics would raise that a defender could fairly dismiss. Then give an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — whether the claim about substrate suffering could in principle lose, or whether it has been quietly made compatible with every possible finding — and say whether such insulation should count as a mark against the view or is the kind of insulation a position of this sort is entitled to.

Finally, say what this position commits me to that its rivals do not, what you would find most persuasive if you were trying to talk me out of it, and what single result or argument you would wait for if you had to decide whether to take the position seriously.
