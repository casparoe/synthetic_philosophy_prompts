In the first lecture of my epistemology course, the professor handed out a one-page account of the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimony, and something about it has been nagging at me. Here is the substance of the handout, as I understand it.

Hume is the reductionist: he denies that testimony is a basic source of justification. On his view, we are justified in trusting what people tell us only through induction from our own experience. I have to have observed, in my own case, a sufficiently regular correlation between reports and the facts reported before any particular report can count for me as evidence. Testimony, for Hume, earns its authority the way any other causal inference does: through repeated observation of the relevant constant conjunction.

Reid is the anti-reductionist. He argues that this cannot be right even as a description of how our minds work, since young children believe what they are told long before they have any inductive track record, and adults routinely trust strangers about names, places, and times without first collecting data on their reliability. So testimony must be an original source of evidence, on a par with perception and memory: we are by default justified in accepting what we are told unless we have positive reason not to.

The handout then concluded that the child case is a decisive refutation of reductionism, because reductionism makes a psychological demand that nobody has ever met; it added that contemporary work on the truth-default — our being wired to believe others absent a reason not to — confirms that Reid was right not merely about the mind but about the epistemology; and it closed by saying reductionism is now mostly a historical curiosity.

I got curious enough to look up the passage the handout rested on. It is from Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X ("Of Miracles"), Part I. He writes: "This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses."

I am inclined to think Reid has the better of the argument, but I cannot shake the sense that the handout is being too quick, and that what it extracts from Hume is not quite what this passage says. Could you audit the handout for me? Specifically: which parts of the summary are faithful to the two positions, and which parts subtly distort them; whether the child case actually refutes reductionism, and whether the truth-default findings establish the epistemological conclusion they are used to establish; and what a careful defender of reductionism would say in response. In short, what in this handout should I correct or hedge before I rely on it?
