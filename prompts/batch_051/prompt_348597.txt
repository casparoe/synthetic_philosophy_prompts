I've been trying to figure out what fairness even is, so I wrote down my gut reactions to a bunch of made-up cases and now I can't find the rule behind them.

- Al and Bo have the same job and same pay. Al has a bad back and spends $500 a month on treatment just to get around. I think fairness means Al gets extra money, even though that makes the incomes unequal.
- Cal and Dee have the same job and pay, and both are healthy. Cal is happy drinking cheap beer. Dee is honestly miserable unless he drinks wine that costs three times as much. I don't think fairness means paying Dee more.
- Eva grew up poor with bad schools, Fia grew up rich with every advantage. I think fairness means spending more on Eva's schooling than Fia's, not the same amount, because Eva starts further behind.
- Gia chose to work part-time so she could write poetry and now has less money than Hal, who works 55 hours at a job he hates. I don't think that gap is unfair.
- Ivy has an anxiety disorder and can only hold a job if she lives alone in a quiet place, which costs more. I think fairness does mean helping her with that. But I told Dee his expensive taste wasn't our problem, and now I can't say what the difference is.
- Jay grew up in extreme poverty and says he's totally satisfied with his life because he never knew anything else and doesn't want what he can't have. If fairness is about how happy people are, Jay comes out equal to a rich person. But I don't feel like he is.

Is there one rule, or a small set of rules, that gets all of these or almost all of them? Wich of my gut calls would it force me to rethink, and why? And what does it say about someone whose religion requires him to buy food that costs a lot more: need, taste, or something else?

Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
