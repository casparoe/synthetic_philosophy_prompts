I've been trying to figure out something about how we treat animal suffering, and I want to see if there's a principle that actually holds up. Here's the clash. If someone deliberately infects a stray cat with a slow, painful disease, almost everyone thinks that's straightforwardly wrong -- cruelty, punish, all that. But if the same cat gets the same disease naturally, from a flea or another cat, we mostly shrug and say "that's just nature," and nobody has a duty to treat it. So far the natural/unnatural line looks like its doing serious moral work. But then a dolphin beaches itself and we'll spend tens of thousands of dollars and send volunteers to save one animal, even though its suffering is just as natural as the cat's. Meanwhile vastly more wild animals die slow deaths from hunger, parasites, and predators along the same coast, and nobody thinks there's any comparable duty to intervene. So the natural/unnatural distinction explains the first pair and then apparently stops mattering in the second. What's the strongest principle you can give that justifies this whole cluster of judgments -- maybe it's about causation, or salience, or how easy the rescue is, or something else -- and does it actually survive nearby cases? If it doesn't, tell me which of these ordinary judgments I should drop or revise.
