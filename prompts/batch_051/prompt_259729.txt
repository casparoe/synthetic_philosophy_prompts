Here's an argument I keep running into, usually in compressed form, and I half-suspect theres a trick in it but I can't pin down where. It's supposed to show that conceptual analysis and conceptual engineering are the same activity.

(1) A concept, in the sense philosophers care about, is whatever fixes how the relevant term applies across actual and counterfactual cases.
(2) What fixes how a term applies is partly determined by our interests and purposes, not just by how the world is.
(3) So an analysis of a concept is never a neutral description: assigning content to a term inevitably means choosing the content that best serves our interests and purposes.
(4) Choosing the content that best serves our purposes is exactly the ameliorative or engineering move.
(5) Therefore conceptual analysis is just conceptual engineering done quietly, and the usual contrast between analysing the concept we already have and fixing the concept we should have collapses.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and is the failure located in an ambiguity that lets one step through on one reading and a different step on another? If it is valid, is the conclusion really as strong as it sounds, or does it just relabel a distinction without removing it?
