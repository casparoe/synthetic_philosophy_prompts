Two people in my complex-systems reading group got into a long argument last week after we read something on Wolfram's notion of computational irreducibility — roughly, the idea that for many systems the only way to learn the state at step n is to run the rule n times, because there's no shortcut formula. I mostly sat there taking notes and now I cant sort out who was right, so I'd like you to referee the whole thing.

The exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it, went like this.

(1) Dan said irreducibility means explanation is dead for those systems: if you have to run the computation to know the state at step n, then the only "explanation" of that state is the computation itself, and that's just a re-description of the thing you wanted explained, not an explanation. (2) Priya replied that he was conflating explanation with compression. Pointing to the rule and the initial condition is a perfectly good explanation even when it doesn't let you skip ahead — understanding and cheap prediction are different jobs. (3) Dan came back: but if the rule plus the initial condition just is the simulation, then you haven't understood anything; you've just re-run the system. Explanation requires seeing a pattern, and irreducibility is exactly the claim that there's no pattern to see. (4) Priya said that's too fast: there can be higher-level patterns even when the step-by-step behavior is irreducible — conserved quantities, statistical regularities, bounds on growth, phase transitions. You can discover those without running every step, so irreducibility doesn't kill understanding; it makes it piecemeal. (5) Dan said those partial results are fine, but they aren't an explanation of why the system is in this particular state at this particular step. His claim was narrower: for the exact state, there's no why beyond brute computation. (6) Priya answered that this assumes the only explanation of a particular event is a derivation from laws plus initial conditions. Explanation can also be contrastive or mechanistic — why this state rather than some nearby one, or what mechanism produced it — and if those count in biology or economics, they count here too. (7) Dan said she had just conceded his point: she can explain why the system stays in a certain class, but not why it's at the exact state, and the exact state was the thing in question.

Please go through it move by move and rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each. Then say who is more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. You're a professional philosopher of science, so start by brainstorming the distinct senses of "explanation" in play before you adjudicate, and think carefully about which sense each move is implicitly using.
