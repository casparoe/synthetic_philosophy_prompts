I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and next month I present a dissertation chapter at my department's moral and political philosophy workshop. The chapter defends a modified version of T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, and in particular his individualist restriction: the thesis that when we assess whether a principle for the general regulation of behaviour could be reasonably rejected, we consider the strongest individual complaint against it, and we are not permitted to sum many people's smaller complaints into a single aggregate large enough to outweigh one person's more serious complaint. My version concedes that numbers may properly break ties when the competing complaints are relevantly similar, and it insists that many supposed counterexamples to the restriction arise only because we have quietly imported aggregative assumptions from the domain of beneficence and rescue into the domain of what we owe to one another. I have spent the term persuading myself that the restriction can be saved; the workshop is the first time I will have to defend it in front of people who actually use aggregation in their own work, and I have begun to notice that my confidence evaporates when I picture the question period.

The audience will be specific. It includes two consequentialists, one who works on aggregation and population ethics and one who does cost-benefit reasoning about public policy; a Kantian who regards Scanlon as a consequentialist in disguise and will likely attack my two-domain split as ad hoc; a bioethicist whose work is on triage and scarce-resource allocation; and several sharp graduate students without settled camps. I can already predict the stock challenges, such as the choice between sparing one person a severe harm and sparing many people milder harms, or the worry that my account gives no determinate guidance once burdens differ in both size and kind. What I need is preparation that goes past the stock versions and tells me how these particular people will press the reasoning, not just the slogan.

Please prepare me for that question period. Give me the objections in the order in which this audience is likely to raise them, phrased as these people would actually put them rather than in generic textbook-consequentialist language. For each objection, tell me whether it has real force, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firm on, what my most effective short reply is, and where that reply is softest. Be honest about the weakest point in my position that I should defend rather than hope nobody notices, and tell me the question I should most fear, which I suspect will come from the bioethicist and will concern many moderate injuries to strangers versus one severe injury to a person whose face I can see.
