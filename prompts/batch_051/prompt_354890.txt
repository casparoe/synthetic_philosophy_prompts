Ok so I used to be an organic chemist, retired now, and I've started doing philosophy for fun. In a few weeks I'm presenting to our senior-college reading group, which is mostly other retired natural scientists plus one retired political theorist. My thesis is that Adam Smith's treatment of what we'd call greed or acquisitiveness is more interesting than the usual vice-or-virtue framing: in Wealth of Nations Book II Chapter III he describes the desire to better our condition as something that comes with us from the womb and never leaves us till we go into the grave, and he treats it as a mostly calm, steady drive that produces capital accumulation and improvement. I want to argue that for Smith this desire is neither exactly a vice nor exactly a virtue; it's a constitutive feature of human nature that under decent institutions becomes an engine of prosperity, but under bad ones turns into something corrupt. The audience will be skeptical: they'll half-remember Mandeville's private vices, public benefits and think I'm trying to pull the same trick, and a few of them think growth itself is overrated. Prepare me for the Q&A. What objections will they raise, in their words, which ones have real force, what should I concede up front, and what's the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive? Also please separate what's settled about Smith's view from what's genuinely contested among Smith scholars, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
