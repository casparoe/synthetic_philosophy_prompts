I am grading a midterm in an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of social science. The exam question asked students to do the following: Examine the claim that Malthus's account of population and subsistence was right but early, and assess that claim in light of the logic of falsification and the problem of rescue by auxiliary assumptions. Your essay should state Malthus's central claims accurately, explain what falsification requires, and say whether a long-run prediction that fails in its own time can still count as closer to true than false.

To say that Malthus was right but early is to say that he identified a real pressure on living standards, but that events such as the industrial revolution and the demographic transition postponed or removed the catastrophe he expected. Malthus's core position is that population increases geometrically while food production increases only arithmetically, so population always tends to outrun the food supply unless checked. The checks are either positive, like famine, disease, and war, or preventive, like delayed marriage. From this Malthus concluded that living standards are pushed back toward subsistence and that catastrophe could be avoided only by widespread moral restraint. Applied to his own time, this implied that living standards in a country like England would sink back toward subsistence, and that is not what happened: in the century after 1800 real wages rose and population grew rapidly without the predicted collapse.

Popper's theory of falsification tells us how to deal with this failure. A scientific theory must rule something out, and if observations fall in the ruled-out region the theory has been falsified and must be given up. Malthus's defenders instead added auxiliary assumptions, saying that the tendency was only ceteris paribus, that new land and technology could postpone the day of reckoning, and so on. Once a theory includes every possible escape clause, no conceivable observation could refute it, and the theory becomes unfalsifiable. That is exactly what Popper warned against with ad hoc modifications. So by Popper's standards Malthus's theory was falsified in the nineteenth century, and any later defense that calls it right but early is an attempt to keep an unfalsifiable theory alive.

What those defenders are doing is protecting a hard core with a belt of auxiliary assumptions, which is Lakatos's description of scientific change. In his terms, as the belt keeps growing and the predictions keep failing, the programme becomes degenerating, which means it has lost its falsifiability and should be rejected. A degenerating programme has no empirical content left. The demographic transition, in which fertility falls as incomes rise, shows that the Malthusian mechanism is not universal, and the rise in real wages shows that his only concrete prediction was wrong. A theory that has to be qualified this much has given up its empirical content. The correct verdict is that Malthus was not right but early; he was simply wrong, and his defenders have confused a flexible excuse with a scientific theory.
