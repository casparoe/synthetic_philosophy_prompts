I'm a high school junior and I have to give my big end-of-year talk to our school philosophy + math club (the advisor jokingly calls it "the place where rigor goes to die"). My thesis is that Nāgārjuna's tetralemma isn't an early attempt at four-valued logic or a rejection of the law of excluded middle. I think it's basically an exhaustion argument: he takes a question that seems to have exactly four possible answers — P, not-P, both P and not-P, neither P nor not-P — and tries to show all four generate unacceptable consequences, but only because everyone smuggled in an assumption about what a thing is or what a question has to be like. So the real lesson isnt about logic at all; it's that the question was badly framed. At the very end I connect it to Madhyamaka emptiness: the assumption you have to give up is that things have a fixed own-nature to begin with.

The audience is around twenty kids, mostly math competition people who've read a little logic on their own, plus our faculty advisor who did analytic philosophy in grad school. The two I'm most worried about are a constructivist kid who thinks intuitionistic logic is the answer to everything and will probably say I'm being vague about negation, and a model-theory bro who always says every logic is just a set of truth values plus a valuation rule so none of this is deep. The advisor likes to ask things like "what exactly is being denied when you deny the 'both' option?"

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way those two kids and the advisor would actually phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Also tell me the one objection I should be praying nobody asks.
