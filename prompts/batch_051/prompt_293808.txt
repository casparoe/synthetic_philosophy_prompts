I work at a private research foundation that runs a quarterly AI capabilities review. Each cycle, our analysts examine a slate of eight to twelve candidate systems or deployments—large language models and multimodal systems, scientific modeling tools, robotics platforms, and compound systems that integrate several of these—and assign each one a formal classification: transformative or not transformative. The classification carries substantial consequences. A "transformative" designation changes our grantmaking priorities, triggers an internal risk review, and shapes how we describe the field in public, so we cannot afford a procedure that is merely a list of buzzwords or that lets each analyst run private intuition under a shared vocabulary. At the same time, the analysts doing the classification are scientists and engineers rather than philosophers, and they typically have no more than one working day per case. I have been asked to write the durable, reusable procedure they will follow. It must be something a person can actually run: ordered questions, clear stopping rules, and explicit places where the output is "not enough information" or "this is a values call rather than a technical finding."

In designing the procedure, I am aware that the term "transformative AI" is used in at least three distinct ways, and I want the procedure to face that rather than paper over it. One usage is macroeconomic: an AI is transformative if its deployment durably and substantially raises the long-run rate of economic growth or output. A second is a capability-and-generality usage: an AI is transformative if it can substitute for human competence across a wide enough range of intellectual tasks that it changes what labor markets and institutions look like. A third is a feedback-loop usage: an AI is transformative if it materially accelerates the rate at which better AI systems or other technologies are produced. These do not always agree: a system could be economically important without being general, and a system could accelerate AI progress without yet being economically transformative. The procedure should therefore specify which question is primary and which of the others serve as tiebreakers or checks, or, if you think a pluralist approach is better, explain how it aggregates them. It must also be able to answer the question "has transformative AI already happened?" rather than assuming the answer is no.

Please write the procedure. It should lay out the exact questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a "no" or "not yet," where it hands the case to a person because the residual question is one of values, and where it returns "cannot be classified on current evidence." The procedure must not require the analyst to know any particular philosophical literature, and it should be stable enough that two analysts applying it to the same evidence mostly reach the same classification. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical cases: (a) a system that matches professional human performance on nearly all cognitive tests administered online, from law and accounting to programming and literature review for scientific research, but that is not widely deployed because institutions are slow to adopt it; (b) a narrow AI used throughout a supply chain that cumulatively adds half a percentage point to annual sectoral productivity growth but generalizes to nothing else; and (c) an AI system that performs no economically valuable final task at human level but materially accelerates the pace of machine learning research itself, roughly doubling the rate at which new models are developed. At least one of the applications should produce a classification that the procedure's designers would find unwelcome. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which distinctions it collapses, where it is likely to misfire, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
