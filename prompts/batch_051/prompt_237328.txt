I'm preparing to teach Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon in a bioethics unit, and I want to use it to put pressure on the therapy/enhancement distinction — the view, central to debates about biomedical enhancement, that interventions which restore a person to species-typical normal functioning are morally different in kind from interventions that raise a person above that baseline. I'd like a careful reading of the novel through that distinction. Charlie Gordon begins below the normal baseline, and the experimental operation is presented as a way to make him normal; but the procedure overshoots, lifting him to genius before it reverses and takes everything back. So how should we classify it: is the operation therapy, enhancement, or a case that shows the distinction doing less moral work than its defenders claim? In your reading, attend to where the novel locates the baseline (whose notion of normal governs the case?), to the relationship between the doctors' intentions and Charlie's consent, to whether the novel treats the heightened phase as a good for Charlie or as a kind of harm, and to what the first-person progress reports themselves reveal about whether the same person can be said to be treated and then enhanced. I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the distinction, and do not force the distinction to fit the novel. Where the novel resists the reading, say so plainly, and close by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate that the standard arguments do not. Finally, tell me where your reading is most likely to fail, and which consideration — about the novel or about the distinction — would most likely change your verdict.
