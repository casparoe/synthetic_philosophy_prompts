So I'm an ML engineer, and I keep getting pulled into arguments with some philosophy friends about whether morality is objective. The anti-objectivist case, as I understand it, runs like this: most of what we think we know about human moral psychology comes from WEIRD samples — Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic, basically undergrads in the US and Europe — and once you go to small-scale or non-Western societies, people make quite different judgments about whether intentions matter for blame, how much to punish a stranger who cheats, what counts as a fair split, who you owe help to. So the relativist concludes that if the supposedly universal moral intuitions dissolve when you change the training distribution, then moral judgment is just an artifact of a particular cultural environment rather than a readout of anything real. My friends reply that surface variation doesn't touch underlying principles — same objective function, different local conditions, different outputs.

I'd like to actually work through this in my own vocabulary instead of just nodding along. Can you translate both sides — the WEIRD-variation argument and the objectivist reply — into ML terms: training distribution, dataset bias, out-of-distribution generalization, inductive biases, invariance across environments, maybe domain adaptation and prior shift? Do it systematically: for each element of both positions, say what maps cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart at all in the ML frame. Then give the residue report: is the stuff that doesn't translate just a limitation of my vocabulary, or does it show the WEIRD argument is quietly assuming something?

And one more thing I'd love your view on: is 'moral objectivity' in this debate better translated as 'generalizes across environments' or as 'the labels are real' — and can those two come appart?
