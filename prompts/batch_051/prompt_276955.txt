I have a colleague whose verdicts on Parfit-style thought experiments I have been collecting over several months, and I can no longer tell which rule she is actually running. Her track record so far is this. In the standard destructive teletransporter case, in which a scanner destroys your body while building a complete replica on Mars with all your memories, beliefs, and character, she says you survive and the replica has everything that matters in survival. In a whole-brain transplant into a different body, she says you go with the brain and survive in the new body. In a case where a lesion irreversibly destroys every autobiographical memory and character trait while leaving a healthy, fully conscious person in the same brain and body, she says the resulting person is not related to you in any way that matters and that no thread of special self-concern remains. And in a case where a non-destructive scan creates a complete psychological duplicate while the original stays alive, she says survival has branched: both future people have what matters to you, although she refuses to say that both are you.

Next week she will consider the combined spectrum. A person undergoes an extremely long sequence of small psychological modifications. Each adjacent stage is as strongly psychologically connected to the previous one as an ordinary person is from one month to the next. But by the end, the resulting person shares almost none of the original's memories, preferences, or character; the chain is unbroken, while the direct overlap between the first and last stages is nearly nil. Does the endpoint have what matters in survival, for her?

I can see at least two rules that fit her four verdicts. One is a degree-of-connectedness rule: what matters is how much of one's psychology a future person directly shares, so survival comes in degrees and fades as that overlap shrinks. Another is a chain-continuity rule: what matters is only that there be an unbroken chain of overlapping psychological links, no matter how unlike the endpoints are, so a continuous chain yields full survival. There may also be an identity-with-uniqueness rule, on which a branched line leaves neither successor with what matters, but her fourth verdict seems to rule that out. Predict her verdict on the combined spectrum; identify which rules fit her track record and which do not; give your best guess about which rule she is implicitly running and explain why; and name the further case you would use to distinguish any rules that remain live.
