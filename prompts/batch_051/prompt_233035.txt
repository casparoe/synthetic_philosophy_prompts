I'm a software engineer, not trained in philosophy, but I've been reading Hume's essay "Of the Balance of Trade" and one bit stuck with me. He writes:

"This seems to me, almost in every case, a groundless apprehension; and I should as soon dread, that all our springs and rivers should be exhausted, as that money should abandon a kingdom where there are people and industry. Let us carefully preserve these latter advantages; and we need never be apprehensive of losing the former."

Hume is assuming money is gold and silver, which is a physical thing with a fixed supply. But today money is fiat — central banks create it with a few keystrokes, and exchange rates float. Does his argument still survive? I get that the core claim is about prices adjusting and money redistributing, but does the switch to fiat change the premise in a way that breaks the reasoning? Or does it just mean the "springs and rivers" are now under the control of a central bank? If it does break, is there a repaired version that still says something true? I'm mostly curious about which specific premise takes the hit.
