I'm taking an AI ethics and war class, and our professor keeps coming back to the meaningful human control debate, and tbh our study group is going in circles. Everyone says they're for meaningful human control until you give them an actual case, and then they disagree about whether a human veto is enough or whether a human has to press go on each individual shot. So I want to make a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz for the group, sort of a decision tree / scoring quiz hybrid, where the questions are all concrete military-AI cases and the scoring key maps people onto the actual positions in the debate.

The quiz should be able to seperate at least these positions:
- strict per-action control: a human has to make the specific decision for each lethal action;
- veto/supervision is enough: as long as a human is really able to monitor and stop the system in time;
- control belongs at the level of commanders, designers, rules of engagement, and institutional accountability, not any one operator;
- control is just a proxy for the deeper question of whether the system can be trusted to comply with the law, and if it can, that's what matters;
- the whole phrase meaningful human control is too vague to do real work.

I want the quiz built from the logical space rather than from the names of positions. It feels like there are two axes our readings keep falling back on: where control sits (operator, commander, designer/institution) and what control is supposed to secure (human judgment, the ability to intervene in time, clear accountability, or just reliable legal compliance). Could the questions target those two axes separately, so the result sorts people by how they actually answer cases rather than by which label they like?

For cases, use some version of: a defensive naval system that engages incoming missiles automatically; a loitering munition that a human lets loose in a limited area and time window and it chooses targets on its own; a normal drone strike where an operator has to approve the recommended target; and a target-recognition system where the human can veto but only has three seconds to do it. Include those four plus maybe two to four more of your own choosing, each with two or three answer options. Add a scoring key, and also flag any logically possible answer combinations that seem empty, noting whether they look incoherent, just unattractive, or underexplored. If any two positions can't be separated by the questions alone, that shoudl be flagged too.

Keep it for undergrads who did the readings but don't know the jargon fluently, and make sure no question just asks people which label they like.
