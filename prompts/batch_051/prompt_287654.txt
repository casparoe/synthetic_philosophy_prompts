I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on the history of political economy, and we have just finished reading Marx's treatment of machinery and large-scale industry in Capital, along with the later notebook material on automation. I want to test whether one of the arguments that emerges from those texts survives a change in assumptions of the kind Marx could not have anticipated.

As I understand it, the argument has several linked theses. Under capitalism, machinery is adopted not primarily to lighten toil but to cheapen commodities and thereby cheapen labor power, raising relative surplus value. It displaces living labor and produces an industrial reserve army that exerts downward pressure on wages. It also deskills the worker and transfers skill and knowledge into the machine. At the same time, Marx holds that as the productive forces develop, the creation of wealth comes to depend less on the direct labor time expended in production and more on the accumulated state of science, technology, and social knowledge; this increasingly strains the category of value as determined by socially necessary labor time, and it supplies the material basis for a post-capitalist arrangement in which the associated producers appropriate this accumulated knowledge and the reduction of necessary labor makes free development possible for all.

Now suppose the leading means of production is no longer a fixed physical machine installed in one workplace and owned by one capitalist. Instead it is a large generative model trained on a vast corpus of text, images, and code produced by past and present society; its outputs are non-rival and, once trained, can be reproduced and run at near-zero marginal cost; it does not require a concentrated workforce on a single site; and it automates cognitive and creative labor rather than only muscular labor.

Does Marx's argument survive this development? I want the answer to (a) identify the specific premise the change puts under pressure — the displacement of living labor, the ownership and physical character of fixed capital, the determination of value by labor time, or some other premise; (b) say whether there is a repaired version of the argument that is weaker but still worth keeping; and (c) sort the component theses — the reserve army of labor, deskilling, the strain on the value category, and the material basis for surpassing capitalism — into those that remain intact, those that fail, and those that survive only after being re-described in this new setting. Be explicit about the point where the updated position stops being Marx's argument and becomes something else.
