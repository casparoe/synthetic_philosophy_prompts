Examination question. In the familiar 'bank cases', a subject has the same evidence in two scenarios but the practical cost of being wrong differs. Using the low-stakes and high-stakes versions of those cases, explain the difference between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment about knowledge. Then outline a scenario in which the two views would deliver different verdicts about a knowledge ascription, and state what each view would predict.

Student's answer. In the bank cases, Hannah is in a bank on a Saturday and sees that it is open; by the following Friday she needs to deposit a cheque. In the low-stakes version, little turns on whether the bank is open on Saturday, and most competent speakers are happy to say that Hannah knows it will be. In the high-stakes version, the very same evidence is present, but an error would now have serious financial consequences, and the same speakers are reluctant to say she knows. The cases are therefore standardly taken to show that knowledge is not settled by the evidence alone. Epistemic contextualism explains this by treating 'knows' as semantically context-sensitive: the standard of knowledge expressed by 'knows' shifts with the practical interests and salient error possibilities in the attributor's conversational context, so the same sentence can express a truth in one context and a falsehood in another. Pragmatic encroachment agrees that knowledge is context-dependent in this way, but locates the context-sensitivity in the knowledge relation itself rather than in the word. It holds that whether a true belief supported by the available evidence amounts to knowledge depends partly on how much is practically at stake for the subject. Hannah therefore fails to know in the high-stakes case, even though her evidence is unchanged. The two views would come apart in a case where a low-stakes speaker evaluates a high-stakes subject. Contextualism predicts that the speaker's ascription is true, because the speaker's context sets the standard; pragmatic encroachment predicts that it is false, because the subject's high stakes prevent knowledge. Thus both views make knowledge interest-relative; they differ only over whether the relevant interests are the speaker's or the subject's.
