I'm a software engineer and I got into forecasting as a side thing, mostly after building a project-estimation dashboard at work and getting curious about why long-horizon predictions fail. Malthus is the one I can't stop picking at. The argument I keep running into goes like this:

(1) Malthus claimed population grows exponentially while food grows only linearly, so eventually population outruns the food supply and famine, disease, or war pushes it back down. He expected this to bite in his own era or not long after.
(2) It didn't: since then food production per person has gone up, the global population has exploded, and the major famines we have seen look more like political or distribution failures than an absolute shortage of food.
(3) The standard rescue is that Malthus was right but early: the underlying mechanism is real, technology just temporarily relaxed the constraint, and the check is still coming.
(4) But that rescue is available for any failed long-run prediction. Claim the mechanism is real, blame a temporary countervailing factor, and ask for more time. Someone who predicted in 1970 that the world would run out of oil by 2000 can make exactly the same move today.
(5) If a rescue move is available for every failed prediction, then right-but-early can't distinguish a prediction that is merely delayed from one that is simply false.
(6) So either Malthus was just wrong and the rescue is face-saving, or long-run predictions are unfalsifiable and calling something right but early is empty.

I tried to code this up once: give a model a free delay parameter and it can fit any observed failure by pushing the deadline back, so the probability never actually drops. That feels like cheating, but I can't tell if it's cheating in the model or cheating in the real argument.

I don't love either horn. It seems like some long-run predictions really are right but early, and I'd rather keep falsifiability than give it up, but I also don't want to conclude Malthus was just wrong by definition. Which step do you reject, or which premise do you give up, and what does rejecting it cost? And what would change your mind: which consideration would flip you to the other side, or where is your diagnosis most likely to break down?
