During a pandemic or mass-casualty event, many hospital triage protocols score patients by their probability of survival and their expected remaining life-years, and allocate scarce intensive-care resources to whoever yields the best combination; a patient with severe organ failure and poor odds may be passed over, or disconnected, in favor of someone healthier. In ordinary, non-crisis practice those same institutions treat the sickest first and treat bedside weighing of one patient's life-years against another's as a serious wrong; meanwhile, drug-assessment bodies in the same political system value a quality-adjusted life-year at one threshold while safety regulators value a statistical life at a figure that implies an order-of-magnitude different price per life-year. What theory of the value of a life, and of distributive fairness, does this whole set of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practices allow, separate what each rule strictly requires from what it merely fits, say whether the crisis rule and the ordinary-medicine rule have consistent presuppositions, and explain what triage would have to look like if the underlying view were prioritarian or strictly egalitarian rather than outcome-maximizing.
