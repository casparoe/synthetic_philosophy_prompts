I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am leading a ninety-minute seminar at an ideas festival on the free-floating rationale: the possibility that a course of action can be favored by a reason no one has actually entertained, so that the reason is in the situation rather than in any individual's head. The audience is bright and opinionated but has no formal philosophy. Plan the seminar as a teaching sequence rather than as an explanation: which distinction I must secure first — roughly, between a fact's being true, a person's having it in mind, and that fact's being in force for what someone should do — and why every subsequent step depends on it; which single running example should carry the concept and what features it must have to keep the audience from sliding back into agent-centered talk; the specific misconceptions to expect, in particular that a free-floating rationale is just a later rationalization and that it presupposes something like a group mind, and exactly where in the sequence to defuse each; what I should openly refuse to adjudicate at this level; and a closing check that would separate genuine understanding from mere interest.
