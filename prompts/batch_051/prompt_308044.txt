I'm a PhD student in political theory, mostly working on emergency powers and the rule of law, and I've gotten dragged into a real argument back home. My sister is a state rep, and about six weeks ago the governor, citing a public health emergency, sealed off the county we grew up in — the whole county became a containment zone, no one in or out without a permit, and there was a 9 pm curfew. The governor didn't have clear legal authority for it: the state's emergency act lists quarantine of individuals and school closures, but not countywide travel restrictions, and the sixty-day declaration that supposedly authorized the order had already lapsed about two weeks before the governor renewed it with just a letter from the health department. Now my sister has to vote on a bill that would retroactively authorize the border closure, the curfew, and the checkpoints, and extend them another ninety days. The governor's office is arguing the legislature should pass it because the order was the right call on the merits — it kept ICU capacity from collapsing, and two nearby towns that skipped the curfew saw much worse outbreaks — so the paperwork was wrong but the substance was right. The attorney general has called the original order illegal and said voting to ratify it would make the legislature complicit in normalizing that kind of thing. My sister asked me whether she should vote yes because the measure was substantively right, or whether the lapsed declaration matters on it's own, and what a retroactive vote even changes: does it turn an illegal act into a legal one, or is that just laundering it afterwards? I keep going back and forth between 'the governor did the right thing, process is a luxury' and 'if you let this stand now, the next time it might be much less justified.' How should I sort this out for her?
