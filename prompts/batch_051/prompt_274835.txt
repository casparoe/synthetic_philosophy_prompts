I spent most of yesterday evening argueing with a mate about Goodhart's law, and I want to run a session on it that actually makes people feel the thing rather than just nod along. He kept saying it's just "incentives matter" with a posh name, and that everyone already knows it so there's no point making a song and dance. I think that's wrong: the interesting bit isn't that people game numbers, it's that the minute a measure becomes a target you can no longer tell whether the original goal is being met, and that happens even to people who know Goodhart's law perfectly well in the abstract. I want the activity to catch people in the act.

Could you design a classroom game for that? Setting: a third-year undergraduate philosophy seminar, about 16 students, 90-minute session (so maybe 60-65 minutes for the game and 25-30 for debrief). They've read the basic Goodhart and Campbell material but I don't want to assume they've internalised any of it.

I picture something like this, but replace or improve it as you see fit: split them into firms, give them a "true goal" they care about (making excellent widgets, say), then reward them on a proxy (widgets per minute, or a quality score they can partly game). Run it in stages so the proxy appears only after the first round, and add a later round where targets are negotiated or observers are appointed. The crucial thing is that the gaming should emerge from the rules, not from me winking at them to cheat. It could use paper, tokens, timers, a scoring sheet, maybe one or two observers per firm.

I specifically want them to feel:
- the difference between gaming the proxy (working to the letter of the scoring) and genuinely improving on the true goal,
- how, once a number is made the target, your own information about quality gets corrupted, so even well-intentioned people stop knowing whether they're actually doing well,
- that making the proxy more complex doesn't necessarily fix the problem, it just moves the pressure somewhere else.

Please lay out: materials, exact rules for each round, how scoring works, roles, a timeline, what the debrief should surface and in what order, and an honest note on which feature of the idea this sort of game can't capture (I suspect it's the way real institutional metrics ossify and outlive the goal they were meant to serve, but tell me if I'm wrong). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.

And if you think Goodhart's law and specification gaming genuinely do collapse into "incentives matter," I'd rather the debrief tackle that head on than dodge it, because that's the exact argument I'll be replaying with my friend afterwards.
