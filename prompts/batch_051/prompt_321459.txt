My opening move: Consider the Ellsberg two-urn problem. Urn K holds one hundred balls, exactly fifty red and fifty black. Urn U holds one hundred balls, each red or black, in an unknown proportion. You claim a rational person may prefer a bet that pays one hundred dollars on red drawn from Urn K to the same bet on red drawn from Urn U, while also preferring a bet that pays one hundred dollars on black drawn from Urn K to the same bet on black drawn from Urn U. But those two preferences, if read as expected-utility comparisons against a single probability distribution, imply that the probability of red in Urn U is less than one-half and that the probability of black in Urn U is also less than one-half, which cannot be, since probabilities of complementary events must sum to one. If you repair this by saying the agent acts on a set of probabilities, I worry you have bought coherence only by making the theory unconstrained, since any behavior can apparently be rationalized by choosing a permissive enough set.
