I'm on the board of a small housing co-op — nine of us — and in our meetings votes are public and we go around the table in a fixed order. I've noticed that after the first couple of confident people vote yes, nobody wants to look like the odd one out, so the rest fall in line even when I know from side conversations that they had doubts. Somebody once called this an information cascade, where it's actually rational to ignore your own read of things because everyone else's votes must mean they know something you don't. I get the logic, but it still feels like we make worse calls than if we could all vote in a booth.

So I want to propose a small rule, and I want to know how it would actually play out before I bring it up, because I'm sure there's stuff I'm missing.

The rule: before any discussion starts, each member writes on an index card which way they're leaning and how confident they are, on a scale of one to five. The cards are sealed and don't constrain anything. Then we discuss and go around the room with public votes exactly like now. After the vote is recorded, the cards are opened, and anyone whos card says something different from their vote has to explain in a sentence or two what they heard that changed their mind. No punishment, no re-vote, just the duty to explain.

My theory is people will be less willing to roll over and vote with the crowd because they'll have to admit on the record that they'd been leaning the other way, and the people who do flip will at least have to check whether they're updating on good information or just caving.

But I can already picture people hedging by writing "really not sure" so a flip means nothing, or writing what they think the eventual majority will be so they don't look inconsistent, and then the whole thing becomes theater. I also worry it might overshoot: sometimes the crowd really does know something I don't, and a smart person should change their mind after hearing the first few votes, and this rule might make people dig in just to avoid looking wishy-washy. Which of these failures are real, which are overblown, and can any of them be fixed without gutting the scheme? If you had to bet, would this leave our decisions better or worse overall? Also, tell me where your analysis is most likely to go wrong — what kind of evidence or argument would change your mind about it.
