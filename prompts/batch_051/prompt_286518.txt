I run a small Stoic practice group and have been asked to write out my own working commitments before leading a session on the dichotomy of control, so that the other facilitators can probe them for inconsistencies. I would be grateful for a careful audit of whether the following five positions, all of which I hold, actually fit together.

1. I take the opening of Epictetus's Handbook to state the canonical dichotomy: the only things that are, strictly speaking, up to us are judgment, impulse, desire, and aversion, while body, property, reputation, and office are not. I regard this division as the foundation of the whole Stoic system, not merely as a therapeutic convenience.

2. In the sessions themselves I teach a three-way sorting rather than the old two-way one: some matters are fully within our control, some are partly within our control, and some are not within our control at all. I present this as a needed correction to the traditional dichotomy, and I advise people to reframe any partly controllable aim in terms of the part that is fully controllable — for example, aiming to prepare for an interview well rather than aiming to be offered the job — so that the middle category shrinks.

3. I also hold the Stoic view of fate in broad outline: every event, including our own choices and judgments, is the unfolding of a single providential causal order, and nothing could have happened otherwise. I nevertheless maintain that the distinction between what is up to us and what is not is what makes praise, blame, and self-reproach appropriate.

4. When sceptical members of the group ask why anyone should bother with the practice, I defend it on outcome grounds. I tell them, and I believe, that steady application of the dichotomy reliably reduces anxiety, resentment, and wasted effort, and makes people more effective and easier to live with in their work and relationships. I take these effects to be the main reason to recommend the practice.

5. At the same time, I keep to the strict Stoic axiology: health, reputation, relationships, and career are indifferents, and none of them can make a life better or worse in the only sense that matters; living well consists solely in the condition of one's own judgments and choices. Improvements in one's work or relationships, however preferred, are not part of what it is to live well.

Please audit the set. Tell me which commitments actually conflict, which only appear to conflict, and exactly which distinction dissolves each apparent conflict. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what would have to give, and identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency. Rank the genuine tensions from most to least severe rather than presenting them as equal, and do not resolve the set by stretching one of the commitments past what I actually said. I would also like you to say how confident you are in each part of the audit and to single out the point on which you are least sure.
