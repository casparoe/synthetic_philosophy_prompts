I'm on the programs committee for a big history-of-science society meeting, and we're weighing a plenary-session proposal titled something like "When did counting become mathematics?" The organizer wants to build the session around administrative and school contexts — tally sticks, Mesopotamian bookkeeping tablets, cuneiform lists — and especially around Plimpton 322, which they say has usually been read as a table of Pythagorean triples rather than mere record-keeping, so the line between calculating and doing mathematics gets blurred. One faction on the committee says none of that is mathematics; it's accounting and quantification, and mathematics doesn't start until you have proof or at least general statements. Another faction says the session is precisely contesting that boundary, and refusing it on those grounds just prejudges the question. Draft a short decision memo for the committee — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with criteria reviewers can actually apply to future proposals, and a fair treatment of the strongest point made by whichever side loses.
