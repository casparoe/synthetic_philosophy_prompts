I got called for jury duty a few weeks ago and sat through jury selection for a disorderly conduct case where the instruction basically came down to whether the defendant's behavior was offensively coarse or annoying, and I couldn't stop thinking about how nobody in the room could have told you in advance what that covers. So I went down a rabbit hole on the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and now I'm trying to sort out what I actually think, because my friends all have there own intuitions and we keep talking past each other.

Could you build me a sorting quiz or decision tree — say six to eight questions ending in around eight or nine leaves — that classifies positions on how precise a criminal law has to be? I want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of named views, so each branch should split on a concrete case or claim someone can answer without knowing any labels. Please use these cases somewhere: a city ordinance that bans conduct that disturbs the peace; a school dress code banning inappropriate clothing (not criminal, but it isolates the who-gets-to-fill-in-the-blank question); a statute criminalizing driving recklessly; a law making it a crime to do something in public that annoys or alarms another person (the sort of language disorderly conduct and panhandling laws use); and an exhaustively drafted statute that lists every prohibited act but ends with something like "or any other device not specified above." Also make sure at least one question separates people who think the core problem is lack of notice (you couldn't know) from people who think it's discretion (the officer or judge gets to decide), and one question about whether vagueness is worse in the criminal law than in school rules or administrative regulations.

The leaves should include at least these positions: strict textualism (if it isn't crisply defined, it's void); the fair-notice-only view; the anti-discretion-only view; the ordinary-person-plus-context view (vague words are fine if a reasonable person could read the situation); the community-standards view; the delegation view (vagueness is fine when the legislature sets policy and leaves specification to agencies or courts); and the standards-over-rules view that thinks some vagueness is a feature. Name each leaf or mark it "unnamed," and add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where the tree can't separate two positions, flag it rather than fudging — for instance, people who want courts to read vague terms narrowly and people who want legislatures to redraft may answer every quiz question the same way, and if so the tree should say that. For my purpose the crucial final split is about the remedy: does the person think the cure is better drafting, narrower construction, or just living with vague standards and trusting institutions to apply them in good faith? So the last question or two should sort by remedy, not just by diagnosis. The audience is my reading group — a couple law students, a couple philosophy people, and a few normies, nobody with a settled view.
