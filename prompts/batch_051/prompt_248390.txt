Ok so in my epistemology seminar we're doing peer disagreement, conciliationism vs steadfastness, and our professor gave us a working definition of an epistemic peer: someone who is (a) roughly as smart and good at reasoning as you, (b) familiar with the same evidence and arguments as you, and (c) not known to have a bias or stake in the question that you lack. I've been trying to test this against a case from my actual life and I keep going back and forth.

My roommate and I are both philosophy majors with basically the same grades, and we've both read the same published papers on whether people's moral judgments are mostly driven by framing effects. We disagree about it. The twist is that she's been an RA in a moral psych lab for two years, running replications of the exact studies we read and coding hundreds of participants. The replications mostly came out like the published papers, so there's no specific finding she can point to that I haven't read — her extra thing is more like a trained sense of which results are robust and which are noise. She's not smarter then me, and by the professor's three criteria she looks like my epistemic peer. But I have this strong feeling that if we disagree I should defer to her way more than conciliationism normally says, because her judgment on exactly this topic has been trained in a way mine hasn't. And I'm not doing the thing where you decide after the fact that the other person is irrational just because they disagree; I'd be pointing to her training, which I knew about before we ever argued.

So what I want to work out: go through the three criteria and say whether she actually qualifies as my peer. If she does, what does a conciliationist say I should do, and is that what I actually should do? If she doesn't, which criterion fails, or does the definition need a fourth condition like "equal domain-specific judgment"? And then the bigger worry: if we can always demote someone from peerhood by pointing to an asymmetry like this, does conciliationism end up toothless, since it only ever applies when we've already decided the other person is our equal in every relevant way? Please write the answer in American English.
