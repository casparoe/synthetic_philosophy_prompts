I keep coming back to the paradox of deontology, and I'd like a precise diagnosis of an argument that seems too quick to me. (1) A deontological constraint — say, against killing an innocent person — is supposed to bind even when violating it would prevent more violations of the same constraint by others; that's the agent-relative part. (2) But nearly every deontologist adds an escape hatch: in a catastrophe you may kill one innocent person to prevent a vastly greater number of killings. (3) Whatever makes a catastrophe catastrophic enough to trip the escape hatch has to be a comparison between the badness of my one killing and the badness of the many killings it prevents. (4) But making the permissibility of my killing turn on that comparison is exactly the consequentialist aggregation the constraint was meant to rule out. (5) So threshold deontology either has no principled way to locate the threshold, or it secretly is consequentialism. Is this valid, and if not, which step fails under which reading? If the deontologist keeps the threshold but rejects step (3), what does the threshold actually turn out to be, and is that stable? Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested here — this is a live area, and I dont want one camp's view presented as the obvious consensus.
