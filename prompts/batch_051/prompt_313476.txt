I have spent the past year assisting a legal philosopher who is drafting model rules for a law-reform foundation on the question of when the bare passage of time should extinguish a claim to compensation or punishment. She works by testing candidate rules against stylized disputes and recording the result she thinks a well-designed rule should produce, and I have been keeping a file of her verdicts in order to reconstruct the principle she has not yet stated in the abstract.

So far she has ruled as follows. She would extinguish a claim for unpaid wages brought twenty-two years after dismissal, when the only witness to the oral agreement has died and the employer's payroll records were destroyed under a routine retention schedule. She would allow a claim against a chemical plant for groundwater contamination brought thirty years after the leak, because the residents could not reasonably have known the water was unsafe until a public-health study was published three years ago and the plant's own monitoring data from the time are intact. She would allow a claim against an accountant who spent two decades forging ledgers to steal the life savings of elderly clients, where the forgeries came to light four years ago and a forensic accountant has since reconstructed the underlying records. She would also allow a claim by a man exonerated last year after twenty years in prison against the detective who fabricated the evidence, where the fabrication became provable only when the detective's original notes and the coached statements were released from a police archive three years ago.

The new case she must decide next week is this. Thirty-five years ago one man lent another a substantial sum to start a business; the original promissory note, the bank transfer record, and a signed receipt all survive and are undisputed. The borrower never concealed the loan or the debt; he simply never repaid and was never asked again, and the lender says he delayed out of embarrassment and inertia. If her model rule is to cohere with the track record above, should the claim be allowed or extinguished, and which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? I am trying to identify which principle best explains her pattern: a purely evidential account, on which time matters only because memories fade and records disappear; an independent principle of finality or repose, on which even well-evidenced claims eventually lapse unless there was concealment, a continuing wrong, or a reasonable failure to discover the harm; or a moral-weight account, on which time extinguishes ordinary economic claims but not serious violations of persons. What would each principle predict about the loan, which prediction do you think she shares and why, and what further case should I put before her to settle the ambiguity that would remain either way?
