I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been reading David Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds* while working through the semantics of modal claims in logic. I got stuck on a passage in section 4.4, where Lewis introduces his counterpart theory and rejects the idea that one and the same individual can be identical across possible worlds. In the passage, Lewis argues that when we say of Humphrey that he might have won the election, the standard view takes that to mean that there is a possible world in which Humphrey himself wins. But, Lewis says, that cannot be right, because if it were literally Humphrey himself in that other world, then that one individual would have to possess conflicting intrinsic properties: in the actual world he lost, and in the other world he won. Lewis then proposes that we should instead say that the individual in the other world is not Humphrey but a counterpart of Humphrey — something that resembles him in the relevant respects and stands in for him for purposes of modal predication. The counterpart relation, he insists, is not identity but a relation of similarity, and similarity is a matter of degree and of which respects are selected by context.

I can follow the qualification that properties are world-relative, so I don't see why the problem of conflicting intrinsic properties is supposed to be fatal to transworld identity. Couldn't the transworld identity theorist just say that Humphrey has the property being-the-winner in one world and being-the-loser in another, and since those are different worlds, there is no violation of the principle that a thing cannot be both F and not-F in the same world? What is Lewis's argument for thinking that move is unacceptable? And then I want help with the counterpart move itself. If the person in the other world is not identical to Humphrey, what makes it true that Humphrey might have won, and why should Humphrey care about what happens to that other person? Is Lewis's answer that our ordinary modal talk was never really about identity at all, but about similarity under some contextually selected respects? If so, how does the formal semantics change? I've seen the standard possible-worlds semantics for quantified modal logic, and I'm not sure where counterpart theory forces a revision: is it in the evaluation of de re modal claims, in the treatment of variables, or in the underlying ontology of worlds? Could you walk through the passage as a step-by-step argument, state precisely what the counterpart theorist takes the logical form of a sentence like 'Humphrey might have won' to be, and then say how a proponent of transworld identity would reply, and what you think is the decisive issue between them?
