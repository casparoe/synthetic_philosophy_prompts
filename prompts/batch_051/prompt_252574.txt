A friend of mine claims he's found a version of the reference class problem that collapses in on itself, and I can't find the exit. Here's his argument, numbered as he gave it to me:

(1) A single-case probability claim -- like 'the probability this patient recovers with antibiotics is 0.9' -- only has content relative to a reference class: you're treating this patient as a member of some class of similar cases and reading off a frequency.

(2) Any particular case belongs to plenty of reference classes at once (pneumonia patients, pneumonia patients over 60, patients at this hospital, patients whose names start with J...), and different classes give different frequencies. No class is stamped 'right' by the world.

(3) Choosing a better class rather than a worse one seems to require a judgment that this case is probably relevantly similar to members of that class -- but that's itself a single-case probability claim, so by (1) it needs its own reference class, and off we go.

(4) The regress only stops if there's some privileged reference class that needs no further justification. But any candidate can be made to look arbitrary by carving out a slightly more specific class that gives a different number.

(5) So no single-case probability claim can ever be justified.

(6) But that's absurd: the doctor's 0.9 isn't just a manner of speaking, it guides treatment, and people bet there lives on such numbers.

(7) So one of (1)-(4) has to go.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? My study group has split: one camp wants to deny (3) and say reference class choice is guided by something other than probabilities; another wants to deny (4) and say there's a principled stopping point after all; a third wants to bite the bullet on (5) and say single-case probabilities really are just conventional. I keep thinking each option is worse than the disease. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it force you to give up elsewhere?
