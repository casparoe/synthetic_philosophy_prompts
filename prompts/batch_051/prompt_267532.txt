Here's ch. 5, sect. 27:

"Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property. It being by him removed from the common state nature hath placed it in, it hath by this labour something annexed to it, that excludes the common right of other men: for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others."

My opening move: I have just described to you a box that, when aimed at a mountain and operated briefly, preserves a flat image of the mountain's light on paper, while the mountain remains exactly as it was and anyone else can do the same. You say labour removes something "out of the state that nature hath provided" and mixes it with something of the labourer's. So: when I operate the box on the mountain, do I mix my labour with the mountain, with the light, or with the paper image? If the image is mine by labour, what exactly did my labour join itself to? And what stops the same argument from giving me property in the mountain itself, which I pointed the box at? If the image is not mine, how does your doctrine distinguish it from the acorns you say belong to the gatherer by the same mixing of labour?
