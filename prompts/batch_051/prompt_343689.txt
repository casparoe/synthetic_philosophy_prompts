Question: Explain Dennett's criticism of the Cartesian theater as a model of consciousness and describe how the multiple drafts model is meant to replace it. Use the color-phi phenomenon to illustrate the difference, and state what Dennett takes the phenomenon to show.

Student answer: The Cartesian theater is the view that everything that comes together in conscious experience is assembled at a single place in the brain and presented to an inner observer, the self. Dennett rejects it for two reasons. First, the inner observer would be a homunculus whose own consciousness requires another Cartesian theater, generating an infinite regress. Second, the theater model assumes there must be a definite time and place at which a content becomes conscious, and that assumption does not fit the evidence. Dennett replaces the theater with the multiple drafts model: the brain continuously produces many parallel and partial interpretations of incoming information at different places and times, and these are constantly revised. There is no single point where they all converge. Instead, the interpretations compete for influence, and the winning ones become famous in the brain and are broadcast to the rest of the system, while the losing interpretations leave no trace. The color-phi experiment illustrates this. A red spot and then a green spot are flashed in quick succession at nearby locations, and subjects report a single moving spot that changes color midway. Dennett's analysis is that the brain revises its earlier draft after the fact, editing the record before the experience is presented to consciousness so that the color change seems to have occurred earlier than it did. The rival account would say that consciousness first stages a false version showing the moving, color-changing spot. Dennett says the data cannot decide between these two readings. This very inability to decide supports the multiple drafts model, because a Cartesian theater would promise a fact of the matter that the brain does not actually provide.
