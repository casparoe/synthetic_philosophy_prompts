I'm the finds recorder for a county archaeological society — trained on the job, never at a university — and I've spent years trying to work out how far the objects by themselves can actually take us. Let me lay out my verdicts on some hypothetical finds, because I want to know whether there is one real principle behind them or just a collection of habits. When I see a scatter of fourteen iron swords, each one deliberately bent double or snapped, deposited in a marshy inlet over many generations, I am confident this was a repeated, deliberate practice — an institution of offering or destroying weapons — and I don't think I need to know which deity, if any, was involved. When I see a stone building whose entrance lines up with the midwinter sunrise and has been rebuilt on that same axis three times, I am willing to say the alignment was intentional and mattered to the builders, but I refuse to say the building was a temple to the sun or that its builders worshipped the sun. Yet when I see small clay figurines of women with exaggerated bellies placed upside-down under the floors of ordinary houses and worn smooth on the belly as if repeatedly handled, I find myself willing to say the people attached some protective or fertility-related meaning to these objects — and that is exactly the kind of content-level leap I refuse to make for the aligned building. A child-sized bow with no use-wear in a child's grave leaves me clear about one thing only: it was deliberately placed as a grave good. Whether it was a toy, a mark of future rank, or a gift, I cannot say. And I am confident that two spindle whorls with the same off-center notch found forty miles apart indicate a single craftsperson or workshop rather than independent invention, just as I am confident that a cache of highly polished stone axes too heavy and blunt to cut anything had some role beyond cutting — display, teaching, or ritual, I won't choose — though the phrase "beyond cutting" makes me uneasy, since I'm judging usefulness by my own hands.

My working rule has been something like this: non-representational objects let me reconstruct repeated, deliberate practices and institutions, but not the beliefs or meanings behind them, unless the objects themselves depict something — as the figurines do — in which case a cautious inference to meaning becomes legitimate. I want you to test that rule against my own verdicts and tell me whether it is the real principle or a post-hoc story. Specifically: which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon — I especially distrust my confidence about the figurines and my refusal about the solstice building — does the simpler competitor that objects never license any talk of beliefs fare better, and what would each candidate principle say about a case I've been turning over, a carved wooden animal mask, painted, found wrapped in cloth inside a storage pit. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five candidate principles, no introduction and no conclusion, each item a sentence or two; in each item state the principle and what it predicts about the carved mask and which of my verdicts, if any, it would overturn.
