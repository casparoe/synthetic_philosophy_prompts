I have been thinking about a decision problem on my own, and it has led me to a conclusion I cannot accept. I want to know exactly where the reasoning breaks, and what I give up by breaking it there.

Consider this arrangement. I am one of many people in an anonymous exercise. I have been paired with a stranger. I am told—and I may take it as stipulated—that my counterpart deliberates in exactly the same way I do, and that many other pairs are in the identical position. Each of us faces a single private choice: cooperate or defect. The choices are made at the same moment, in secret, and afterward no one will ever be punished, rewarded, or even informed how the other chose. The stakes are arranged so that, whatever the other person does, defecting would leave me individually better off; but if we choose the same way, we would both be better off if we both cooperated than if we both defected.

From this I reason as follows.

(1) Since my counterpart deliberates exactly as I do, if I choose to cooperate, that is overwhelming evidence that my counterpart cooperated; if I choose to defect, that is overwhelming evidence that my counterpart defected.

(2) The rational way to choose between two actions is to compare the outcomes that each action gives me reason to expect.

(3) By (1) and (2), choosing to cooperate gives me reason to expect the mutual-cooperation outcome, while choosing to defect gives me reason to expect the mutual-defection outcome.

(4) The mutual-cooperation outcome is better for me than the mutual-defection outcome.

(5) Therefore I should cooperate.

(6) Nothing in steps (1) through (5) turns on the choices being simultaneous, on any causal influence of my choice on my counterpart's, or on future consequences of my choice. The argument needs only the correlation between our deliberations.

(7) So the same reasoning should hold wherever that correlation holds: with someone whose choice was made years ago and sits unread in a vault, with a stranger in another galaxy who reasons the way I do, or with the enormous number of near-copies and simulations of my reasoning that the universe would contain if it is large enough. In each such case, my small private choice would be a vote cast on behalf of every correlated reasoner at once.

(8) But that cannot be right. It would make an ordinary, isolated decision—whether to leave an extra tip in a city I will never revisit, whether to keep a costly promise to a stranger I will never meet again, whether to finish work no one will ever inspect—count as though it were part of a silent negotiation with an invisible constituency my action cannot actually reach.

I would most like to keep step (5), because it seems to capture something real about recognizing my own reasoning in another person. But I cannot see how to stop the slide to (8) once (1) and (2) are granted. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
