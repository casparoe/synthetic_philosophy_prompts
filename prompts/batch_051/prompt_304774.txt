I have a policy in arguments that I would like you to evaluate. I will only treat what a person explicitly says as a claim they have made or as evidence I should update on. If someone communicates something by implying it, insinuating it, or asking a pointed question, I act as though no such claim is on the table. My reason is that implications are deniable: if I object to what was merely implied, the speaker can always say they never said that, and in some narrow sense they are right. I have been accused too many times of putting words in people's mouths, so I now take the literal meaning of the words as the only meaning I have to answer to. For example, when a student council opponent said, "I'm not saying the recycling program was rigged, but it is interesting that the chair's two best friends were the ones who counted the votes," I treated the remark as if it contained only the observation that those two friends counted the votes, and I refused to let the insinuation of rigging either change my confidence or require a response. I want to know whether this rule is a defensible epistemic safeguard or a way of pretending not to understand ordinary speech. Is implicature genuinely less usable as evidence than explicit assertion, or is its deniability only a social fact about what a speaker can be made to answer for rather than a fact about what they communicated? Is there a modified version of my policy that would still protect me against unfair paraphrase while allowing me to learn from what people clearly but indirectly mean? And how could I tell, from the inside, whether I am being careful or just shielding a position I already hold? Please respond in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
