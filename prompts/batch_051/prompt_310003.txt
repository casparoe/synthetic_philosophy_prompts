I am scheduled to give a colloquium talk this term, and I want you to prepare me for the discussion period. My thesis is a deliberate provocation: I will defend the claim that taxation is not theft, but in a form that should make everyone in the room uncomfortable. On my view, the reason taxation is not theft is not that the state has a right to take what is independently yours; it is that a taking counts as theft only relative to a baseline of legitimate holdings, and that baseline is unavailable prior to the legal-property regime that the tax system itself helps constitute. Property rights are not natural entitlements that taxation thereafter invades; taxation and the definition of what may be owned, transferred, inherited, and excluded from others are fixed together, so the charge of theft cannot get started without already smuggling in an answer to the question of what is mine. I will also insist, so that the egalitarians do not mistake me for an ally, that none of this conceptual point shows any particular tax or transfer scheme to be just, and that my view is perfectly compatible with thinking that a government taxes too much, taxes the wrong base, or spends the revenue badly.

The audience is not a neutral venue. The department includes two natural-rights libertarians who regard self-ownership as a pre-legal fact and treat forcing someone to labor for the state's purposes as morally continuous with forced labor; several liberal egalitarians, mostly Rawlsian or Dworkinian, who would like my conclusion but will worry that this conventionalism about property dissolves any principled objection to expropriation and scuttles the very idea of rights against the state; one public-finance economist who thinks the whole conceptual debate ignores incidence and the fact that taxes change real behavior; and a legal historian likely to observe that historical property regimes have always been entangled with tax, status, and sovereign discretion in ways my neat picture understates. Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument. For the objections these people will actually raise, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it does, phrase each objection the way they would phrase it, say which part is right and how serious it is, tell me what I should concede and what I should hold, and provide the two-minute reply I should give rather than whatever I would improvise. Then identify the genuinely softest point in my position, the one I should preempt in the talk itself rather than hope it goes unnoticed, and state the one question I should most hope nobody asks, with a one-sentence explanation of why it would hurt.
