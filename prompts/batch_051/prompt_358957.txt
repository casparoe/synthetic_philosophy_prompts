The exam question was: "Explain Sartre's account of bad faith. Distinguish it from straightforward lying and from a mere false belief, explain the role of facticity and transcendence in making it possible, and discuss at least two of Sartre's examples." One student wrote (this is the full answer):

Bad faith is Sartre's term for the special structure of lying to oneself. In an ordinary lie the deceiver and the deceived are different people and the liar knows the truth, but in bad faith the one deceiving and the one deceived are the same consciousness, so the lie never has to pass through another person. Sartre is careful to say this is not the same as just having a false belief or being ignorant; a person who is simply wrong is not in bad faith. He also thinks the Freudian unconscious cannot explain self-deception, because the censor would already have to know which thoughts to block, which just pushes the problem back one step. For Sartre, bad faith is possible because the human being has a double structure: facticity (one's past, body, situation, what already is) and transcendence (one's freedom to surpass this through future projects). Bad faith plays these two poles off against each other. A person can flee transcendence by trying to collapse into facticity, and the café waiter is the standard case: he tries to be a waiter as if being a waiter were an inherent property of him, the way a stone is a stone, rather than a role he has to continuously perform. Or a person can flee facticity and treat themselves as pure freedom with no past or situation. Sartre's other central example is the woman on a first date: she is in bad faith because she tells herself the man's compliments and hand-holding are purely respectful and that nothing romantic is going on, even though at some level she knows otherwise. The way out of bad faith is sincerity. If we honestly acknowledge what we are and commit to being exactly that, we stop fleeing ourselves, so sincerity is the opposite of bad faith.
