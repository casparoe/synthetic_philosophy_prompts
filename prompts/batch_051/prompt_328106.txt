After a long argument with a friend about whether AI systems can genuinely collude, I've concluded that we were mostly quarreling over the word collude: he held out for a meeting of minds or a secret side-channel, while I suspect that independent pricing agents can settle into behavior that is collusive in every way that matters for the customers who pay the resulting prices. I now need to turn that dispute into something usable at work.

I am a compliance officer at a mid-sized logistics firm. We run several autonomous pricing agents that quote freight rates to customers, and some of these agents serve overlapping markets and can observe competitors' or one another's published rates through an industry rate index. I have to sign a risk assessment for the board before any new pricing-agent deployment goes live, and I review each live deployment quarterly. I have about a day per review, I am not a machine-learning researcher, and I cannot run counterfactual pricing experiments in the actual market without committing the very harm I am trying to prevent.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for determining whether a given multi-agent pricing configuration poses a serious risk of collusive or collusion-like outcomes, grounded in whatever economics and multi-agent safety actually know about how simple independent learners converge on supracompetitive prices. The procedure should be something I can run without specialist training: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear "low risk," where it stops with a "flag for human review," and where it must hand off because the question is one of values or business risk rather than one that can be read off the system. Be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls disguised as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot detect, such as collusion that would only appear across deployments the procedure considers separately.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a single agent that learns from its own win-loss history and never observes a competitor's price; two agents that see each other's daily published rates through the rate index and are allowed to match or undercut; and many agents in a shared spot market who can respond to one another's quotes within seconds but have no channel except the quotes themselves. Make sure at least one of the worked cases is one where the procedure tells me something I would rather not sign off on.
