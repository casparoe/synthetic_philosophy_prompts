so i got into a long argument last night with a freind about promises—specifically whether binding yourself is a real thing you do, or just a tidy description of social pressure. he kept saying a promise is nothing over and above an announced intention plus the cost of losing trust, and i kept saying that misses the weird part: when i break a promise i don't just get punished, i feel like i violated something i created. at one point i pulled up hume. the passage that keeps rattling around, from his Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5, "Of the obligation of promises", is:

"A promise creates a new obligation. A new obligation supposes new sentiments to arise. The will never creates new sentiments."

hume's point, as i read it, is that if obligations track sentiments, and the will can't just manufacture a new sentiment, then a promise can't bind by a bare act of will. my friend thought this settled it in his favor, but i think it just relocates the question: if the will can't create the obligation, what does?

so i've been playing with a concept i call promissory inflation. the idea is that each person is the issuer of their own private currency—call it word-credibility—and every promise is a note drawn against it. keeping a promise feels like a must because defaulting debases your own currency: dishonor the small notes and the big ones stop being accepted. over-promising is inflation, which is why the person who says "i'll call you back" to everyone has trouble getting anyone to count on them for anything real. on this view promises bind the way money has value—not because any single act of will creates an obligation, but because everyone behaves as if the notes will be honored, and that shared behavior is what makes them worth honoring.

i know this sounds like hume with a finance filter, and maybe its just that. but give me an honest read: is the concept coherent, does it already have a name, what does it actually get right, and where does it break? the case bugging me most is a promise to someone on their deathbed—there's no future market of reputation in play at all, and my theory seems to predict the note would be worthless. that feels wrong, but maybe it isn't.
