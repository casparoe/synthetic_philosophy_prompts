I am a novelist planning a work of speculative fiction, and I want the economic logic of the invented world worked out more rigorously than premises of this kind usually are. The premise is a near-future counterfactual: in a large, advanced economy, by the middle of this century, automation has taken over the production of goods, logistics, and most routine services, so that fewer than ten percent of working-age adults are needed for paid employment in commodity production and circulation broadly understood. The automated systems remain private property, bought and sold as capital; prices, profit, interest, wages, and competition persist at least as legal and accounting forms, even though the wage relation has become marginal to most people's lives. Assume no exogenous catastrophe and no political revolution during the first decades; let the system run on its own logic.

Act as a scholar of Marx's critique of political economy and of the philosophy of technology. Think through the problem carefully. Before committing to a prediction, lay out the mechanisms from Marx that you intend to use—for example, the role Marx assigns to living labour in producing value and surplus value, the rising organic composition of capital, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, the industrial reserve army, and the idea that machinery under capitalism is shaped by the drive to extract surplus value rather than being a neutral tool. Flag the ones you consider most fragile before you use them.

Then reason forward over the following fifty years. I want the chain of reasoning, not a story: which mechanisms bite first, which check or reinforce one another, and what classes, political forms, and ideologies result. In particular, say what happens to the source of profit in a system where living labour is, on Marx's account, the source of new value; whether the system can keep reproducing itself for decades once that source has thinned to a sliver; and under what conditions the contradiction between the material possibility of leisure and the persistence of private appropriation turns into transformation—and what kind of transformation—rather than into stagnation or a repressive dead end.

Be explicit about confidence. Mark each link as robust on Marx's own categories, a defensible extrapolation that knowledgeable readers could resist, or a guess, and for each important prediction state what would have to be different for it to fail. Keep settled Marx scholarship separate from live interpretive disputes: the labour theory of value, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, the question whether Marx regarded technology as liberatory in itself, and the question whether automation as such dissolves capitalism or only changes its form are contested, and you should not present one side of those debates as the consensus.

This is for a novel, but do not give me plot or characters; give me the underlying logic of the world so that its institutions and conflicts behave as they plausibly would.
