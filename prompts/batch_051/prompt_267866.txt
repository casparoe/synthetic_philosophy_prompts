I write software for a living, and I tend to think about minds the way I think about systems: in terms of state, information flow, and how the parts constrain one another. That habit has pushed me toward a view of animal consciousness that I find compelling and almost surgical. On this view, consciousness is not a special substance or a biological add-on; it is what a particular kind of information processing is like from the inside. Concretely, for any system you can ask how much information the system as a whole carries that its parts do not carry separately — how much the system integrates information — and you can assign that system a number. Then you set a threshold: above the threshold, there is something it is like to be that system; below it, there is not. The attractions are obvious to me as an engineer. The view is mechanistic, it does not smuggle in anything nonphysical, it gives a criterion I could in principle implement, and it treats consciousness as graded and quantitative rather than as an all-or-nothing mystery.

The problem is that when I run this view over the animal kingdom in my head, it hands back verdicts I cannot accept. A fish, on the numbers I end up estimating, lands below the threshold: too few neurons, too little recurrent structure, processing that looks mostly reflex-like and feed-forward. And yet I cannot make myself believe that a fish flopping on a dock is not feeling anything, having watched one struggle and learn to avoid a hook. From the other side, the same measure seems to say that a honeybee colony — thousands of tightly coupled bees passing signals through the hive — crosses the threshold as one integrated system, and I find it very hard to believe the colony has a single unified conscious subject over and above the experiences of the individual bees. This is not a merely academic issue for me; getting the distribution of felt experience across animals right seems like something I should settle before I decide how I may treat them. So the procedure I picked because it was supposed to be principled draws a line through the animal world that my gut rejects from both directions.

Which should give way: the theory or the intuitions? Do not just pick a side. Work through the candidate moves: (a) debunk the intuitions as overgeneralization from mammals, or as a projection of feeling onto mere behavior; (b) amend the theory — change the threshold, change the measure, or change what counts as one system, so that fish land above it and bee colonies do not count as a single subject; (c) reinterpret the terms so the conflict is only apparent, for example by distinguishing two senses of "consciousness" or by saying the number tracks something real that is not the same thing as felt experience. For each move, say what it costs and what would justify making it — what evidence, argument, or modification would show the move was correct rather than merely convenient. Then give your negotiated verdict.

I would like the response formatted as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "verdict": one or two sentences stating which move you endorse overall, or how the conflict should be resolved.
- "strongest_case_for_theory": the best version of the argument that the integration view is right and the intuitions should yield.
- "strongest_case_for_intuition": the best version of the argument that the fish and bee-colony intuitions are tracking a real feature the theory misses.
- "move_assessment": an object with keys "debunk_intuition", "amend_theory", and "reinterpret_compatibility"; under each key, an object with "cost" (what you give up by making this move) and "justification" (what would make the move correct rather than merely convenient).
- "confidence": one of "low", "medium", or "high".
- "remaining_question": the single most important unresolved issue after your verdict.
