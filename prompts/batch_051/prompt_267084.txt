I work as a software engineer on machine-learning systems, and I have no formal background in philosophy. Last week two colleagues on my team got into a long argument during a design review, and I have not been able to settle who was right about what. I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

The context is this. We are building a system that performs multi-step software engineering tasks: given a repository and a ticket, it produces a plan, edits files, runs checks, and iterates until the requested change is implemented. During training, we need to give the model feedback, and the dispute was about where that feedback should attach. One side argued for outcome-based supervision, in which we score only the final result; the other argued for process-based supervision, in which we also score the intermediate steps. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Ana, defending outcome-based supervision: We should score only the final result — does the implementation pass the tests and satisfy the ticket? The end product is the only thing a user or customer ultimately cares about. If we start labeling intermediate steps as good or bad, we are smuggling in our own assumptions about the "right" way to solve the problem, and we risk penalizing unorthodox but correct solutions. Step labels are also expensive and inconsistent across reviewers, which injects noise into the training signal.

(2) Ben, defending process-based supervision: Final-result feedback is too sparse and too permissive. Most long tasks fail at some later step; if we only say "the whole trajectory failed," the model gets no usable signal about which earlier step was the culprit, even when the first several steps were good. And outcome-only feedback will reinforce any solution that happens to slip through the final checks, even if the intermediate reasoning is wrong and would fail on the next case. Scoring each step gives a denser signal and teaches transferable procedure, not just test-passing.

(3) Ana: "Transferable procedure" is just a flattering name for "a procedure we happen to like." A step has no value independently of its downstream consequences; there is no standard for whether a step looks right except how often sequences containing it succeed. So outcomes are not a noisier alternative to process labels — they are the ground truth, and process labels are at best a lossy approximation of them. Worse, process labels are just as gameable: the model will learn to produce steps that human raters approve of without actually advancing the task. That is the same reward hacking you are accusing me of, moved one level up.

(4) Ben: A step label is not gameable in the same way once it is defined relationally. A step gets positive credit only if the plan it belongs to still has some feasible path to a successful result, and a rater can often judge that earlier and more cheaply than by waiting for a full trajectory to end. Besides, for the tasks we actually care about, there is no exhaustive final check we can write cheaply; a test suite and a ticket are partial heuristics for "the change the user really wanted." If we train only against a partial outcome check, the model will overfit to that check exactly where the check is most incomplete. Process feedback lets us correct a step that is heading toward the wrong result before the final check can.

(5) Ana: That only shows the final check was bad, not that the process itself matters. The remedy for a bad final check is a better final check — more tests, held-out reviewers, user acceptance — not a stack of intermediate judgments, each of which is itself an unvalidated check and would need its own supervision. And notice what you have just conceded: if a step is good exactly when it preserves some feasible path to a successful result, then "good step" is just an expected-value estimate of eventual success pushed back into the middle of a trajectory. That is outcome-based supervision implemented by hand. Process-based supervision only looks like a distinct position because it uses a human as the value estimator.

(6) Ben: You are treating "a better final check" as if it were always available, but that is precisely the situation in which we would not need to train the model to reason at all — we could just search against the oracle. The reason we scaffold with human feedback is that for open-ended engineering tasks, the evaluator is incomplete and we cannot yet specify success in advance. And there is a real asymmetry. Under outcome-only feedback, a model that solves the training cases through a flawed path is fully reinforced and then fails silently on new cases. Under process feedback, the flawed step is flagged before the trajectory completes, so the model cannot cash in the false positive. That asymmetry remains even if both signals are imperfect proxies.

Neither would concede, and I could see the force of both positions. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say what it establishes and whether it is sound, overstated, or mistaken, and why. Then identify the most important consideration that neither engineer raised, and state which position is more defensible overall.
