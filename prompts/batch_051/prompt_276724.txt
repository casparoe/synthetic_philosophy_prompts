I'm in a seminar on the philosophy of legal proof this semester, and the unit on naked statistical evidence has left me more confused than convinced, which I gather is sort of the point. You probably know the two stock examples: the blue bus case, where a pedestrian is hit by a bus and the only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that, say, four out of every five buses on that route belong to them, and the gatecrasher case, where a thousand people attend a rodeo, only four hundred and ninety-nine pay, and a randomly selected attendee gets sued for the ticket price. Almost everyone, including people who are pretty hard-nosed about probability, feels uneasy about letting the plaintiff win the blue bus case or finding the gatecrasher liable, even though the numbers clear "more likely than not" without any trouble. The strange thing is that people who share that unease explain it in completely different ways, and the explanations pull in different directions. Some say the whole problem is the burden of proof: a bare statistic leaves a specific doubt about this defendant, so it flunks proof beyond a reasonable doubt and would be totally fine on a preponderance standard. Others point out that the blue bus case stays unsettling even though it's civil, so the burden-of-proof story can't be the whole story. Some argue statistical evidence isn't really "about" this defendant—it's evidence about a class or a base rate, not about the particular event or person, so it fails some requirement of individualized proof or blame. Others reply that all evidence is ultimately about classes and frequencies anyway, so that requirement is either mysterious or incoherent. And still others think the issue is what verdicts do socially: they have to be something the public and the parties can accept, and a table of frequencies can't do that kind of work.

What I can't figure out is why this debate has been going in circles for so long. My working hypothesis is that its not one debate but three or four debates sharing a name: one about epistemology, one about moral blame, and one about what a trial is for. But I'm not confident in that, and I don't know wether the impasse would survive the distinctions. So I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "proof," a clash of bedrock intuitions about fairness that neither side can argue for, or something about which examples the two sides silently treat as data? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a formal result—or should I expect the queasiness to just stay unargued?
