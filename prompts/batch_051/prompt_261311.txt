I've been playing with a way of thinking about scapegoat rituals and I want it stress-tested, not just validated. I'm calling it moral debt consolidation. Start from the observation that after a disaster or a run of bad luck, a community's guilt is usually distributed and unpayable: lots of people are each a little responsible, or the causal chain is too tangled for anyone to know their share, so the blame just circulates and festers. My thought is a scapegoat ritual works by consolidating all that diffuse, contested moral debt into a single instrument — one person or animal gets designated as the bearer of the whole community's guilt, and then killing or expelling that bearer settles the debt the way retiring a bond settles a balance sheet. The transfer is the mechanism, not the violence. This would also explain why the victim so often has to be marginal or innocent: you need a clean vehicle with no outstanding greivances of its own, because if the victim already had claims against the group, the ritual would reopen the ledger rather than zero it out. I know this overlaps with functionalist and catharsis accounts, and with the standard scapegoat-theory stuff about the arbitrary victim, but I think the consolidation-and-settlement idea is doing different work. Where does the analogy actually break? Give me the three strongest objections, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
