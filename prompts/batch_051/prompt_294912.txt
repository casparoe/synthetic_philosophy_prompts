Me and my friend got into a long argument last night about whether a finite sequence of coin flips can ever be called random. She kept insisting randomness is only a property of the generating process: a fair coin can produce HHHHHHHHHH and that run isn't any less random than a mixed-looking string, it's just as likely. I pushed back that some finite strings clearly have less structure — 0101010101 has a two word description and a string from random.org doesn't — but she said that's about compressibility, not randomness, and anyway every particular string is astronomically unlikely. By the end we had about five different senses of the word on the table and no idea which one either of us actually held, so I want to make a "what do you actually mean by 'random'?" quiz for my blog.

Here's what I have in mind. Write me a sorting quiz with somewhere between eight and ten questions. Each question should present a concrete finite sequence or a short situation involving one (say, the first twenty digits of pi, a string from a quantum random number generator, a string from a pseudo-random generator, an all-zeros string that genuinely came out of a fair coin, a string that is provably the output of a short program but looks patternless, that sort thing) and give two or three answer options about whether the person would call it random and why. Then provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto at least these positions: randomness as a property of the generating process, randomness as incompressibility of the sequence (Kolmogorov complexity style), randomness as passing every effective statistical test (Martin-Löf style), randomness as subjective unpredictability relative to what an agent knows, and the view that no finite sequence is intrinsically random, only processes or infinite sequences can be. If some of those positions can't actually be told apart by any cases you can think of, say so and merge them rather than forcing a fake separation. The quiz should sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like, so none of the questions should ask anything like whether randomness lives in the sequence or in the process. Build it from the cases outward: first figure out which strings or scenarios would force the positions apart, then write the questions around those, then say which answer combinations correspond to which position. I'd also like you to flag answer combinations that don't map onto any defended position and give a one-line guess about why nobody lives there — because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has bothered.

A couple of format things. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Numbering the questions inline is fine, but describe the scoring key in paragraphs rather than laying it out like a spreadsheet. And please don't assume my readers know any of the jargon; the questions themselves should be plain, and the technical names can live in the scoring key. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the mapping from answer patterns to positions, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd also rather you think it through carefully before committing: approach it as if you're a researcher in the foundations of probability and algorithmic information theory, brainstorm the concrete cases first, and only then settle on the questions and the key.
