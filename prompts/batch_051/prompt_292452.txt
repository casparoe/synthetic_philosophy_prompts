I've been reading Mill's Chapters on Socialism. There's a bit near the start where he argues—I'm paraphrasing, not quoting—that the defects of private property might be the fault of how property rights have actually been distributed and protected rather than of private property itself, and that worker cooperation would need a fair test before we conclude socialism is better. That got me thinking about a concrete mechanism.

Suppose a government introduced a law: any firm with more than, say, 50 employees can be bought out by its workforce if two-thirds of the employees petition for it. The price is set by an independent valuer at roughly the market value of the firm as a going concern. The employees don't pay up front; instead the state covers the purchase through bonds, and the newly worker-owned firm pays back the bond out of profits over a fixed term. Until the bond is repaid, the workforce cannot sell their capital shares to outsiders and cannot dismiss employees except for gross misconduct. After the bond is repaid, the firm becomes an ordinary worker cooperative.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it incentivise among workers before they petition, among workers after the buyout, and among owners/managers who see it coming? How would it be gamed? What second-order effects appear on hiring, risk-taking, and access to credit? Are the failure modes fixable by tweaking the rule, or do they point to the deeper worries Mill had about self-management?

Also, if you think I'm misreading Mill or the scheme as posed is confused, just say so rather than playing along. And please write the answer in British English.
