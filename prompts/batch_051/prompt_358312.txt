Luck egalitarians like Dworkin used to lean on a clean division: if you chose the gamble and lost, that's option luck and the resulting inequality is on you; if something just landed on you, that's brute luck and you're owed compensation. That line made sense in a world where most bad breaks genuinely couldn't be seen coming. But now there are cheap genetic risk scores, continuous health data, predictive models for everything from heart attacks to loan defaults: more and more of what used to count as brute luck is foreseeable in advance. Does the luck egalitarian argument survive this shift? It looks like the brute luck / option luck joint is being pried apart, because the difference now may hinge on what a person could of found out rather than what they actually chose. Say which premise is under pressure, whether a repaired version can still do the original work, and whether the view collapses into something like "if you didn't run the numbers, you're on your own." I'm asking about the fate of the argument itself, not about what policy should follow. Write the answer in American English.
