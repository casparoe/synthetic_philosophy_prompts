I run the content team at a small education-technology company that produces short browser-based two-player finite games for university microeconomics courses. A new game ships roughly once a week, gets played by a few thousand students within the month, and is meant to make exactly one idea intuitive—backward induction, mixed-strategy equilibria, or the difference between common knowledge and mutual knowledge. Before anything ships, our junior designer (who has roughly a semester of game theory) does a sanity check that is currently a loose set of heuristics: find strictly dominant strategies, delete them, look again, and confirm the intended lesson is still visible. This has failed in an instructive way: we have shipped several games in which a strategy was strictly dominated only by a lottery over two or three other strategies, not by any pure strategy, and the check missed it. Students immediately found an apparently live strategy that no reasonable agent would ever play, and the exercise's point collapsed. I know, mostly from the usual rationalist-blog sequence material, that pure-strategy elimination is a special case and that mixed-strategy domination can delete strategies that no single pure strategy kills, but I do not know how to turn that knowledge into a daily procedure.

I want a reusable pre-shipment checklist that operationalizes iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies—including the case where the dominating object is a mixture over other strategies, applied round by round—that a junior designer can execute in about 45 minutes on a normal-form game with up to four players and six strategies each. The checklist should be operational rather than a compressed lecture: the tests in order, what counts as a pass, where the procedure stops, and where it must stop and hand the decision to our lead designer because the question has become a judgment call rather than a computation. It should show how to check whether a pure strategy is dominated by a mixture on the small games we actually see, with a worked inequality or linear-programming sketch simple enough that someone who has not rederived LP duality can follow it. It should flag the classic traps: deletion order when several dominated strategies are present at once; why the procedure should not lean on weak dominance; how to handle a strategy that becomes dominated only after an opponent's dominated strategy has already been removed; and what the checklist should say when nothing is dominated but some strategies are not rationalizable. Then run the procedure on at least three invented games: one in which a strategy survives pure-strategy elimination but falls to a 50/50 mixture; one in which the checklist returns a result we will not like because it deletes a trap option the designer wanted to keep; and one in which the procedure must escalate rather than compute, with the reason stated. Be explicit at the end about what the checklist cannot tell us—for instance whether the surviving game is fair, fun, or good at teaching the intended lesson.

I would rather you commit to one definite version of the procedure and defend the choices you make (why strict rather than weak dominance, why this order of tests rather than another) than lay out a menu and leave me to pick. Please write the answer in American English.
