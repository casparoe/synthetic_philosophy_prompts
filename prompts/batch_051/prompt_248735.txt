I write about science and ideas for a living, and I'm working on a story about how we think about small everyday risks. Could you write me an essay on whether driving is worth it, using the micromort frame — a micromort being a 1-in-a-million chance of dying. You don't need exact statistics; I care about the reasoning, not the arithmetic.

But I'd like you to go a bit into the weeds instead of giving the usual "driving is safer than people think" peice. Weigh whether treating micromorts as a common currency hides the fact that risk is distributed unevenly: the same expected number of deaths can come from a tiny risk spread over millions of people or a much larger risk concentrated on a few drivers, a particular road, or a particular time of night. Should that change the verdict on whether an ordinary commute is worth it? Also weigh the replacement-risk problem: if someone doesn't drive, they might walk, cycle, or take transit, and those carry risk too, so the right comparison isn't against zero risk. Weigh the main considerations, but don't end on a non-committal note — defend a verdict. Put your verdict in machine-readable form at the very end, as \answer{a short phrase or label}.
