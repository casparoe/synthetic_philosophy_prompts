My epistemology reading group has spent the better part of a year on the post-Gettier problem, and the discussion has started to feel circular. We all accept that a justified true belief can fall short of knowledge; we also accept that each standard repair fails: adding a no-false-lemma condition, adding a defeasibility condition, replacing justification with reliability or truth-tracking or safety, causal theories, and virtue-theoretic accounts all meet counterexamples, often of exactly the kind the condition was meant to exclude. From that shared starting point, the group splits in two. One camp thinks the lesson is that knowledge cannot be given a reductive analysis at all: our verdicts about cases are too disorderly, and the search for an extra condition is chasing a mirage. The other camp thinks the lesson is only that the right extra condition has not yet been stated, and that each failed analysis is not a sign that the project is misconceived but a normal step in it. What puzzles me is that neither camp disputes the data appealed to by the other; they just draw opposite conclusions from the same data. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about which proposed analysis of knowledge is true, but about what an analysis of knowledge is supposed to do — in particular, whether it must preserve our verdicts on hypothetical cases, or must capture what the concept does in licensing assertion, guiding action, and grounding testimony. Is that the right diagnosis? If so, what kind of disagreement is it — verbal, methodological, or a clash of brute intuitions about which considerations count as evidence — and is there anything, such as a new kind of case, a formal constraint, or a rephrased question, that could move the two camps? If not, what better account explains why the debate stays stuck? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of attempted analyses.
