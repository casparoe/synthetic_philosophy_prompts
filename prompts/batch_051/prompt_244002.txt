I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, an English idealist writing around the turn of the twentieth century, keeps a commonplace book of poems she uses to think through arguments before she puts them in prose. One of the entries is a poem about what later came to be called Bradley's regress, and I would like you to write that poem.

Make it a villanelle: nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, on two rhymes, with the first line and the third line recurring as refrains, alternating through the body of the poem and coming together in the final couplet. The form should do the arguing, not decorate it. One refrain should state the unifying move, the thought that a relation or tie is what joins the terms of a fact or proposition into one whole — that a's being F, or a's standing in R to b, is held together by the relation or instantiation that binds its constituents. The other refrain should state the regress-step: if that tie is now another constituent alongside the terms, what unites the tie to its terms? And whatever new tie is supplied, the same question returns. Let the two refrains alternate the way the dialectic alternates between supplying a unifying relation and finding that the relation has become merely another term waiting to be unified.

The middle tercets should render that dialectic exactly, without merely gesturing at it. A reader who knows the problem should recognize its shape: the unity one starts with, its decomposition into terms and a relating element, the relating element's collapse back into the collection it was meant to unify, the threatened infinite ascent, and one of the standard responses — that the relation is not a third thing over and above its terms, that unity is primitive and internal to the whole, or that the regress is not vicious because it simply unfolds a structure that was already achieved. The final couplet, where the two refrains stand side by side, should do genuine philosophical work: close the regress, or refuse to close it in a way that shows why it matters. The poem should be real verse — controlled meter, concrete imagery, no jargon as padding — and should sound like a sharp idealist's notebook poem rather than a versified handout.
