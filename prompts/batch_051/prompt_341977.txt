My father died earlier this year after a short illness, conscious and lucid almost to the end. In the months since, I have noticed that my grief keeps turning on a question I cannot answer cleanly: whether what happened was bad for him, not merely for those of us who survive him, and if it was, in virtue of what. I have read enough of the debate around this question to know the familiar positions — the Epicurean claim that death is nothing to the one who dies, the deprivation account, the idea that some harms are posthumous, the Lucretian symmetry problem — but the positions seem to come apart less in their slogans than in how they handle particular cases, and my own reactions to those cases are too inconsistent for me to trust my self-description.

I would like you to build a diagnostic instrument for me: either a decision tree whose branch nodes are concrete yes-or-no questions, or a quiz of eight to ten questions with a scoring key. Every question should present a concrete case and ask what I say about it, phrased so that someone can answer without already knowing the labels of the positions; no question should simply ask which view I endorse. The instrument should be usable by an intelligent reader without formal training in philosophy, so any technical vocabulary should either be avoided or introduced through the cases themselves.

Build it around the following cases: (1) a person who dies instantly and painlessly in his sleep without warning, whose life would otherwise have continued comfortably and in good health for years; (2) a person already in a permanent, irreversible coma, whose body is kept alive for several months before he dies; (3) a scholar whose sole manuscript, containing his life's work, is destroyed after his death, before he could have known of the destruction; (4) the length of time a person did not exist before he was born, and whether a shorter prenatal nonexistence would have made his life go better; (5) an infant who dies a week after birth; and (6) a person whose remaining years, if he survived, would be filled with pain and progressive dementia. These cases must actually drive the branching or the scoring; they are not optional illustrations.

Organize the instrument around the following three dividing lines: (a) whether a person can be harmed by something he never experiences; (b) whether a life's going well for the person is a matter of experienced quality of life, or of the satisfaction of desires, projects, and interests; and (c) whether the badness of death, if there is any, attaches to the state of being dead or to the deprivation of further life.

The leaves should include at least: the unqualified Epicurean view that death is never bad for the one who dies; a hedonist version of the deprivation account; a desire- or project-satisfaction version of the deprivation account; a posthumous-harm view on which events after death determine how well a person's life went; and the Lucretian symmetry view in both of its forms, on which either both prenatal and posthumous nonexistence are bad for a person, or neither is. Add any further leaves the underlying logical space requires. Each leaf should carry the standard name of the position if it has one, or a descriptive label if it does not, and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first set out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions among those combinations. Where a combination is unoccupied, say so and briefly note whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because no one has thought to defend it. Flag any two positions that your instrument cannot separate, and say what further question would separate them. If the tree or scoring key cannot reach a position I listed, say so rather than forcing it.
