Okay so I've been going down a rabbit hole on conditionals and probability for this paper I'm writing, and I feel like I've talked myself into a corner and I don't know which part to give up. The nice idea is Adams's thesis (I might be spelling that wrong): the probability of an indicative conditional like "if I flip this fair coin, it'll land heads" should just be the conditional probability P(heads | I flip), so 1/2. That seems super natural. When someone says "there's a 90% chance that if we leave now we make the movie," they don't seem to be doing anything weird, they're just sort of reporting P(make the movie | leave now).

But then there's Lewis's triviality proof, and as far as I can follow it, the idea is this. Suppose "if A then C" is an ordinary proposition with a genuine probability, and suppose the equation P(if A then C) = P(C|A) is supposed to hold for all A and C, and to keep holding after you conditionalize on C or on not-C. Then you can apparently derive P(if A then C | C) = 1 and P(if A then C | not-C) = 0. Plug those into the law of total probability and you get P(if A then C) = P(C). But the original equation said it equals P(C|A). So P(C|A) would have to equal P(C) for any old A and C, meaning they'd always be independent. That's absurd, so something in the setup has to go.

The part I'm stuck on is what exactly the theorem is telling me. My friend says the upshot is that people are just systematically irrational when they assign probabilities to if-sentences, because there's no coherent number that answers to "the probability of a conditional." But that feels way too strong: conditional probability itself is perfectly fine mathematically, and ordinary people don't run into any contradiction when they say "if the car's out of gas, then we're not getting home tonight." So is the right takeaway that "if A then C" doesn't express a regular proposition at all, or that Adams's thesis needs to be restricted, or what? Basically, does the math actually show that people are doing something wrong, or dosen't it just show that a particular way of modeling their judgments is impossible?
