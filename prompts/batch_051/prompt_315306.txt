ok so I've been trying to sort out performance legitimacy — the idea that prosperity, order, and competent government can legitimate a regime — for a comparative politics seminar on East and Southeast Asia, and I'd like a consistency check on where I've landed before I present. Here's roughly the set of views I'm walking in with:

(a) Legitimacy is a normative notion, not just a sociological one: a regime is legitimate only if its subjects actually have a reason to comply with it, not merely because they happen to obey or tell pollsters they're satisfied.

(b) Singapore looks legitimate to me, or close to it. If I grew up there and got the housing, safety, schools, jobs, and clean administration, I'd probably regard the arrangement as fine rather than spend my life resenting the PAP's paternalism.

(c) But China's system doesn't look legitimate to me, even though the same performance argument — growth, poverty reduction, stability, competent one-party rule — seems to apply at larger scale. I worry the difference is just that China is bigger and scarier, which isn't a principled distinction.

(d) I also think preferences adapt: in a system that has never permitted a real alternative, professed support from surveys is weak evidence of genuine consent. It barely counts, as evidence goes.

(e) But I don't want to be the kind of liberal who sets the bar absurdly high: if people's lives go better under an arrangement, and they themselves judge that their lives have gotten better, that has to count for something even when their options were constrained.

(f) Some things can't be bought off by GDP: jailing dissidents, muzzling the press, extrajudicial detention, using the courts to crush opponents — these are wrong even when growth is real.

(g) I'm consequentialist in my policy instincts generally, but when I defend democracy I reach for procedural, not outcome-based, grounds: accountability, the chance to remove leaders without violence.

Does this hang together? I can see some friction but I'm not sure wich bits are the real problem.
