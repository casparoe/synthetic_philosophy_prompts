I spent a long time last night arguing with a friend about whether the time-average/ensemble-average distinction at the core of ergodicity economics is a genuine challenge to expected-utility decision theory or merely risk aversion dressed up in logarithmic utility. We kept talking past each other, and I came away convinced that the real difficulty lies less in the mathematics than in the order in which the ideas are introduced. Next month I have to lead a ninety-minute session on this for a reading group of quantitatively literate PhD students, none of whom is an economist and none of whom has any stake in the decision-theory controversy. Do not explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it to them. In particular: what must be in place before the ensemble average and the time average can even be distinguished, and what depends on what? What single example should carry the whole session—what features must it have and what must it avoid—and why is it better than the familiar multiplicative coin-flip, or is the coin-flip still the right vehicle if framed correctly? Which misconceptions should I expect this audience to form, at which moment in the sequence should each be headed off, and what is the full map beyond the ones I already anticipate: that the point is only about risk aversion, that it is only the familiar gap between arithmetic and geometric means, and that ergodicity is more or less stationarity under another name? What should I openly decline to formalize at this level, and how should I phrase that refusal? Finally, what one question should I ask at the end to tell whether they have understood the distinction itself rather than merely computed the right number?
