I have a friend who works on why social norms die, and I've been collecting her takes on the classic cases. She says dueling in Britain didn't end because the arguments got better; it ended because middle-class men copying the duel made it look ridiculous, so refusing a challenge stopped being a status loss. She says footbinding collapsed because modernizing Chinese elites and foreign powers made bound feet a national embarrassment, not because health campaigns persuaded anyone. She says the old ban on charging interest faded once rich merchant families made lending respectable, even pious. I can't tell whether her actual rule is a status-payoff story (a norm dies when its performers stop getting status and start looking absurd) or a material-interest story (the people who police the norm flip when it stops paying), since both fit everything she's said.

Now I want to know what she'd say about the norm in professional firms that serious people, especially men, don't take full parental leave. The material interests of the firms have started to flip: they're losing mid-career people and worrying about attrition and liability. But taking the full leave still gets you quietly mocked, and no high-status senior partner has done it publicly, so the norm hasn't visibly budged. Would she predict it collapses soon or stalls for now? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and what extra case would best tell the status-payoff rule apart from the material-interest rule?
