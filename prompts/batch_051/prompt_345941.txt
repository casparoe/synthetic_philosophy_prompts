I'm teaching a unit on ancient theories of religion and want a sorting exercise for my students, on euhemerism broadly — the idea that the gods were originally outstanding humans who got deified. Please build a decision tree (or a "which euhemerist are you?" quiz with a scoring key) that sorts positions on this. I don't want it organized around a list of names; I want the branches to be the claims that actually do the separating. Start from the question of what the claim is supposed to explain: the historical origin of the god, the psychological process that makes communities call someone a god, the political function of the deification, or the literary/mythographic process that turns a biography into a god's cult story. Then make the tree separate at least these: deification during life versus after death; one-off apotheosis versus a regular social institution (like imperial cult); the human original is still remembered in the stories versus fully replaced by a natural force or personification; and euhemerism advanced as a debunking reduction versus advanced in a way that leaves the cult's value mostly intact. Where possible, write the diagnostic questions as concrete cases: "If archeologists found a tomb with 'Zeus son of Cronus' inscribed in Crete, would that confirm the core euhemerist claim?" or "Does the Roman Senate's vote to deify a dead emperor count as the same mechanism Euhemerus describes, or a different one?" Each leaf should get a short label if there is one — hard euhemerism, soft euhemerism, allegorical euhemerism, Euhemerus's own royal-benefactor version, Lactantius's apologetic version, imperial-apotheosis model, reverse euhemerism — or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it, plus one sentence on that leaf's most awkward commitment. Also mark adjacent positions the tree can't tell apart and say why, and flag empty branches with a quick diagnosis: is nobody there because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? Aim for something I can use with undergrads who've read only excerpts of Euhemerus and Lactantius; the point is to force them to locate themselves by their own answers, not by which label sounds nice.
