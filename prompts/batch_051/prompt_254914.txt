I keep hearing people say that a perfectly rational agent is logically omniscient: if it believes P, and P entails Q, then it believes Q too, or at least is committed to Q. Nobody means a real person by that, it's supposed to be an idealization. But I want to know whether this is a testable claim about actual systems—a person, a language model, maybe a theorem prover—or whether it's just a stipulative definition of "rational" with no empirical content.

So here's the question. Suppose I give a model a bunch of random facts, like "all wugs are zibbers" and "no zibber is a plonk," and then later ask it "are all wugs plonks?" It gets the right answer. Is that evidence that it has something like logical closure over its beliefs, or just evidence that it can do a local inference task when prompted? Design the best test you can to distinguish a system that's actually closing its beliefs under logical consequence from one that's just good at answering the particular questions you happen to ask it. Be concrete about what you would measure and what result would count for the strong claim. Then be honest about what even a positive result could not settle—for example, whether the system "believes" the conclusions in the right way, or whether the idealization is doing any real work.
