I've been going back and forth on the repugnant markets stuff for years — kidney sales, vote selling, price gouging, paid queue-jumping — and I've ended up with a specific version of the anti-commodification view that I want to stress-test. The version I hold is this: for at least some goods, a fully consensual exchange that makes both parties better off in the moment can still be objectionable, because the exchange doesn't just move the good around; it teaches the participants and the bystanders to value the good in a different way, and that can quietly erode the background norms that made the good function in the first place. I don't think the wrongness is intrinsic to buying and selling, and I don't think it's just that the trade feels gross. I'm committed to the idea that it's a causal, spillover claim about what happens downstream to non-participants. The standard rationalist-ish response is that if both parties consent and no third party is harmed, my corruption-of-meaning talk is just aesthetics, and I think I've answered that — but I've realized I've never actually listed what would falsify me.

So treat the view as a live empirical hypothesis rather than a slogan, and tell me what would count against it. What would have to be true for it to be false, or badly in need of amendment? Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Order them from fatal down to merely awkward, and for each one say whether it's a live possibility or just a logical escape hatch, and whether a honest defender of my position should concede it as a real blow or can swat it away without cost. Be genuinely adversarial — I'd rather find the weak points now than keep defending something that's insulated against everything.
