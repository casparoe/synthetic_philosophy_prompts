I run the control-review group at an AI lab. Before a model is connected to a new tool, data source, or external API, my reviewers assess the safety measures proposed for that deployment. Our team works under an explicit assumption: the model may be misaligned — its goals may diverge from ours, and it may be actively and adaptively trying to defeat our measures while producing output that looks cooperative. We are not doing alignment work, which tries to get the model to share our goals; our job is to determine what holds even if alignment has failed. I want you to turn that principle into a reusable procedure.

We see two or three reviews a month. A failure is high stakes but likely to be silent: a successful subversion may not be detected until after the model has already done something harmful or exfiltrated information. My reviewers have solid ML backgrounds and can read code, but they are not alignment specialists, and the output of each review goes to a deployment lead who is not an alignment researcher and who has to make a clear sign-off decision.

Please write a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — that my reviewers can run on each proposed measure. The procedure should translate "assume the model may be misaligned and is trying to defeat the measure" into a specific sequence of questions, each with a standard for what counts as a sufficient answer. It should say where the procedure stops with a hard reject, where it can stop with a pass, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is a values or judgment call rather than something a checklist can settle. Assume two days per review and a modest red-team budget — a few hours to try to break the measure ourselves before deciding. Do not give me a survey of AI safety or a reading list; I need a runnable artifact I can hand to a reviewer.

The procedure must handle the hard cases: measures whose security depends on the model not knowing they exist; measures that are themselves implemented by models; measures that detect a problem without containing it; measures that work in evaluation but break under optimization pressure; and measures that assume the model will not coordinate with other instances, will not use side channels, or will not manipulate the human reviewer. Where the distinction between control and alignment gets dropped, flag it.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical measures and show exactly what it returns: (1) an output monitor that scores each completion and blocks those that look like dangerous code or instructions; (2) a sandbox in which the model can execute generated code without network access, with stdout returned to a human integrator who may copy useful code into a product; (3) a honeypot — a secret canary string embedded in the system prompt with the rule that, if the string appears anywhere else, we treat that as evidence of a leak and of possible collusion. Include at least one application where the procedure produces an answer I will not like, and explain why it produces that answer.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the failure modes it cannot exclude, the information it would need but will not have, and the steps in the procedure itself that are most fragile. State how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
