Over the break I got into an argument with a geologist friend about the Anthropocene, and I've been chewing on it since. Her line was that the Anthropocene isn't just a political slogan or a vague way of saying humans have altered the planet; it's an empirical claim about Earth history, the kind of thing future geologists millions of years from now would be able to read straight out of the rock record. I want that to be right, but I can't figure out how much of it is actually testable. Obviously human activity leaves physical traces: carbon isotope shifts, plastic particles, radionuclides from bomb tests, changes in what gets fossilized. But every boundary in deep time is also partly a decision, isn't it? Someone had to decide that the Jurassic starts at that horizon and not a few meters lower, and the choice is as much about convenience and tradition as it is about the rocks themselves.

So here's what I want. Design the best test you can of the claim that we are genuinely in a new interval of Earth history, not just that we've rearranged some chemistry near the surface. What observations would count for and against the claim, concretely? And be honest about what the data could never settle: which parts of "the Anthropocene is real" would remain a matter of convention, politics, or taste in classification even after the best possible result comes in?
