I'm on the ethics committee for our robotics lab, and a clause I helped write is finally being invoked, in a situation we absolutely did not have in mind. The clause says: 'Any artificial system that exhibits persistent, goal-directed avoidance of states that the system evaluates negatively shall be treated as having interests for the purposes of this policy, and no further experimentation involving such a system may proceed without the committee's approval.'

Here's the case. We have a robot-arm policy trained by imitation from human teleoperation. The human operator always steered around workstation C, because C used to pinch; so the learned policy now also avoids C—it takes a much longer route, hesitates at the same corner, sometimes doubles back—even when C is empty and powered down. There is no reward function, no cost map, no explicit value representation anywhere in the system. Its literally next-frame prediction plus a learned policy. In one sense the system is a textbook case of what the clause was meant to catch: persistent, goal-directed avoidance. But the phrase 'evaluates negatively' seems to demand an internal negative valuation, and this thing doesn't have one, unless you count the fact that the avoidance is encoded in the weights.

Our disagreement: one faction reads the clause as covering behavior only—avoidance is the test, and inner mechanisms don't matter. Another reads it as requiring some actual evaluation, which the imitation-trained policy lacks, so the clause doesn't reach it even if the behavior looks aversive. I keep going back and forth, because the words cut one way and the whole point of the policy cuts the other.

So: does the clause apply? Argue it from the plain text, from the purpose of the clause, from what we (the drafters) would have said if we'd been asked about imitation-trained systems, and from what a reasonable member of the lab would have understood the policy to promise. Then tell me which approach should win here and why, and what that means for whether we can keep running the experiment.
