I lead a small applied machine-learning team at a mid-size company; we build ranking, recommendation, and fraud models, most of which are retrained weekly. A recurring situation: someone on the team returns from a paper or a blog post with an optimization technique imported from evolutionary biology — a population-based initialization scheme, a mutation step layered on top of stochastic gradient descent, a genetic search over a discrete subcomponent, or a diversity penalty meant to keep models spread across different loss basins. The suggestions are never absurd; they are easy enough to prototype that refusing them feels arbitrary; and the cost of a serious trial is several engineer-days plus the risk of slipping a retraining cycle. In our experience, most of these lose to a carefully tuned standard optimizer, but occasionally one has helped, so a blanket ban would also be wrong.

I would like you to turn what is known about natural selection considered as an optimizer — and specifically about the respects in which it differs from gradient-based learning — into a decision procedure my team can run in under an hour before committing to an experiment. It should be a reusable checklist or ordered set of questions: what features of our problem would give an evolution-style method a real chance (for example, a non-differentiable objective, discrete choices, deceptive or very sparse feedback, or a pressing need for exploration rather than exploitation), what features should rule one out early, what minimal evidence or cheap test we should demand before authorizing a full trial, and which steps cannot be mechanized and remain a person's judgment call. Then run the procedure on three concrete proposals: (1) using a genetic search to select among a discrete set of feature-engineering operations inside an otherwise differentiable pipeline; (2) adding small Gaussian mutations to the weights of an already Adam-trained model and accepting every mutation that lowers validation loss; (3) beginning each hyperparameter search with a small population of configurations and reallocating trials toward the better-scoring ones. I expect the procedure to reject at least one of these, and I would rather hear that honestly than have the procedure adjusted to approve all three. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
