I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, not an academic, so be patient if my terminology is a bit off. I've been going down a rabbit hole on the old money-pump argument for transitivity: if your preferences go in a circle — A over B, B over C, C over A — a bookie can trade you around the loop and return you to where you started, minus a fee each time. The standard line is that this shows rational preferences can't cycle.

I've been calling my objection "choice scarring." The money-pump argument seems to assume the preference map stays put while you walk around it. But making a choice can change what you want next. Suppose I prefer coffee to tea, tea to juice, and juice to coffee in the snapshot. The bookie starts by charging me a little to swap tea for juice. Once I've actually drunk the juice, the next leg of the cycle might not go through — after juice, I no longer prefer coffee to tea, so I stop the pump right there. The cycle only exists before any choices are made. Acting on one edge of the loop scars over the other edges, so the loop doesn't actually close. If that's right, the real norm isnt "have transitive preferences over options." It's something weaker: dont leave a closed loop through a fixed preference map that you will actually walk. And the standard bridge from "no cycles" to full expected utility maximization starts to look shakier too.

I'm sure this has been noticed before under some other name. So: is "choice scarring" a real distinction, or am I just quarantining the cycle in the history of the chooser — which a textbook decision theorist would say is the same thing as changing the outcomes you're choosing between? Where does the idea break? In particular, doesn't it just let someone rationalize any costly walk around a cycle by saying their preferences changed, and if so, is there a patch that doesn't collapse back into the usual account? Please act as a philosopher of decision theory, not a cheerleader. Start by steelmanning the idea and brainstorming the hidden assumptions of the money-pump argument, then tell me candidly what it gets right and where it stops being distinctive.
