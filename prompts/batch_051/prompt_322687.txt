I'm teaching a unit on time in a film-and-philosophy class and I keep going back and forth on Arrival (2016). On it's face the movie looks like a textbook dramatization of eternalism: once Louise internalizes the heptapod language, she stops experiencing time as a moving now and starts seeing her past, present, and future as all there at once, the way the heptapods see a sentence as a whole rather than word by word. That is basically the block-universe picture — all times equally real, and the sense of passage as just something about how human minds are built (or about a moving spotlight sweeping over an already-fixed block, which is a slightly different view). But I worry the film runs together two distinct claims: that Louise can know or remember the future (an epistemic thing) and that the future already exists waiting for her (a metaphysical thing). And the free will material makes it murkier: she knows her daughter will die young, she knows Ian will leave, and she still has the kid, and the movie seems to want that to read as tragic aceptance rather than fatalism or determinism, but maybe that's just a confusion the plot is exploiting.

Write me a reading of the film through the presentism/eternalism debate. Where does Arrival dramatize eternalism in an honest, illuminating way, and where does it trade on a slide from seeing the future to the future being settled in a way that presentism would deny? Use the classroom scenes or the ending if they help, but don't just summarize. I want you to be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of a philosophical slogan, and don't force the philosophy into the film where it doesn't fit. In particular, point out if any scene only makes sense on presentism, or if the film ends up endorsing something more like fatalism than eternalism. And say whether the movie adds anything to the debate that a straight argument wouldn't — or whether it mostly makes a tempting mistake vivid.
