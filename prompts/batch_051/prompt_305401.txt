I've been reading around in teleosemantics for a term paper, and I hit on a way of thinking about misrepresentation that I keep wanting to defend. Everyone frames the problem as tracking: the frog's fly-detector fires for flies, but a dark speck or a shot pellet can set it off too, and then we have to say why the content isn't just "fly-or-pellet-or-shadow" — that's the disjunction problem. I want to say content isn't fixed by what the detector tracks; it's fixed by what the downstream system is willing to spend on it, which I've been calling representational credit. The fly-detector means fly because the tongue-flick system bets on actual flies — the sensorimotor loop is staked on that outcome the way you can only spend money you've actually been advanced. On my telling, misrepresentation isn't tracking failure or malfunction. It's overdrawing the credit: the frog snaps at a pellet and the representation spends fly-credit where the world only had pellet on offer. This is supposed to block the disjunction problem, because the frog never earned credit for the disjunction "fly-or-pellet" — no downstream consumer is built to cash it out.

I know this needs alot of work. The obvious worry is that "what the downstream system bets on" just smuggles back the very modal facts naturalism was supposed to explain (what it's designed to do, the conditions where it would fail). When I tighten this up, is it just consumer semantics — the Millikan-style idea that content is fixed by what the representation's consumers need from it — with a finance metaphor bolted on? Or does the credit idea actually decide cases about content and misrepresentation that the etiological-function story can't already decide? And where does it collapse first: is "credit" doing any work beyond redescription?
