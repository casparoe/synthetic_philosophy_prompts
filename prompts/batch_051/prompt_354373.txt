I spent last night arguing with a friend about whether the international anti-corruption consensus is a Western export dressed up as a neutral principle. My friend kept insisting that what outside observers call clientelism — a politician finding jobs for supporters, a businessperson giving an official an extravagant gift, a party distributing benefits to loyalists — is often, for the people inside those relationships, a matter of loyalty, reciprocity, or ordinary care for one's own. I wanted to insist that some things are corruption no matter how a community describes them. But after the argument, I kept turning over a pattern in our own institutions that I could not stop puzzling about.

Consider how one system treats three things at once. A civil servant in a procurement office may not accept a modest gift from a firm competing for a contract; doing so is treated as corruption, sometimes criminally. The same firm may legally donate large sums to the election campaigns of the politicians who oversee the agency, write the procurement rules, and shape the contract; politicians solicit these donations as a routine part of politics. And once the civil servant or politician leaves office, the same firm may, after only modest restrictions, hire them to lobby their former colleagues. There is no secrecy in any of this: all three are ordinary features of the institutional design.

I want to know what theory of corruption this pattern presupposes. On its face, it seems to presuppose that corruption is essentially a direct personal exchange — a discrete benefit given for a discrete official act — while ongoing structural influence through campaign finance or the revolving door is not corruption at all but legitimate political participation. Does the pattern also presuppose a particular ideal of the official as an impersonal applier of rules, and is that the very ideal my friend would call parochially Western? Or is the practice compatible with several rival theories, including a relational or clientelist view on which holding office is partly the legitimate management of personal obligations? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as the practice will bear, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify the places where its parts presuppose inconsistent things. Then say what would have to change in our institutions if we replaced the implicit theory with, on one side, a relational/clientelist conception of public office, and on the other, a strict impartiality-based conception that applied to campaign contributions and the revolving door the same logic it already applies to a gift to a civil servant.
