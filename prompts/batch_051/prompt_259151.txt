You are a philosopher working at the intersection of environmental ethics, the philosophy of risk, and conservation biology. Think carefully and take your time. I would appreciate it if you began by brainstorming several candidate reconstructions, then told me which parts of the practice force a particular commitment, which parts are merely compatible with several theories, and where the practice is simply underdetermined. If the honest answer is that the practice has no single coherent implicit theory, say so and explain where the incoherence lives.

I am a PhD student in conservation biology, and I want to understand what commitments—about value, reversibility, naturalness, responsibility, or something else—the current governance of two kinds of intervention presupposes.

The contrast is this. On one side, gene drives are treated as a novel class of risk. Suppression drives are being developed to crash invasive rodent populations on seabird islands, and modification drives to make malaria mosquitoes refractory to the parasite. Because a drive is a self-propagating genetic construct that can, in principle, spread through an entire interbreeding population from a few released individuals, even a small island field trial is treated as a decision with planetary stakes. Before any release would be countenanced, the community expects molecular containment, a credible recall or reversal mechanism, genuinely local consent, and in many quarters something close to a moratorium; the prevailing thought seems to be that no actor should unilaterally set in motion a change it cannot stop.

On the other side, de-extinction is governed far more permissively. Serious projects aim to produce a cold-adapted Asian elephant as a woolly-mammoth proxy, a modified band-tailed pigeon as a passenger-pigeon proxy, and a thylacine proxy built from museum-specimen genomes and edited cells of living marsupials. The plan, in the public-facing versions at least, is to establish self-sustaining populations on landscapes whose ecology has adjusted to their absence. A breeding population of large herbivores in the tundra is not obviously more recallable than a gene drive; it too could reshape vegetation, permafrost, and fire regimes; and it too would be, for practical purposes, irreversible. Yet de-extinction proceeds on something like the ordinary conservation-breeding model: privately funded, framed as rewilding or restorative justice rather than as a global-risk decision, with no comparable demand for reversal criteria, global consent, or a moratorium.

The same community, in other words, treats a self-spreading mosquito construct as too consequential for any one actor while treating the creation and release of a novel megafaunal species as a legitimate act of repair. What would have to be true for this asymmetry to be principled?

Candidate explanations occur to me, and I want them sorted rather than simply rehearsed. (1) The difference is reversibility: a drive continues on its own, whereas mammoth proxies can in principle be culled. But would that remain true of ten thousand widely dispersed large herbivores? (2) The difference is reparative duty: we owe the mammoth something because human ancestors exterminated the last ones. But humans also introduced the island rats, and no one in the field thinks that past introduction, by itself, settles the moral case for a suppression drive. (3) The difference is natural kind: the proxy counts as the lost species in a way a gene drive does not. But the proxy is an engineered organism with an incomplete nuclear genome, assembled in a surrogate, with edited alleles; it is not the extinct species. (4) The difference is killing versus creating: suppression drives kill many sentient individuals, while de-extinction mostly creates them. But that sits uncomfortably in a field that ordinarily treats species and ecosystems, rather than individual welfare, as the units of conservation value—except, apparently, when invasive-species management is on the table.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of environmental value, risk, or responsibility that the current pattern presupposes. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with. Then tell me what would change under two counterfactuals: first, a world in which de-extinction had to satisfy gene-drive-style precaution; second, a world in which gene drives were governed by de-extinction-style optimism. I am less interested in which policy you prefer than in which differences between the two cases are doing the real normative work, and whether the field's current treatment can survive that diagnosis.
