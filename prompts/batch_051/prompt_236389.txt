I'm a software engineer. I write probabilistic models for a living (mostly Bayesian networks, posterior inference, that kind of thing), and I'm used to thinking of events as sets of possible outcomes. Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole on conditionals, and I've hit a conflict I can't think my way out of.

On the one hand, I'm basically convinced by the idea that the probability of an indicative conditional just is the conditional probability. Like, when I think about "if the server gets a sudden traffic spike, it will fall over," the credence I assign to that sentence feels exactly like P(falls over | traffic spike). The "equation" P(if A then C) = P(C | A) seems almost too obvious to deny. It handles how I actually use conditionals under uncertainty, and it matches the way I'd implement conditional inference in code.

On the other hand, I have a rock-solid intuition that "if A then C" is a genuine proposition, something that's either true or false at each possible world. I can negate it, conjoin it, wonder whether it's true, store it as a boolean in a model. It doesn't feel like a special non-propositional thing.

Here's the problem. I saw Lewis's triviality proof, and it goes roughly like this. Suppose the equation holds for every probability function, including probability functions after conditioning on an event. And suppose "if A then C" is an ordinary proposition, so I can law-of-total-probability it like anything else. Pick any A and C with P(A∧C) > 0 and P(A∧¬C) > 0. Then:

P(if A then C) = P(if A then C | C) P(C) + P(if A then C | ¬C) P(¬C)
 = 1·P(C) + 0·P(¬C)
 = P(C).

But the equation says the left side equals P(C | A), so P(C | A) = P(C). That says the antecedent is never informative. Which is obviously false of most of the conditionals I care about.

So I can't keep both things I find compelling: conditionals-as-ordinary-propositions, and the equation P(if A then C) = P(C | A). I don't want a menu of positions and a "you pick." I want a committed answer. Which one should go, and why? Is the intuition that conditionals are truth-valued propositions the thing to abandon, or is the equation the thing to abandon? Or is there a precise move—like saying conditionals are propositional but context-sensitive, so the proof fails because the proposition shifts between the two conditioning steps—that lets both survive in some form? And what would defending each option actually cost? Give me a definite verdict with reasons, not a survey.
