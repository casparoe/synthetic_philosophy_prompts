I've been keeping a notebook lately in which I try to write down three things I'm certain of before bed, and the list has shrunk to almost nothing — which is how I ended up, in my mid-thirties, lying awake wondering how I know I exist at all. That unsettled me enough that I joined a small reading group working through Descartes's attempt to doubt everything, and at our last meeting two of the members, Tomas and Priya, got into a long argument about what exactly the cogito proves. They asked me to referee, since I'd been writing about the problem in my notebook, and I couldn't. The exchange went roughly like this.

(1) Tomas said Descartes had already seen how to doubt his body, the external world, and even the simplest claims of mathematics, since a malicious demon could be producing all of it. But even such a demon cannot make doubting occur without there being someone who doubts — being deceived requires a someone. So the act of doubting establishes the existence of something that thinks during that very act, and the cogito proves at least the existence of a thinking subject, whatever else it may prove.

(2) Priya replied that this is already a step too far. All the fact of doubting gives us, she said, is that thinking is occurring. The "I" — the subject, the thinker — is imported rather than found. She brought up the old objection, often associated with Lichtenberg, that Descartes is entitled at best to an impersonal formulation, the way we say that it is raining rather than that something rains. What the cogito proves is an episode of thought, not a thinker.

(3) Tomas answered that the rain analogy is misleading because doubting is not a mere process like weather; it is an act, and acts require agents. He conceded that the cogito does not yet show whether this agent is a persisting substance or only a momentary one, but he insisted it does secure some agent — enough to ground the claim that I exist at this instant. Ownerless acts, he argued, are stranger and harder to make sense of than the thin self he was defending.

(4) Priya came back that this "momentary agent" is a retreat, and she was not sure it is even available: if the demon can produce thoughts the way a storm produces lightning, each thought is an occurrence rather than something performed by an owner. But even granting the momentary agent, the "I" Descartes goes on to rely on is something stronger — a thinking thing that remains through time and can exist apart from any body. Nothing in the cogito licenses that, she said. So either the cogito proves the full thinker, or it proves something so minimal that calling it a self is misleading; what she doubted is that there is a stable middle.

They circled around these moves for another hour, and I have since written and erased three attempts at sorting it out. Please adjudicate the disagreement step by step. Open with a one-sentence overall verdict about who is more right, then assess (1) through (4) in order, saying for each move how strong it is and why, and finally identify what each of them fails to consider. Keep the entire response under 600 words.
