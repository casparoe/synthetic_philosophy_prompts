Okay, here's one. I'd like you to act as an expert philosopher who works on personal identity and Buddhist philosophy, and to take your time with it — first brainstorm two or three candidate cases, then pick the cleanest one and write it up carefully.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specification: a person who fully accepts Buddhist no-self (anatta) ends up taking better care of their future self than a person who holds a psychological-continuity theory of identity — the opposite of the usual direction, since on the standard story denying a self should undercut prudence while psychological continuity should ground it. The no-self person's prudence has to fall out of their actual view plus ordinary concern for suffering; it must not come from them secretly believing in a self, being inconsistent, or appealing to rebirth, karma, or anything supernatural. Keep the scenario realistic (a medical, financial, or career case an ordinary person could face), and keep it to one paragraph. Both characters should agree on all the non-philosophical facts; they differ only in what follows about what the person should do.

To make sure we're working with the same ideas: by anatta I mean the view that there is no unchanging owner or essence behind experience, and that a person is just a conventional label for a causally connected bundle of physical and mental processes. By psychological-continuity theory I mean the view that my stake in a future person is grounded in overlap of memories, intentions, character traits, and the like.

After the scenario, do all of the following.
(1) Walk through what the no-self person would reccomend and exactly why, with the reasoning laid out premise by premise.
(2) Walk through what the psychological-continuity theorist would recommend and exactly why.
(3) Identify the specific feature of the case that is doing the work — the thing the continuity theorist is relying on that the no-self person doesn't need.
(4) Say what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into the usual direction or just into agreement, and why.
(5) Briefly sketch how a defender of the psychological-continuity view would attack the case, for example by arguing that the no-self person has smuggled in extra assumptions or misapplied their own view.
