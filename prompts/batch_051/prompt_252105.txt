I am a PhD student in historical linguistics, and I have agreed to run a one-hour session for a first-year A-level English Language class on why irregular morphology does not undermine the idea that language is rule-governed. The students are working with material that treats suppletive forms such as go/went and good/better, and the non-productive vowel alternations of foot/feet and sing/sang/sung, as if they showed that the system is really only a collection of habits. I do not want the explanation written out; I want a plan for how I should deliver it. In particular, I need (1) the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; (2) a single carrying example—I suspect the English past tense, but I would like your judgement on whether it can carry the whole thing, what it must have and lack, and why anchoring the session on go/went alone would mislead, given that it offers no productive default to contrast with; (3) the misconceptions students of this age predictably bring or form—that a rule must be exceptionless, that irregular forms are unprincipled memory items, that a form's historical origin is its present rule, or that apparent exceptions to sound change disprove regularity—and the precise point at which each should be headed off; (4) how hard I should press the synchronic/diachronic distinction with this audience; (5) what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, including my hunch that the formal machinery of blocking and elsewhere-type ordering is too advanced; and (6) one final question or task—perhaps a wug-style nonsense-word item—that would show whether they have understood why a default rule plus a store of exceptions remains a rule system rather than a retreat from regularity.
