In an ethics of technology working group, the debate about whether autonomous systems create responsibility gaps has stalled in a way I find frustrating. On the pessimist side, people argue that when a system learns from data, the designers cannot track or foresee its specific decisions; the operator cannot guide it moment-to-moment; and the system itself is not a moral agent. So after a harmful decision, no individual satisfies the normal conditions for blame—control, knowledge, and wrongdoing—and we are left with a gap between “someone ought to answer for this” and “no one can be blamed for it.” They treat this as structural: it is built into the very idea of an autonomous system, not a temporary limitation. On the optimist side, people argue that this rests on an outdated picture of responsibility as a single individual’s blameworthiness. They say responsibility should be distributed across designers, training-data providers, deployers, auditors, and regulators, and that strict liability, insurance, and institutional accountability can ensure that every harm has an answerable party. Both sides agree on the examples—a self-driving car striking a pedestrian, a learning-based hiring system disadvantaging applicants, a medical triage algorithm misclassifying patients—and both sides agree that the outcome is unjust and that no one intended it. Yet neither side moves. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is really about the control condition: the pessimists treat control as necessary for responsibility and therefore see a gap, while the optimists are implicitly changing the question to one about answerability and compensation. But I am not sure. Diagnose this disagreement, with reasons: is it substantive or verbal? Are the two sides disagreeing about facts, about what responsibility is, about which cases count as data, or about bedrock intuitions about blame? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, an empirical result, a change of question—or should no progress be expected?
