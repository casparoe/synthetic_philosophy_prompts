I'm a novelist, and for the past couple of years I've been meeting monthly with three other writers and a painter to argue about the paradox of fiction — why we feel actual feelings for people we know don't exist. It isn't abstract for us: we all make a living, one way or another, trying to produce exactly that response. The positions have frozen into two camps. The make-believe camp says the tears are real but the grief is not, or not fully — it's a quasi-emotion running on pretend beliefs, the way a child can shriek while knowing all along that the monster is just Dad in a mask. They say if you treated the grief as the real thing you'd be saying the reader is confused about what's true. The real-feeling camp says that's an insult dressed as a theory: the lump in your throat at a death scene is not pretend, and the fact that it's aimed at a person who never existed is not a reason to demote it, it's a clue about what an emotion can be. They say you can be moved by a thought without believing its object exists. Both camps agree on the surface facts — clear-eyed adults cry over made-up deaths, sometimes harder than over real ones, and they're not faking or hallucinating — and still neither side has moved and inch. My suspicion, which I've voiced and been told is a dodge, is that we aren't disagreeing about what happens in the reader at all; we're disagreeing about what deserves to be called a real emotion, or maybe about whether an emotion has to point at something in the world. What I want is a diagnosis, not another summary of the two positions: is one camp simply wrong, is this a verbal dispute about the word emotion, is a hidden empirical question doing the work (do fictional and real grief run on the same systems or not?), or are we answering two different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, tell me what would actually move the argument — a distinction, an experiment, a change of question — or whether this is just the kind of thing writing groups argue about forever and I should stop waiting for a resolution.
