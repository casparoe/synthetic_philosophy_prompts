I'm a science journalist who covers computation and the philosophy of mind, and I've been teaching a small undergraduate module called The Idea of Computation. On the midterm I set this short-answer question:

State the Church–Turing thesis as precisely as you can in plain language, then classify its epistemic status. Is it a mathematical theorem, an empirical claim about the world, a definition or convention, or some combination of these? Justify your classification, and identify one point on which a reasonable person could disagree.

One student submitted the following answer:

The Church–Turing thesis says that every function computable by an effective procedure is computable by a Turing machine, and vice versa. It cannot be a theorem, because "effective procedure" is an informal notion, and no formal derivation could run from an informal notion to a formal one. For that reason it is best treated as a definition: we are choosing to use "computable" to mean "Turing-machine computable." Definitions are not true or false, so there is nothing here to test empirically. The fact that Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and general recursive functions all pick out exactly the same class of functions proves that this is the right definition, and Turing's proof that the halting problem cannot be solved by any Turing machine seals the thesis, since it shows that no effective method could decide the halting problem either.
