I'm on a campus committee that's rewriting the rules for a small fleet of autonomous delivery robots, and we're stuck on something that feels philosophical. The current draft says that if one of the robots hits a pedestrian, the university pays for the injuries by default, even if the vendor's software, the maintenance crew, the pedestrian, and the remote monitor all did what they were supposed to do. The only exceptions are clear cases like someone deliberately jumping in front of the robot or a student physically tampering with it. But we can't agree about what this policy actually commits us to. One committee member keeps quoting Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics Book III, where he says that "when the origin of an action is in oneself, it is in one's own power to do it or not." Her argument is that our policy treats the robot, or maybe the university itself, as the kind of thing that can be the origin of an action, even though no individual human decided to make the specific turn or braking decision that led to the crash.

I'd like you to act as an expert in moral responsibility and philosophy of law, and think carefully about this. Start by brainstorming the different ways someone could interpret our policy. Then say what theory of responsibility, if any, the policy presupposes. Does it actually presuppose that responsibility can be assigned without a voluntary human act, or is it better read as a no-fault compensation scheme that leaves the philosophical commitments open? Where is the policy underdetermined, so that several rival theories of responsibility would license it? And what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a control-based view of responsibility, like the one my colleague sees in Aristotle? The practical bottom line matters too, but I mostly need help seeing what we are implicitly signing up for.
