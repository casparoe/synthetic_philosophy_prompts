I am leading a one-off two-hour session for a philosophy reading group of analytic-leaning graduate students and faculty, mostly logicians and epistemologists, with real training in classical logic and no prior exposure to Buddhist thought. The topic is Nāgārjuna's tetralemma as an instrument for establishing Madhyamaka emptiness, and I need a teaching plan, not a draft of the explanation.

For this audience the live question is whether the tetralemma hides a reconstruction-worthy argument rather than a paradox. Their default will be that rejecting all four alternatives — in my chosen case, the arising of a sprout from itself, from something other than itself, from both, and from no cause at all — is either a quarrel with the law of excluded middle or a cheap trick. I want the session to build to the opposite conclusion: the fourfold rejection is aimed at inherent existence — svabhāva, a thing's having its nature from its own side, independent of causes, parts, and conceptual designation — and emptiness, śūnyatā, just is the absence of that kind of existence. That is why the doctrine does not slip into a fifth position.

Map the dependency structure for me. I am inclined to think the sequence must be: first make the target svabhāva concrete, so that students can feel what it would be for the sprout to arise from its own side and depend on nothing; then let the four alternatives stand as the only ways an inherently existent arising could go and show why each one is unavailable, without ever denying the ordinary fact of seed-to-sprout causation; only then introduce dependent arising as the positive counterpart that keeps the whole apparatus from sounding like causal skepticism. Tell me whether that order is right, what depends on what, and where a necessary piece is missing.

I intend to carry the session with the seed-and-sprout example rather than the chariot, because the chariot risks making the session about mereological vagueness rather than about arising and inherent nature. Tell me whether that choice is sound, what the seed-and-sprout example must have and must not have, and how to set it up so the ordinary fact that seeds produce sprouts is never in question.

Then give me the misconception map. The ones I expect, roughly in order: that the tetralemma violates classical logic; that Nāgārjuna is denying that seeds produce sprouts; that Madhyamaka is nihilism; that emptiness is an ineffable something behind appearances. For each, state at which point in the sequence it will have formed and exactly how I should head it off there. Finally, give me one closing question or exercise that would show whether someone can hold together the negation of inherently existent arising and the ordinary causal claim without reaching for either nihilism or a hidden essence, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle — whether the logic admits formalization, whether the two truths name two realms, or what distinguishes the various Madhyamaka schools.
