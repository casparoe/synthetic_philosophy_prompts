Consider Laplace's familiar thought experiment, in which an idealised intelligence that knows the present positions and velocities of every body and the forces acting on them could, by applying the laws of mechanics, compute the whole future course of nature. Now vary it in two respects. First, the intelligence is not a disembodied calculator standing outside the world with unlimited computational power; it is a finite physical computer embedded in the universe it predicts, so its computations must obey the same dynamical laws and take time. Second, suppose this universe is computationally irreducible: although each state is strictly determined by the rule and the previous state, there is in general no shorter way to determine a later state than to run the dynamics through every intermediate step, with the result that no prediction can be finished before the predicted state has already arrived. Work through what remains of the original thought experiment. Does the conclusion usually drawn from it — that determinism implies predictability in principle, and that chance is only the name for our ignorance — survive this variation, or does computational irreducibility force us to detach determinism from explanation and prediction? In such a world, what sort of understanding of a system is available to a rational agent, and what could it mean to say that the system's future has been explained?
