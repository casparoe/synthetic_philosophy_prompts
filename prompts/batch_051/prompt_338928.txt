Ok, I've been going around in circles on the Buridan's ass case and want to see someone actually put a number on it instead of waving it off. Setup: a donkey exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay, no reason to prefer either, so it starves. The standard line is that a rational agent just picks arbitrarily and the puzzle dissolves. But I suspect the 'just pick' move quietly changes the subject: picking is still an action, and if you have no reason to prefer bale A to bale B, you also have no reason to prefer the tie-breaking procedure 'grab A' over 'grab B' over 'keep deliberating.' The symmetry seems to reappear one level up.

So give me your actual credence that the Buridan case exposes a genuine hole in rational choice — that is, that there is no way to say what the agent should do that both (a) doesnt smuggle in a preference the agent doesn't have and (b) doesn't collapse into 'every option including starving is equally rational, so the puzzle just dissolves.' Decompose it. Terms I can see mattering: (i) whether rational choice requires a reason that favors the chosen option over its alternatives; (ii) whether 'pick arbitrarily' can count as a sufficient reason on its own, or whether it just relaunches the same symmetry at the level of tie-breaking procedures; (iii) whether real agents always have tie-breakers (deliberation costs, time, boredom) so the idealized case is irrelevant; (iv) whether the honest response is that symmetry makes every option including inaction equally rational, and whether that's a dissolution or a cop-out. Put a rough probability on each with one line of justification, show how they combine into the final number, and tell me which term your credence is most sensitive to and what argument would move it most.
