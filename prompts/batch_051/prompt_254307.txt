I serve on the Motor Vehicle Code Commission of a state preparing to authorize a large fleet of automated delivery vehicles on public roads. The draft under consideration includes a provision under which an automated vehicle that commits a moving violation is deemed to be the operator of the vehicle: the citation is issued against the vehicle and the fine and demerit points are then charged to the registered owner of the automation system. One side on the commission supports the provision, arguing that legal fictions are a routine and useful instrument — the law already treats corporations as persons and measures conduct by a reasonable person who has never existed — and that a handful of bespoke owner-liability rules would be clumsier without being more truthful. The other side regards this as a pernicious fiction: it says, as a matter of law, that a machine acted, and thereby hides the law's real choice about where responsibility falls behind a false claim about the world. Draft the commission's written decision memo. It should come to a definite recommendation — adopt the deeming provision, replace it with a no-fiction owner-liability rule, or pursue a specified third option — give a principled test for when a legal fiction is legitimate and apply it to this case, and give an honest and sustained treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, the memo should not treat the disagreement as merely verbal: it should say what, if anything, makes a deeming provision false, and why that falsity matters or does not matter for a legal order.
