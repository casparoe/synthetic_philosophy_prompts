I recently became the lead flood-risk officer for a regional planning authority, and the post has forced me to think harder than I ever expected about a principle in formal epistemology. I review roughly a dozen floodplain development applications a month and must recommend approval, denial, or conditions such as raised foundations, setbacks, or retention basins. The stakes are high, I seldom have more than an hour of staff time per file, and an elected board reviews my recommendations and expects explicit, non-hand-wavy reasons. Our office licenses a well-validated hydraulic model that outputs, for each parcel, a probability — for instance, an 18% chance of at least one meter of inundation within 30 years — calibrated against historical streamflow and rainfall records. A colleague with philosophical training keeps telling me I should defer to these numbers. He invokes something he calls the Principal Principle: once you know the objective chance of an event, your rational degree of belief should equal that chance, unless you have inadmissible information, such as a crystal ball or some other direct indication of how the event will turn out. But my case files are full of local evidence the model does not ingest: a new dam upstream has changed the hydrology, a hillside burned two years ago, a landowner's engineer argues that recent drainage works have shifted the effective floodplain, neighbors report that groundwater has been rising. When I override the model, the board accuses me of going with my gut; when I defer to it, the same board accuses me of hiding behind a black box.

I want you to turn the Principal Principle — and whatever surrounding formal epistemology is genuinely usable — into a fixed decision procedure I can run in about ten minutes per case, without re-deriving the philosophy each time. My staff and I are hydrologists, planners, and administrators, not philosophers. The procedure should be a sequence of checks in order: tests for whether a vendor model's probability deserves the same deference as an objective chance in the first place; tests for classifying my outside evidence as the admissible kind whose bearing is already mediated by the chance or the inadmissible kind that would license departing from it; and instructions for what to do when the underlying chance itself may be drifting rather than fixed, as in a changing climate, or when the model gives no usable number for a parcel. It should mark plainly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls — above all the moment I must decide whether my extra evidence bears on the outcome itself or on what the chance really is — and it should be honest about where the procedure will still misfire. Then apply the procedure to two or three concrete cases, and make sure at least one is a case where the procedure produces an answer I will not like, for instance forcing me to defer to the model exactly when local evidence feels decisive, or forcing me to set the model aside when I most want its cover. Throughout, please say how confident you are in each major claim and single out the point you are least sure about. End with a final line reading "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence distillation of the procedure.
