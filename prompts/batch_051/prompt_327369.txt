I'm a grad student teaching a seventy-five minute seminar session for sixteen upper-level undergrads on the epistemology of markets, and I want to run a game that makes the no-trade theorem feel like something they discovered rather than a result they memorized. They can all state the theorem — common prior, common knowledge of rationality, so a willingness to trade is itself information and the trade shouldn't occur — but they don't believe it, and honestly in some moods neither do I. I want them to actually make trades and then discover that the fact the other person accepted their offer should have changed what they belived, so that by their own lights most of the trades they made were mistakes.

I have 75 minutes and I can print cards or slips and bring chips; I can't run a computer market, and payoffs need to be non-monetary — points toward a course prize, extra credit, snacks, whatever you think won't make them stop optimizing. The class is a mix of econ majors who will try to game the game and philosophy majors who will suspect the whole exercise is a trap. Design the exact game: roles, rules, materials, how the private signals are generated from a common prior, whether trades happen in rounds or in a free-for-all, and a timeline that fits 75 minutes including setup and cleanup. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments from the session — an offer accepted that should have been refused, an offer refused that should have been accepted, a pair that kept trading out of boredom — onto the theorem and onto the assumptions it needs, especially common prior, common knowledge of rationality, and no liquidity or hedging motives. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it, like people trading just because trading is fun, someone lowballing themselves into a sure win, or the room splitting into mathy traders and confused spectators, and how to fold those failures into the debrief instead of treating them as noise. Finally, be honest about which feature of the theorem the game can't make them feel, the part I'll have to just supply in words afterwards. Keep the whole writeup under 800 words.
