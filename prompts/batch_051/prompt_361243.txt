I'm a journalist writing a long-form science-and-ideas piece about how to weigh the value of different states of consciousness when resources are scarce. The piece will contrast three ways of combining two dimensions of a patient's value: the patient's level of consciousness and the happiness the patient stands to gain. For the purposes of the worked thought experiment I want you to build, define the three views as follows. The additive view treats the two dimensions as separate goods that trade off against one another at a constant rate, so that a life's value is something like α·consciousness + β·happiness; in the case I have in mind, the candidate programs change only happiness and leave each patient's level of consciousness unchanged, so the additive view will rank them by the total amount of happiness produced. The multiplicative view treats a patient's contribution as the product of consciousness and happiness, and the value of a program as the sum of those products across patients. The lexical view treats consciousness as lexically prior to happiness: a program that serves patients at a higher level of consciousness is always preferred to one that serves patients at a lower level, however large the difference in happiness, and happiness decides only between programs tied at the same level of consciousness.

Please construct the case: a fixed-budget allocation decision in an ordinary long-term-care or regional health setting, with three candidate programs aimed respectively at patients in three bands of a realistic consciousness spectrum — fully alert patients; patients whose consciousness is clearly present but substantially impaired; and patients in a minimally conscious state. Engineer the numbers so that the three views recommend three different programs: the additive view favors the program for the minimally conscious group, because it produces the largest total happiness gain across patients; the multiplicative view favors the program for the substantially impaired group, because it has the best product of group consciousness level and total happiness gain; and the lexical view favors the program for the fully alert group, because it serves patients at the highest level of consciousness even though it produces the smallest total happiness gain.

Constraints: the scenario must be ordinary and realistic — no exotic minds, no science-fictional consciousness-altering technology, and no stipulating the verdicts; the disagreement should emerge from the arithmetic and the structure of the case rather than being asserted. The programs should be interventions a real health system might plausibly weigh, such as pain relief, mood treatment, environmental enrichment, or expanded stimulation therapy. Describe the differences in level of consciousness in plain terms that do not depend on controversial diagnostic thresholds.

Keep the scenario to one paragraph. Then deliver three things. First, walk through each of the three views for each program, laying out the comparative arithmetic and showing why each view picks the program it does. Second, identify which feature of the case is doing the work to produce the three-way split, and explain what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed or softened. Third, adopt the perspective of a defender of whichever view the case treats least sympathetically, and give the strongest objection that case would draw, together with a modification that would answer that objection while still preserving the three-way split.
