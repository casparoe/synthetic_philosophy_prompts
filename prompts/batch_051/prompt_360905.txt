I've spent enough years on rationalist blogs to be suspicious of the one-liner that arrives precisely when an argument needs to be settled, and there is one I have never managed to pin down: "sometimes the rational thing is to be irrational." It came up most recently in a thread about the hitchhiker story usually credited to Parfit: a stranded motorist will be rescued only if the driver is convinced he will pay afterward, but a perfectly rational egoist will not pay once safe, so the driver, reading him correctly, simply does not stop. The comment-section conclusion is that the motorist should have made himself into the sort of person who keeps promises, even though each individual act of paying is then a loss against that moment's incentives. One commenter in the thread was using the slogan to argue that we should therefore cultivate dispositions that ignore local incentives — honoring small promises, paying one's rescuer, refusing to recalculate. I think there is a genuine insight in there, but the sentence keeps changing shape as it is deployed, and I do not know which claim the argument actually requires. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "sometimes the rational thing is to be irrational" could be asserting — from the nearly trivial to the obviously false — with a verdict on each? I am not asking you to pick a single interpretation and defend it; I want the disambiguation. And I would like you to identify which reading, if any, actually underwrites the lesson people draw from the hitchhiker story. Please separate, at minimum, the evaluation of an individual act from the evaluation of a disposition, a policy, or an act of self-modification, and say for each reading whether "irrational" means locally payoff-diminishing, epistemically unjustified, or preference-inconsistent.
