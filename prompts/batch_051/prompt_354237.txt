I help run a philosophy club at a community college — about 15 people, ages 18 to 50ish, one 75-minute session. I want a hands-on activity about translation under formal constraint, the kind Hofstadter obsesses over with Clément Marot's tiny poem A une Damoyselle malade. This recently became kind of personal: I got roped into translating a short poem for my sister's wedding (grandma's half-remembered French, long story) and every line I fixed felt like a betrayal of something else. What I want the group to feel, not hear me lecture about, is that there's no neutral way to separate what a poem says from how it says it. Preserving the rhyme means sacrificing word choice; preserving the meter means moving the punchline; preserving an acrostic means bending the sense. A translation is really a set of losses you choose on purpose, and different choices can each feel "faithful."
