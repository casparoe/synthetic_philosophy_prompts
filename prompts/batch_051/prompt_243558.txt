I've got a distinction I keep falling back on when I read about satisficing, and I want it checked before I build anything on it. When people defend satisficing, I think they're actually conflating two very different stopping rules. The first is what I call target satisficing: you pick an aspiration level in advance — say, a restaurant that's at least a 7 out of 10 — and you commit to taking the first option that clears the bar. The second is budget satisficing: you decide in advance how much search you'll tolerate — I'll look at five restaurants and then pick the best of those five — and you stop when the budget runs out. My claim is that almost everything worth calling rational satisficing is budget satisficing in disguise. Target satisficing, as usually described, gives no answer to the question of why you'd stop at the first 7 when continuing to look is cheap and might get you a 9; the target only looks sensible because it's secretly tracking an implicit search budget (you set the bar at 7 because you have a rough sense of how long it takes for a 7 to show up, and that's a budget fact). So satisficing seems to collapse into ordinary maximizing under search costs, or into optimal stopping theory.

Take a concrete case: I need to replace a laptop charger. If I set a target of "at least 65W and under forty dollars" and buy the first one that meets it, that looks like target satisficing. But the only reason the target is set where it is, is that I already know more or less what the market looks like and what another hour of browsing is worth to me — which is a budget. If search were genuinely free and instantaneous, I'd just sort by quality and take the best one. So I want an honest assessment: is this distinction already in the literature under a name I don't know? It feels to obvious not to be, but everything I read just says "aspiration levels" and never splits the two apart. And is my second claim actually true, or am I strawmanning target satisficing? I have a suspicion the idea that satisficing is a distinct mode of rationality might survive in cases where your preferences themselves make "good enough" a better outcome than "the best" — where there's no leftover wish that things had gone better — rather than in cases where you merely stop because search costs caught up with you. Tell me what the distinction gets right, where it breaks, and whether I should keep it or give up on it.
