I am a high school junior, and next week I am presenting a proposal to my school's Discipline Reform Committee. The thesis I will defend is that the school should replace its current honor code — a system of pledges, reflection essays, and individualized penalties meant to build character — with a discipline system modeled on Han Feizi's Legalism: a fixed, published schedule of offenses and corresponding consequences and rewards, applied uniformly regardless of a student's intentions, circumstances, or prior record. My argument is that rule by predictable incentives is fairer and more reliable than rule by virtue, because it does not depend on anyone being especially good, impartial, or consistent. I draw on Han Feizi's two handles, reward and punishment, and on his view that clear laws yield order even under an ordinary ruler, whereas rule by virtue requires a sage. I concede up front that the honor code expresses a Confucian ideal I take seriously: that ruling through rules and penalties produces merely outward compliance, while ruling through virtue and a cultivated sense of shame changes character itself. My contention is that this ideal has failed in practice, because it vests too much discretion in administrators whose judgment is no more reliable than the students' own.

The committee includes four people I expect to push back. The Head of School is committed to character education as the school's central mission and speaks in terms of moral development and intrinsic motivation. The Dean of Students has a restorative-justice background and believes consequences should be tailored to the individual and the context rather than fixed in advance. The history teacher on the committee knows enough about Legalism to worry that Han Feizi designed his system for the strength of the ruler rather than the flourishing of the governed, and he argues that institutions should shape character over time. The student representative is concerned with fairness and with whether a fixed code simply invites gaming by the most advantaged students; she is likely to ask whether the proposed system is any less arbitrary than the discretionary one it replaces.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet, not a full defense speech. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from these four people, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased as that member would actually put it. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which points I should concede rather than hold, and where the Confucian case has genuine force. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the most dangerous.
