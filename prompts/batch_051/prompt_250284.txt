For a handout in a first-year PhD seminar in a joint philosophy-and-economics program, I'd like a decision tree that sorts readers into positions on whether and how one person's welfare can be compared with another's. The audience has encountered Pareto efficiency, utility functions, and social welfare functions, but has not read the interpersonal-comparisons literature; each branch node must therefore be a concrete question or case they can answer without already knowing the labels of the positions. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first set out the branching questions and which combinations of answers are coherent, then locate the actual positions in that space, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied combinations as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Organize the tree around two distinctions: whether the comparison is about a subjective welfare magnitude or about objective advantage, and what is supposed to ground the comparison (a shared hedonic currency, a normalization of preferences, imaginative or extended preferences, or a partly normative stipulation). Aim for four to six branch questions and eight to twelve named leaves. The leaves should include at least a Robbinsian skepticism denying any scientific basis for interpersonal comparison, a classical hedonist view grounding comparisons in a shared currency of pleasure and pain, a preference-satisfaction view that fixes comparisons by normalizing each person's preferences to a common 0–1 scale, a Harsanyi-style extended-preferences view that compares by imagining oneself in each person's position, a Sen-style capability view comparing real opportunities or functionings rather than utility, and a constructivist view treating comparisons as choices of a standard rather than facts to be discovered. After the tree, flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate and say which further question would separate them if any would. End with a short note on where the tree's main branching choices are most contestable, and which argument or consideration would lead you to reorganize it.
