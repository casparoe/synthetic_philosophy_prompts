Consider the following argument, which is meant to show that effective altruism's method is systematically biased against structural change. (1) Effective altruists allocate resources by ranking interventions according to expected value. (2) An intervention can enter an expected-value ranking only if its outcomes can be assigned probabilities and magnitudes, which in practice means working from measurable or at least closely estimable quantities. (3) The outcomes of structural interventions—legal reform, institutional redesign, norm change—are often diffuse, delayed, and entangled with other causes, so in many cases they cannot be measured or closely estimated. (4) Such interventions therefore cannot enter expected-value rankings on equal terms with direct interventions whose outcomes are closely estimated. (5) Therefore, when resources follow these rankings, effective altruism's method is systematically biased toward countable, direct-delivery interventions and against structural ones, whatever their true impact. Is the argument valid? If not, identify exactly which step fails and state the reading under which it would be true. If it is valid, say whether the conclusion is as damaging to effective altruism as it appears or only establishes something weaker.
