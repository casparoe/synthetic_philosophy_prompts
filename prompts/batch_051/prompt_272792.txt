I'm a backend engineer on a fraud team at a payments company. We run Bayesian models to score transactions, retrain them nightly as chargebacks trickle in, and my standing policy is that priors are basically a waste of engineering time. I throw a weak, flat-ish prior on the coefficients and tell new teammates "dont burn a week eliciting priors from the risk team, we have enough data that the likelihood will crush the prior anyway." I've repeated that line often enough that I've stopped checking whether it's true. Recently a new hire with an actual stats background pushed back: he pointed out that in a logistic model a flat prior on the coefficients is a weird, strong prior on the predicted probability, and that for the rare, sparse features that matter most in fraud, we'll never log enough positives for the prior to wash out — the tail just never gets enough data, no matter how big n gets. I could feel myself reaching for my usual slogan again, and that's what made me write this.

So my question is about the general habit, not just the work argument. Is "don't spend scarce time on priors because data swamps them eventually" defensible as a rule for someone in my position? What exactly does it take for a prior to wash out — is it enough that the data are huge, or do I need the prior to put mass where the truth is, or something about the parameter actually being identifiable? And when does big-n stop being an excuse, the way my colleague says it does in the tail? If the clean version of my rule is false, is there a patched version that keeps the time-saving and still protects against the cases where the prior never washes out?
