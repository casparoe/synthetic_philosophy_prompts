You're a moral philosopher who works on consent, especially in research settings. Work through this carefully — start by briefly laying out what you take the conditions for valid consent to be (deception, coercion, competence, and maybe ongoing consent and withdrawal), then use the Milgram obedience experiments as your case.

Take the Milgram studies at Yale in the early 1960s, with the facts as I'll supply them: participants were recruited for what they thought was a study on learning and punishment. They were told to give electric shocks to a "learner" (actually a confederate, who recieved no real shocks) whenever he gave a wrong answer, with the voltage increasing each time. As the shocks rose the learner screamed, complained about his heart, and eventually went silent. The experimenter, in a lab coat, used standardized prods to the effect that the experiment required them to continue and that they had no choice. A large majority — something like two thirds — went all the way to the maximum voltage. Some people did withdraw, and the pay didn't depend on finishing.

I want a specific analysis of this through the lens of valid consent — not the usual stress-and-long-term-harm ethics discussion. In particular:

- Deception: they consented to a learning-and-memory study, not to a study of their own obedience. Is that deception about something material enough to invalidate consent? Does it matter that the deception was arguably necessary for the phenomenon to show up, or that the harm was mostly to their self-conception rather than their body?
- Coercion: are the experiments prods coercive in the sense that matters for consent? They aren't threats, and leaving was physically possible; but there's authority, a lab coat, and the participant's sense of being the kind of person who finishes what they started. Where does coercion end and mere pressure begin?
- Competence: assume the participants were competent adults. But some showed extreme stress — trembling, sweating, laughing nervously. Can a person be competent at the start and yet have their ongoing consent undermined by that level of distress? Is consent a one-off event or something that has to survive contact with the actual experiment?
- Withdrawal: what does the setup do to the participants ability to exercise a right to withdraw, and is a right of withdrawal part of consent being valid at all, or a separate ethical requirement on top of it?

Then run the analysis in reverse: what does Milgram show about the consent framework itself? Be disciplined here — don't force the consent framework to explain the obedience result itself. Where does the deception/coercion/competence triad do real work in this case, and where does it strain or go silent, for instance about situational pressure, role expectations, or the gap between "the participant validly consented" and "the study was ethically fine all things considered"? Be honest about whether Milgram is a case consent theory handles easily, or a case that shows its categories were never built for this kind of thing.
