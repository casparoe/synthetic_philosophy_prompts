After three decades in a physical chemistry lab I've started reading philosophy seriously, and I keep getting caught on Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind, especially the chapter called Knowing How and Knowing That. The passage I mean is the one where he denies that an intelligent performance is just the outward effect of some inner act of consulting propositions; he says the skill is not a matter of first avowing rules and then applying them, but is exercised in the performance itself. I don't have the book in front of me, so that's paraphrase, not a quote. That thought seems right to me. But my old colleague, who is much better at this than I am, handed me the following argument and said it shows Ryle's distinction proves to much:

(1) If knowing-how is genuinely different in kind from knowing-that, then no amount of knowledge-that can by itself add up to knowledge-how.
(2) Whatever is transferred by lectures, textbooks, manuals, or verbal coaching—at least in the moment of instruction—is knowledge-that: propositions, facts, rules, algorithms.
(3) So if Ryle is right, lectures, textbooks, manuals, and coaching cannot transfer knowledge-how.
(4) But it is absurd to say that instruction cannot transfer skill. A well-written procedure lets a novice reproduce a technique in the lab, and pilots, surgeons, and machinists are partly trained through manuals and verbal correction.
(5) Therefore Ryle's distinction forces an absurd conclusion, so it should be rejected.

I want to resist the conclusion because I find the distinction illuminating, and I suspect the word 'transfer' is doing something slippery between premise (2) and (4). But I can't point to the exact step or say what the least costly fix would be. Could you act as a professional philosopher of mind who works on know-how and skill, and diagnose this? Do some initial brainstorming on what each premise could mean before committing, then tell me which premise or inference you would reject, why it looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs for the broader distinction.
