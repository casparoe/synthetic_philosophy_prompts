The rules of the residents' association for my building include the following: 'No resident shall cause any damage to the communal garden, and any resident who does so shall pay the cost of making it good.' Apart from this, I have no duties in respect of the garden: it is maintained by a contractor, I have no key to the irrigation cabinet, and I am not on the garden committee. One afternoon I notice that the automatic irrigation timer has jammed and is flooding the bed around a mature Japanese quince. I know the waterlogging will probably kill the shrub before morning, and the only realistic step open to me is to telephone the managing agent's emergency line. I do not telephone, and by the next morning the quince is dead.

The rule plainly has in mind damage by positive acts—trampling the beds, digging them up, letting children erect goalposts—and says nothing about a resident who merely notices a hazard and fails to intervene. Does my not telephoning 'cause' the damage within the meaning of the rule? I am less interested in whether the association would actually pursue me than in the underlying question: what would have to be true for the rule to reach me, and is there a coherent reading of 'cause' on which my silence is something I did to the garden?

Argue the question from the words of the rule, from its purpose, from what its drafters would have said if asked, and from what a reasonable resident would have understood it to mean; then say which of these approaches should decide the case and why, and in doing so take a view on whether an omission can be a cause at all. Please answer in British English.
