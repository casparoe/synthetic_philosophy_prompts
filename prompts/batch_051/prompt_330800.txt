I'm putting together a handout for an undergraduate course on distributive justice, and the unit compares three answers to the question of what egalitarians should equalize: welfare, resources, and capabilities. The standard classroom examples all turn on an expensive taste or a disability, and my students now believe the disagreements between the views reduce to those two cases. I want a thought experiment built to a stricter specification. Construct a case in which a resourcist egalitarian and a welfarist egalitarian come apart in this particular direction: the resourcist says the state should make a straightforward recurring cash transfer from one character to the other, while the welfarist opposes exactly that transfer on the grounds that it would not bring the two characters closer to equal welfare and may even widen the welfare gap. A capability theorist should then reject both verdicts: she should say that neither the transfer nor the absence of the transfer addresses the real capability shortfall, and she should recommend a third intervention, ideally one that changes an environmental or social conversion factor rather than moving money around. The characters should be two working-age adults in an ordinary setting, with no physical or cognitive differences, no adaptive preferences, no expensive tastes, and no extreme poverty or wealth; the divergence must come from the structure of the situation, not from a gerrymandered taste or a medical need. Keep the scenario to one paragraph, and use the rest for analysis.

The exercise is for teaching, so the case cannot simply stipulate the verdicts: the verdicts should be generated by the theories as the students have learned them. After the scenario, walk through what each of the three views says about it and why, in enough detail that a student could reconstruct the reasoning. Then identify the single structural feature of the story that does the work — I suspect it is something like a gap between a person's resource holdings and her conversion factors, but do not take that as settled — and say what happens if you remove that feature: does the case collapse back into a standard resourcist-versus-welfarist dispute, or dissolve entirely? End the analysis with the strongest reply available to the view that comes out of the case looking least attractive, rather than leaving it as a straw man.

Throughout, state how confident you are in each verdict and explicitly single out the point you are least sure about. After the prose, close with a JSON object summarizing the piece, using these fields: scenario (a one-sentence compressed description), resource_egalitarian_verdict (what the resourcist would do and the rationale), welfarist_verdict (whether the welfarist would block the transfer and why), capability_verdict (the third intervention and its capability rationale), structural_feature (the single feature that produces the three-way split), feature_removed (what the case becomes if that feature is removed), defender_reply (the strongest response from the view that fares worst), confidence (your overall confidence and where it is high or low), and least_sure (the specific point of lowest confidence).
