I'm a journalist who covers courts and ideas, and I'm two weeks from filing a profile of a law professor who has spent years arguing about what's genuinely real in a legal system and what's just a useful pretense. I've been going back through her old columns and talks, and she's been remarkably consistant. She says a corporation is a real legal entity, not a fiction. A general partnership is just shorthand for the partners. A trust is a fiction—there is no thing that owns the property, just a relationship between trustee and beneficiary. A labor union is a real legal entity. Suing a ship in rem is a fiction, because the ship is not actually a defendant. A decedent's estate is a real legal entity. An unincorporated neighborhood association is just shorthand for its members.

Now another state has passed a law declaring a river to be a legal person, with guardians who can sue and be sued in the river's name over pollution. Here's the wrinkle: the bill's own drafters admit the guardians could have gotten most of the same remedies through an ordinary citizen-suit law, and what's mainly new is whose name goes on the caption. I'm trying to predict what she'll write about the new law. Will she call the river a real legal entity or a fiction/shorthand? I care less about the label than about the rule she's actually running, the other rules that fit her track record just as well, and which additonal case would cleanly separate those rules. If her prior verdicts don't settle it, tell me the most charitable statement of the principle she'd be committing to under each possible answer. And please write the analysis in American English.
