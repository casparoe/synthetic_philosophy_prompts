In mature sciences, a surprising or disconfirming experimental result is, as a matter of settled professional practice, not enough on its own to dislodge an established framework. The first response is to look for error in the apparatus, the auxiliary assumptions, or the model used to reduce the data; if the anomaly resists explanation, it is often tolerated as an open problem rather than treated as a refutation. A framework tends to be given up only when a rival is available that can do the old framework's central explanatory work, and the transition is contested rather than settled by any agreed algorithm. The same communities, in their public accounts of method, often describe science in Popperian terms: theories are bold conjectures, genuine tests are attempted refutations, and a well-conducted failed prediction can in principle force the surrender of a formerly successful theory.

Take that combination of practice and self-description as given. What philosophy of scientific rationality does it presuppose? In particular: does it presuppose a broadly Kuhnian view, on which a scientific revolution—when an old framework is failing and a new one is not yet entrenched—offers no neutral, rule-governed standard that both sides can apply, so that the rationality of the transition must be understood in some non-algorithmic way? Or can the practice still be reconciled with a Popperian view, on which refutation and rational theory-choice remain governed by logic and evidence even during revolutionary episodes? And does the practice commit us to saying that the decision to abandon a framework can be rational for an individual scientist at the moment of choice, or only that it can be rational for the community in retrospect?
