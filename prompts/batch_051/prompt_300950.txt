Design a runnable 80-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate moral philosophy seminar of 18 students that makes participants feel the force of the aggregation problem behind the torture vs. dust specks thought experiment: that a sequence of pairwise trade-offs, each of which feels acceptable, can push one toward holding that an astronomically large number of barely perceptible harms outweighs a single person's severe suffering. The activity must make participants construct the sequence themselves — first by recording individual trade-off thresholds, then by voting as a group on a ladder of burden distributions — so that they notice where they have ended up only afterward. No lecture up front; do not name the problem or use terms of art such as aggregation, lexical priority, separateness of persons, or expected value until the debrief. Use only ordinary classroom materials (index cards, tokens, a whiteboard, or similar), and designate one preselected round as binding with real but minor, safe, non-stigmatizing stakes, so that votes are felt rather than merely imagined; specify how the bearer of any concentrated cost is selected and how consent and opt-out work. Include small-group moments in which students must defend the exact step at which their own sequence becomes unacceptable.

The debrief should map moments in the activity onto the underlying structure point by point: where their pairwise choices implicitly accepted additive aggregation, where a threshold or lexical priority would have stopped the slide, what it costs to insist on that threshold, how the separateness of persons bears on the difference between one severe harm and many tiny ones, and why the thought experiment is usually run with astronomically large numbers rather than the classroom's modest ones. It should also flag what will predictably go wrong on a first run — students gaming the exercise rather than reporting their own judgments, a minority feeling coerced by the binding majority vote, or participants preemptively denying that tiny harms matter at all — and say how to handle each failure. Finally, say frankly which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce: the severity, duration, and certainty of real torture and astronomical populations, and the gap between a mild classroom inconvenience and the harms in the thought experiment; the facilitator must supply that imaginative extrapolation. The response should begin with the activity itself, with no preface, no restatement of the task, and no commentary.
