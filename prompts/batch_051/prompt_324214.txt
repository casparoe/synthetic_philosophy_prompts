I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been trying to figure out whether state apologies for old wrongs can ever be more than political theater. A philosopher friend handed me an argument that made me put the piece down for a week. It goes roughly like this:

(1) A genuine apology is a speech act that only the wrongdoer can perform. If a bystander says the same words, it's compassion or condemnation, not apology.
(2) In a historical wrong—a massacre, a colonial land grab, a forced removal—the wrongdoer is the state as it existed then: the officials, citizens, laws, and institutions of that time.
(3) A state at a time just is its officials, citizens, laws, and institutions; there is no persistent state-soul underneath them that survives while all the parts turn over.
(4) Today's state has different officials, different laws, and nearly all of its citizens were born after the wrong.
(5) So today's state is not the wrongdoer.
(6) So, by (1), when today's state says "we apologize," it is either speaking falsely or performing some other act—regret, maybe, or damage control.
(7) But that conclusion proves too much. For some wrongs, refusal to apologize is itself a continuing harm, and the apology seem to matter to survivors and descendants in a way that isn't just symbolism.

I want to keep both (1) and (7): I don't want state apologies to become a cheap way to get credit for remorse without changing anything, but I also can't bring myself to tell people their demand for an apology is confused. I can see two exits. Maybe the state today commits a fresh wrong every year it refuses to acknowledge the old one, so the apology is for the cover-up rather than the original crime—but then it did not apologize for the actual thing. Or maybe a state really is a transgenerational agent that can inherit guilt, which lets me reject (5) but leaves everyone carrying moral debts from before they were born. Niether exit feels right, and I can't find a third. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? And if I keep (1), is there any way for a state to genuinely apologize for what it didn't cause without dragging individual citizens into guilt they don't deserve?
