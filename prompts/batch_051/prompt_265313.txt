I'm in a seminar on hope and the emotions, and last week we read the part of Spinoza's Ethics where he defines the passions. I've been chewing on it since, partly because a friend keeps arguing that hope is basically an epistemic vice: it's what you feel when you really want an outcome but lack the evidence to expect it, so it ends up skewing how you read the evidence. Then I hit this passage, from the "Definitions of the Emotions" at the end of Part III, in R. H. M. Elwes's translation of the Ethics:

"Hope is an inconstant pleasure, arising from the idea of something past or future, whereof we to a certain extent doubt the issue.

XIII. Fear is an inconstant pain arising from the idea of something past or future, whereof we to a certain extent doubt the issue (cf. III. XVIII. note).

Explanation—From these definitions it follows, that there is no hope unmingled with fear, and no fear unmingled with hope."

I think I get the surface meaning, but Im having trouble seeing what he thinks actually follows from it. Why does Spinoza think hope can't show up without fear? Is it simply that both are defined as responses to the same epistemic state—doubt about how things will turn out—so they have to travel together? And if so, does that mean the hopeful person is always partly in pain, even when hoping feels good? My friend says this supports his view: hope is inconstant, tied to uncertainty, and mixed with an unpleasant state, so it's not this lovely virtue we should cultivate; we'd be better off replacing it with knowledge or confidence. But I cant tell whether Spinoza is making a normative point like that at all, or just giving a descriptive account of an affect. I'd like help unpacking what's definition here, what's supposed to follow from the definitions, and whether Spinoza's account gives us reasons to treat hope as an epistemic vice, a moral virtue, or neither.
