Analyze the July 20, 1944 plot against Hitler through the lens of Aquinas's doctrine of tyrannicide, and then run the analysis in the other direction: say what the case does to the doctrine. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — an overall verdict, by Aquinas's standards, on whether the plotters were justified in attempting to kill Hitler — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

For the doctrine, stated in paraphrase: Aquinas treats sedition, the tearing apart of a political community's unity, as a mortal sin, but he does not count every attack on a ruler as sedition. A government directed to the ruler's private good rather than the common good is unjust, and overthrowing such a government is not strictly sedition unless the disturbance it causes harms the people more than the tyranny it removes; indeed, it is the tyrant, on Aquinas's account, who is guilty of sedition among his subjects. He also cautions that an oppressive rule is sometimes better endured than violently resisted, because the attempt may fail and produce something worse, and he remains reserved about whether private individuals may act on their own initiative rather than through public authority.

The case, in bare outline: on July 20, 1944, German military officers centered on Claus von Stauffenberg attempted to kill Hitler with a bomb and mount a coup; the bomb failed, and the coup collapsed within hours. Many of the plotters were officers who had sworn a personal oath of loyalty to Hitler, and several framed the attempt as a duty to the common good and the only defensible way to free Germany of an unjust regime.

Analyze the plot with these categories. Was Hitler, as Aquinas would parse the term, a tyrant rather than a legitimate ruler? How should the plotters' mixed motives — patriotic, religious, institutional, and personal — be assessed under the public-spirit-versus-private-ambition test? Does a personal oath bind once the ruler to whom it was sworn has become tyrannical? How does the caution that an attempted cure may be more destructive than the disease apply when tyranny takes the form of a totalitarian state, when the realistic alternatives to assassination included defeat and occupation, and when a failed coup would predictably bring mass reprisals? Then reverse direction: does July 20 vindicate the Aquinian framework as an adaptable instrument for reasoning about legitimate resistance, or does it expose silences in applying a medieval scholastic doctrine to a modern totalitarian state — or both? Say at least one place where the fit is illuminating and one where it is strained.
