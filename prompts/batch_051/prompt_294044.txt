I am trying to work out whether my intuitive verdicts about imposing risk on others without their consent hang together, and I would like a precise principle that captures them—or an honest statement of which ones must go. Here are my cases. Driving to work in city traffic, knowing that I am imposing a small accident risk on pedestrians and other drivers, strikes me as acceptable. Burning garden waste in a way that reliably triggers my neighbor's asthma is not acceptable. A factory that vents a chemical it could safely capture for $40,000 a year, raising each of 40,000 nearby residents' lifetime cancer risk by one in a million, is not acceptable to me—even though each resident's risk is far smaller than the risk I impose by driving. A hospital that enrolls patients in a trial, with their informed consent, to a one-in-ten-thousand chance of serious adverse effects is acceptable. A pharmacy that sells a drug with a rare but serious side effect, warning buyers in the package insert, is acceptable, but quietly adding a chemical to a public water supply that gives each of a hundred thousand people a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of serious illness is not—despite the expected number of serious illnesses being identical to the pharmacy case. Finally, walking through a crowded station while mildly contagious with an ordinary, non-quarantinable illness strikes me as acceptable, though it does impose infection risk on strangers. Is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these? State it precisely, indicate what it predicts about cases I have not listed—say, an airline rerouting an approach path over a residential neighborhood to save fuel, or someone setting off fireworks in a dry, windy residential street—and tell me which of my original verdicts your principle forces me to revise.
