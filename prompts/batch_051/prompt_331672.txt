I am a philosophy professor, and I have been asked to adjudicate an argument adapted from Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment. Here it is:

(1) Suppose a person is created all at once as a mature adult, suspended in empty space with his eyes covered and his limbs spread so that they cannot touch one another, and suppose he receives no sensory input from the world or from his own body. He would still know with certainty that he himself exists.

(2) In that state he would have no awareness of his body or of any of its parts; he could not report that he has limbs at all.

(3) If the self were identical with the body, or with some part of it, then his awareness of his own existence would itself be an awareness of a body.

(4) But step (2) says he has no awareness of a body.

(5) Therefore, the self is not identical with the body or with any of its parts.

I keep suspecting that the argument must fail somewhere around step (3), but I cannot say whether (3) states a defensible principle, whether the argument shifts the sense of "awareness," or whether the conclusion follows after all. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, under what reading, and why; if it is valid, say precisely what it entitles one to conclude. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Answer in American English.
