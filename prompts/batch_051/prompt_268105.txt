Consider an established feature of multilingual law and diplomacy. A treaty may be adopted in five or six languages at once, with each text declared to carry the same legal force; a bilingual country may enact its statutes in two languages with neither version given priority; and when two versions pull apart, a court is expected to reconcile them by appeal to the instrument's purpose rather than by asking which text is older, which language the drafters actually worked in, or which wording comes closest to some single underlying original. At the same time, many of the same institutions behave elsewhere as though meaning does not move cleanly between languages: literary translators publish prefaces regretting what could not be carried over, diplomats treat certain terms as effectively untranslatable and leave them in the source language with a gloss, and courts in bilingual systems occasionally concede that two statutory terms do not quite coincide.

Reconstruct the theory of meaning and translation that the core practice---treating several versions as equally authoritative---presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that a law or a proposition exists as a common content independent of any particular natural language, or is it compatible with the view that every translation is a new construction and that equivalence is always partial? Separate what the practice must assume to remain workable from what it merely sits well with, and mark the places where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would have to change in the practice---how divergences are adjudicated, how translators and interpreters are briefed, and what counts as a faithful version---if one instead adopted the view that languages divide up experience differently enough that full semantic equivalence is the exception.
