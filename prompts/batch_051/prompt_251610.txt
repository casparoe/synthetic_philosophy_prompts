I am a sixth-form student who spends much of my free time programming, and I help maintain a small open-source plugin with four or five regular contributors; none of us has any real background in decision theory or philosophy. Recently one of us merged a change that no one would have shipped had we known that the others were uneasy about it, and the resulting bug took the project down for the better part of a day. While trying to understand what went wrong, I came across the idea of the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it, the curse arises when several people each have the power to take, on everyone else's behalf, an action that is costly and difficult to reverse, and they each rely on their own private estimate of whether the action is worth taking. In that situation, the person with the lowest threshold for acting tends to act, even when the group as a whole would have preferred that nobody acted, and even when no individual is being reckless by their own lights.

I am considerably more fluent in the vocabulary of software development than in decision theory, so I would like you to restate the unilateralist's curse in the terms I actually use: merge rights and pull requests, continuous integration and automated checks, code review and approval rules, feature flags, canary deployments, rollbacks, and the distinction between a change being merely deployed and being genuinely safe. For each component of the curse — unilateral power, shared consequences, private estimates, the lowest threshold winning, and collective regret — tell me what translates cleanly into that vocabulary, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in it at all. Then give me the residue report: is the part that fails to survive a sign that my software vocabulary is missing something the curse genuinely needs, or a sign that the curse is doing less work than it appears to? I want to know whether my intuition that our merge problem was an instance of the curse is correct, or whether I am stretching the idea.

Please write the response in British English and keep it precise rather than introductory.
