For some years I have taught Berkeley's immaterialism in my survey of early modern philosophy and in a graduate seminar on perception, and I have come to regard the so-called master argument as the load-bearing element of the entire position. As I reconstruct it, the argument invites you to try to conceive of a sensible object existing wholly unperceived by any mind; it then observes that the object you are conceiving is, by that very fact, an object of thought, present to a conceiving mind, so that the act of conception never actually delivers a mind-independent thing as its content. Berkeley takes this to show not merely that we lack evidence for material substance, but that the very idea collapses under inspection, leaving being-perceived or being-perceivable as the only coherent account of sensible existence. I am not asking for a history of the argument's reception, and I am not asking for a list of standard objections. I want a map of the position's exposure. Which premises and which inferential moves would a defender of the master argument need in order to reach idealism, and which could be surrendered without losing the conclusion? What would the world, or our cognitive relation to it, have to be like for each of the argument's escape routes to be live rather than merely logically possible? Which consequences of the resulting idealism should even its sympathizers concede as counting against it—cases it strains to describe, or commitments that ought to give a reasonable person pause? Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly whether the position has been qualified over time into something that risks nothing at all, and whether that insulation is itself a theoretical vice.
