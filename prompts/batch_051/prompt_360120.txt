I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the structure of practical reasons, and I have been trying to decide whether a resemblance I keep noticing is substantive or merely superficial.

On one side, a central strand of value pluralism — the tradition usually associated with Berlin and Williams — holds that there is no single master value in which all goods can be measured. When honesty conflicts with kindness, or autonomy with welfare, it is not merely that we do not know the exchange rate; the pluralist says there is no fact of the matter about the rate, and the comparison can be indeterminate. On the other side, multi-objective optimization in engineering and economics gives up on a master objective in a clearly specified way: it works with a partial order. One option dominates another when it is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on at least one, and the Pareto frontier is the set of options not dominated. To act, one typically maximizes a weighted sum of the objectives, but the weights are supplied from outside the problem, and each choice of weights selects a different point on the frontier.

The parallel I want tested is this: value pluralism's denial of a master value looks like the claim that the choice-worthiness relation is only partially ordered, and the undominated options of pluralism just are the Pareto frontier. Choosing weights in scalarization would then correspond to adopting, for practical purposes, an arbitrary master value to settle comparisons the pluralist says were never determinately there to be settled.

I want to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. In particular:

1. Where does the mapping break? Does pluralism's incommensurability make claims stronger than a partial order — for example, claims about parity or rough equality — that a Pareto representation cannot capture?

2. If the parallel is real, does anything transfer from optimization to ethics? The scalarization theorem says that, under convexity conditions, every Pareto-optimal point can be generated as the maximum of some weighted sum. If that transferred, it would imply that for any admissible resolution of a value conflict there is a weighting that vindicates it, even though no weighting is privileged. Would that be a reductio of pluralism, a harmless formalization of it, or a misapplication of the theorem?

3. What does the parallel predict that turns out false? I am especially interested in whether the fact that weighted-sum scalarization recovers all Pareto-optimal points only under convexity — and otherwise misses unsupported optima — is a deep point about value conflict or just a mathematical artifact with no normative significance.

Please assess this at the level of structure rather than analogy: state the exact mapping, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and identify at least one place where the two structures come apart.
