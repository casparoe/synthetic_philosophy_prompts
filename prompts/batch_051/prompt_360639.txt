In a seminar on distributive ethics, I twice heard "the separateness of persons" used as though it settled the question. First, a discussant rejected a proposal to choose between two public-health policies by summing the expected well-being each would produce, saying the calculation "just ignores the separateness of persons." Later, someone appealed to the same phrase to argue that a serious harm imposed on one person cannot be justified merely by a greater benefit to someone else. I have a rough sense of what the slogan is gesturing at, but I cannot tell what precise proposition is supposed to do the work in each argument. Please treat it as a piece of language and disambiguate it. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise claims the phrase could be used to express. The readings will probably differ along several dimensions—whether the claim is about the number of persons, the unity of a single life, the availability of a common unit in which to compare different people's gains and losses, or the conditions under which one person's loss may be traded against another's gain—but do not feel bound by that list. For each reading, give a sentence or two on what it commits one to, whether it is near-tautological, substantive but defensible, or arguably false, and whether it genuinely cuts against utilitarianism or only restates a disagreement. Then say which reading, if any, would actually underwrite the two seminar moves, and which readings would leave those moves unsupported. I want to leave able to say exactly which version of the slogan I would be granting or denying.
