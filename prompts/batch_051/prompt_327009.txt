In Joseph Butler's sermon "Upon Self-Deceit," he argues that self-deception often works not by changing a person's explicit principles but by making them apply a laxer or narrower standard to their own case than they would apply to another person's. The moral standard stays intact; what bends is the application. Butler's proposed remedy is, roughly, to force a person to judge their own case by the same rule they would use for a stranger.

Suppose an institution tries to operationalize Butler's remedy. Every proposal to commit significant resources—a new project, a pilot, a partnership, a hire—must be accompanied by a "stranger-version review." The proposing officer writes a one-page summary of the proposal with all identifying ties removed: no names, no history, no indication that it is their own or their ally's. Then they must answer the question: "If a stranger submitted this proposal, what would I recommend?" The proposal, the officer's actual recommendation, and their stranger-version answer go to a three-person self-deception panel. If the panel judges that the officer would likely have recommended rejecting the stranger's version while recommending the actual version, it can force an independent second review. If that happens twice in a year, the officer loses proposal rights for six months.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually play out, rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. What does the scheme assume about self-deception—in particular, about whether a person has reliable access to the relevant partiality at the moment it is operating? What incentives does it create for how officers write the stranger-version summaries? How might the summaries themselves become a site for gaming, strategic over- or under-description, or performance of impartiality? What happens to the panel—its capture, its incentives to find or not find discrepancies, its own exposure to self-deception? Distinguish between failure modes that could be fixed by better design and failure modes that would persist under any version of the scheme because of what self-deception is. Can the mechanism be repaired without giving up on Butler's underlying idea, or does it depend on a contested view of self-knowledge?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. In particular, mark where the analysis turns on a disputed philosophical account of self-deception—for example, whether it is intentional, a form of motivated reasoning, or something that bypasses conscious judgment—versus on more settled institutional-design considerations.
