I'm a TA for an undergrad course on free will, and I'm grading the take-home short essay question below. The question I set was:

"Explain the main argument that quantum indeterminacy helps the libertarian about free will, then explain the standard objection to that argument. Does quantum mechanics give the libertarian what they need? Defend your answer."

One student wrote this (I'm pasting it exactly, except I cleaned up a couple spelling slip-ups):

"The libertarian about free will says that an action can't be free if it's determined by the past plus the laws of nature, because then it's settled before the agent even deliberates. So libertarians need at least some actions to be indeterministic. Quantum mechanics looks like it supplies this: events like radioactive decay don't have sufficient causes, so the same initial conditions can lead to different outcomes. If the neural events underlying a decision involve quantum-level indeterminacy, then at the moment of choice multiple outcomes are possible and the choice isn't fixed in advance. That's the main argument for quantum indeterminacy helping the libertarian.

The standard objection is that indeterminacy gives us randomness, not freedom. If whether I keep a promise comes down to a quantum coin flip in my brain, the outcome is no more under my control than a reflex; it just happens to me. So randomness replaces causation with luck.

The libertarian can reply by distinguishing mere randomness from being uncaused in the right way. A coin flip is uncontrolled because it's caused by irrelevant physical forces, but that doesn't mean every indeterministic event lacks control. Quantum mechanics shows that some events are uncaused, and an uncaused event is one nothing forced to happen. If a decision is uncaused by anything outside my reasons and character, then it's not forced, and that's all the control the libertarian needs. The objection would only work if every cause had to be a deterministic cause. Since quantum mechanics takes away that assumption, the randomness objection fails. So quantum indeterminacy does help the libertarian, and refuting determinism does the main work: once the decision isn't determined, the only remaining task is to show it reflects my reasons, which is a fairly ordinary causal story."
