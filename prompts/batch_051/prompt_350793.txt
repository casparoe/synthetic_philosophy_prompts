My mother signed an advance directive in 2003 containing this paragraph:

“If I am ever diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state, or if I have an end-stage condition with no reasonable prospect of recovery, I direct that life-sustaining treatment, including artificially administered nutrition and hydration, be withheld or withdrawn. This instruction is to be honored even if I am then unable to make or communicate my own decisions.”

Six years ago she suffered a severe traumatic brain injury and was diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state. Her care was not withdrawn, and she has remained in a long-term care facility. This year, using a brain–computer interface that did not exist when she signed the directive, a research team found that she can answer yes/no questions with high accuracy. She is profoundly impaired and nothing like her former self, but she shows reproducible evidence of awareness, and when asked she signals clearly that she wishes to go on living.

I need to decide whether the directive still governs her care. The literal trigger is unsettled, because the detection of awareness means she may no longer meet the criteria for the condition the clause names, even though her underlying deficits have not improved and the only thing that changed is our ability to detect what was always there. The evident purpose of the clause—to spare her a life she regarded as worse than death—may or may not be served by applying it now. And the woman who wrote it did not foresee a state in which she could be both severely cognitively impaired and able to express a stable wish to live. How should the clause be applied under each of the standard approaches—its plain words, its evident purpose, what she would have said if asked about this possibility and this technology, and what a reasonable reader would understand it to mean? And what does this case show about why an earlier self has authority over a later self in the first place, and whether that authority survives once the later self can speak?
