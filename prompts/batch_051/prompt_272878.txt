Suppose a major auction house adopted the following mechanism for any work offered with incomplete or contested provenance. At the time of sale, the seller would be required to place in escrow an amount equal to thirty percent of the hammer price. The escrowed sum would collateralize a transferable "authenticity certificate" that matures after thirty years and is auctioned separately from the artwork itself; neither the buyer of the work nor anyone else is obliged to hold the corresponding certificate. At maturity, a standing panel with a contractual right to inspect the work would judge whether the attribution is sound. If the panel finds a forgery or misattribution, the escrowed sum is paid to whoever holds the certificate; if the attribution is upheld, the escrow reverts to the seller and the certificate expires worthless. The panel's members are paid from the auction premium and named in the sale record. Please analyze how this mechanism would actually behave rather than whether it is wise in the abstract. What would it incentivize for sellers, buyers, forgers, authenticators, and the auction house itself? How would it reshape the pricing of uncertain attributions and the premium carried by unquestioned originals? What failure modes, gaming strategies, and second-order effects would emerge, and which of them, if any, could be fixed by modifying the design without abandoning it?
