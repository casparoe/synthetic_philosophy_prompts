Hey — I'm a science journalist trying to frame a peice about whether your phone is literally part of your mind. The obvious reference point is the extended mind thesis: Clark and Chalmers' Otto and Inga story, where Inga stores the museum address in her head and Otto keeps it in a notebook he always carries. Their claim was roughly that if Otto's access to the notebook is just as constant, easy, and trusted as Inga's access to her biological memory, then the notebook is part of Otto's memory — the mind spills out into the world and there's no principled difference between the two cases.

But here's the development I want the piece to hinge on. For most people now the notebook isn't a notebook. It's a cloud note app, an auto-tagged photo library, a search history, an LLM that summarizes your old journals. These aren't inert props; they're actively managed by companies that rerank, rewrite, compress, or delete entries under terms of service, and sometimes the "memory" itself talks back — an assistant turns your rambling note into tidy bullet points and you later read those as your own recollection.

Does the extended mind thesis survive that shift? It feels like the development bites the trust and automatic-endorsement conditions hardest, but maybe it undercuts constancy more fundementally. Can you say exactly which premise fails, whether the view can be patched with a new condition (something like "the resource must not be curated by an agent with its own agenda" — but is that even the right fix?), or whether the whole idea only ever worked for stable, privately controlled props, so we should stop saying the phone is part of the mind? And for the piece: is there a crisp way to put when an outsourced memory that someone else curates stops being mind and becomes just an information service you consult?
