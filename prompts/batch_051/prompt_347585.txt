Here is an argument I have been worrying over, and I would like you to say exactly where it goes wrong. A visitor at a zoo sees the animal in the zebra enclosure. It looks in every way like a zebra, and on Nozick's tracking theory it is natural to say she knows it is a zebra: in the closest worlds where the enclosure held an antelope or a llama instead, she would not believe it was a zebra. She also knows, as a matter of simple understanding, that if the animal is a zebra, then it is not a cleverly disguised mule. Now:

(1) The visitor knows the animal is a zebra.
(2) The visitor knows that if the animal is a zebra, then it is not a cleverly disguised mule.
(3) If someone knows P and knows that P entails Q, then they can come to know Q by competently deducing Q from those premises.
(4) Therefore the visitor knows the animal is not a cleverly disguised mule.

I cannot see which step to reject: the argument appears valid, and (1) and (2) look secure. But Nozick's tracking account seems to deny (4). It says knowledge requires sensitivity: if (4) were false—if the animal were a cleverly disguised mule—it would look exactly as it does, the visitor would still believe it was a zebra, and so she would still believe (4). Thus her belief in (4) does not track the truth. That points to (3), the closure of knowledge under known entailment, as the false step.

Have I located the failure correctly? Say which step, if any, fails, whether the argument (1)–(4) is valid, and whether the real problem is instead that the method by which the visitor believes (4) has been left unspecified. If (3) is indeed false, state the restricted form of closure, if any, that Nozick's theory can still support, and explain how it handles this case.
