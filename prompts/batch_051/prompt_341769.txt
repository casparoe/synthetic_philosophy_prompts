I am a senior software engineer at a mid-sized company, and I sit on our hiring and promotion committee. Several times a month I have to make the same kind of judgment call under time pressure: whether some costly, highly visible thing a candidate or employee has done — maintaining a popular open-source project, volunteering for the most punishing on-call rotations, speaking at conferences, completing a selective bootcamp — should count as real evidence of the underlying trait we are trying to hire or promote for (engineering judgment, conscientiousness, ability to learn), or as a display that mainly proves they could afford the cost in time, money, or reputation. These decisions are expensive and hard to reverse, and we have to justify them in writing. I have read a little about the handicap principle and costly signaling, enough to know that the naive version of the argument — the idea that a signal must be honest simply because it was expensive — is wrong: a signal only carries information if the cost falls more heavily on a sender who lacks the trait than on one who has it. But I do not know how to turn that into something my committee can actually run, since none of us has formal training in philosophy or statistics.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for the committee: a checklist of questions to ask, in order, for deciding whether to treat a costly observable behavior as a signal of a particular underlying trait rather than as noise or as a display aimed at some other receiver. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is a value judgment rather than a factual one. The procedure must be usable by engineers with no background in philosophy or statistics in under fifteen minutes per signal, and it should leave a short written trail so the committee can defend its decision if it is challenged. It should handle signals whose costs are paid in different currencies — money, time, reputation, stress — and cases where the cost was paid partly by someone other than the sender, such as an employer that paid for the conference slot or a spouse who covered the living expenses. Throughout, it should preserve the distinction between a signal being expensive and a signal being informative, and it should say explicitly where that distinction can no longer be operationalized and a human has to decide.

Then run the procedure on three cases the committee actually argues about: (1) a promotion candidate who maintains a widely used open-source library but whose day-job code reviews and architecture work are only average; (2) a hiring candidate who completed a selective two-year bootcamp and produced a polished portfolio of tutorial projects, but whose public activity drops off almost completely after the program; and (3) an employee who consistently volunteers for the most punishing on-call shifts and is widely liked, while their long-term design and debugging work is only average. For each case, state the trait the signal is alleged to be evidence of, work through the checklist, and give the verdict. Make sure at least one case is one where the procedure tells us not to weight something at least one member of the committee currently treats as a strong positive; say exactly which feature of the case drives that verdict, and what we would have to believe about the trait, the sender pool, or the cost structure for the answer to flip.
