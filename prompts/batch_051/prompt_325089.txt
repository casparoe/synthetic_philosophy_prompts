I have been reading through arguments about the future of large historical entities—empires, dynasties, states, and a few unusually durable firms—and I keep meeting a particular methodological claim. The claim is that one should not reason from the internal story of the entity itself, because that story seduces the analyst with rich but biased detail; instead one should collect the lifespans of a set of past entities judged comparable, take a central tendency, and treat that as the rational, bias-corrected forecast. I am sympathetic to the instinct. Yet in practice the whole result appears to be driven by a decision that nobody defends: which past cases count as comparable. Restrict the comparison set to agrarian empires of a similar size and region and one lifespan emerges; condition on having already survived two centuries and another emerges; exclude states that were conquered rather than collapsed internally and the distribution shifts again. Each restriction can be justified by some real similarity, but there seems to be no independent standard for deciding which similarities matter, because deciding which similarities matter just is a theory about what drives the durability of the entity in question—which is close to the very thing the external, story-free method was meant to bypass.

My question is whether this methodological claim can be put to the test. Design the strongest feasible test of whether one way of assembling the comparison set yields forecasts that deserve more credence than another. Be concrete about the evidence you would collect, what result would count for the claim and what result would count against it, and then be precise about what even a favorable result would and would not settle. In particular: would a strong result show that the chosen comparison set captures the actual causal mechanism behind a given empire's survival, or merely that it has been calibrated to observed outcomes? I want to know where the empirical content of the method stops and where the conceptual choice begins.
