In my modern history class we have been comparing how South Africa, Argentina, and Rwanda dealt with the aftermath of mass political violence, and one asymmetry continues to trouble me. Consider a familiar amnesty-for-disclosure design. Two members of the same paramilitary unit are each responsible for a comparable politically motivated killing; one comes forward and gives a full public account to the truth commission and walks free, while the other stays silent and is prosecuted for the same kind of act. Their guilt is the same, their act is the same, and the only operative difference is the willingness to narrate the crime in public. Most people I know would find it unthinkable for ordinary murders, where a confession may reduce a sentence but does not extinguish the offense. Is there a genuine principle that justifies this asymmetry, or is it a pragmatic bargain dressed up as principle? I want the strongest defense you can build for making accountability for serious political violence turn so heavily on what the perpetrator is willing to say, tested against the nearest cases—for example, applying the same rule to ordinary violent crime, or the cooperation discounts of ordinary plea bargaining—and then an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up.
