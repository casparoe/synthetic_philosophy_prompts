The question the students were given was this: "Distinguish faithful from plausible chain-of-thought reasoning, and evaluate the claim that the accuracy gains produced by chain-of-thought prompting give us reason to think that a model's stated intermediate reasoning is a faithful report of how it reached its answer." Here is one student's answer, reproduced verbatim: "Faithfulness and plausibility are both properties of a model's stated reasoning, but they track different things. A chain of thought is faithful when it corresponds to the actual process that led the model to its answer, and plausible when it simply looks like competent reasoning to a human reader. The important point is that these can come apart: a post hoc rationalization can be highly plausible while failing to describe what actually happened. Still, I would argue that for autoregressive language models the distinction is less serious than it appears. In such models, each chain-of-thought token is generated before the final answer and becomes part of the context that conditions the answer, so the chain is by construction part of the causal history of the output. Whatever the internal representation underlying those tokens may be, the stated steps are what the model actually used to arrive at the answer. If that were not the case, the chain would be mere decoration, and it is hard to see how mere decoration could systematically improve accuracy on arithmetic and multi-step reasoning problems. Yet chain-of-thought prompting does produce such systematic improvements. So the performance gains are evidence that the reported reasoning is, in practice, faithful, even if careful definitions keep the conceptual distinction between faithfulness and plausibility alive."
