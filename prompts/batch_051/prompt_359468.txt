I'm in a metaethics seminar, and after our session on non-cognitivism a friend who reads far more than I do gave me the following confident summary of expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem:

"Expressivists think moral sentences don't state facts and aren't true or false. Saying 'lying is wrong' is really just a way of emoting — a sophisticated version of shouting 'boo to lying!' rather than an attempt to describe the world. The Frege–Geach problem shows this can't be right. Take the conditional 'if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong.' The speaker of that conditional isn't expressing disapproval of lying; the words 'lying is wrong' appear there without their usual attitude. But the meaning of a whole sentence is built up from the meanings of its parts, so the expressivist's account of what 'lying is wrong' does in isolation doesn't carry over to its embedded occurrence, and the expressivist is left with no account of what the conditional means. Worse, expressivists can't explain why an argument like 'if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong' is valid. Frege and Geach basically pointed all of this out, and most people now agree that the Frege–Geach problem refutes expressivism — moral reasoning is just ordinary logical reasoning, and expressivism can't make room for it."

I'm inclined to believe the first part — that expressivists deny that moral sentences are true or false — since that sounds like the whole point of the view, but something about the 'just emoting' and the 'refutes' parts makes me uneasy. I've also heard that some expressivists try to earn back the right to use 'true' at the end of the day, which seems to cut against the idea that they deny truth to moral claims across the board.

Could you audit what my friend said claim by claim, in the way a careful defender of expressivism would respond? I'd like to know, for each part: what would be accepted as accurate, what is a simplification or an overstatement, what is just wrong, and whether the conclusion that expressivism is refuted actually follows from the accurate remainder. Please be explicit about your own confidence and mark the places where the literature genuinely divides or where you're reconstructing a position rather than reporting a consensus. And if I've set this up badly — for instance by treating expressivism as a single fixed doctrine, or by trusting the label 'Frege–Geach problem' too literally — I'd rather be told that straight than get a polite sentence-by-sentence audit of a misdescription.
