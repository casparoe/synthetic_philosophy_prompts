I am a high school senior preparing a fifteen-minute talk for my school's political philosophy club, followed by a half hour of questions. The club is roughly two-thirds progressive and one-third libertarian or conservative, mostly juniors and seniors. The progressive members think and argue almost entirely in terms of harm and fairness, and they tend to treat loyalty, authority, and sanctity as names for bias, hierarchy, and taboo. The libertarian members are individualists who are suspicious of any psychology that makes group loyalty or reverence look like a legitimate part of moral life.

The thesis I will defend is this. Moral foundations theory is descriptively right that progressives lean almost entirely on care and fairness while conservatives draw on all five familiar foundations—care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity—but the standard lesson drawn from this, that each side is simply stuck in its own moral dialect, understates what the theory shows. If the theory is right about how moral intuitions work, then a progressive who refuses to argue in the language of loyalty, authority, and sanctity is making a strategic error, because those foundations are doing real motivational work in the audience. My deliberately provocative conclusion is that progressives should treat loyalty, authority, and sanctity as legitimate rhetorical resources to be used honestly for progressive ends, rather than as rationalizations to be debunked, while still insisting that the psychology cannot tell us whose political conclusions are right.

I will argue roughly as follows. First, the theory's descriptive asymmetry between left and right is robust enough to take seriously. Second, when an audience's moral intuitions are organized around a foundation, an argument that engages that foundation can move that audience where care-and-fairness language alone cannot. Third, nothing internal to the theory says care and fairness are the only foundations that can serve progressive ends: loyalty can underwrite solidarity, authority can protect expertise and democratic institutions, and sanctity can motivate environmental and bodily-integrity commitments. Fourth, the refusal to speak those languages is therefore a contingent, self-imposed handicap rather than a moral requirement. I will close by conceding that moral foundations theory cannot tell us whether any of these foundations deserve weight; that is a separate normative question.

Please write my Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections I will actually hear from this audience, ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way one of these students would actually ask them, with a note saying whether each comes from the progressive or the libertarian side. For each, give a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front because it costs little and defuses hostility, and what I must not concede even under pressure. Include the one question I most hope nobody asks. End the answer with \answer{...} containing a short label for the single objection most likely to sink the talk.
