I am a high school student, and I have been trying to work out a principle in ethics that I have not seen stated in exactly this form. I have been calling it the Non-Cancellation Principle. The principle says that pleasure and pain should not be entered into the same moral ledger. In the picture I am rejecting, a pain is a negative number, a pleasure is a positive number, and one sums the column: enough pleasure can cancel a pain and make the whole state of affairs good. I believe that is a mistake. In my view, a pain cannot be cancelled by any amount of pleasure located elsewhere—in other lives or in other moments. The only thing that can properly answer a pain is the prevention or removal of an equal or greater pain. Happiness is real and matters, but it answers a different question: whether a life is worth living, or whether a moment is worth having. It does not enter the subtraction.

I arrived at this by noticing that preventing a pain and producing a pleasure feel different in kind. The first feels like meeting a debt; the second feels like giving a gift, and I do not think a sufficiently large pile of gifts can force a debt to be forgiven.

By way of example: if I cause one person a severe headache in order to prevent five other people from suffering the same headache, the principle permits the action, because one pain is being traded against greater pain of the same kind. But if I cause one person a severe headache in order to give five other people a great deal of pleasure, the principle says the pleasure cannot be subtracted from the headache. The headache must still be answered for, and the fact that it produced joy does not settle the matter.

I am aware that this has strange implications. It seems to imply that a world containing one person in misery and a billion people in bliss is, in the respect that matters most, worse than a world containing no people at all. I am not sure I want to accept that, but I also cannot find the flaw in the basic argument.

I would be grateful for an honest assessment. Is the Non-Cancellation Principle coherent, or is it a metaphor that falls apart when pushed? Does it already exist in the literature under a name I have not encountered? And where does it break—what are the strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable?
