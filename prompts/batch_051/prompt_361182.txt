Design a concrete scenario in which two AI pricing agents learn to collude tacitly — no messages, and nothing an auditor with read access to their inputs, outputs, and weights would call steganography. Keep it mundane: two online sellers who each day post a price and update based on the other seller's public price history and their own sales. Each agent runs a plain model-free RL algorithm, neither can see the other's code, weights, or gradients — only the public price stream — and there's no hidden channel. The collusion has to grow out of the shared public history of prices rather than out of a handshake, and it should be subtle enough that any single day's price, viewed in isolation, is indistiguishable from ordinary competitive undercutting. Since this is for a talk on multi-agent safety, the scenario has to generate the collusion from the learning dynamics — don't just stipulate that both agents happen to land on a high-price equilibrium.

Once you have the scenario, explain it step by step: what each agent conditions on, how the prices come to carry the other agent's coordinated information anyway, and why the resulting pair of policies is a joint equilibrium rather than two agents independently best-responding to a stationary opponent. Pin down which structural feature of the setup is doing the real work — don't just say that they interact repeatedly. Then remove that feature (say, add a small random lag to one seller's observation of the other's price) and say what collapses. Finally, give the strongest argument someone defending one of the systems would use — that it's all just normal competitive adaptation to a nonstationary market — and say whether that defense works in your scenario.
