My friend and I have been going back and forth about a biopic that came out recently. It's about a composer, and the movie invents a whole secret marriage, moves the dates of his life around, and gives him a big teary speech that (as far as anyone knows) he never gave. My friend says I'm wrong to care about any of this. His line is that a biopic is a painted portrait, not a photograph: the goal is a recognizable likeness and an interpretation, not a record, so complaining that a detail is false is like complaining that a portrait has the wrong number of eyelashes. On his view the filmmakers owe the dead person the same thing a portraitist owes a sitter — capture something essential about them — and once they've done that, the invented stuff doesn't wrong anyone, whether it's adding an affair to a resistance fighter's life, giving a politician a redemption arc he didn't earn, or giving a singer an illness they never had.

I don't buy the analogy, but I can't tell how much of my resistance is just that I disliked the movie. Could you audit the analogy for me: lay out what maps onto what (the sitter, the painter, the pose, the likeness, the brushwork — maybe the gallery too), then go through the places where it breaks, and say which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's using it for. The conclusion I care about is whether, in a dramatization of a real dead person, it's a mistake or a wrong to invent or distort major facts as long as the portrait stays "recognizable." Tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy he should be reaching for instead. Keep it under 600 words.
