I find myself attracted to a strong view about moral worth: an action has moral worth only if it is done from the motive of duty — the agent acts because they take the action to be right or required. The view has the kind of systematic power I normally trust: a single test that explains why accidentally beneficial acts lack worth, why self-interested compliance with duty lacks worth, and why the same outward act can be creditable in one agent and not in another. But I also have a firm intuition that resists it. When someone comforts a grieving friend out of spontaneous love, with no occurrent thought of duty, or helps someone despite having been raised to believe the helping is wrong, I find myself granting the act full moral worth — sometimes more than I grant a cold or grudging performance of a duty the agent explicitly recognizes. I do not want a verdict delivered from the sidelines. Give me the strongest version of each side, then say what should yield, what should be amended, and whether there is a reading of acting for the right reasons on which the theory and the intuition are compatible. For each possible resolution — abandoning the theory, amending it, debunking the intuition, or reconciling them — tell me what would justify that move. Keep your answer to at most 600 words.
