Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Sen's liberal paradox, and by the end I was frustrated: every response we could formulate seemed, on reflection, to fall into one of five families. As I understand the setup, the paradox arises because three plausible requirements cannot all hold in a single social ranking: unrestricted domain for individual preference orderings; weak Pareto, so that if everyone prefers one alternative to another the social ranking must agree; and a minimal liberty condition under which each of at least two people is decisive over some pair of alternatives that touch only that person's own private choice. The standard two-person, one-book example shows how the three principles force a strict cycle.

Those five families are: (1) restricting the domain to exclude meddlesome or nosy preferences; (2) replacing pairwise decisiveness with a richer model in which rights are game forms or constraints on what agents can do rather than demands on the collective ranking; (3) weakening or abandoning Pareto so that it cannot override a protected personal choice; (4) accepting that the social ranking may be intransitive or cyclic; and (5) rejecting the social-choice formalization of rights wholesale, on the grounds that rights are side-constraints on action rather than inputs to a collective preference relation.

I would like a sixth response to Sen's paradox that is not on this list and is not a rephrasing, special case, or combination of any of the five. It should differ from them in structure—for example, by targeting a feature of the formal setup that none of the five currently targets. After stating the response, assess in a few sentences whether it genuinely dissolves the paradox or only shifts the difficulty elsewhere. End your answer with a final line beginning with "Summary:" followed by a single sentence stating the new response.
