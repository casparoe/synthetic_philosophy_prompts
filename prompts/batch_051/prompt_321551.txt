I am a retired research chemist, and since leaving the lab I have been working through the literature on mental causation. The causal exclusion argument has come to seem to me both the sharpest challenge to the reality of mental causation and, at the same time, guilty of overreaching. Below is an argument I have built to make that suspicion precise. I would like a committed assessment of it. If the argument is invalid, identify the first step at which it fails and give the exact reading of the term on which the failure turns. If it is valid but unsound, state which premise is false and what a defender of the exclusion argument should say in reply, including whether the reply ends up being special pleading. Please do not present a menu of options; commit to one diagnosis and defend it.

(1) The exclusionist is committed to a principle roughly like this: if a property F is distinct from a property P, and an instance of P is by itself nomologically sufficient for an effect E, then F is not causally relevant to E. Systematic overdetermination is set aside as an unacceptable general account of ordinary causation.

(2) For any mental property M that is supposed to produce a bodily effect E, there is a neural or other physical property P of the agent, distinct from M, whose instantiation is by itself nomologically sufficient for E.

(3) From (1) and (2), no mental property is ever causally relevant to a bodily effect.

(4) The same pattern holds throughout the special sciences. For any higher-level property invoked in a genuine causal explanation — an enzyme's being catalytically active, a solution's being acidic, a market's being in recession — and any effect it is invoked to explain, there is a distinct microphysical property of the system whose instantiation is by itself nomologically sufficient for that very effect.

(5) From (1) and (4), catalytic activity, acidity, economic recession, and their like are never causally relevant to the effects they are invoked to explain.

(6) The conclusion in (5) is scientifically unacceptable. It does not show that special-science explanations are compressed or coarse-grained; it shows they are not causal at all, which would make the practice of chemistry, biology, and economics a systematic error. I do not think a philosophical principle can force that on us.

(7) Since (5) follows from (1) and (4) together, and since (4) is not seriously deniable for the sciences, the fault lies with (1). The exclusionist's principle is false, and therefore the argument for epiphenomenalism about the mental does not go through.

Question: is this argument valid? If it is invalid, where exactly does it break? If it is valid, which premise should a sophisticated exclusionist reject — (1), (2), (4), or (6) — and can they reject it without giving up the conclusion that mental properties are causally excluded? I want a definite verdict, not a catalogue of interpretations.
