A few weeks ago, the social choice reading group I belong to worked through Sen's liberal paradox: the theorem that no social decision rule can satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism, where minimal liberalism requires at least two individuals each to be decisive over some pair of social alternatives (for instance, each over whether they themselves read a certain novel). Afterwards, two members of the group, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about what the theorem actually shows. (1) Elena said it demonstrates a genuine tension between individual rights and the Pareto principle, and that the conflict is a fact about liberal societies, not an artifact of how rights are formalized. (2) Marcus said that was backwards: the "liberalism" condition is a crude formalization of rights, and the real culprit is unrestricted domain; once you restrict admissible preferences to non-meddlesome profiles, rights and Pareto are compatible, so the paradox teaches a lesson about domain restrictions, not about liberalism. (3) Elena replied that the needed domain restriction is itself a substantive liberal moral commitment, roughly a principle of tolerance, so Marcus has not shown that liberalism and efficiency are compatible — he has relocated the problem to justifying that restriction; meanwhile, the theorem still forces a liberal to choose between honoring unanimous preferences and honoring even minimal personal spheres. (4) Marcus answered that this "choice" only arises because the model represents rights as decisive social rankings rather than as constraints on permissible action: a right not to be interfered with is not a veto over society's preference ordering, and no actual liberal theory expects the social ranking to reflect it in the way Sen's condition demands. (5) Elena came back that even if you redesign the representation of rights, the underlying point remains that there are possible preference profiles in which honoring each person's protected choices while satisfying Pareto would leave the social ranking intransitive, so you cannot dissolve the incompatibility by redescribing rights — you must either restrict the domain, drop Pareto, or drop the rights. (6) Marcus said he accepts that lemma but not the moral: the fact that a bad model yields cycles tells us nothing about liberalism; it tells us the model is bad, and the real question is which axiom to abandon. (7) Elena said that was exactly her point — "which axiom to abandon" just is the substantive conflict the paradox identifies.

I couldn't tell how much of that exchange was genuine dialectical progress and how much was each of them re-asserting a starting position. Please adjudicate it move by move: for each numbered move, say what is right and what is wrong in it, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and explain how it bears on what came before. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything significant that both Elena and Marcus are missing about the theorem. Please write your answer in American English.
