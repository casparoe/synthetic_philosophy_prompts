For my free will module I need a thought experiment that does a very specific job, and I keep talking myself in circles trying to build it. Construct a case where a decision turns on genuine quantum indeterminacy — not a coin flip, not mere ignorance of causes, but real quantum randomness, say a radioactive decay event gating a tie-break — and yet the agent is clearly less in control than if the same choice had been settled by an ordinary deterministic rule. The line students always reach for is that quantum mechanics breaks determinism and that's all libertarianism needs; I want the reverse, a case where the indeterminism subtracts agency instead of adding any. Constraints: keep it realistic (a courtroom, a clinic, a hiring decision — no demons, no perfect predictors), write the scenario itself in one paragraph, and don't just stipulate that the agent gets bypassed; the loss of control has to fall out of how the quantum event enters the decision. After the scenario, walk me through what a libertarian incompatibilist would have to say about it, and name the single feature of the case that's doing the real work. Then swap the quantum tie-breaker for a deterministic one — say a rule the agent adopted in advance — and tell me whether the agent looks any freer, and why. I mostly want to know whether the indeterminism is actually doing any of the work or whether I've just build a case about non-authorship. And briefly: how would a libertarian defend herself against the case?
