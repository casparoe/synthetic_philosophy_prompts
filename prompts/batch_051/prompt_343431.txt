I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and there's a habit in my own reporting that I'd like someone to audit. Whenever the overall numbers and the subgroup numbers point in opposite directions — the Simpson's paradox setup — I notice the rule people reach for flips depending on the case. In a college admissions story where men are admitted at a higher rate overall but the gap disappears or reverses department by department, everyone tells me to trust the department-level picture: the aggregate is just an artifact of who applies where. In a drug trial story where a treatment helps overall but the benefit vanishes or reverses within every age bracket, the same people warn me not to slice the data post hoc and say the overall result is what matters. I can't articulate a single principal that tells me when the whole is the artifact and when the parts are. Is there one that justifies this asymmetry, or is it just an inconsistency data journalism lives with?
