The long-running dispute about whether the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were justified has a peculiar feature: the same facts now get recruited in support of opposite conclusions. One side treats the prospective casualty estimates for an invasion of the home islands, the state of Japanese decision-making in July 1945, and the Soviet Union’s planned entry into the Pacific War as reasons for using the bombs; the other treats the same intelligence about Japanese peace soundings, the refusal to modify the demand for unconditional surrender, and the brief interval between Hiroshima and Nagasaki as evidence of avoidable haste. Neither side denies the underlying facts, yet no amount of additional archival detail seems to move the judgments.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse originates in an unnoticed shift between an ex ante question about the decision problem facing Truman and his advisers and an ex post question about the bombings as historical events with known consequences. The Franck Report, written before the bomb had been tested and before the decision to use it was made, seems to encode the ex ante standpoint in an especially pure form. Its authors write:

"Scientists have often before been accused of providing new weapons for the mutual destruction of nations, instead of improving their well-being. It is undoubtedly true that the discovery of flying, for example, has so far brought much more misery than enjoyment or profit to humanity. However, in the past, scientists could disclaim direct responsibility for the use to which mankind had put their disinterested discoveries. We cannot take the same attitude now because the success which we have achieved in the development of nuclear power is fraught with infinitely greater dangers than were all the inventions of the past."

I read this passage not as prediction or policy advice but as a claim about the temporality of justification: the responsibility it describes attaches before the act, to those who know enough to foresee what the act would mean. So here is what I want: a diagnosis of the stalled debate in light of that passage. Is the ex ante/ex post split the real structure of the disagreement, or is that itself a red herring covering some deeper division — say, over what counts as a relevant alternative, whether "military necessity" is an empirical estimate or a moral threshold, or whether the killing of noncombatants can be justified by a merely probable saving of lives? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal decision-theoretic result, a criterion for when ex post knowledge may be used in moral judgment, or something else?
