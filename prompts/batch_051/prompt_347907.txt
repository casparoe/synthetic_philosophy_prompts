I'm a public-health policy analyst who reads moral philosophy on the side, and I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about the consequences of a policy — and in my own head — that I don't have a name for. Take a soda tax. One person traces the consequences: the tax raises the price of sugary drinks, consumers buy fewer, sugar intake falls, diabetes rates fall. That looks like a point in favor. Another person accepts every step but keeps going: manufacturers reformulate with cheaper sweeteners or smaller bottles, retailers shift their promotions, consumers substitute to other caloric products, and the health gain shrinks or reverses. The first person replies that if you go further still — new norms about sugar, firms redirecting product development — the balance shifts again. The puzzling thing isn't just that remote consequences are hard to know. It's that the conclusion seems to be a function of where one stops the chain, and the stopping point never announces itself: each further step is a reasonable thing to think about, but no step comes labeled 'the last relevant one.' I see the same structure in disputes about a new cancer screening program: stop at the cancers caught and it looks clearly good; include the false positives, the crowded clinics, and the budget displaced from something else, and it looks worse; include the long-run pressure on the screening technology, and it looks different again. Experienced arguers, I've noticed, can often feel which horizon will favor which conclusion, and they choose where to stop the way a litigator chooses a venue — yet the stopping point is never stated as a premise; it just looks like 'following the consequences a little further.' I know there is a general philosophical discussion of how badly we know long-run consequences. But I'm after the more specific phenomenon: the systematic dependence of the verdict on an unacknowledged choice of horizon, and the way the horizon silently carries the moral conclusion. Does this have an established name? If the closest existing concepts capture only part of it, I'd want to know exactly what part they miss and where the edges are; if none fits, what would a good name be — one that carves at the joints rather than merely relabeling the fact that we cannot know the future?
