I’m writing a novel built around an industrial disaster, and I’ve found myself tangled in a philosophical dispute among three characters: an engineer who insists that the cause is whatever you would manipulate to prevent the outcome, a lawyer who says the cause is the but-for condition without which the accident would not have happened, and a sociologist who thinks this whole quarrel is misguided because, in Mill’s terms from A System of Logic, singling out one condition as “the cause” while demoting the rest to mere background conditions is something we do for our own practical purposes, not because nature makes that distinction for us. I want the book to dramatize the argument rather than merely gesture at it, so I need to understand what is actually at stake.

The conceptual claim I want you to test is this: the distinction between a cause and a background condition is a real feature of causal structure, not just an interest-relative projection. Design the best concrete test you can of that claim. It may be a formal modeling exercise, a psychological experiment about how people assign causal responsibility, or a combination; but make it specific enough that someone could actually carry it out or simulate it. What would you vary, what would you measure, and what pattern of results would favor the interventionist reading, the counterfactual-but-for reading, or Mill’s deflationary reading? Then tell me what a clean positive result would and would not settle: could it show that the world itself privileges some conditions over others, or only that human judges reliably converge under certain prompts? Where is the residue that no test can reach, and is that residue a limitation of our methods or a sign that the original claim is not fully empirical?

End with a one-sentence summary of your final position on whether the cause/background distinction is real or projected.
