I'm a software engineer and I've never studied philosophy formally, but I recently went down a rabbit hole on revealed preference theory after a collegue mentioned it in the context of the recommender system we're building at work. The core idea, as I understand it, is that you can't observe utility directly, so you define it through choice: if someone consistently chooses A over B when both are available, that just is what it means for A to have higher utility for them. Utility isn't some hidden quantity sitting behind the choices; it's a compact, mathematically tidy representation of the choice behaviour, kind of like a loss function you fit to observed clicks. I find this deeply attractive. It's clean, it's falsifiable, it doesn't require prying into anyone's head, and it gives you a principled way to talk about preference without hand-waving about happiness or wellbeing.

But I can't shake the opposite intuition. Some choices obviously don't track what's good for a person. An addict keeps choosing heroin even while saying it's ruining their life; a person raised to believe they deserve mistreatment picks an abusive partner; someone hands over their wallet at gunpoint. In ordinary life I'd say those choices are wrong about their welfare — that their utility, in the sense of genuine wellbeing, comes apart from what they reveal by choosing. If utility can stand over and above choice and call the choices mistaken, then utility isn't just a representation of choice, and the whole tidy theory starts to look like it's describing something thinner than what I actually care about. So I want the negotiation, not just a winner: what would it take for the intuition to be debunked or reinterpreted so the theory stands, what would it take for revealed preference to be amended or abandoned, and is there a coherent reading of 'utility' where both survive — where utility can be both a representation of choice and a substantive notion of welfare? Please answer in British English.
