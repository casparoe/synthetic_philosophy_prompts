I direct continuing education for a regional 9-1-1 emergency medical dispatch center. Our dispatchers make dozens of high-stakes triage and resource-allocation decisions per shift, many in under a minute, using scripted protocols and the pattern recognition that comes with years on the desk. Senior leadership has been promoting "think about your thinking" as an unqualified good, but our outcome data suggest experienced dispatchers make fewer errors when they do not interrupt a live call to second-guess their own reasoning, while newer dispatchers appear to need more explicit self-checking, not less. Turn the claim that metacognition is not always helpful into a reusable decision procedure our trainers can apply. For a given call type and a given dispatcher's experience level, the procedure should determine whether to prompt deliberate self-monitoring—pausing to state one's assumptions, list alternatives, check for anchoring or fixation—or whether to leave trained, fluent performance alone. Specify: the questions to ask in order; which answers push toward self-monitoring and which toward letting expertise run; where the procedure stops with a no or hands the decision to a person; and the cases it cannot settle on its own. Then run the procedure on three incidents: a veteran dispatcher handling a multi-casualty call with conflicting witness reports; a trainee handling a routine chest-pain call; and a mid-level dispatcher who has just committed a near-miss error and is now second-guessing every decision—including at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer the staff will dislike.
