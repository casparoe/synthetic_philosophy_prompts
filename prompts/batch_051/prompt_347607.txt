In *Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren* Keynes appears to say two different things about avarice and the love of money. Early in the essay he argues that for a long time yet we will have to pretend that fair is foul and foul is fair, because avarice, usury, and precaution are the only forces that can carry us out of the tunnel of economic necessity. Later in the same essay he says that once the economic problem is solved we will be free to return to older moral principles: avarice is a vice, usury is a misdemeanour, and the love of money is detestable. Are these two verdicts genuinely in conflict, or can they be reconciled by reading the first as a conditional endorsement of avarice as a temporary means and the second as an unconditional condemnation of it as an end? What exactly is the distinction between means and ends doing here, and is Keynes entitled to it? If the same character trait is being praised as instrumentally indispensable now and condemned as intrinsically objectionable later, what has to be true about the economic problem for that to be coherent rather than special pleading? And does the reconciliation preserve Keynes's view that we can enjoy both the benefits of the accumulation era and the moral transformation he predicts, or does one of the passages have to give way?
