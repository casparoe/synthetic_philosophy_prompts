One of the moves I keep seeing in otherwise serious readers of Machiavelli is to lean hard on his river image from chapter 25 of The Prince: fortune is a river that floods, and virtù is preparing the dikes and channels in the calm years so that when the flood comes it does less damage. People use the image to license a pretty specific conclusion: fortune isn't really capricious for Machiavelli, its more like bad weather — you cant stop it but you can prepare for it, so the lesson is sober institutional preparedness, not radical contingency or total helplessness. I want an audit of how far that image can actually carry that conclusion. Give me the correspondences first: river, flood, dikes, channels, the engineer, the calm season, the damage done, and anything else worth mapping. Then the disanalogies: what does the river picture quietly smuggle in about fortune that the rest of Machiavelli's text denies or at least complicates — that fortune is seasonal and predictable in kind, that the same defenses work every time, that preparation is a one-off capital project, that the flood is impersonally indifferent to who gets hit, and so on. Also, Machiavelli himself switches metaphors in the same chapter to something much less engineering-like, where fortune looks like it favors boldness, youth, and timing over patiently built defenses. So does the preparedness conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the river doing the arguing? If you think the river is the wrong image for what Machiavelli actually means by virtù, propose a better one and say what it gets right that the river gets wrong.
