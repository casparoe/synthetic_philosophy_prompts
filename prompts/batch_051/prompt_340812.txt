I am a retired natural scientist—a physical chemist by training—and I have only recently taken up philosophy. I have found myself troubled by something I have observed for decades in the church I occasionally attend, and I should like an analysis that goes beyond the usual sermons. In the intercessory prayers each Sunday, the congregation asks God for quite specific outcomes: that a sick child recover, that fighting in a particular country cease, that rains come after a drought. Yet the same tradition teaches, and the prayers themselves often affirm, that God is omniscient, already knowing our needs before we ask, and immutable, unable to change His mind, will, or plan. To a scientist, the practice therefore looks strange: it appears to be addressed to an agent who cannot receive information He lacks and cannot be moved to revise a decision. I am not asking for a theological defence or refutation of petitionary prayer. Instead, I would like you to treat the practice as a datum and reconstruct the philosophical commitments it presupposes. Specifically: what view of God's relation to time, of divine action, of human agency, and of the role of the request itself must be presupposed if such praying is to be more than a piece of self-administered therapy? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say whether those presuppositions are jointly coherent, and tell me what would have to change in the practice, or in the surrounding doctrine, if one were to replace them with the contrary view that God's knowledge and will are fixed timelessly and that the whole course of history is already fully determinate. Please write your answer in British English.
