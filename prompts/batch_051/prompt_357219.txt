I have been thinking about an old view of language and a recent development that seems to put pressure on it. The view is that grammatical categories—things like noun, verb, and preposition, or the case distinctions in languages such as Latin or Finnish—are not just labels that school grammars impose on speech, but real joints in how language is organized. The reason this view appeals to me is that the categories seem to do genuine work: they let grammarians state rules compactly, they show up again and again in unrelated languages, and learners pick them up in ways that would be a surprisingly lucky fit if the categories were mere conveniences invented by teachers.

The development that troubles me is the success of language models trained on raw text to predict the next word, with no one marking which words are nouns or which endings are dative. These models still produce grammatical sentences and can translate passably. From what I understand, when people inspect the internal groupings such models form, those groupings sometimes line up with noun or verb and sometimes slice the language in ways a traditional grammarian would never have recognized.

I want to know whether this development undermines the idea that grammatical categories are natural kinds. Does getting so far without explicit categories show that noun, verb, preposition, and the cases are only one useful summarizing scheme among many? Or could the model have quietly rediscovered the real kinds, just represented in a form that does not look like traditional grammar? I would like you to spell out what the natural-kind view actually needs to be true, and then say whether these models give us good reason to think the view fails, survives, or needs to be revised.
