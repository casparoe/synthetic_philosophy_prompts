I'm taking a philosophy of biology unit and my flatmate keeps saying if it has DNA it's alive, which is obviously too thin, but the alternatives aren't cohering for me either. I've been collecting my gut calls on the classic edge cases and I want to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I should just give up and say 'life' is a family resemblance thing.

My calls: a virus isn't alive, but it's not just a molecule either — I'd say biological rather than alive. A prion isn't alive. A mule is alive even though it can't reproduce, same with a worker ant. A single mitochondria isn't alive even though it metabolizes and has its own genome; a free-living bacterium is alive. A lichen is alive, and I count it as one organism, even though it's a fungus and an alga symbiotically fused. A Portuguese man o' war is alive, but I don't think it's one organism; it's a colony of specialized zooids. I know that pair's weird — the lichen is genetically distinct species and the man o' war's zooids are clones — but that's how my gut lands. A frozen tardigrade that can revive is alive while it's frozen. A brain-dead human body on a ventilator isn't alive as an organism, even though most of its cells are alive. An ant colony feels like a superorganism but not literally alive. A self-replicating program running on a computer isn't alive. A crystal growing in solution isn't alive.

Is there one definition or set of criteria that gets all of these, or close? And what does it say about the one I keep flipping on: a 30,000-year-old seed that can still germinate — alive or not? If you have to drop or revise one of my verdicts to keep the principle clean, which one would you drop and why?
