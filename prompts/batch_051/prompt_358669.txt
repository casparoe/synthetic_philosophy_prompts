I have been keeping a decision log on a colleague in our root-cause-analysis group. She reviews incidents in which an operational system fails in several ways at once and chooses between proposed explanations. Her verdicts are strikingly consistent, and I want to predict her next call before I ask her directly. The log records only her calls, not my glosses.

The question underneath the log is which virtue of inference to the best explanation is doing the work in her judgments — parsimony of causes, mechanistic specificity, or avoidance of coincidence — and the entries below are the data I have.

Entries so far:

1. Botanical garden. Three separate conservatories developed leaf scorch, root rot, and stunted growth in the same month. Proposed: (a) one recirculation-pump failure changed water pH and salinity for all three conservatories, with a specific pathway for each symptom; or (b) three independent problems — a fungal outbreak in one house, a heater fault in another, and a fertilizer error in the third. Her call: (a).

2. Call center. In the same week, average handle time rose, first-call resolution fell, and agent attrition ticked upward. Proposed: (a) one routing-software update misassigned calls, with a documented mechanism for each downstream effect; or (b) three independent causes — a seasonal illness wave, a revised post-call survey, and a competitor hiring. Her call: (a).

3. Analytical lab. After a long weekend, a chromatograph, an analytical balance, and a spectrophotometer all gave drifted readings. Proposed: (a) one HVAC fault let the lab overheat, affecting each instrument through a known mechanism; or (b) three independent instrument faults that happened to appear over the same weekend. Her call: (a).

4. Farm. Spring yields came in low, the well water was cloudy, and the barn's metal roof showed new corrosion. Proposed: (a) one fuel-tank leak contaminated the groundwater, with a specified route to each symptom; or (b) three independent problems coinciding — an over-applied herbicide, sediment in the well, and acid rain. Her call: (a).

Now she is considering a new case. An e-commerce platform had a bad afternoon: payment authorizations began failing, the checkout page slowed severely, and transactional email stopped going out. The two proposed explanations are:

(a) a single recent deploy is responsible, but no one has reconstructed an actual pathway from any element of the deploy to the three symptoms, and two of the three systems sit on entirely different code paths;

(b) three independent failures, each with a specific mechanism: an expired TLS certificate on the payment gateway, a misconfigured cache policy on the checkout service, and a rate limit reached at the email provider — but the explanation requires the three to occur independently on the same afternoon.

What should I expect her to call? Then specify the future case that would settle which of the leading rules she is using.
