I've come to think that purity and sanctity form an irreducible family of moral values: that judgments about contamination, desecration, defilement, and the sacred cannot be paraphrased away as judgments about harm, rights, or welfare, and that a sound moral theory should retain them as genuine sources of reasons. I'd like a map of what would count against this position. Please treat it as a substantive normative and metaethical claim, not merely a description of what people moralize: which empirical findings, moral cases, conceptual arguments, and theoretical pressures would threaten it? For each, say whether it would be fatal, serious, moderate, or merely awkward, ordered from fatal to awkward. Also mark which of these a defender of the view should honestly concede as damaging rather than dismiss as question-begging, and whether the position can be qualified to absorb the awkward ones without becoming trivial or unfalsifiable. In particular, consider whether the position's fate hangs on how we explain moral dumbfounding, on the way disgust transfers from bodily to social contamination, and on there being a principled boundary between the sacred and the merely strongly valued. Finally, present the assessment as a table with exactly these columns: Defeater; What it would show; Severity (fatal, serious, moderate, awkward); Conceded by defenders? (yes, contested, no); Available reply and whether it removes or merely blunts the problem.
