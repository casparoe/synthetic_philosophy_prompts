Consider the following contrast in the regulation of third-party reproduction. In much of Western Europe, commercial surrogacy is prohibited, and egg donation is permitted only on a no-payment or strictly limited expense-reimbursement basis; some countries in this group permit unpaid surrogacy, and some will recognize children born through surrogacies arranged abroad. In much of the United States, by contrast, commercial surrogacy is enforceable in many states, and egg donors are openly recruited with substantial payments above expenses. A legal scholar says she cannot tell what the European position is actually presupposing, because it seems to permit the acts of egg provision and gestation while forbidding payment for them, as though the money itself were the wrong-making feature, whereas the American position appears to treat these acts as services that can be priced like other labor. She asks you to work out what view of reproductive labor, bodily autonomy, commodification, parental status, or human dignity each approach presupposes, and whether the European focus on payment is coherent or depends on a distinction between gift and market exchange that the wider law cannot sustain. Reconstruct the implicit commitments of each approach, distinguish what the rules strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and say what would have to change in each regime if its implicit view were replaced by a straightforwardly market-based account of reproductive services, or by a thoroughgoing dignity-based account. Write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
