In the course of researching a novel about a small town flooded by a state-built dam, I keep colliding with a legal asymmetry that strikes me as strange. If a private company had destroyed the same houses, its engineers and executives could expect to face wrongful-death and property suits. If the state does it, the residents often cannot even get a hearing unless the state has consented to be sued. The law does not say the damage was justified; it says the forum is closed. I am trying to decide whether my characters are confronting a real principle or just a fossilized piece of king-craft. In Book I, Chapter 7 of his Commentaries on the Laws of England, William Blackstone quotes Sir Henry Finch as saying: "The king hath a prerogative in all things, that are not injurious to the subject; for in them all it must be remembered, that the king's prerogative stretcheth not to the doing of any wrong." That sentence seems to promise that sovereign power stops at the boundary of wronging a subject. Yet sovereign immunity, as a piece of practice, often means that the state's wrongs are not actionable by the very subjects it has wronged. I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry between private defendants and the state, and more specifically whether the Finch–Blackstone principle can be squared with the procedural shield. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
