I run a small local-history project in a town that's gentrifying fast. We collect oral histories and we decide which of the documents and photos people offer us actually get kept. We can do about forty interviews a year and everything has to fit in two filing cabinets and one old server, so every yes is a no to something else. I've started to think the real product of the archive isn't what we keep, its the silence around what we don't, and I want a procedure that takes that seriously instead of pretending we are neutral collectors. None of us is an archivist, and the board of trustees wants a checklist, not an essay, because our decisions shape how the town remembers itself for decades. The way I've been thinking about it, silences come in three kinds. Kind one is nobody thought to record the thing at the time. Kind two is somebody chose not to record it, because it was embarassing, or it seemed unimportant, or the person was hard to reach. Kind three is it was recorded once but then thrown out, lost, or deliberately destroyed. If you think my three kinds are a bad taxonomy, say so and give me a better one, but the thing I actually need is the procedure. Turn the distinction into a decision procedure we can run every month when people offer us interviews or material. Tell us what to ask and in what order when someone brings us something. Flag the cases where the procedure can't settle it and a human with judgment has to decide, instead of the checklist pretending to be complete. Give me at least three worked examples of the procedure actually being run, and make one of them a case where the answer will annoy me: our biggest donor wants us to digitize her family's papers for the fourth time, while we still have nothing from the guys working in the warehouses down the road. And be honest about what even a well-run procedure cannot fix about the archive's silences.
