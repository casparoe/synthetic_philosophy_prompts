I've been reporting on organ donation and keep getting tangled up in a point about donation after circulatory death (DCD) — the protocol where a patient who isn't brain-dead has life support withdrawn, the heart stops, the team waits a short interval, and then declares death and takes the organs. It seems to force you into a weird conclusion, and I can't spot the sophistry. Here's the argument as I understand it.

(1) A person is dead only when the cessation of their vital functions is irreversible.
(2) In DCD, at the moment death is declared (after a coupl of minutes without a pulse), the brain is not yet irreversibly destroyed. The cells are still alive, and if someone began resuscitation right then the patient could plausibly recover.
(3) So the cessation is irreversible only in the sense that the team has committed not to reverse it — the irreversibility comes from a decision and a policy, not from the biology.
(4) But whether you're dead is a biological fact, and it can't depend on what anyone has decided to do, or not do.
(5) So the DCD patient is not yet dead when declared dead, and if organs are removed then the removal is part of what kills them.
(6) Therefore current DCD practice violates the dead-donor rule, which says you can't take organs from someone who isn't dead.

I keep going back and forth on (3). A transplant surgeon told me the medical-ethics literature draws a distinction between "permanent" and "irreversible," and on the right definitions of those words the argument dissolves — but I've never been able to make that explanation stick. So: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which premise is doing the work, and what's the best reading of it that a defender of DCD would give? If it is valid, say so rather than flinching from the conclusion.
