The student wrote:

"The best candidate is ideal conceivability, i.e., a scenario surviving careful rational scrutiny without us finding a contradiction, as opposed to mere picturing. That matters because lots of impossible things are easily imagined: a badly drawn four-sided triangle, or water that boils at room temperature in a sloppy mental picture. The zombie argument needs this stronger notion, since zombies—physical duplicates of us with no phenomenal consciousness—are ideally conceivable: no amount of conceptual analysis turns up a contradiction between 'all the same physical facts' and 'no experience.' And since ideal conceivability entails possibility, zombies are possible. Their possibility is incompatible with physicalism, so physicalism is false. Kripke's worry, that conceivability can outrun possibility because water not being H2O is conceivable but impossible, doesn't sink this. Chalmers' two-dimensional semantics shows such cases are only primarily conceivable—conceivable when we evaluate the scenario before empirical facts fix what 'water' refers to—not secondarily conceivable. And it's only ideal primary conceivability that needs to entail possibility. That's enough, because the zombie scenario is ideally primarily conceivable, so it's possible in the sense the argument needs, and physicalism fails. So the only real concession is that ordinary imaginability is a weak guide, which everyone already grants."
