I have been trying to work out how Nozick's tracking theory handles a modified version of the familiar zoo case, and the standard verdict keeps coming apart under pressure. On the tracking theory as I understand it, S knows p via method M only if S would not have believed p via M had p been false, and S would have believed p via M had p been true, setting aside the separate truth and belief requirements. In the stock case, you see an animal in a zoo's zebra enclosure and know that it is a zebra; yet you seem not to know that it is not a mule the zoo has painted to look like a zebra, because had it been such a mule you would still have taken it to be a zebra, and so would still have believed it was not a mule. This failure of sensitivity for the second proposition is the usual tracking-theoretic basis for denying that knowledge is closed under known entailment.

Now vary the case. The zoo has forty animals, and at the entrance it posts a true notice stating that each day exactly one animal in the zoo is an impostor: a member of one species altered to look convincingly like another, chosen by a random draw each morning. You read and accept the notice, then go to the zebra enclosure, where a single animal stands that looks like a zebra. You form the belief that the animal is a zebra and, on the strength of that, the belief that the animal is not an impostor. Does the original diagnosis survive? Do you know the first proposition but not the second, or does the notice make the impostor possibility near enough that you fail to know even that the animal is a zebra? Work through each belief in turn: state the method, identify the relevant possible worlds in which the belief is false, and say what sensitivity and adherence give in each case. Then take up the threshold problem: if the zoo had four hundred or four thousand animals instead of forty, so that the chance that the animal before you is the impostor is correspondingly lower, at what point would the tracking theorist say the zebra belief counts as knowledge, and can that line be drawn in a principled way? Conclude by saying whether the original closure-failure argument depends on keeping the impostor possibility merely possible rather than assigning it a concrete frequency.
