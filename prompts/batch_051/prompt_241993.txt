I'm a novelist, and I keep running into a question I can't settle for a book I'm writing. It's about which self has authority when a person's earlier wishes and later wishes come apart. I've assembled a bunch of cases, and I have strong verdicts about each one, but I can't seperate those verdicts into a principle. I want you to find the principle, or tell me which of my verdicts I have to drop, and then apply the repaired view to a case I haven't made up my mind about.

The cases:

Daniel. A 63-year-old philosophy professor, two years into an Alzheimer's diagnosis, signs an advance directive saying that once he cannot recognize his wife or daughters, he wants no antibiotics for pneumonia and no artificial nutrition. He'd watched his father die through late-stage dementia, and in a recorded video he says, do not keep the shell alive. Three years later he doesn't recognize his daughters, but he's content: he likes music, pudding, and sitting in the garden. He gets pneumonia. When a nurse asks if he wants to go to the hospital, he says he's fine and wants to stay where he is. My verdict: honor the directive. The contented later self doesn't get to revoke.

June. A 34-year-old woman in a severe depressive episode signs a directive refusing intubation, dialysis, and tube feeding, saying her life is worthless. After ECT she recovers, writes a formal revocation, and says the directive was the disease talking. Months later a medication accident leaves her unconscious and she needs temporary ventilation; the old directive and the revocation are both in her chart. My verdict: the depressed self's directive had no authority from the start; ventilate her.

Ravi. Raised a Jehovah's Witness, at 25 he signs a durable no-blood card. At 35 he leaves the faith, tells his sister and partner the prohibition is nonsense, and texts his sister, if a transfusion would save me, give it to me. At 36, before he has gotten around to formally rescinding the card, a car wreck leaves him unconscious and bleeding. My verdict: transfuse. The later self wins.

Lena. At 28, after a friend's traumatic ICU stay, she signs a directive refusing any life support if she is ever in a minimally conscious state or worse. At 40 a brainstem stroke leaves her locked in: fully conscious, paralyzed, communicating with eye movements. She signals clearly that she wants to live. My verdict: honor the current self; the directive didn't anticipate this situation.

Marta. At 50 she signs, and re-affirms for ten years, a directive refusing artificial nutrition, hydration, and antibiotics if she ever has severe dementia. At 62, with mild cognitive impairment but still oriented and still passing every cognitive screen, she tells her daughter over and over that she's changed her mind, that she's terrified of decline and wants everything done if she worsens, but she keeps putting off signing the revocation form. At 64 she's severely demented, content, and gets pneumonia. She can't express a settled preference. My verdict: honor the original directive; a string of anxious, never-executed changes of mind doesn't count.

The standard way of framing this that I keep reading is that a competent earlier self gets to bind a diminished later one, but that framing alone doesn't handle June or Ravi, so I keep reaching for something more. The pair that bugs me is Ravi and Marta. In both of them, a later self clearly moves away from an earlier signed wish, but I let Ravi's later self win and I won't let Marta's. My working theory is something like the last fully competent self with a settled, informed view gets to decide. But then Daniel's later self gets ignored because it's not fully competent, June's earlier self gets ignored because it wasn't competent, Lena's current self wins because it's competent, Ravi's later self wins because it's competent and settled, and Marta's later self is sort of competent but I don't trust its settledness. Am I fooling myself? Is there one clean principle that gets all five verdicts, or do I have to drop one? State the principle precisely, say which clause handles which case, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then tell me what it predicts for the reverse of Ravi: a 25-year-old writes a directive accepting all treatment, including blood; at 35 he converts into a faith that forbids transfusions and says he now refuses blood; at 36 he's in the same car wreck. Does the newly religious self win the way Ravi's deconverted self won, and should it?
