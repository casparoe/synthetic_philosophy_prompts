I am a PhD student working on coordination failures and the way decentralized systems can produce outcomes that no participant actually wants. In his 2014 Slate Star Codex essay “Meditations on Moloch,” Scott Alexander gives a compact statement of the puzzle:

> The implicit question is – if everyone hates the current system, who perpetuates it? And Ginsberg answers: “Moloch”. It’s powerful not because it’s correct – nobody literally thinks an ancient Carthaginian demon causes everything – but because thinking of the system as an agent throws into relief the degree to which the system isn’t an agent.

I would like you to help me test a definition against a borderline case. I propose the following criteria for a multipolar trap:

(1) Decentralized maintenance: the outcome is produced and sustained by the independent, locally rational choices of many actors, not by a single commanding authority.

(2) Collective dispreference: most or all of the actors would prefer a different, achievable arrangement to the one they collectively produce.

(3) Costly unilateral exit: any actor who alone deviates from the trap’s behavior bears an immediate, significant cost relative to complying, while the others continue their behavior.

(4) Missing coordination mechanism: there is no available rule, authority, or contract that would let the group move to the preferred arrangement without some party risking a severe loss.

(5) Side-effect harms: the bad outcome emerges as the unintended aggregate of actions that are sensible, or at least defensible, from each actor’s local point of view.

Now consider a case. A large open-source software project has no formal leadership and relies on a small group of maintainers. Everyone in the group privately wants to spend a larger share of their effort on security audits, documentation, and the flaky test suite. But the project’s perceived momentum is what brings in funding opportunities, conference invitations, and new contributors. A maintainer who spends a quarter on maintenance rather than visible features will be seen as less productive, and if two others keep shipping features, the maintainer loses status and may be passed over for informal steering decisions. There is no mechanism to enforce a universal freeze on feature work: any attempt to call for one is met with polite agreement and then immediate violation as soon as the next contributor shows up with a pull request. No one wants the resulting security debt, but each person believes it would be naive to unilaterally change course.

Is this maintainer community in a multipolar trap by the five criteria above? Go through them one by one, and where the answer is no, say whether the case is not a trap or the criterion is misspecified. In particular, does criterion (4) require that the missing coordination mechanism be literally absent, or can it be present but inert because no one trusts it? And does Alexander’s passage suggest a sixth criterion—that participants themselves must be able to describe the system as an agent-like “Moloch” for the concept to apply—or is that only a rhetorical way of seeing a structural fact?
