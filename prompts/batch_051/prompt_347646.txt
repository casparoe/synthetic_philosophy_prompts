I organize an interview series in philosophy of mind, and our next guest is a philosopher who defends a higher-order thought theory of consciousness: on her view, a mental state is conscious just in case a suitable higher-order thought represents the person as being in that state. I just came away from a long argument about the view with a friend, and the conversation convinced me that the theory's standard responses sound stronger in summary than they do under sustained pressure. My friend would not let go of two points. First, the theory seems to make consciousness too intellectually demanding, because the higher-order state is thought-like and therefore seems to require conceptual capacities that infants and many nonhuman animals do not have. Second, the theory's treatment of misrepresentation is hard to credit: if a higher-order state can misrepresent a lower-order state's qualitative character, or can occur without any lower-order target at all, the view seems to allow that a person can consciously seem to be in a state she is not in, so that how things seem can come apart from how things are.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking the guest to restate the theory. Each question should be followed by a further question keyed to the reply the guest is most likely to give. Include at least one question on each of the following:

(i) the conceptual-sophistication objection and which creatures the view counts as conscious — where exactly the line falls, and whether the resulting verdicts can be made independently plausible;
(ii) misrepresentation and targetless higher-order states, and what they imply about the relation between how things seem and how things are;
(iii) the status of the higher-order state itself — why it need not be conscious, and why this does not simply generate a regress;
(iv) how the view fares against first-order representational and self-representational accounts in explaining what it is like to be in a conscious state;
(v) what empirical or neuroscientific findings, if any, would count against the theory rather than merely being accommodated by it;
(vi) whether the view answers the explanatory gap or only relocates it.

The questions should sound like they come from someone who knows the view and the surrounding debate, not someone meeting it for the first time. Write the questions in American English.
