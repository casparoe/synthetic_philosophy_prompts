I'm a philosophy PhD student working on naturalism, and I keep running into a tension in Quine's treatment of value that I cannot resolve. Quine argues that epistemology should be naturalized: questions about justification and evidence are not prior to science but are themselves empirical questions about how sensory stimulation gives rise to theory, to be pursued within psychology. Yet about ethics he takes what looks like a much more deflationary line. As I read him, he holds that moral judgments are not the sort of claim sensory evidence could settle; they sit closer to matters of taste than to items of knowledge, and no empirical science of rightness is waiting to be built. He treats moral valuation as lacking the empirical control that keeps even the most normative parts of epistemology answerable to experience.

What I cannot see is how this asymmetry is available to a naturalist. If the lesson of naturalized epistemology is that there is no first philosophy standing outside science to draw principled limits on empirical inquiry, then either moralizing is a natural phenomenon like any other—in which case a naturalized account of it should look like the naturalized account of belief formation, a chapter of empirical psychology—or Quine owes us an account of why evidence runs out exactly there. Is the asymmetry between his naturalized epistemology and his treatment of ethics actually principled, or is it an unexamined leftover of his empiricism that his own naturalism should have dislodged? I suspect I am misreading him, but I cannot identify the step where the confusion enters.
