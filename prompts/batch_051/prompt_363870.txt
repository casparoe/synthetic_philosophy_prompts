I've been turning this over for a while and I can't get my gut reactions to line up. Are there real private facts about what an experience is like, or is that whole way of talking just a kind of trap we fall into? I've got verdicts about a bunch of cases, and I want to know what principle makes them hang together, or which of my verdicts I'd have to give up.

1. Two guys on a coffee tasting panel have both said for years that they love the coffee. Now both say they don't. Nothing changed in the coffee, the lab checks, and their tongues are fine. One of them says the taste is exactly the same as always and he's just gotten sick of it. The other says no, the taste itself changed, it now tastes sour to him. My gut says there's no fact of the matter about which one is right. They're both being honest, and the question "did the taste itself change?" isn't a well-formed question.

2. Someone has had their colors swapped since birth. What I call red looks to them the way green looks to me, and the other way around. They've learned all the right words, they stop at stoplights, they call grass green, all of it. I used to think there had to be a hidden fact about whether their red looks like my red. Now I don't think there is. Everything they do lines up, and there's nothing underneath that.

3. A person has been totally colorblind their whole life but somehow knows every physical fact science has about light, eyes, and the brain's color systems. One day they get color vision. My gut says seeing red for the first time teaches them something new. I can't say what it is, but I won't say they already knew it. Maybe it teaches them something, but it isn't a new fact?

4. After a small stroke in the part of the brain that handles sound, a man says music doesn't sound worse to him. He says the notes sound fine, nothing changed at all, but he can't recognize his favorite song when he hears it. He can hum it from memory, but hearing it he says it's someone else's song. My gut says here there IS a real change to something in him, even though the music didn't change and he says the heard notes are fine.

5. Two people watch the same sunset. One says the orange is warm and soft, the other says it's sharp and thin. They aren't arguing, they just say it's a private thing. My gut says neither is right or wrong, but I also don't think they're reporting anything that could be checked. It sounds more like they're pointing at something with words than stating a fact.

Here's where I can't square it. In the coffee case and the color-swap case I want to say there's no hidden fact about experience. In the stroke case, and maybe the colorblind case, I want to say something real about experience is going on that we just don't have words for yet. What's the actual difference? What principle tells me when a question like "did the experience itself change?" has an answer and when it's a confused question, and which of my five verdicts does that principle force me to drop? Then run the principle on a case I didn't list: two friends who always agreed a certain smell was sweet as kids, and as adults one still says sweet and the other says it now smells musty to them, even though the smell hasn't changed and neither one's nose is damaged. Be straight with me about whether your principle says there's a fact there or not.
