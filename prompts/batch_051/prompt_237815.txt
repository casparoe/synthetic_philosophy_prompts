I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I would like you to assess a policy I follow in my intellectual life. When a persistent disagreement about a factual or statistical matter survives a conversation in which each of us has stated our posterior probability, I conclude that our priors differ. I then treat the disagreement as rationally stable: I do not take the bare fact that the other person disagrees as evidence against my view, and I do not conclude that either of us is being irrational. My justification is a kind of reverse use of Aumann's agreement theorem. If a common prior plus common knowledge of the posteriors would force agreement, then from the failure of agreement I infer the failure of the common-prior condition. But I have begun to worry that this policy is too convenient, because an appeal to differing priors is unobservable and can be invoked after any disagreement whatever, without my ever needing to say what my prior actually was. Is this a legitimate application of Aumann's theorem, or am I using an unobservable to shield my beliefs from correction? What, precisely, would the theorem commit me to in a live disagreement?
