I'm a software engineer, and lately I've been building a system that estimates how likely different failure modes are for our services, mostly by working through old incident reports. When there's genuinely no data about some new component, my go-to move is to list every way I can imagine it breaking and give each of those possibilities equal probability. I know that's crude, but it feels better than pulling a number out of thin air, and it at least forces the team to name the possibilities before we decide which ones to monitor. A friend of mine who did some probability theory in college told me I'm reinventing something called the principle of indifference, and that Bertrand's paradox shows its basically broken: the same state of ignorance can be carved up in different ways, and you get different probabilities for the same underlying event depending on how you slice it. He mentioned something about a circle and a chord, but I dont remember exactly.

I've tried to look it up, but the internet gives me either hand-wavy explainers or straight-up math papers, and I can't tell whether the takeaway is that I should stop doing this entirely, or just that I need to be more careful about how I make the list. So my question is: is my "equal probabilities when you don't know" policy defensible? If it only works under certain conditions, what are those conditions, and what would a better version of the policy look like for somebody who has to ship a number by Friday? Please end with a line that says Verdict: followed by one of Defensible, Defensible with caveats, or Not defensible, and then give a one-sentence version of your reasoning after it.
