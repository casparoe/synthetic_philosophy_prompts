I'm the student rep on our school's essay prize committee, and we've hit a decision that's split the room. The prize rules say the award goes to the best essay, and one essay is clearly strongest — nobody on the panel disputes that. But the strongest one is by a Year 13 who already has plenty on their CV and is leaving anyway, and the runner-up is a Year 12 whose teachers say winning would genuinely change their confidence and possibly their university chances. Half the committee says the only reasons that can count are reasons about the essay itself, and facts about what happens afterwards (who benefits, whose morale goes up) are the wrong kind of reason — like the demon who'll pay you to admire him, the money is a reason for something, but its not a reason that counts in favour of the admiration. The other half says that's overcomplicating it: awarding a prize is just an action with consequences, and consequences are reasons for actions, so we should pick the award that produces the best overall outcome. I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo. It should have a clear bottom line (award to the strongest essay, award to the runner-up, or amend the prize rules), the reasoning, a line we could use as a principle for future years, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses.
