I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been reading Kant's short 1797 essay On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives. The essay's central move is that lying to a would-be murderer is still impermissible because a lie always harms someone—if no particular person, then humanity in general, since it undermines the very source of law or right—and because we cannot control the downstream consequences that turn a well-intentioned falsehood into a disaster. I want to test this argument against a modern case. Suppose the falsehood is not uttered by me at the door but produced by a smart-home assistant under a policy I configured in advance: it tells an intruder that the police are already on the way when they are not, or that the house is empty. At the moment of the speech act, no human agent is making the decision to deceive; the deception is a preprogrammed default. Does Kant's argument still go through in this setting? Which premise is actually under pressure—the definition of a lie as an intentional false declaration to another person, the claim that every lie harms someone, or the claim that deception undermines the source of right—and is there a repaired version of the argument that still delivers a strong conclusion here, or does the argument become inapplicable?
