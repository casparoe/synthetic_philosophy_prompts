A colleague of mine sits on a small municipal board deciding whether to spend the last of its discretionary fund to protect a population of an obscure plant growing on a single abandoned lot. The plant has no known use, is visible from no public path, and no resident has expressed any view about it; the one botanist who ever studied it has said she no longer takes any interest, professional or personal, in its survival. One board member argues that it would nevertheless be a loss if the plant disappeared—even though, he concedes, it would be no particular person's loss. Another board member replies that this is confused: a reason to spend public money must be someone's reason, and if no person has a reason to care about the plant, then the board has no reason to act. My colleague must cast the deciding vote and has asked me which argument is stronger. Which of the two positions is more defensible? In your answer, give the strongest case for and against the idea that there can be reasons in favor of an outcome that are not reasons for any person.
