In Politics I.9, Aristotle writes:

With every article of property there is a double way of using it; both uses are related to the article itself, but not related to it in the same manner—one is peculiar to the thing and the other is not peculiar to it. Take for example a shoe—there is its wear as a shoe and there is its use as an article of exchange; for both are ways of using a shoe, inasmuch as even he that barters a shoe for money or food with the customer that wants a shoe uses it as a shoe, though not for the use peculiar to a shoe, since shoes have not come into existence for the purpose of barter.

He goes on to say that money was introduced for the sake of exchange, and that usury is the most unnatural form of gaining wealth because it makes money itself produce a yield.

Now consider a contemporary economy in which banks pay interest on demand deposits, currency exchange is itself a profit-seeking industry, and money is issued primarily through interest-bearing loans. What does this set of practices presuppose about the proper use of money, and about whether an exchange can be assessed against a thing’s natural function? Reconstruct the implicit theory of money and exchange that would license these practices, say whether it is coherent, and then run Aristotle’s double-use test against it: does the modern system treat money according to its peculiar use, its exchange use, or some third possibility that Aristotle’s framework cannot easily express?
