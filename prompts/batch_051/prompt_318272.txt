I've been chewing on the old line that history can't have laws the way physics does, which I ran across in a summary of Popper's argument. Here's the criteria I took away for what would count as an actual law of history: (1) it has to be a general statement about all cases of a kind under stated conditions, not a one-off claim about, say, the fall of Rome; (2) it has to let you predict something before it happens, not just make sense of it afterward; (3) it has to be falsifiable and non-trivial, so it actually sticks its neck out; and (4) the conditions can't secretly include the very result your trying to predict, or it's just "whatever happened had to happen" in dress-up.

Now the case I can't decide about: the demographic transition. Here's the pattern demographers all seem to treat as about as close to a law as their field gets. When a country industrializes and child mortality starts dropping, the death rate falls first, and then after a lag of a generation or so the birth rate falls too, until population growth levels off. The order has held across Europe, East Asia, and Latin America, and it's not just hindsight—UN population forecasts lean on it to predict that today's high-fertility countries will come down, and those predictions have broadly held so far. Still, it's conditional: it only kicks in with industrialization, female education, and access to birth control, and it says nothing exact about how long the lag is or how low fertility goes.

So: does the demographic transition qualify as a law of history under my four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one; where it falls short, say whether that's because my criteria are too strict or because the pattern really is just a strong trend. And tell me if the right fix is to add a third category between a law and a one-off event—say, a robust conditional pattern—and if I do that, whether it means history does have laws after all, just thinner ones than physics.
