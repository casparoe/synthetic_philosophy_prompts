I was arguing with a friend about whether a new city policy on policing will actually reduce violent crime. We went through the same studies, the same crime stats, the same expert takes. By the end neither of us moved an inch, and I caught myself thinking that he just wasn't weighting the evidence properly — anchoring on a couple of scary anecdotes and refusing to update. But then it hit me that he was probably thinking the exact same thing about me. And in my own case, my not-moving doesn't feel like stubborness; it feels like the evidence really is mixed and the studies have real limitations. So there's an assymetry: when you walk away from a full exchange still disagreeing, I count your stability as a flaw in you; when I walk away still disagreeing, I count my stability as a property of the evidence.

Aumann's agreement theorem seems relevant here. The usual gloss is: if two people have the same prior, both update by conditionalization, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge, then those posteriors can't differ — rational Bayesians with common priors can't agree to disagree. If that's right, then after two people have genuinely pooled their evidence and made their views known, at least one of them is being irrational; it can't just be that the evidence is hard.

What I want to know is whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry between how I audit your continued disagreement and how I audit my own. Candidate rationales jump out: I have direct access to my own deliberation but not to yours; I know I'm not lying about my posterior in a way I can't know about you; my own stability may reflect extra private evidence I can't fully transfer. But do any of these survive the theorem's setup, where the relevant information has already been exchanged and the posteriors are common knowledge? Or is the asymmetry just a self-serving bias dressed up in epistemic clothes?

Please speak as a philosopher of probability or decision theorist. Before committing, work through the candidate justifications from several angles, but when you write the answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in American English.
