Two colleagues of mine, both of whom work in clinical research and like to argue philosophy, got into a dispute at dinner over a person we all know, J. I do not know which of them is right, and I would like it adjudicated move by move. The agreed facts are these: J. is a talented researcher who, over roughly eighteen months, fabricated data in a grant application and in one progress report, and was caught. It came out afterward that during much of that period she was in an untreated manic episode and under intense pressure from a supervisor. She has since been treated, has apologized, and is returning to clinical work but not to research.

The argument ran as follows.

(1) A said that our lingering resentment toward J. is not just permitted but appropriate. She did the deeds, she knew what data fabrication was, and to stop resenting her now would be to stop treating her as a person who can be held to the same standards as everyone else.

(2) B replied that the mania changes the picture entirely. Once the acts are seen as episodes of an illness, they are properly seen as we see a fever: something to be managed and treated, not resented. Resentment has no target here, because there was no act of the person to resent.

(3) A shot back that this proves too much. Everything a person does has causes, and if a sufficient causal story removes resentment, then no one is ever an appropriate target of resentment. We need a line between ordinary causes and excusing conditions, not a blanket exemption for the mentally ill.

(4) B answered that the line is not between caused and uncaused actions. The line is between an agent who is answerable to reasons and an agent who, at the time, is not. The mania put J. in the second group, and no amount of insisting that she did it returns her to the first. Holding her to standards she could not then answer to is not respect; it is cruelty.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating, using Strawson's distinction between the participant stance, the ordinary attitude in which we hold one another to expectations and feel reactive attitudes such as resentment, and the objective stance, the attitude in which we view a person as something to be managed, treated, or understood rather than held to account. Also use the related distinction between excuses, which bear on particular actions, and exemptions, which bear on whether the agent is genuinely subject to the reactive attitudes at all. Then say what each side is missing or misdescribing, and whether the disagreement is ultimately about the nature of resentment, the facts of J.'s case, or the boundary between the two stances. End with a single-sentence last line beginning "Verdict:" that states your overall adjudication.
