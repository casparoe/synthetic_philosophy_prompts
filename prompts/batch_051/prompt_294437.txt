On a rationalist forum I follow, two posters spent an afternoon arguing about the sorites paradox, and neither clearly won. I want a neutral, point-by-point adjudication of the dispute as it actually occurred. The argument ran roughly as follows.

The first poster offered a sharp-boundary argument: one grain is not a heap, ten thousand grains plainly is, and if you build the pile grain by grain there must be a smallest number of grains at which it becomes a heap; otherwise every heap-sized pile would be preceded by another heap, and by a long chain of modus ponens a single grain would have to count as a heap. The only escapes, they wrote, are rejecting classical bivalence or conceding that ordinary speakers cannot know where the boundary lies.

The second poster replied that this is a false dilemma. The word heap is vague because speakers never settled where the boundary goes, so statements about borderline cases are not determinately true or determinately false in the way statements about clear cases are. The chain of conditionals stalls, because some of its steps are not determinately true.

The first poster countered that the phrase not determinately true merely relocates the problem: there must now be a sharp boundary between the cases that are determinately heap and the cases that are not, and if that boundary is itself vague, the same move can be repeated at higher levels without resolving the original paradox.

The second poster answered that higher-order vagueness does not force them to posit a hidden boundary anywhere. They are only committed to saying that it can be indeterminate in a natural language where the cutoff lies, and that this is a fact about how the word is used, not about the grains.

The first poster closed by accusing them of making natural language mysteriously exempt from an otherwise sound principle for finite sequences: if a monotone property holds at one end of an ordered sequence and fails at the other, then some specific step is the last at which it holds. Denying this for heap, they wrote, is just a refusal to accept that language itself has an inaccessible cutoff.

Who is right about which specific claims in this exchange, and what are both parties missing?
