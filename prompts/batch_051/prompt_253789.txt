Ok so my job recently moved me onto a project that uses machine learning on a big historical dataset to forecast civil conflict risk over 5 and 10 year horizons, and I've been having a low-grade argument with myself about Popper ever since. In The Poverty of Historicism he says there can't be laws of history in the way there are laws of physics. As I understand it, part of the argument is a distinction between trends and laws: a trend you find in the past doesn't have to continue, and calling it a law just smuggles in the assumption that it will. Another part is that the future course of history will be shaped by future knowledge, and you cant predict that knowledge before you have it. But now we have digitized historical records on a scale Popper didn't, and people are getting out-of-sample predictive accuracy on things like state failure and civil war — not by having a grand theory of historical development, just by fitting lots of local regularities and letting the data choose among them. Does the argument still go through? I'm not looking for "the models aren't perfect." I want to know what actually happens to the anti-historicist argument when large-n calibrated historical prediction becomes possible. Which premise, if any, is really under pressure, and is there a defensible weaker version of Popper's point that survives even if some of those predictions hold up?
