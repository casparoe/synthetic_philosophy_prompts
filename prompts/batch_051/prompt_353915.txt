Consider the following practice in several American jurisdictions. If a condemned prisoner becomes mentally incompetent after sentencing—so that he cannot understand the punishment or the reason it is being imposed—the state will not carry out the execution. But it will not commute the sentence either. Instead, the state may postpone the execution and treat the prisoner's mental illness, sometimes by forcibly administering antipsychotic medication, precisely in order to restore the competence that the execution presupposes. Once the prisoner is restored to competence, the execution proceeds. The clinicians who carry out the treatment are agents of the same state that will put the prisoner to death.

What theory of punishment does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as possible: is it retributivist, consequentialist, a hybrid, or something else? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes about desert, agency, and the purpose of punishment from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories could justify it. Then test the practice against two pure theories. Under a strict retributivism on which punishment must fall on the same morally responsible agent who committed the offense, what would have to change, if anything, and why? Under a straightforward consequentialism on which a death sentence is justified only by its expected future benefits, what would have to change, if anything, and why? Finally, identify which feature of the practice—the postponement, the forcible treatment, the execution after restoration, or the use of state-employed clinicians—does the most work in exposing the tension between retributivism and consequentialism.
