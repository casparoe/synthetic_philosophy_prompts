I am a novelist, and I have been asked to give the ten-minute opening address at a small international workshop on Montaigne's essay about the cannibals and its long afterlife in debates over cultural relativism. The audience will be mostly Montaigne specialists and early modernists, with a smaller number of writers in residence and members of the public who have never read the Essays, so the address has to make the essay's central move legible to a newcomer without making the specialists feel they are being reintroduced to their own field. My novel, which I will not be there to promote, is built around the essay's most famous maneuver: Montaigne relays what the visiting Tupinambá are reported to have found astonishing about France — that grown, armed men submit to a child king, and that some are gorged while others starve — and he lets that reported gaze travel back onto his own society. I want the speech to be a working opening, not a miniature lecture: it should leave one sharp question in the air that the workshop can then spend two days worrying at, and it should make plain why a novelist cares about that question in ways the scholars might not.

Let me specify the question the speech should turn on. The familiar reading of the essay finds in it an early statement of cultural relativism — the idea that barbarism is only ever a name for what lies outside one's own customs, so the remedy is to stop judging other peoples. I do not think that reading is wrong so much as too easy, because the essay never stops judging: it judges the European wars of religion and their cruelties to be worse than Tupinambá cannibalism, and it praises the visitors' virtues in terms any moralist would recognize. So I want the speech to ask whether the essay's reversal amounts to a prohibition on cross-cultural judgment, or to something harder: an obligation to judge, but only after one has taken in how one's own world looks from the outside. The speech should come down, however provisionally, on the second side, while leaving the workshop room to disagree; it must reject the comfortable conclusion that everyone is a barbarian by someone else's lights and therefore nobody is, and it must equally reject the fantasy that the Tupinambá are simply noble and the Europeans simply worse. What I would like the speech to add, as a novelist's distinctive contribution, is a worry the scholars may not foreground: that using the visitors as a mirror for Europe, however sympathetically Montaigne does it, comes close to using actual people as instruments of someone else's self-criticism, and that a novelist retelling their story faces that risk in an acute form. If the speech can say something about what a novelist owes to the people behind the reported answers, without turning into an apology for my book, so much the better.

Please keep the speech to roughly nine hundred words and write it for delivery. The philosophical position must do the structural work: once the speech takes its side on the question above, that side should determine what the speech can and cannot say, and I should be able to explain why certain conciliatory things a softer keynote would say are unavailable to it.

After the speech, append a short unsigned note, not for delivery, saying plainly where the speech has been obliged to take sides in a contested reading of the essay; which finding, text, or argument would most likely show that side to be wrong; and how the speech would then have to be altered.
