I am presenting a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop next month, and I want you to prepare me specifically for the question period that follows, not to restate the argument or write me a defense. The deliverable is a Q&A prep sheet.

The paper's thesis is that the boundary problem in democratic theory — the apparent circularity that democratic procedures cannot themselves determine who belongs to the demos that is to use them — is not one problem but two problems that have been run together: a scope problem and a membership problem. The scope problem asks which individuals are entitled to participate in a given decision or domain of decisions. The membership problem asks which individuals hold the standing status of co-author of a democratic order and how the boundaries of that order can be legitimate at all, given that no demos was yet constituted to draw them. My central negative claim is that the two familiar criteria — that all who would be affected by a decision should have a say in it, and that all who are subject to the laws or to coercion should have a say in it — are each made to look inadequate because each is asked to solve both problems at once. I argue that, once the problems are separated, the standard over-inclusion and under-inclusion objections become far less damaging. The constructive half of the paper proposes that decision-scope be treated as a scalar and procedural notion: it is to be settled piecemeal, in forums whose jurisdiction is itself open to challenge. Polity-membership, by contrast, is a constitutional status whose justification does not need to be democratic ab initio; it may rest partly on the historical contingencies that produced viable political units, so long as the resulting boundaries are maintained non-arbitrarily and those inside are afforded voice, exit rights, and genuine standing to contest how the boundaries affect them. I explicitly decline any premise about the global reach of the scope principle, and I insist that the position is compatible with a great many existing state borders.

The audience is a mixed workshop of about fifteen people. The largest contingent is political philosophers who work on democratic legitimacy; among them, two defend versions of the coercion or subjection account of political inclusion and will think my separation of decision-scope from membership cuts democracy away from its raison d'être, namely justifying coercive law to those who live under it. Two others are deliberative democrats who take the all-affected principle to be the non-negotiable core of democratic inclusion; they will read my deflationary treatment of that principle as an excuse for powerful states to ignore outsiders whose interests they shape. There is a political theorist who writes on citizenship and state boundaries and is a moderate statist; she will suspect the paper is cosmopolitanism wearing a distinction as a disguise. Finally, there are two more empirically oriented colleagues, one from comparative politics and one from European Union governance, who will press on whether any real institution can unbundle scope from membership or whether the franchise inevitably ties them together.

Prepare me for the questions. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, ordered by a combination of how likely each is and how much damage it can do, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them — not the way a generic philosopher would. For each objection give me a reply of about two minutes, say which part of the reply is weakest, and tell me what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to concede in the room. End with the one question I should be hoping nobody asks, and why it is the genuinely dangerous one.
