I have been trying to get clear on what "physical" means ever since a physicist friend remarked, over dinner, that current physics is almost certainly false in some of its details and that most of the entities we now take seriously will eventually go the way of phlogiston. I had been defending a physicalist view of the mind, and the remark unsettled me. If "physical" means "whatever current physics says exists," then physicalism is probably false, since current physics will be revised; if it means "whatever an ideal, completed physics would say exists," then it is either empty or unknowable, and in any case I could not say what I am actually committed to right now. I know this has a name, Hempel's dilemma, but I have been trying to work out a way around it for myself, and I would like an honest assessment of the position I have reached.

The position is this. I call it the survivor account of the physical. The physical facts are the survivors of theory change: the entities, magnitudes, and structural claims that persist across the succession of successful scientific theories rather than being discarded in the next revolution. On this view, mass, charge, and spacetime structure count as physical because something answering to them survives the move from Newtonian mechanics to relativity to quantum theory, even as the surrounding theory changes around them; caloric, phlogiston, and the celestial spheres were posits that theory change killed off. Physicalism then becomes the thesis that everything, including consciousness, is nothing over and above the survivors. The attraction is that the definition is anchored in actual scientific practice rather than in a fantasy of finished physics, and it lets us evaluate physicalism now rather than at the end of inquiry.

I am aware of the places where the view feels soft. "Survives" and "something answering to it" are vague, and which facts count as survivors can depend on how finely we individuate them and on which episodes of scientific history we select. The account also makes a metaphysical category hostage to the contingent course of human inquiry, which strikes me as wrong. And I worry that the view is not new at all, but rather collapses into structural realism, or into a restatement of the pessimistic meta-induction, or into some careful theory of the invariants of scientific change that has already been worked out elsewhere. Is this a genuine position or only a metaphor with a definition attached? Has something like it already been developed under another name, and if so, where does the serious version of the idea part ways with mine? Most of all, where does the account break: does it exclude things we should count as physical, include things we should not, or fail to make physicalism a determinate claim about the mind?
