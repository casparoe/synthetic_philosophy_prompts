I hold a version of imprecise probabilism about rational credence. On this view, a rational agent's doxastic state is not always best represented by a single sharp probability function. Rather, in many cases—including idealized cases where the agent's total evidence is fully precise—the agent's beliefs are better represented by a set of probability functions, a representor, together with the corresponding lower and upper probabilities. On this view it can be rationally appropriate, and sometimes rationally mandatory, for an agent to have interval-valued credence in a proposition rather than a point-valued one.

I want a careful map of what would count against this position, where that means more than listing standard objections. I am interested in what would have to be true for imprecise probabilism to be false, or at least materially worse than its precise-Bayesian rival, and in how a committed imprecise probabilist should honestly sort these considerations. Please address at least the following.

First, the formal and semantic pressure points. What results would have to be true, or have in fact been shown, for the view to be internally unstable? Consider, for example, dilation: cases in which conditioning on any cell of a partition widens rather than sharpens the agent's interval estimate for a hypothesis. Consider also the charge that an agent with imprecise probabilities is exploitable across a sequence of choices, and the argument that rational deference to an expert—or to one's own later, better-informed self—is incompatible with genuinely imprecise credence. Which of these, if sound, would be fatal, and which would be merely awkward?

Second, the decision-theoretic costs. Under imprecision, the ranking of acts is often incomplete: an agent may be unable to choose between options whose expected utilities are ordered one way under some members of the representor and the opposite way under others. I want an account of when this kind of indecision should be treated as a reductio of the position, and when it is a feature the view is entitled to own. What exactly would a compelling argument have to establish in order to turn the incompleteness from a feature into a bug?

Third, the empirical and psychological question. What patterns of choice or judgment would support the view, and what patterns would undercut it? In particular, to what extent do ambiguity-aversion phenomena of the Ellsberg kind actually favor imprecise probabilities as a normative account of rational belief, as against merely describing a heuristic or bias that a precise Bayesian could model in other ways?

Fourth, the insulation worry. Has imprecise probabilism been qualified in a way that threatens to make it unfalsifiable—for instance, by allowing that agents may always behave as if they held some precise credence once a real decision is forced, while retaining imprecision only offstage? If so, is that a decisive problem for a view of this kind, or an acceptable division of theoretical labor?

Throughout, I want the candidate defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit statement of which ones a defender of the position should concede as counting against it rather than dismiss. I am especially interested in the strongest possible precise-Bayesian case against imprecise probabilism, put as sympathetically as its best proponents would put it.
