At a group dinner last week, two friends of mine got into it about voting, and I still can't split them. The setup was a voter in a district where one major party wins by twenty points every cycle, who thinks the safe party's candidate is mediocre-but-tolerable and the other major candidate is much worse, and whose real sympathies lie with a small left party.

Marcus said the person should vote for the safe party's candidate, as a duty. His argument: even if the chance of casting the tie-breaking vote is one in several million, the stakes (a much worse candidate in office, judges, etc.) are large enough that the expected value still outweighs an hour of inconvenience. And "my vote doesn't matter here" can't be universalized: the district is only safe because people like him vote anyway, so treating the safe-district status as a fixed fact free rides on other people's turnout.

Priya said the whole frame is off. A vote is an expressive act before it's a causal lever. The ballot is secret, and in a safe district it won't change the outcome, so making it a duty on the basis of tiny probabilities makes personal conscience hostage to arithmetic. She said the person should vote for the small left party: that signals actual support, tells the big party which way to move if it wants those voters back, and avoids having to stand behind a candidate you think is bad. And if someone genuinely can't in good conscience vote for any of them, abstaining is a legible act too.

Marcus came back: a third-party vote in a winner-take-all system doesn't signal "move left" to anyone; by default it's read as a protest that changes nothing, and meanwhile the worse candidate benefits from every vote not cast for the only viable alternative. He also said you can express your reservations plenty outside the ballot — primaries, organizing, public criticism — so making the ballot do the expressing conflates the one instrument we have for coordination with therapy.

Priya answered that he's smuggling in the conclusion: calling the ballot an instrument of coordination only works if you already think it should be treated as strategic rather than expressive. And forcing someone to pull the lever for a candidate they think is complicit in serious harm has a moral cost to the voter herself — integrity, or whatever you want to call it — that his expected-value calculus doesn't count because it treats all costs as fungible. She also said the "what if everyone stayed home" argument proves too much, since it would oblige people to vote even in totally hopeless jurisdictions.

I ended up with no idea who was right about which part. Please break the disagreement down move by move, say who's right about what, where each of them overreaches, and whether the best position is closer to one of them or something neither said.
