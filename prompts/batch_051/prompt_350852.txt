I'm a retired microbiologist, so I spent forty years working with organisms and somehow never settled what "alive" actually covers; now that I've started reading philosophy I'd like to pin it down by going with my gut on cases. Here's where I land:

- the mule in the back pasture: alive, obviously, even though it can't reproduce.
- a Bacillus endospore that's been dormant for decades: alive, even though it isn't metabolizing at all.
- a flame: not alive, even though it metabolizes and can spread.
- a prion, like the one behind scrapie: not alive, even though it makes copies of itself inside a host.
- Rickettsia, a bacterium that can't grow outside a host cell: alive.
- a mimivirus particle: not alive, even though its got more genes than some bacteria.
- a minimal synthetic cell assembled in the lab from a few hundred genes: alive.
- a self-replicating computer program that mutates and evolves under selection: not alive, but I can't defend that one as clearly.

Is there a principle—or a short list of candidates—that gets all of these, or close to all? And what does it say about the case I actually keep going back and forth on: a cell that's been infected and taken over by a virus, turned into a factory for making more viruses. Is that thing still alive, or is it something else? If one of my verdicts has to go, tell me which and why.
