My reading group has two people who've been arguing for months about whether novelty and adventure are a genuine ingredient of well-being or just maintenance for other goods. One of them holds the constitutive view: engaging with variety and newness is a basic component of a good life, alongside pleasure, achievement, close relationships, etc., so a life can be worse simply in virtue of being monotonous, even if the person never gets bored, depressed, or adapts. The other holds the instrumentalist view: novelty has no value in itself; it matters only because it slows hedonic adaptation, keeps skills and relationships from decaying, and opens doors to things that are independently valuable. Write the joint statement their good-faith adversarial collaboration should have produced. Include a numbered agreed-claims section (about what both accept regarding hedonic adaptation, boredom-proneness, the way variety typically affects engagement, and the limits of the existing evidence), then a disagreements table with the exact residual disagreement, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would endorse. Then identify the cruxes—the subquestions that would actually move at least one of them if settled—and for each crux give a concrete case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each sides prediction written down in advance. If part of their dispute seems merely verbal to you, say so and explain why.
