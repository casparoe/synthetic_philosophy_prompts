I've been trying to get clear on when the passage of time should morally matter to criminal punishment, and I've come up with a rough-and-ready test for whether an old offense is still punishable. It needs all four: (1) the defendant is still, in the relevant sense, the same person who made the choice; (2) punishment now would do something forward-looking—deter, denounce, or incapacitate—not just settle an old score; (3) the people wronged haven't forgiven or waived the claim in a way the state ought to respect; and (4) the harm is the kind that time, restitution, or the wrongdoer's later decency can't actually repair. Here's the case I want to push through it. In 1981 a 24-year-old environmental chemist fabricates the key data in a small groundwater study because she's broke and terrified of being scooped. A developer relies on the study, a subdivision goes up, and over the next forty years a few hundred residents are slowly exposed to a contaminant plume and develop chronic illnesses; the fabrication didn't single-handedly cause the construction—the local agency missed the plume too—but it removed the one red flag that might have stopped it. The chemist is now 68, long retired, and has spent the last decade doing unpaid water testing for poor towns; she's never committed another offense. A cold-case review turns up her old lab notes, and the fraud statute in that state has no limitation period. The residents' association says it does not want a trial, but the prosecutor charges her anyway. Using my four criteria, does the case for punishing her still exist at this point? Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then go criterion by criterion and say which one is actually doing the work—and whether the case shows my test needs revising.
