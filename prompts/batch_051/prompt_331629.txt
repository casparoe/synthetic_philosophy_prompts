Read Steven Spielberg's The Terminal (2004) through the international-relations debate between declaratory and constitutive theories of statehood. The declaratory theory holds that statehood is a matter of fact — a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and a capacity to enter relations with other states — and that recognition merely acknowledges what already exists. The constitutive theory holds that an entity becomes a state only by being recognized and treated as one by other states, so that recognition is partly what makes it a state. The film offers an extreme test case: Viktor Navorski, a citizen of the fictional Krakozhia, lands at JFK just as a coup topples his country's government; the United States no longer honors his passport, and he is trapped in the international transit lounge, unable to enter the United States and unable to be deported to a country that has, for the film's purposes, come apart as a recognized political entity. He becomes a person whose standing evaporates the moment others withdraw recognition. Teach the film through this lens. Where does it dramatize or endorse the constitutive picture, for example in treating Krakozhia's existence as equivalent to its recognition by the United States, or in equating Viktor's usable status as a person with the validity of his documents? Where does it resist that picture, perhaps in the miniature society Viktor builds inside the lounge, or in the suggestion that his dignity and obligations survive his legal erasure? Attend to the distinctions the film compresses: recognition of a government versus recognition of a state, and recognition of a state versus recognition of an individual's rights. Are those compressions the point — a reductio of a purely attitudes-based view of statehood — or are they a confusion the viewer should not endorse? End by saying what the film adds to the abstract debate: what does the lived experience of a man in transit show that the declaratory/constitutive dispute misses, and what would a defender of each theory have to concede after watching it? I am teaching a unit on recognition and want a critical reading that treats the film as arguing something, not merely illustrating a textbook distinction.
