I have repeatedly encountered a design maneuver in consumer software and online services that I would like to identify precisely. The interface presents me with a choice, and nothing material is withheld: the options, their consequences, and the fact that one option is the designer's preferred outcome are all visible. But the dispreferred option is worded so that choosing it means, at the same time, accepting an unflattering description of myself. A refusal to enable a feature is written so that clicking it is equivalent to affirming that I do not care about saving money, that I am content with a worse experience, or that I am happy to leave my data exposed. The outcome, the information, and the material costs are the same as a neutral refusal would have produced; what has changed is that the designer has arranged for my own self-regard to be the penalty for declining. I know the general vocabulary of dark patterns, nudges, and choice architecture, but none of these seems to capture this exact maneuver. It is not deception, because nothing is hidden. It is not a nudge in the usual sense, because it does not rely on defaults, opt-out structures, or inertia. It is not mere framing, because the wording does not just present the same option under a different aspect; it makes choosing the option an act of self-description. And it is not coercion, because I remain fully informed and at liberty to choose it. Does this maneuver have an established name of its own? I am less interested in the label alone than in the carving. If the nearest established term fits only imperfectly, say which term comes closest, define this species precisely, and distinguish it carefully from deception, coercion, nudging, and framing, including where the boundary lies between this tactic and harmless or humorous wording. If no established term fits well, coin one and define it with the same care.
