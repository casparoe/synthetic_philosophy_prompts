I am a novelist, and over the years I have drifted toward something close to Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism: the conviction that language is not in the business of mirroring or picturing reality, that vocabularies are tools for coping with the world rather than attempts to get it right, and that although the world can cause us to hold beliefs and can frustrate our projects, it cannot by itself arbitrate between two vocabularies or tell us which one is closer to how things are in themselves. This is not a merely aesthetic preference on my part; I think of a novel as a device for doing something to a reader rather than as a representation of characters and events, and I am genuinely unsure whether that way of thinking extends as far as I have been letting it.

What would count against this antirepresentationalism, taken as a serious thesis about language and inquiry rather than as an edifying stance? I want a map of its exposure, not a restatement of standard criticisms. In particular: which findings or arguments would a committed Rortyan have to treat as damage rather than as noise? Would robust cross-linguistic universals, persuasive evidence of a modular language faculty with fixed semantic primitives, the predictive success of the sciences if it seemed to require accuracy rather than mere coping, or a successful naturalization of reference or truth put real pressure on the claim that vocabularies face no test but human purpose? Are there conceivable results that would be decisive, or has the view been built so that any apparent counterexample can be redescribed as one more vocabulary doing one more job? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest statement of which ones the view's defenders themselves would concede as counting against it, and whether its capacity to absorb apparent counterevidence is a strength or a symptom of having been insulated into unfalsifiability.
