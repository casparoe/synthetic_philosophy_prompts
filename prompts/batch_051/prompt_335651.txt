I keep noticing people reach for the tornado-in-a-junkyard image to shut down worries about Boltzmann brains. The worry, as I understand it, is this: if the universe is big enough and lasts long enough, random thermal motion will occasionally assemble a working conscious brain floating out in the dark—a brain that briefly thinks it is a person looking at a stable world. If you count observers naively, most observers would be such freak fluctuations, and our 'ordinary' picture of the cosmos would just be the biased dream of brains that don't know they're brains. My friend's retort is always that this is like a tornado sweeping through a scrapyard and assembling a functioning 747 by chance: so improbable that the whole problem must rest on a mistake we havent found yet.

I half buy it, but the two pictures differ in ways that seem like they might matter. A 747 actually has to fly, while a Boltzmann brain only has to feel coherent enough to fool itself; a scrapyard is not much like a near-equilibrium near-vacuum; and the claim was never about a single pass by a single tornado but about effectively unlimited chances. I can't tell whether these differences make the worry worse or better. Could you take the analogy seriously and audit it? Give me the mapping—tornado, yard, parts, plane, the odds, the one pass—then walk through where it breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for my friend's conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic. Then commit: does the conclusion that Boltzmann brains can be waved off survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the tornado doing all the argumentative work? If some better analogy captures what's genuinely worrying, give me that one. Don't leave me with a menu of options; tell me where you land and why.
