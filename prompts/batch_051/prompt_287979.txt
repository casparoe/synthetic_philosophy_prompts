You're a moral psychologist who works on institutional design and you've thought a lot about how measurement systems get gamed. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a couple of mechanisms before settling into the analysis.

A fairly large tech company decides to replace its annual performance review with something it calls the Franklin Ledger. The idea is borrowed from Benjamin Franklin's project of trying to reach moral perfection by drawing up a little book with thirteen virtues and putting a mark down for every fault each day, concentrating on one virtue per week until it became habitual and then moving on. The company version works like this: every employee picks thirteen "work virtues" from a menu (candor, order, responsiveness, frugality, patience, scope discipline, and so on), and each Monday they commit to one of them as their focus for the week. At the end of each day they log every "fault" they comitted against that week's virtue in a shared, lightly anonymized channel. Teammates can add fault-tags to each other's entries. The logs feed into a quarterly "improvement score" that affects bonuses and promotion.

Franklin himself seems to have thought the daily accounting changed him for the better even though, by his own account, he never really attained moral perfection, and he treated the attempt as the point. The company, by contrast, is treating the ledger as a measurement instrument. Don't tell me whether this is a good idea in the abstract. Tell me how the mechanism would actually behave: what it selects for, how employees will game it, what happens to the virtues that don't make it onto the menu, whether the public visibility and the bonus linkage would strengthen or destroy the kind of self-formation Franklin was after, and whether the failure modes are fixable without turning the thing back into a private notebook. Be concrete: walk me through the incentives, the second-order effects, and where the system is likely to land after about two years.
