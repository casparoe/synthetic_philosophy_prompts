In my high school philosophy of science seminar, I have been defending an etiological account of biological function, and I want to stress-test my working criteria against a case. My criteria are: an effect E is a biological function of a trait T only if (1) T was selected for E, meaning that E's contribution to fitness explains why T became or remained common in the population, and (2) E presently contributes to survival or reproduction. Here is the case. In a lake fish population, a mutation produces a liver enzyme that happens to break down an industrial chemical. For several generations the chemical is too dilute to matter, and the mutation spreads to about half the population by drift alone. Then a new factory sharply raises the chemical level. From that point on, fish with the enzyme survive and reproduce at a higher rate, and natural selection carries the mutation the rest of the way to fixation. Does the enzyme's breaking down of the chemical qualify as a biological function under my criteria? In particular, does selection that finishes a trait's spread, after drift got it halfway there, satisfy criterion (1), or should selected for require that selection explain the trait's initial spread? And does the case show that my criteria should be revised, perhaps toward the causal-role view by letting present contributions to survival count on their own? Please answer with a committed position rather than a survey of possibilities. Structure the response in three sections: "Criterion-by-criterion verdict" (about 150 words); "What the case exposes" (about 120 words); and "My revision" (about 80 words), where you state exactly how you would change, or keep, my criteria and why.
