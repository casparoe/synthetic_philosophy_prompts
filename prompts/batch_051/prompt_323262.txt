I chair the resource-allocation committee of a three-hospital system in a mid-sized city, and we have one unfunded initiative left to place this cycle. Two complete proposals are before us. Program A would build a specialized treatment service for a rare inherited metabolic disorder: about a dozen newly diagnosed infants a year would be treated, and for the large majority the treatment is expected to prevent death in early childhood or profound lifelong disability, though follow-up is too short to know whether the benefit persists past adolescence, and the per-child cost is very high. Program B would fund a system-wide early-access physiotherapy and pain-education pathway for chronic low-back pain, serving on the order of sixty thousand adults a year with a well-evidenced but modest average gain in pain, function, and return to work. The two programs cost roughly the same each year, and we cannot fund both from this year's surplus.

The committee is sharply divided, and our own analyst has not settled it: by central estimate, Program B produces somewhat more total quality-adjusted life-years than Program A, but each of B's gains is small and distributed thinly across tens of thousands of people, while A's gains are fewer but each is the difference, for one child, between a roughly normal life and death or severe disability. One group argues that the numbers are what matter, and that sixty thousand modest improvements plainly outweigh a dozen large ones. The other group argues that a decision is defensible only if we can justify it to each affected person on grounds that person could not reasonably reject, and that no parent of one of those children could reasonably accept a principle that lets their child die or be severely disabled so that many others can each have somewhat less back pain, no matter how many small complaints are piled together. Our charter says allocation must give priority to the most urgent individual need and must also maximize health benefit across the population we serve, so each side can appeal to settled institutional language, which is part of why the committee is stuck.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's vote: about two pages, in language the full board, including community representatives and a health economist, can act on, not a philosophy seminar. Recommend a concrete course of action, such as funding one program, funding part of one now with conditions, or adopting an explicit priority rule for this and future cycles, with a timetable, and engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the side you are not recommending. Then, in a separate short note to me, identify the one empirical finding or moral consideration that, if it came out differently from what the evidence now suggests, would most plausibly overturn your recommendation, and state where the memo's reasoning is most exposed.
