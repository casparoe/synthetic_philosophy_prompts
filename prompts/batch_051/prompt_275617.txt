Ok so I've ended up with an argument I don't know how to get out of, from reading Schopenhauer's On the Basis of Morality alongside Nietzsche's Daybreak. It seems to prove that Schopenhauer's morality of compassion can't get started, which surely can't be right. The steps:

(1) For Schopenhauer, an action has moral worth if and only if it proceeds from compassion, understood as immediately sharing in another's suffering with no regard for one's own welfare.

(2) Nietzsche's main suspicion is that what we call compassion never actually works that way: even when we feel bad at another's pain, the experience typically includes hidden self-regard -- relief that it isn't me, a little thrill of being the one who cares, or the pleasure of feeling superior to the sufferer.

(3) If actual compassion is always mixed with hidden self-regard, then no actual action proceeds from the pure motive Schopenhauer requires.

(4) So either no actual action has moral worth, or Schopenhauer has to count the hidden self-regard as part of the morally decisive motive, in which case the distinction between compassion and egoism breaks down.

(5) Both alternatives are unacceptable: one makes his ethics empty, the other makes it incoherent.

(6) Therefore Schopenhauer's criterion can't be right.

I want to reject (2), because I think Schopenhauer is onto something even if actual pity often is pathological. But I can't see whether the flaw is that (2) attacks the psychology of compassion rather than its normative status, or whether Schopenhauer can just deny (2) for the cases that matter. Which step should I actually give up, and what would that cost? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
