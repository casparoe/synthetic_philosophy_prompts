I chair the government-affairs committee of a forty-thousand-member association of public-health professionals. Our board votes next month on whether to abandon the association's longstanding policy of not endorsing candidates for state office. The immediate occasion is the governor's race. The incumbent has just proposed dissolving the state's independent epidemiological-surveillance office and folding its duties into the tourism-promotion department; the challenger supports the office but has a documented history of retaliating against career staff and has advanced measures that our general counsel believes are plainly unconstitutional. The committee has met three times and keeps circling the same disagreement.

The membership has split roughly three ways. One group argues that the association has a duty to minimize foreseeable harm and should therefore endorse the challenger as the lesser evil, even while acknowledging serious misgivings. A second group argues that an endorsement is not merely a harm-reduction tool but an expressive act that states who we are, and that we should instead endorse a minor-party candidate closer to our values, even though that may draw votes from the challenger. A third group argues that a professional body should not instruct or pressure its members about their votes at all, since the vote is a private conscientious act and the association's proper role ends with informing members about the candidates' records. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the board meets. It should recommend a concrete course of action, including the form any public statement should take, and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side you reject, whether that is the appeal to complicity in avoidable harm or the worry that the association would be coercing its members' private judgment.
