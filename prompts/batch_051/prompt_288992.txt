The slogan that evolution is a stupid optimizer invites a comparison with stochastic gradient descent: both are said to climb a noisy, high-dimensional landscape using only local information, and both are supposed to make progress without understanding what they are doing. But the comparison only becomes theoretically interesting when someone asserts the stronger claim that a particular evolving population is, in an empirically contentful sense, performing approximate stochastic gradient ascent on a fitness function. Take that stronger claim as your target. Choose a concrete evolutionary system, actual or realistically specified — for example, a bacterial population evolving drug resistance in a laboratory, or a bird population subject to sustained directional selection — and design the strongest feasible test of the claim. What would you measure, over how many generations, and what pattern in the trajectory would count as genuine support rather than mere consistency with post hoc curve-fitting? What result should force an honest advocate to stop saying the population is optimizing at all, even while it is plainly adapting? Then state what a clean positive result would still leave open: whether the apparent ascent excludes drift or developmental constraint as the better explanation, whether the landscape was itself moving during the ascent, and whether optimize is explaining the data or merely re-describing it. Say whether that residue is a limitation of the measurements or a flaw in the very idea that evolution and gradient descent are doing the same kind of thing.
