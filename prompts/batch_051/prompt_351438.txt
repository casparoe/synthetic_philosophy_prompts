Last week in the department lounge I overheard two friends going back and forth about Williams's integrity objection, and I honestly can't sort out whose right. Maybe you can referee.

They started from the drowning case, where a man sees his wife and a stranger drowning and of course pulls his wife out. Miriam said Williams's point was the one-thought-too-many point: the man doesn't need the thought that saving one's wife is the permissible thing to do, and if he had to run through that thought before jumping in, something about his relation to her has already gone wrong.

Dan pushed back that this only refutes a straw utilitarian who tells people to calculate at the moment of action. A sane consequentialist wants the man to already have the disposition to save his wife without thinking, because a world where people love their spouses and act on it has much better consequences. So the extra thought never happens.

Miriam said the problem just moves inside. If he's a sophisticated consequentialist, he keeps that disposition because it does well in the impartial ledger, and he should dump it the moment it stops paying. The conditionality is Williams's target, not whether a thought happens on the beach.

Dan answered that no, first, the man can just have ordinary love, since the theory doesn't have to live in his head. And second, every reflective person has conditional commitments: if you learned your spouse had done something monstrous, you'd revisit the love too. So the one-thought-too-many worry isn't special to consequentialism.

Miriam replied that those aren't the same conditionality. You'd revisit the love because of something about your spouse that matters to you; the sophisticated consequentialist revises it because an impersonal ledger changed. That switch in the kind of reason is the alienation Williams is talking about. It's a point about what the reason is, not about how deliberation feels.

Dan said that still sounds like a psychological complaint dressed up as a metaphysical one. Why can't the consequentialist say: the fact that she's my wife is a perfectly good partial reason to act on, and since having a disposition to act on that partial reason has the best consequences, it's the right disposition to have? No conflict, no extra thought.

Miriam said that proves her point. If your partial reason only gets to operate because an impartial story approves it, then it isn't doing fundamental work; it's a permitted convenience. A person of integrity sometimes has to say: this project is mine, and I won't trade it even if the impartial story says I should.

Dan replied that this is the part of Williams he finds romantic or maybe even quietist. If someone's projects are cruel or wildly antisocial, we don't admire their refusal to trade them. We need some standard for when integrity is a virtue and when it's a vice, and Williams seems to leave us without one.

Miriam said Williams doesn't have to be read as saying all projects are beyond criticism. The objection is narrower: consequentialism can't make sense of the kind of commitment integrity involves, because it converts every reason into one currency. That's a structural claim, not a claim that everyone's actual projects are fine.

Dan said he still thinks that's a claim about how consequentialist deliberation would feel rather than about whether consequentialism is correct.

Then someone came in and the conversation moved on, but I've been turning it over eversince. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what both of them are missing? I'd also like the strongest version of each side once you clean it up.
