Question. Distinguish original sin from original guilt. Is it coherent to affirm the former while denying the latter? Defend your answer.

Student's answer. Original sin is the inherited corruption of human nature—the loss of original righteousness together with concupiscence, or disordered desire—that all human beings receive through descent from Adam. Original guilt, by contrast, is the imputation of Adam's first sin to his descendants, such that each person is born already liable to punishment for a sin they did not personally commit. The distinction matters because one might hold that human beings enter the world damaged without holding that they enter the world condemned.

Many theologians have affirmed the first claim while denying the second. They argue that our fallen condition explains the universality of sin, but that guilt attaches only to what a person freely does. The obvious objection is that this seems to let us off the hook: if my disordered nature was not chosen by me, and if it drives my wrongdoing, how can I be judged for the wrongdoing? The usual reply is that the inclination is not coercive; it disposes the will without determining it, so each actual sin remains freely willed and therefore culpable.

This reply, however, gives away the position in the end. If original sin makes actual sin inevitable in every human life, then no one can freely avoid it, and if no one can freely avoid it, no one is culpable for the sins that flow from it. So either original sin does not really make sin inevitable—which is simply Pelagianism under another name—or the Augustinian view is right after all, and inherited guilt follows from inheriting a corrupt nature. The attempt to affirm original sin while denying inherited guilt therefore collapses.
