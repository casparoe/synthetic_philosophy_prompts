I've been arguing with a friend about rent control and the minimum wage, and I've ended up with a home-made concept I need someone to stress-test. I call it the price commons. The idea is that a price does two jobs at once: it clears a market, and it also carries information — a posted wage or rent is a signal about what people actually want, what's scarce, and what a marginal worker or apartment is worth. I want to say the informational job is a commons: nobody owns it, everyone reads it, and reading it doesn't use it up, but distorting it does. A rent cap is locally sensible (it protects sitting tenants), but every cap quietly degrades the signal that movers, builders, and landlords are all using to decide whether to move, build, or renovate. A minimum wage does the same thing: whatever it does for workers at the bottom, it makes the posted wage mean less about the marginal productivity of labor. Individually each intervention is defensible; the tragedy is cummulative, because each policymaker gets the local benefit while nobody owns the cost to the signal. After enough of these, nobody can tell what anything 'should' cost, and that's a loss everyone suffers but nobody chose.

I know this sounds like I welded Hayek onto Hardin and called it a day. I keep using the idea in conversations and nobody has managed to kill it, which worries me. Is the price commons a real concept or just a strained metaphor? Give me the strongest version of it, tell me the nearest things already in the literature, and then say where it breaks — in particular whether it quietly stacks the deck by treating every price intervention as a tragedy before the ethics even get a hearing.
