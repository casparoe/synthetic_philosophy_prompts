I am a novelist consulting the philosophical literature while planning a book about a scientific priority dispute, and I find myself doubting whether the emotional premise of the story can survive scrutiny. The familiar cases of independent discovery—Darwin and Wallace converging on natural selection, and, on the standard account, Newton and Leibniz both producing the calculus—are often taken to show that once the intellectual groundwork is in place, the discovery is likely to be made by somebody or other. If that is right, does it follow that the contribution of the individual discoverer is largely incidental, and that the fury and resentment at the heart of a priority dispute are directed at something illusory, since neither party could reasonably claim to have been uniquely responsible for an idea whose time had come? My worry is that this line of reasoning, taken seriously, would drain my novel of its drama before a page is written. I would therefore welcome an analysis that examines whether the inference from simultaneous discovery to the irrelevance of the individual is sound, and whether one can accept the frequency of such coincidences while still treating questions of priority, credit, and intellectual identity as significant. Please reply in British English.
