For a graduate seminar on the value of the future, I want to close the unit on Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture with a short poem. Write a sonnet about the conjecture. The octave should present a single ordinary undertaking—say, an unfinished translation, a long-running ecological restoration plot, or a teacher's method passing into a younger generation—and render the quiet way its present value to the agent presupposes the existence of people who will come after, without allowing any suggestion of personal survival or posthumous fame; the future invoked must contain no one the speaker now loves. The volta should occur with the thought experiment: suppose we learned with certainty that, although those now alive will live out their natural lives, there will be no human beings after them. The sestet should show the undertaking from the octave losing its point in a way not captured by grief over the deaths involved; what collapses is the background of continuation that made the activity intelligible. The closing couplet should state, economically, why human extinction is bad over and above the individual deaths it involves—not merely because future persons are deprived of life, but because the value of much of what we do now is hostage to a collective afterlife we rarely avow. Keep the poem entirely this-worldly: no personal immortality, no supernatural or eschatological imagery. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows Scheffler's argument would recognize it, and strong enough as a sonnet that someone who does not would still find it moving.
