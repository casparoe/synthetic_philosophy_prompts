As a doctoral student in moral philosophy, I have been working on the golden rule and on whether it can be reformulated so as to survive its standard objections, and I have been struck by how rarely that debate engages with narrative cases that are not already treated as utilitarian set pieces. Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' is such a case: it is standardly taught as a problem about whether aggregate welfare can justify the suffering of the innocent, but I would like you to read it specifically through the lens of the golden rule, its formulations, its problems, and the question of whether it can be fixed.

To fix the terms: the story describes a city whose general flourishing depends on keeping one child in abject and permanent misery; citizens learn of the arrangement when they come of age, most accept it after an initial shock, and a minority simply leaves. I do not want a summary or general remarks about utilitarianism. I want a disciplined golden-rule reading that distinguishes the positive formulation, the negative or silver-rule formulation, and the underlying role-reversal test, and that says which of these a citizen must be applying when she judges that the child's treatment is required, permitted, or intolerable.

Please attend to the following. First, be explicit about whose perspective the role-reversal test mobilises: the citizen's actual preferences, the child's actual preferences, or some rationally reconstructed preference. The story matters here because the child has no voice, and the citizens can only imagine what the child would or should want; does the golden rule give them licence to fill that silence with their own values? Second, use the story to work through the familiar objections, such as the moral masochist, the fanatic who is genuinely willing to be sacrificed, differing preferences, and the absence of consent, and say whether Omelas exposes a repairable defect in the rule or a structural limit of it. Third, address the relation to utilitarianism directly. An ex ante reading of the role-reversal test might ask whether a person would accept the social arrangement before knowing whether they would be the child; if the golden rule is read that way, it may end up endorsing precisely the bargain it was supposed to test. Say whether that is a reason to reject the ex ante reading, or a reason to think the golden rule cannot by itself condemn Omelas. Fourth, assess the proposed fixes in this particular case: the so-called platinum rule, which directs one to treat others as they themselves wish to be treated, appears to require asking the child, which the city cannot honestly do; a Kantian humanity principle would forbid using the child merely as a means; a contractualist test would ask whether the arrangement could be justified to the child, who would reasonably reject it. Which of these is least ad hoc, and does it retain anything recognisably golden-rule-like? Finally, be faithful in both directions: identify what the golden-rule lens reveals that the standard utilitarian reading of the story obscures, and also where the story resists the reading, since the ones who walk away do not articulate a principle and do not know where they are going. What, if anything, does the story add to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not?
