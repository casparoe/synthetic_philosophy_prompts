One of the most relied-upon comparisons in the philosophy of mind is the claim that the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware. The comparison is normally put to a specific use: because the same program can run on machines built from quite different materials, the same mental state should be realizable in quite different physical substrates, which would mean mental states are individuated by their functional or computational role rather than by the stuff that implements them. From this it is then inferred that a silicon system, or a sufficiently different biological one, could in principle think and feel, and that insisting on a neural implementation is as confused as insisting that a word processor be made of a particular alloy. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: what is supposed to play the role of the program, the hardware, the instruction set, memory, input and output devices, and the act of porting software from one machine to another. Then identify where the mapping breaks down. Which features of software are being quietly imported into the target domain that mental states need not have — for instance, being an artifact with an explicit specification, being cleanly separable from the hardware that executes it, or being insensitive to implementation details — and which of these disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion, as opposed to merely making the analogy imperfect? Finally, assess what survives. Once the disanalogies are on the table, how much of the multiple realizability thesis is supported independently of the analogy, what exact version of it remains defensible, and is there a better comparison that would carry the functionalist conclusion without smuggling in the problematic features of software?
