Anglo-American legal practice contains a device that lawyers openly call a fiction. A court states, and then reasons from, propositions that are false or at least unproven as matters of ordinary fact, while treating them as true for legal purposes. In adoption, the child is treated as if born to the adoptive parents, with old ties severed and new ones created by decree rather than by birth. In corporate criminal law, the knowledge of scattered employees is sometimes aggregated and imputed to the corporation as if a single mind held it all, so that a firm can be found to have known or intended something that no individual employee knew or intended. Under the lost-grant doctrine of the older common law, long enjoyment of a right could be taken to rest on an actual grant made at some earlier time, even when no grant could be produced and none may ever have existed. Under the doctrine of relation back, an instrument or act done today is treated as having taken effect at an earlier date. The law does not present these as empirical findings or as probabilistic guesses about what likely happened; it uses them as fixed premises from which further legal consequences flow, even while ordinary fact says otherwise.

What view of legal reality—of what makes a legal proposition true—does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the law to an institutionalist or constructivist account of legal truth, on which legal propositions are made true by rules and official recognition rather than by correspondence to extra-legal facts, or can a more realistic account of law absorb fictions as harmless shorthand for rules that could in principle be stated without them? The practice also looks internally divided: the same system punishes witnesses for stating falsehoods under oath, yet has judges stipulate known falsehoods and reason from them. Does that pattern presuppose one theory of truth in the witness box and its rival on the bench, or can the difference be drawn in a principled way? Reconstruct the implicit ontology of the practice, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in legal reasoning—or in our theory of law—if that presupposition were abandoned.
