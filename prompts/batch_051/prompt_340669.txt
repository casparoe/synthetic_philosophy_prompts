I would welcome your comments on the proposed argument before I begin writing.

My essay idea, in rough note form, is as follows. Keynes's notion of the *weight of argument* in *A Treatise on Probability* and his later treatment of uncertainty in the *General Theory* are usually discussed as separate topics. I want to treat them as two expressions of a single underlying difficulty: both the probability of a conclusion and the amount of evidence behind it can be vague, non-numerical, and only partially comparable.

The tension I want to press in the *Treatise* is this. Keynes maintains that many probabilities are non-numerical and that probabilities are not always fully ordered. Yet his discussion of weight seems to presuppose that we can at least sometimes compare bodies of evidence with respect to their completeness. I want to ask what could justify such comparisons of evidential completeness when the probabilities based on those bodies of evidence resist measurement themselves.

I suspect the later account of conventional judgment under uncertainty can be read as an answer to this. Economic agents fall back on convention precisely where the evidence is too thin and its weight too unstable to support even a rough ordering of alternatives. On this reading, the *General Theory*'s account of the state of confidence is less a break with the *Treatise* than a practical account of how agents cope with probabilities of low and uncertain weight.
