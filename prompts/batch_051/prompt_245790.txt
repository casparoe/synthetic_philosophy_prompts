Examination question:

Explain Brandom's claim that conceptual content is inferential role. In doing so, clarify why he takes material inference, rather than merely formal-logical inference, to be central to content, and discuss whether his account can meet the compositionality objection pressed by Fodor and Lepore.

Student answer:

Brandom's inferentialism is best understood as an inversion of representationalism. On the representationalist picture, the content of a sentence is fixed by what it represents—standardly, by its truth conditions or the reference of its parts—and inference is then read off as truth preservation over those fixed contents. Brandom reverses that order of explanation. For him, conceptual content is constituted by the role an expression plays in inference: to grasp a concept is to have practical mastery of what follows from claims that contain it and what it would entitle one to claim. He spells this out in terms of deontic scorekeeping, in which interlocutors keep track of one another's commitments and entitlements, and in which making an assertion is understood as undertaking a commitment that reconfigures the conversational score. Crucially, this is a normative story rather than a causal or psychological one; what fixes content is what speakers treat as correct or incorrect moves, not which associations happen to be triggered.

Brandom accordingly stresses that the relevant inferential connections are material inferences, such as the move from 'Pittsburgh is west of Philadelphia' to 'Philadelphia is east of Pittsburgh,' or from 'It is raining' to 'The streets will be wet.' Still, on his view these material transitions are only the outward sign of an underlying formal structure. What actually confers content on an expression is the set of formally valid inferential relations in which it stands—relations fixed by the logical constants and by logical form—because only those relations are universally shared and rationally obligatory. The material transitions that Brandom discusses in examples are, properly speaking, psychological associations, and they belong to the phenomenology of concept use rather than to its semantic content.

The most serious objection to inferentialism is the compositionality objection. As Fodor and Lepore press it, if a sentence's content is its inferential role, and inferential roles are holistic—a sentence's role shifting as the surrounding theory changes—then there is no stable content associated with a word to compose, and no finite theory could generate the meanings of complex expressions from their parts. But this objection need not trouble Brandom. It presupposes the representationalist demand that meanings be assigned in a systematic, truth-conditional way, and once that picture has been given up, compositionality can be given up with it. Inferentialist content is a practical, holistic affair, so it is no defect of the view that it fails to satisfy a principle that only makes sense inside the framework Brandom is rejecting.
