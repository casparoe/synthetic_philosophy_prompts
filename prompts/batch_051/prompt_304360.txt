I am writing a seminar paper on sola scriptura and the regress of interpretation—the worry that once Scripture alone is made the supreme rule of faith, every appeal to what Scripture teaches simply raises the further question of who is competent to say what it means. Two verses in the King James Version of the New Testament keep troubling me because they appear to point in opposite directions on exactly that question, and I want to know whether they can honestly be reconciled.

The first passage is 2 Peter 1:20–21 (KJV): "Knowing this first, that no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation. For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." Read flatly, verse 20 seems to deny that the sense of a scriptural prophecy is fixed by any individual's private construction of it. The second passage is 1 John 2:26–27 (KJV): "These things have I written unto you concerning them that seduce you. But the anointing which ye have received of him abideth in you, and ye need not that any man teach you: but as the same anointing teacheth you of all things, and is truth, and is no lie, and even as it hath taught you, ye shall abide in him." Read flatly, the second says believers do not need any human teacher, because the anointing itself teaches them.

Please act as a rigorous New Testament exegete who is also a philosopher of interpretation. Before settling on a view, start by brainstorming the candidate reconciliations: different senses of "private," a difference between the origin of prophecy and the ongoing instruction of believers, different contexts the two passages address (such as false teachers in 1 John and the source of prophecy in 2 Peter), different roles for human teachers and the Holy Spirit, or a distinction the authors never explicitly drew. Then work out, for each candidate, exactly what it would commit a defender of sola scriptura to about how interpretive disputes over Scripture should be settled. Be blunt about which of these reconciliations are honest and which are special pleading or question-begging—that is, which merely restate the conclusion that the text is self-interpreting. Finally, say whether the best available reconciliation actually stops the regress of interpretation, and where, if anywhere, the residual tension lies.
