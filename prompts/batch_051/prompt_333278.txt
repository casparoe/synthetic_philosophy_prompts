I am a novelist, and I have agreed to give the commencement address at a small liberal arts college with a well-regarded creative writing program. I would like you to write the speech around a problem I have been living with rather than around the usual ceremony. I am no longer convinced that effort pays in my profession in any dependable way: a writer can spend years on a book, strip it down, rebuild it, and still watch it fail or be abandoned, and that failure does not reliably become useful later. I keep a drawer of abandoned manuscripts, and I cannot honestly tell whether the hours in them were meaningfully spent. Yet I also cannot bring myself to call those hours nothing. I want the address to ask plainly whether effort and struggle are valuable in themselves, apart from what they produce, and to answer that question with some honesty without pretending that hard work guarantees success, that failure is always instructive, or that struggle automatically ennobles the person who endures it. The speech should draw on a novelist's specific experience of drafting, revising, and abandoning work rather than on abstract examples, and it should be suited to a mixed audience of graduating students in writing and other fields, their parents, and the faculty. The philosophical problem should do real structural work here: it should determine what the speech may and may not honestly say, and it should prevent the address from collapsing into either follow-your-passion optimism or the empty consolation that the struggle is its own reward. If the honest answer is that effort is not valuable in itself, or only valuable under certain conditions, the speech should have the courage to say so. It should run roughly twelve minutes when spoken. Please write the speech itself, in full, beginning with its first sentence.
