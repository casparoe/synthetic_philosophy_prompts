I keep going back and forth on sunk costs and I want a second opinion that doesn't just pick a side and cheer for it. The rational-choice line seems basically airtight to me: what I've already spent is gone either way, so the only thing that should matter is which option is better from now on; honoring a sunk cost is the Concorde fallacy, throwing good money after bad. But I can't shake that some of the times I did honor sunk costs weren't mistakes. I finished a novel I'd worked on for two years even though I'd realized a different project would probably do better, and it felt like I was keeping a promise to my past self rather than being irrational. The theory says that feeling is just a bias I should override. But here's what nags at me: if I knew ahead of time that I'd treat those two years as a reason to finish, that might make future-me more likely to start long projects at all. So maybe honoring sunk costs is rational as a kind of policy about yourself — not because the past costs can be recovered, but because your future choices aren't independant of your past ones. I want to know whether that's a real amendment to the theory or just a slick way of sneaking the sunk cost back in. Give me the strongest version of both sides: the strongest case that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and the strongest case for a view where it can sometimes be rational. Then tell me which should yield in my novel case, or whether there's a reading where both survive. And be explicit: what argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
