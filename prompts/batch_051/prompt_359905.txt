I'm TAing this epistemology and philosophy of science course, and I've got a pile of short-answer questions to grade. Here's one prompt I wrote and a student's answer to it. Could you grade it?

Question:
Explain the birthday paradox and why it isn't really a paradox. Then consider this: your friend tells you she bumped into her childhood next-door neighbour on a random street in Tokyo, a city neither of them lives in. "What are the odds?" she says. "It can't be just luck." Using the birthday paradox and the distinction between a specified and an unspecified event, explain how an epistemologist should assess her inference, being clear about what the birthday paradox does and doesn't license.

Student's answer:
The birthday paradox says that with just 23 people, the chance that at least two of them share a birthday is already a bit over 50%. This feels shocking because people implicitly think of the chance that someone shares their birthday, which really does need about 253 people to get to 50%. But in a room of 23 there are 253 pairs, and since each pair has about a 1/365 chance of matching, all those mostly independent chances add up. So it's not a paradox, it's a lesson about how fast the number of pairs grows. The same logic applies to my friend in Tokyo. Over a lifetime there are just a huge number of "pairs" of possible coincidences: every person you might run into, every city you visit, every day something could happen. This is the law of truly large numbers: given enough opportunities, even very unlikely events become almost inevitable somewhere. So the coincidence of running into her childhood neighbour isn't especially strong evidence of any hidden connection. The odds of that coincidence are actually pretty high once you count all the other coincidences that could have happened to her but didn't. Her "can't be just luck" inference is therefore unsound, and we should lower our credence that something non-accidental is going on.

Give it a mark out of 20 and then point-by-point comments that justify the mark: what's right, the specific places where the answer goes wrong or moves too fast, and roughly what each issue costs. When you're writing those comments, please seperate the parts that are settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among epistemologists, and don't present a contested take as the consensus.
