I'm a retired organic chemist, and after getting into philosophy a few years ago I've been running a small reading group. We've just read some Kant, R.M. Hare, and the generalization argument (the "what if everybody did that?" style associated with Marcus Singer), and I'd like a one-page decision-tree handout that sorts people by which version of universalizability they actually rely on, not by which label they prefer. Act as a professional moral philosopher and think carefully: start by brainstorming the underlying questions, then build the final tree.

Use five or six concrete diagnostic cases; at minimum include (1) making a lying promise to return money, (2) a free-rider case about dodging taxes or skipping vaccination, (3) a harmless trivial case where universal compliance would only be mildly inconvenient, like everyone crossing the same patch of grass, and (4) a case where universalizing seems to miss what is wrong with the act, such as humiliating someone. For each branch, ask a question a person could answer from intuition without knowing the labels. Keep the leaves: Kantian (with contradiction in conception and contradiction in will kept seperate if you can operationalize the difference), Hare-style universal prescriptivism, Singer-style generalization, rule-consequentialist, and something like a deflationary view where the universalization test just encodes other reasons. Then flag which leaves the cases cannot seperate, and name any empty cells in the logical space, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why nobody lives there. It should be usable by non-specialists in fifteen minutes, so keep it tidy.
