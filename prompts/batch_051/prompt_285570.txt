i've been stuck on pascal's mugging for a while now and keep going in circles with it. the setup i mean is: a guy says he can give you some absurdly huge reward if you just hand over your wallet, you think the chance he's telling the truth is tiny but not literally zero, and if you multiply it out the expected value of paying is still way bigger than whatever's in your wallet. so the math says pay, and almost nobody would.

I want you to write a dialogue between two people who disagree about what to do with that. A thinks the expected value calculation is basically right, and that refusing is just letting your gut overrule the math. B thinks there's a principled reason you can tell the mugger to get lost without throwing out expected value reasoning in ordinary cases like insurance or a casino bet. neither of them should be an idiot or a strawman; they should actually answer each other.

have them work through the hard versions. what if the mugger only asks for a penny? what if he just asks for thirty seconds of your time to hear him out? what if the reward isn't absurdly huge, just ten million dollars at a one-in-a-billion chance, the kind of thing you'd obviously jump at if the odds were one in a thousand? if B tries to draw a line and say probabilities below some cutoff should just be treated as zero, A should push on where exactly the line goes, and why it woudln't also let you ignore small real risks like a car crash or a house fire. if A tries to say your utility is bounded or money stops mattering after a point, B should push on whether that actually solves it or just moves the problem down to a smaller mugging. and somewhere in there i want them to compare it to pascals wager and argue about whether the mugging is just a secular version of it or whether something important is different.

they don't have to agree at the end. honestly i'd rather see each of them forced to admit something uncomfortable than watch them settle for a fake handshake.
