I am trying to work out a colleague's implicit philosophy of proof from her track record on nonstandard proofs, because a new case has come up and we disagree about it. Her past verdicts are these. She counts the original computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem as a proof, though she says it establishes that the theorem is true without illuminating why it is true. She likewise accepts the computer-assisted proof of the Kepler conjecture. She accepted the proof of a combinatorial lemma once the computer-generated argument had been rewritten as a short, human-verifiable proof. She rejected a claim about a dynamical system that rested entirely on a long numerical simulation, treating it as evidence rather than proof. And she regards a fully formalized, proof-checker-verified proof of an old theorem as a genuine proof, while describing it as verification rather than discovery.

The new case is this. An automated theorem prover has produced a derivation of a theorem in finite group theory from standard axioms. The derivation runs to roughly fifty million steps; no human has read more than a small fraction of it, and no one can describe its overall strategy, because the humans supplied only the axioms, the theorem statement, and the search heuristics. The derivation has been independently checked by two proof checkers written by separate teams, and the prover's code is public. Would her past verdicts commit her to counting this as a proof? What rule or rules best make sense of her record, which rival rules fit it just as well and what does each predict here, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what further case would most cleanly separate the live rules?
