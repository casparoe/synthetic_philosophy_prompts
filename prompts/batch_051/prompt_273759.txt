I got called for jury duty a few months ago and ended up obsessed with a rule I caught myself already following, one I'd never actually inspected. The rule: I won't come to believe that a specific person or company is responsible for a harm on purely statistical evidence, no matter how high the probability, and I definitely won't act on that belief by assigning blame or liability. The actual trial didn't test this—it was a contract dispute about a deck—but during voir dire the judge brought up the blue bus hypothetical: a bus hits a pedestrian, and all you can prove is that 80% of the buses on that route are owned by one company. I said I'd still refuse to find the company liable even in a civil case, where the bar is just more likely than not. Another prospective juror said I was being irrational: 80% clears 51%, full stop, and if I'd rely on an eyewitness with a false-id rate worse than 20%, I can't claim I'm just being careful.

I've been trying to turn the gut feeling into an actual rule. Something like: bare base rates can raise the probability that this named individual did it, but they can't justify a verdict against this individual, because the evidence never touches them—it's about the class they happen to fall in. I'd say the same about the gatecrasher case, where 501 of 1000 rodeo attendees snuck in and it's more likely than not that any randomly picked attendee is a gatecrasher, but I still wouldn't make that one person pay on that alone. My partner says this is the base-rate fallacy in a prinicpled costume, and that narrative evidence just feels more real to me without carrying any extra information.

Is there a defensible principle here, or am I just squeamish about using base rates against actual people? If there is a principle, what's the cleanest version of it, and under what conditions would my rule fail? And does the criminal standard—proof beyond reasonable doubt—change the story, or is it the same principle with the dial turned up?
