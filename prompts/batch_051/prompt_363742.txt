I'm a novelist, and I've been trying for months to write a novel set a century or so from now without making the future either a horror show or a toothpaste advert. The obstacle is what a few friends of mine, who are much too online about these things, called fun theory: the problem of saying what a future would have to be like for the lives in it to be actually good to live in, rather than merely comfortable or survivable. In practice I keep being pulled toward a view that has one enormous systematic virtue, which is that it yields a definite, non-arbitrary ranking in nearly every case. The view is that the goodness of a future is fixed by how far it satisfies the informed preferences of the people living in it, everyone weighted equally, and that any other standard just smuggles in the ranker's own taste in lives. You have to say informed preferences because otherwise you can make any future optimal by raising people not to want what they won't get, which is obviously a cheat. But once you accept that view, the best future seems to be one engineered around the elimination of frustration: no projects that fail, no loves that stay unrequited, no creative blockage that lasts longer than an afternoon, no long detours, no boredom that isn't instantly answered.

And I find that when I imagine that future as a setting, my characters suffocate in it, and the intuition that it would be a thin or counterfeit kind of life feels firmer to me than most of my actual judgements about how to live. I honestly believe trouble, misdirection, longing, and the experience of being stuck in a way that later pays off might be part of what makes a life worth living, not merely the price we pay for it. I don't know which side should give way. Should I dismiss the intuition as an occupational deformation of a novelist, whose job is to need conflict, and keep the theory? Should I amend or abandon the theory, and if so on what principle exactly? Or is there a reading on which they don't conflict because people's informed preferences can include wanting a measure of dissatisfaction, and is that a reconciliation rather than a fudge? Give me the strongest version of each option, including what would justify it, and a reasoned verdict on which is least self-deceiving. And please write the answer as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and in British English.
