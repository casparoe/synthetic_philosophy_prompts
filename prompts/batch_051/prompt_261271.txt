I'm a retired physical chemist, and after I left the lab I started reading my way through political philosophy—Rawls, Audi, Wolterstorff, the whole public reason debate—and I've got to say it's harder to operationalize than I expected. Last month I got appointed to the school board in my district, and now I'm the guy who has to sit through three-hour meetings where people make claims about what God wants for the curriculum. I was discussing this with a Presbyterian minister friend of mine, and he said the problem isn't that people bring religious reasons, it's that we don't have a reliable way to seperate when they're being used as reasons for public policy from when they're being used as something else. I don't fully buy that, but it made me wonder if the philosophy can be turned into a procedure.

So here's what I'd like. Can you write me a decision procedure—a checklist or a set of questions in order—that a school board member could actually use in a meeting to decide whether a religious argument may legitimately enter public deliberation, and when it should be set aside as a public reason? It should be usable by someone without formal philosophy training, under time pressure, and it should say clearly which steps are judgment calls and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person. Also, I'd like you to run it on three concrete cases: (1) a parent argues that a health class must not teach about certain contraception because it violates God's design; (2) a board member, who is Quaker, argues against a school resource officer program because of a religious commitment to nonviolence; (3) a coalition opposes a bathroom policy on grounds that it violates natural law as understood in their faith. I'd like one of the cases to be one where the procedure gives an answer I'll find uncomfortable. And be honest about what the procedure cannot settle.
