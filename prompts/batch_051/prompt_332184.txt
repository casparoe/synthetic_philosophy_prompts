I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've built an argument I now don't trust because it seems to prove too much. It says Jeffrey conditionalization is either redundant or wrong. I'm hoping you can tell me where it breaks.

Here's the argument.

(1) Take a routine Jeffrey case: my credence in E drops from 0.8 to 0.3 after an experience, but the experience doesn't make me certain of E, not-E, or anything else in particular — I just come away less confident.

(2) After the experience, there is nevertheless something I'm completely certain of: that my new credence in E is 0.3. Let F be the introspective proposition "my credence in E is now 0.3."

(3) If I'm certain of F, I should be able to update on it by ordinary conditionalization: for any A, my posterior should be P(A | F).

(4) But Jeffrey conditionalization says my posterior should be Q(A) = 0.3·P(A | E) + 0.7·P(A | not-E), with my conditional probabilities given E and not-E held fixed.

(5) These are not the same formula, and there's no obvious reason they'd give the same numbers. If they ever differ, the "certainty about my own credences" update and the Jeffrey update can't both be right.

(6) The certainty-based update seems unavoidable — introspection gives me at least one new certainty, and when you're certain of something you conditionalize on it. So the pressure is on Jeffrey's rule.

(7) The same move is available for any shift in credences, so Jeffrey conditionalization ends up redundant in general, not just in this case.

I have a stake in this because I use Jeffrey conditionalization all over my dissertation. I'd like to keep it, or at least know what I'm really committing to. Please don't hand me a menu of "some philosophers say X, others say Y." Commit: tell me which step I should reject, why it looked fine, and what rejecting it costs for how I should think about updating on uncertain evidence. If you think the argument actually goes through, then defend the conclusion — but in that case explain why Jeffery conditionalization is still used as if it earned its keep.

You're an expert in formal epistemology; think carefully, and before you settle on an answer, briefly brainstorm the possible ways out, then tell me your actual diagnosis.
