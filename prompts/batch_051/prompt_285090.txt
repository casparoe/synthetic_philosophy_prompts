I'm writing up my views on the norms of assertion and would like a consistency check before I present them. I hold all of the following:

(1) Assertion is governed by a knowledge norm: one may assert that P only if one knows that P.
(2) The knowledge norm is what best explains why it is defective to say, "P, but I don't know whether P"; a mere truth norm or justified-belief norm cannot capture that defect as cleanly.
(3) But I also want to say this. Suppose your phone's navigation app has been perfectly reliable all day, and on its say-so you tell your passenger to turn right; the app is in fact glitching, and the turn is wrong. I think your assertion was fully proper and that you merit no criticism for it—not merely that we should excuse you for a violation.
(4) I do not think "knows" in (1) requires certainty or infallibility; fallible knowledge is still knowledge, so (1) does not make most ordinary assertion improper.
(5) I am drawn to epistemic contextualism: whether someone knows P, and so whether they satisfy the norm, can shift with the conversational stakes.
(6) But I also think that what you may assert should not depend on how high the stakes are for your audience. Only your own epistemic position should matter.

Please walk through these commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, explain for each what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost, and identify the single revision that would make the package most coherent. Please also say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you are least sure about. I'd like the answer in American English.
