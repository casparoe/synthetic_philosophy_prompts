Last month I attended a public hearing about a proposed ordinance in my city that would make it a misdemeanor to loiter or remain in a public place under circumstances that reasonably warrant alarm for the safety of persons or property nearby. The ordinance would directly affect the plaza outside my apartment, where I sit most evenings, so I stayed after the hearing and listened while two acquaintances argued about it over coffee. One of them, Caroline, a former public defender, said the proposed language is void for vagueness: an ordinary person cannot know in advance whether sitting on a planter with a backpack "reasonably warrants alarm," and language of that kind exists precisely so that officers can decide after the fact who looks suspicious. The other, Daniel, a legislative aide who helped draft the ordinance, replied that the phrase is deliberately modeled on the reasonable-person standard that runs through criminal and tort law, and that no legislature can enumerate every way a person might genuinely alarm others; a standard, he said, is not the same as a blank delegation of power.

Caroline answered that reasonable-person standards have content because they are anchored in settled community expectations about care and risk, whereas "warrants alarm" is anchored in nothing but an observer's fear — and arbitrary enforcement based on fear is exactly what the vagueness doctrine exists to prevent. Daniel said fear is not contentless: the phrase asks what a reasonable observer would perceive, and courts give such phrases content through jury instructions and consistent interpretation. Caroline said that only relocates the problem: if the phrase acquires content only through later judicial narrowing, then it gave no fair notice to the person in the plaza before those decisions existed. Daniel replied that fair notice has never required the precision of a tax code; the law is full of terms like "reckless" and "unreasonable noise," and demanding bright lines would yield overbroad and underinclusive rules that people can game, which is its own failure of legality. Caroline granted that bright lines have costs, but said those costs are the price of preventing officers from making on-the-spot judgments about who counts as a threat, which is more dangerous than some gaming at the margins. Daniel said the remedy for bad judgment is training, supervision, and equal-protection review, not the fiction that a legislature can codify every case in advance.

They asked what I thought, and I said I would need to think about it. I am still thinking. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each round, say who has the better of that specific point and why, and then say what, if anything, both are missing. In particular, I keep wondering whether the real question is less about how much linguistic precision is achievable than about who should bear the risk of underinclusive and overinclusive rules, and whether there is any principled line between a standard that invites judgment and a law that is void for vagueness.
