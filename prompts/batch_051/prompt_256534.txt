I sit on a design review group at a consumer app company, and we have to rule on a new feature. Our own past decisions are these. We rejected a sale countdown timer because it reset on every visit and thus advertised a deadline that did not exist. We approved a badge showing how many people were viewing an item, because it was computed from live traffic data and displayed as a plain count without urgency language, on the condition that the data remain genuine. We approved a streak counter in a learning app because its mechanics were fully explained and its reminders were opt-in, though we required those reminders to be off by default. And we rejected an energy system aimed partly at minors that sold recharge packs in odd denominations, on the ground that users could not easily compare the real cost per session. The new proposal is an endless feed for our reading app: nothing hidden, no false signals, no autoplay; the feed simply refills so there is always more to read and the app never signals a stopping point. Test users routinely read far longer than they had intended and said afterwards that they wished the app had stopped them. If we want to stay consistent with our own rulings, what should we decide, which earlier decision is the closest analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, what principle would we be adopting with each choice: are we really ruling against deception, against designed pressure that overrides users' own intentions, or against features that predictably leave users worse off by their own lights? What further proposal would most cleanly separate those principles?
