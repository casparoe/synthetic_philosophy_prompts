Okay, so a guy in my dept keeps pushing this argument about illusionism and I'm stuck. I want to believe introspection is normally fine, and I also think illusionism about consciousness is more plausible than people admit. The argument:

(1) Illusionism says phenomenal consciousness — the what-it's-like aspect of experience — is a kind of introspective illusion. There's no qualia; there are introspective representations that present us as having qualia, and those representations are false.
(2) So when I judge introspectively that this headache has a throbbing, awful feel to it, that judgment is false. Same for everyone, and not rarely: the error is built into the very act of introspection.
(3) But introspection is also the main source of evidence for the claim that there's a problem of consciousness in the first place, and illusionists themselves rely on introspective reports and on a story about how introspection misleads to motivate their view.
(4) If introspection is systematically wrong about something as basic as whether my current pain feels like anything, then why trust it about anything else it reports — beliefs, desires, moods, imagination? The parity seems hard to avoid.
(5) But no illusionist wants to conclude that first-person reports are globally unreliable; their view is meant to be a localized debunking of one contested posit, not a general skepticism about the mind.
(6) So illusionism appears to undercut the very evidence that supports it: the more convinced I am by the illusionist picture, the less reason I have to accept any introspective evidence — including the evidence that got me there.

I don't see which premise to give up. I'd most like to keep (1) and (5), since giving up (5) feels catastrophic and giving up (1) just abandons the whole issue. So where does this go wrong? Or if it doesn't, what do I have to accept?
