I keep encountering the same way of introducing assembly theory, and I want you to audit it rather than accept it. The introduction goes roughly like this: think of an object's complexity as the length of its shortest recipe. There is a fixed pantry of elementary ingredients, a small set of allowed operations for combining them, and the rule that once you have produced an intermediate compound you may reuse it as a new ingredient without paying again for its production. The assembly index of an object is then the minimal number of operations needed to produce it. This picture is then made to do real argumentative work: it is used to conclude that a molecule with a high assembly index could not plausibly have formed by chance, so its presence is evidence of life or at least of a non-random selective process, and, more generally, that assembly index is the right measure of how complex an object is. Audit the recipe analogy. Give the correspondence table: ingredients, steps, sub-recipes, the pantry, the person following the instructions. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Pay particular attention to the features recipes quietly import from their own domain: a recipe has an author with a goal; its length depends on what is treated as a basic ingredient and what counts as one step in a given culinary tradition; and a recipe can include redundant or conventional steps even though they are not part of a minimal construction. Do these imported features matter for the conclusions the analogy is being used to support? In particular, does the inference from high assembly index to not-formed-by-chance survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the recipe picture doing that inferential work? If the analogy fails at its load-bearing points, say what a better analogy would be, or whether the conclusion has to stand without this kind of analogy.
