I have been trying to work out why climate scientists both rely on multi-model ensembles and regularly caution that the spread of those ensembles should not be read as a probability distribution over future climate, and what the ensemble spread should in fact contribute to decision-relevant uncertainty. It struck me that the situation has a structural twin in machine learning, and I cannot tell whether I have found a real isomorphism or just two fields sharing the word "ensemble."

The parallel, as I see it, is this. Both start with a collection of imperfect, partially independent representations of an underlying generating process. In ensemble learning, methods such as bagging and random forests combine high-variance base learners whose errors are at least partly decorrelated; under the bias-variance decomposition, averaging reduces variance while leaving bias roughly unchanged, and the gain depends on the errors' not all being correlated. In climate science, a multi-model ensemble combines structurally different general circulation models that discretize the same underlying physics and parameterize unresolved processes differently, and the spread across those models is treated as carrying information about structural uncertainty. Climate scientists also worry, for reasons that sound like the error-correlation point, that the models are not independent because they share code, parameterization schemes, and tuning targets, so the effective sample size is smaller than the number of models.

If the analogy is deep, several results should transfer. The benefit of averaging should depend on comparable skill and sufficiently decorrelated errors; weighting ensemble members by their skill on the historical record should be dangerous when the future is not like the past; and diversity should be sought deliberately, in a way that preserves skill, rather than accumulated haphazardly. The analogy would also predict that enlarging an ensemble with near-duplicate models produces false confidence rather than reduced uncertainty, which I believe climate scientists already accept.

But there seem to be sharp disanalogies as well. Climate models are not trained against a long out-of-sample record for the target quantities of interest; the system is nonstationary under external forcing; there is one observed trajectory to compare with at any given time, not a reusable held-out distribution; and the ensemble is not a designed sample from a model-generating distribution but an ensemble of opportunity, shaped by which models happen to exist. If these disanalogies break the parallel, then calling both practices "ensembling" is a superficial resemblance, and I want to know exactly which feature does the real epistemic work on each side.

So my question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. If it is deep, which results from ensemble learning genuinely transfer to climate projections, and which fail, and what is the feature that blocks each failed transfer? If it is superficial, what is the actual structure on each side, and does anything in the climate case reveal a blind spot in machine-learning thinking about correlated errors in a population of models? I am looking for the precise mapping and the points where it comes apart, not a general caution that analogies have limits.
