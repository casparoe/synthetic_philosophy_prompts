One thing I keep circling back to as a science writer is the old argument about sexist default language—whether the generic he, chairman, mankind are harmless conventions or quietly load the dice whenever they appear. Hofstadter did that decades-old satirical piece where he writes a mock-defense of using white as the generic term for all people, treating person as a clunky neologism; the whole essay is framed as a person paper rather than a white paper, so the reader gets race flipped in a context where we usually tolerate gender. It lands as a kind of reductio: arguments that sound flimsy when transposed to race probably should have sounded flimsy for gender too. But a satire is an intuition pump, not evidence. So I want to know how much of the underlying claim is actually testable.

Suppose a defender of the old style says: everyone knows he is meant generically; the pronoun has a genuinely neutral reading and readers process it exactly as the writer intended. Thats a claim about cognition, not just dictionaries. How would you test it? Design a study—a thought experiment is fine, but concrete enough that a psycholinguist could actually run it—that would distinguish three possibilities: the pronoun really is processed as neutral by default; it is processed as male first and the generic reading is a correction; or reader background dominates and the pronouns barely do any work at all. Point to the sort of measures you would use (reaction times, pronoun resolution, who readers assume a sentence is about, that kind of thing) and say what pattern of results would count as support for each possibility. Then tell me what would remain unsettled even if the data came back clean—for instance, whether a processing bias cashes out as real-world exclusion, or whether whatever fix is proposed actually works. I'm not after a reading list, just the experimental logic.
