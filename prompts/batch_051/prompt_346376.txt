I teach a second-year philosophy of cognitive science seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and I want to open the embodied cognition unit with an activity rather than a lecture. Design something that makes students feel the pull of the enactivist challenge to representationalism: the thought that not all intelligent behaviour runs off an inner model of the world, and that some of the smoothest things we do depend on our being coupled to the world in real time instead of representing it first. I want the distinction to show up in what they do, not in me explaining it afterwards.

I've got a seed idea: split the room so one group has to write a full step-by-step recipe that a blindfolded first-timer could follow to catch a soft ball thrown from a few metres away, then have someone follow the recipe literally and watch it fall apart. Meanwhile the other group just catches balls, and we ask what they were actually doing. But treat that as a seed rather than a brief — if you think a better structure (navigating from a written description vs. walking the route, describing how to balance vs. standing on one leg, using the world as its own memory vs. building an inner map) would get the idea across more reliably, use that instead.

What I need back: the full setup, with rules, roles, materials that cost nothing, and a timeline for 90 minutes; then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the representationalist/enactivist distinction. I'd like the debrief to draw out the funny asymmetry where the effortless-looking skills (catching, balancing) turn out to be the ones that resist being written down, while the slow deliberate stuff (like arithmetic) is easy to spell out. Finally, be honest about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the debate this hands-on version can't reproduce — so I know what I still need to supply in discussion. By the end they should see the 'inner model of the world' story as an empirical bet about how skills work, not a description of what it's obvious they are doing.
