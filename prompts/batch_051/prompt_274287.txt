I cover science and ideas for a living, and I was reporting on the ethics of low-probability, high-severity risk — automated cars, vaccine side-effects, that kind of thing — when a source laid this on me. I keep poking it and can't find the seam. I want to know whether the argument is valid and, if not, exactly wich step fails. Could you also write the answer in British English?

(1) Imposing a truly negligible chance of death on an unconsenting stranger is plainly permissible. If it weren't, switching on a lamp or posting a letter would be wrong, since almost nothing carries zero risk.

(2) Imposing a near-certain chance of death on an unconsenting stranger is plainly impermissible. That's killing by lottery rather than by any single act of force.

(3) Between the negligible probability and the near-certain one there is no sharp, non-arbitrary boundary; the risk slides continuously from one end to the other.

(4) If there is no non-arbitrary boundary between permissible and impermissible risk, then the difference between the two ends is only a difference in degree, not a difference in kind.

(5) A difference in degree cannot by itself justify treating one end as fine and the other as gravely wrong; if you refuse to draw a line anywhere along the continuum, you are not entitled to treat the two ends as morally different at all.

So the argument seems to show that either ordinary life is morally on a par with Russian roulette, or playing Russian roulette with another person is no worse than posting them a letter. I don't believe either. So tell me: where exactly, if anywhere, does this go wrong? And if it does not go wrong, which horn should I bite?
