Hey, so I've been going back and forth with myself about this for the last couple weeks, ever since I got dragged into a local project on preserving my town's old immigrant neighborhood. You'd think I'd love it, but the more I dig into the history the less I want to actually do anything in the present, and it's made me wonder whether some kinds of historical awareness are actively bad for living. You're a professional philosopher who knows Nietzsche's early essays pretty well, so I'd really appreciate if you could think carefully and help me check an argument I've been building from his distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history.

Here's my attempt to turn the essay into an argument:

(1) Living and acting require both remembering and forgetting; if you become too historically aware, every possible action starts to look like just another repetition or an ironic commentary, and you can't move.

(2) Monumental history is healthy only insofar as it gives present actors inspiring models from the past; antiquarian history is healthy only insofar as it preserves and cherishes a given inherited form of life; critical history is healthy only insofar as it breaks the spell of an inherited form of life enough to allow new action.

(3) But these three modes can't operate together in one life at the same time. Monumental history needs simplified, maybe even distorted examples from the past, and critical history would undermine those simplifications. Antiquarian history needs loyalty and reverence, and critical history trains suspicion and rupture.

(4) So in any given period a person has to choose which mode will dominate.

(5) Critical history is the most dangerous of the three, becuase monumental and antiquarian history can go wrong only by becoming one-sided, whereas critical history can go wrong by cutting the roots of any identity at all.

(6) Therefore, if my main goal is to live well rather than to be a historian, I should mostly avoid critical history, and if I do use it, it should only be as a rare instrument when a particular inherited practice is demonstrably blocking life.

I half-suspect step (3) is overstating the incompatibility—couldn't someone use critical history on some parts of the past while still remaining loyal to others? But maybe Nietzsche really is committed to something like (3), because these aren't just methods but different attitudes toward time. Please start by brainstorming the strongest reading of each step, then say whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and what a more defensible version of the argument would have to say.
