I'm on the board of a small community garden in my neighborhood. There's a rule that you can only grow vegetables in your plot, not flowers, and nobody currently involved knows why it's there — the people who wrote the rules are long gone. Some younger members want to drop it, and their arguments make sense to me on their own merits. But my gut says vote to keep it: a rule that old was probably created for a reason, and the fact that I can't see the reason just means I'm missing information, not that there isn't one. I once read something about not tearing down a fence until you know why somebody built it, and that stuck with me, but i dont know how far it actually goes. Is my policy defensible, or is it just status-quo bias dressed up as caution? I want a real test, not a lecture. When is the age or durability of a rule actual evidence that it's doing a job, and when do rules stick around only because they're cheap to keep and annoying to challenge? Does the answer change if the rule is doing real harm right now, or if the people who made it had very different values than we do?
