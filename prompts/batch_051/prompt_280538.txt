In my sixth-form debating society, people frequently attach numbers to one-off events: a 35 per cent chance that a particular bill passes, a 60 per cent chance that a specific defendant is guilty. My policy, adopted last year, is that a probability is only meaningful if it can be derived from a frequency across repeated, comparable cases; anything else I treat as empty precision and give no weight. One friend says this is a sound frequentist rule that protects me against fabricated numbers; another says I am confusing different senses of probability and discarding the estimate I most need when a decision must be made. Privately, I notice I do not follow my own rule, since I still carry an umbrella when I judge rain likely without consulting any frequency table. I would like to know whether this habit is defensible, or whether it is an inconsistency presented as a principle; and if it is defensible in part, what a better rule would be for deciding when a one-off probability should carry weight. Please write your answer in British English.
