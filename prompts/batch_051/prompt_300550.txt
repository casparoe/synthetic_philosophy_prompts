I retired from geochemistry a few years back and got into philosophy, and I keep ending up at omission causation becuase of the contamination cases I used to consult on: was it the company that stored the solvent badly that caused the plume, or the regulator who never inspected the liner? Ive written down my current commitments below. I dont want a lecture on each one; I want an audit of the set — which pairs or larger clusters are in tension, how bad each tension is, and what the cheapest fix would be. Also tell me which conflicts dissolve once you read the items carefully; I want discrimination, not a blanket flagging.

(i) Omissions can be genuine causes. If the plant died because I didn't water it, then my not watering it caused the death; if the liner failed because nobody inspected it, the missing inspection was part of the causal story. I'd need a very good argument to give this up, since this is how science and the law actually talk.

(ii) But I'm a physicalist about what exists. I don't think the world contains negative facts or absences as extra ingredients, just actual events and processes and the laws connecting them. An omission is a true description of what didn't happen, not a thing with a spatiotemporal address.

(iii) The account that strikes me as most promising is difference-making: c causes e roughly when e counterfactually depends on c, or when c raises e's probability, or when c is an actual difference-maker in the circumstances. It handles omissions cleanly, since had I watered the plant, it wouldn't have died.

(iv) Still, I have a stubborn physicist's intuition that real causation requires some actual physical link — a continuous chain that transmits energy, momentum, or information. Causation that is just a fact about what would have happened sounds like bookkeeping, not like what the world does.

(v) I also think legal and medical talk of the cause quite rightly depends on expectations and duties. The doctor who didn't prescribe the anticoagulant and the person on the bus with a packet of the same drug who didn't offer it both omitted something, but we only call the doctor's omission the cause of the stroke. I think institutions should go on this way, because their job is partly to assign answerability, not just describe what happened.

(vi) But I'm a realist about plain causation: there is a fact of the matter whether an omission caused an outcome, and our blame practices, salience, and institutional interests don't make it true or false.

(vii) Last, I'm sure causes can't come after their effects, and I'm suspicious of simultaneous causation. But omissions don't seem to have a start time: when exactly did my not watering the plant begin? At my last watering, at the first moment I should have watered, or when the plant began to wilt? If an omission has no onset, what exactly precedes the death?

Go through the list and tell me: which pairs, or larger clusters, are in tension and how severe; which conflicts dissolve under a more careful reading; and what the cheapest revision is for each real tension. Then tell me which single change to this list would do the most work overall.
