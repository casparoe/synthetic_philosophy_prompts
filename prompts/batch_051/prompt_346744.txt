Write a deadpan parody of an ISO-style design specification for a universal picture language, issued by a fictional institute clearly modeled on Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE project. The document should share the Neurathian conviction that a carefully standardized system of pictures can outdo verbal language—escaping its ambiguity, local idioms, and metaphysical confusions—while serving as a neutral medium of public information. The humor should come from the actual difficulties of any such project, handled with smooth bureaucratic confidence: every supposedly self-evident icon turns out to require its own separately stipulated convention; the clauses for expressing tense, negation, possibility, and causation pile pictograms, flags, and diacritical marks on top of one another until the system quietly approximates an ordinary written language; the standard's apparently neutral figures and glyphs reflect the institute's own unexamined cultural defaults; and disputes about what a pictogram really means get referred to an appeals process written in exactly the verbal language the pictures were meant to replace. Never state these problems in the abstract—let the deadpan numbered clauses demonstrate them. Roughly 600 words, formatted as numbered sections of a standard.
