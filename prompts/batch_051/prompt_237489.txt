I'm taking a class on evidence and there's something I can't get out of my head. Suppose a bus hits a pedestrian and nobody saw it happen. We know the Blue Bus Company runs 80% of the busses on that street at that hour, so the probability the bus was blue is 0.8 — but no court would let the pedestrian win on that, and most people would balk at it too. Now change just one fact: a witness says the bus was blue, and we know from testing that she's 80% reliable under those exact conditions (dusk, tired, slightly colorblind, whatever). Same probability, same degree of confidence, but now a court would let the pedestrian win and we'd all feel fine about it. So we're treating a naked base rate as worse evidence than a fallible witness, even when both put the probability at exactly 0.8.

The usual move is to say the witness is individualized and the base rate is merely statistical, but I don't see how that cuts it. DNA evidence is basically a population frequency too — one in three million people would match this sample — and courts treat it as the gold standard. The DNA frequency feels individualized only because there's a trace at the scene tying the defendant to the statistic, but the bus route is also a trace at the scene tying the company to the 80% statistic. So is there a real principle separating these cases, or is the law's aversion to naked statistics just a bias toward stories over base rates that's dressed up as a theory? If you think there's a principle, give me the version that keeps the witness and the DNA while still excluding the bus company, without just relabeling things.
