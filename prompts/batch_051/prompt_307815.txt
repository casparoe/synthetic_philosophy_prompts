I am part of a philosophy reading group that has spent the better part of a year watching two of its members argue about what rights are, and the argument has become frustratingly circular. One of them holds a deontological view: rights are trumps or side-constraints, norms that do not merely assign high weight to certain interests but remove certain trade-offs from the table in advance, so that whether one may violate a right is not settled by asking how much welfare lies on each side. The other holds an instrumentalist view: rights are devices for protecting especially vulnerable interests such as bodily integrity, security, and freedom of expression; they earn their stringency from the gravity of those interests and from the dangers of allowing case-by-case calculation, and they may in principle be overridden when the interests they protect are threatened by something far more serious. In ordinary cases the two nearly always reach the same verdict, and both reject the idea that rights language is empty rhetoric. Yet the discussion keeps collapsing at the same point, with the deontologist saying the instrumentalist has quietly given up on the very idea of a right and the instrumentalist saying the deontologist has offered nothing but brute intuition for treating constraints as authoritative.

Suppose the group finally asks them to produce the document of an adversarial collaboration, and you are the one drafted to write it. I would like you to draft the joint statement they could both sign. It should set out the substantive claims they can agree on, stated strongly enough not to be vacuous; the precise formulation of their residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position; and the cruxes on which settling the underlying question would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether the rule that a right may not be overridden for a large aggregate benefit is a basic moral norm or a highly reliable rule of thumb, and a second crux is whether the concept of a right adds anything over and above the concept of a strongly protected interest. For each crux, describe a concrete case or argument that both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about what that case or argument shows. Please write the entire document as continuous prose, the way one would write a jointly signed letter or a statement prepared for publication, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
