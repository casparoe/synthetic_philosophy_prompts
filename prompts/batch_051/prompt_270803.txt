Compare the account of freedom and happiness in the Grand Inquisitor episode of Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov with the argument Robert Nozick builds around the experience machine.

Dostoevsky's Inquisitor charges Christ with overestimating human beings: the Inquisitor holds that most people experience free choice as a torment they cannot long endure, and that they will knowingly hand their freedom over in exchange for happiness, security, and relief from doubt. Human flourishing, on his account, turns out to be a condition of willing submission, sustained by the miraculous, by a mystery to revere, and by an authority to which conscience can be surrendered.

Nozick's experience machine runs the other way. He imagines a life of guaranteed pleasant experience, indistinguishable from ordinary life from the inside, and claims that most people would refuse the machine, because they want to be a certain kind of person, actually to do things, and to be in contact with a deeper reality — not merely to have the experience of being, doing, and making contact. On this view, what people want from happiness cannot be detached from reality and agency.

Compare the two arguments with some precision. In particular, address these questions. Does the intuitive refusal of the experience machine, if it is to be trusted, falsify the Inquisitor's empirical claim about what people will choose — or can the Inquisitor defuse it by insisting that his subjects knowingly surrender freedom itself, whereas the machine merely conceals their surrender behind a pleasant illusion of freedom? Does Nozick's demand for contact with a deeper reality instead hand the Inquisitor an opening, since the longing for a mystery and an authority that unaided freedom cannot supply is exactly what the Inquisitor claims to satisfy — and does it matter that the Inquisitor himself regards that mystery as an instrument rather than a truth? Finally, is the real dispute here about what happiness consists in, or about what finite agents are capable of bearing? Conclude by stating which position is more seriously damaged by the other's strongest point, and why.
