I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I run a small reading group whose members have similar backgrounds. For the past several years we have been working through the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and as an exercise we built up a shared file of trait-attribution cases. In each case we were given a person's recorded behaviour, and the group had to decide whether to attribute a virtue to them, and at what level of generality. We have started treating our past verdicts as precedents rather than as one-off intuitions, because the group kept gesturing at principles after the fact without ever quite naming them.

Our track record is roughly this. We attributed honesty to A, a bookkeeper who for years returned overpayments to clients unprompted, refused to inflate expense claims, admitted a costly error to a customer who would never have noticed, and was also truthful with family and friends across a long run of lesser temptations. We declined to call B honest, although she reliably tells harmless truths in staff meetings; we said she displayed workplace tact rather than honesty, because the only evidence was low-stakes and role-bound. We said C was courageous on patrol but not a courageous person: his record of facing enemy fire was long and consistent, but every incident occurred inside a command structure and in front of an audience, and nothing was known of him outside that setting. We said D was compassionate towards patients but not a compassionate person: her warmth in her professional role over two decades was not matched by her behaviour towards subordinates or her family. We also refused to infer generosity in E, who tipped lavishly and regularly stood rounds of drinks for colleagues; because the behaviour was cheap, public, and plausibly aimed at status, we called it affable performance rather than generosity.

The new case is F. For nine years, F has anonymously donated about forty per cent of her after-tax income to global health and poverty charities recommended by independent evaluators. She does not mention it to friends, does not gain status from it, and she kept giving through two spells of unemployment when the donation required real personal sacrifice. Outside the domain of money the record is thin and mixed: she once refused to lend her car to a friend who could plausibly have managed without it, she has never volunteered, and in one emergency she froze rather than helping. There is no established pattern of stinginess, but there is also no cross-domain record of generosity.

If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, how should we rule on F? Which of our past cases is the closest analogue, and does our track record force a verdict or leave the matter underdetermined? If it is underdetermined, what are the candidate principles that would reproduce all of our earlier rulings, what would each principle require in F's case, and what further case would cleanly discriminate between them?
