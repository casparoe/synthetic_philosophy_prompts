It's weird to me that in debates about civil disobedience we draw a big moral line between breaking the actual law you're protesting and breaking some neighboring law for the same protest. Sit in at a segregated lunch counter and most people think the injustice of the law you broke nearly justifies you on its own; block a highway to protest that same segregation and suddenly you owe a whole second argument for the traffic law, the inconvenience, the method. But once you've said the normal obligation to obey is suspended by the injustice, it's not obvious why the exact statute you break should carry so much weight. I can't find a candidate principle that tracks the line. Communication doesn't, because both acts can announce the same grievance to the same audience. Proportionality doesn't, because a sit-in can inconvenience more people than a brief roadblock. Willingness to accept punishment doesn't, because you can do either openly and stay for arrest. So is the direct/indirect distinction doing real moral work, or are we just smuggling in a preference for symbolic lawbreaking at the site of the injustice? If it does work, say exactly what it tracks; if not, what should replace it in our judgments. Keep it under 400 words.
