At work the other day I ended up refereeing an argument between two of my colleagues about two alarms our predictive-maintenance system flagged last month, and I still don't know how to rule on it. One alarm was on the pasteurizer on a Tuesday—the pressure upstream spiked for about forty minutes before the line slowed—and the other was on the homogenizer two weeks earlier, same pressure-characteristic. The first one turned out to be a worn seal letting air into the system, and the second was a cracked valve plate. The debate started over whether to merge the two incidents into one anomaly class in our monitoring model.

Maya said they should be merged: at the level that matters for detection they're the same mechanism—a pressure-balance disruption that builds until the sensor trips. She pointed out that if you don't lump them, the model treats each as a singleton and never learns the early pattern.

Dan said that's confusing a signature with a mechanism. Same observable effect doesn't mean same underlying process. In one case air is entering past a worn seal; in the other a broken plate is producing the pressure change. Different components, different order of events, different timescales. He said calling them the same mechanism because they share a variable is like saying fever always has the same mechanism because body temperature rises.

Maya came back: yeah, the repair differs, but the mechanism for detection is the stable middle layer between the physical fault and the sensor; that's what gets picked up and that's what generalizes. If you individuate mechanisms by finest causal detail, every incident is its own mechanism and the model can't generalize at all. You need an intermediate grain, and at the intended grain, pressure-balance disruption is the mechanism.

Dan answered that intermediate grain is fine, but it's goal-dependent. There's no fact about "same mechanism" until you say what the mechanism is for. For forecasting a shutdown, use the pressure-level description; for prescribing maintenance, use the component-level description. The mistake is reifying one level as the real mechanism.

Maya said that's close to her view, but then it's not "no fact"—it's that mechanism talk is level-relative, and both can be true at once. A single incident instantiates a fine-grained mechanism and a coarse-grained one; the original question was just under-specified.

Dan replied that if level-relativity is all she means, the disagreement is only verbal, but the original practical question remains: should the two incidents sit in one class for this model? And that's an empirical question about whether the shared pressure signature actually predicts the failures we care about, not something you can settle by saying both descriptions are true. Maya said that's too quick, because what you count as the same mechanism is partly what fixes what's "the same prediction problem" in the first place.

Neither would budge. Please adjudicate move by move: which specific claims are right, which are wrong or overstated, and who is more right overall about whether the alarms should be merged. Also say what both of them are missing, if anything. And wherever you're making a judgment call, tell me how confident you are in it and pick the single point you're least sure of.
