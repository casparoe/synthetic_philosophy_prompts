I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophical significance of the Neolithic transition, specifically the debate over whether the shift from foraging to agriculture was an unambiguous improvement or something closer to a trap. The class has eighteen students and meets for one 75-minute session. Design a tabletop simulation, playable in that time without computing or real money, in which participants play small foraging bands facing repeated decisions about whether to adopt early cereal farming, intensify it, or remain mobile. The activity should be arranged so that students run into the core argument themselves before it is named: that farming can be locally rational at every step, since it yields more calories per unit of land, a storable surplus, and a hedge against lean seasons, while producing a collectively worse outcome by several reasonable measures, including more hours of work per calorie, poorer dietary breadth and health, an expanding population that consumes the surplus, and durable inequality, since stored grain can be defended, counted, and taken. The option of remaining a forager should feel increasingly unviable once neighboring bands have begun to farm, not because foraging stopped working but because farming bands grow and encroach. The initial foraging condition should be made tangible first, as a baseline in which needs are met at a relatively low cost in labor though with real risks, so that participants can later see what is lost.

Give me complete rules and materials: the number of rounds, what each band decides each round, how payoffs change between rounds, the exact mechanism by which population grows, land pressure bites, surplus appears, and inequality emerges, and how refusal is punished not by the facilitator but by the game's dynamics. Then give a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the parts of the idea: the low-labor foraging baseline, the per-acre productivity increase versus the per-calorie labor increase, the ratchet that makes a collective return to foraging difficult, the rise of appropriable surplus and hierarchy, and the question whether the transition was a mistake decomposed into for whom, by what standard, and compared to what counterfactual. Do not front-load the lecture; the name of the thesis should appear only in the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, such as groups treating farming as obviously correct out of modern progress assumptions, the trap failing to emerge because the payoffs are too lopsided, or the inequality mechanism being ignored, and how to adjust for each. Be explicit about which features of the real historical debate the simulation cannot reproduce and that I should supply afterward.
