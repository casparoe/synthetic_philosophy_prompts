In the classic surprise exam paradox, a teacher announces on Friday that there will be a surprise exam next week, and the students reason backward from the last possible day to the first and conclude the announcement is self-defeating. I've been turning over a variant. Suppose the teacher doesn't leave surprise vague: she writes Monday through Friday on five slips of paper, says she'll draw the exam day uniformly at random the night before, and this lottery procedure is common knowledge between her and the students. She also defines surprise precisely: at 8am on the day of the exam, before it happens, the students' rational posterior probability that the exam is that day — conditional on it not having happened yet and on the uniform lottery — must be no higher than 1/2. The students are Bayesian and trust the lottery, but they don't treat the surprise clause itself as guaranteed. Does the backward induction still eliminate all five days? If not, which days actually meet the 1/2 condition, and what's left of the original paradox? Also, at what threshold, if any, does the old 'no day can be a surprise' conclusion come back — only at certainty, or somewhere lower, and why?
