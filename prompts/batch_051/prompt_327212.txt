Okay so I've been going back and forth about Dutch book arguments for ages, and I want someone to diagnose the argument itself rather than pick a side. Here's the thing: the basic move is old news — if your credences violate the probability axioms, there's a package of bets, each of which looks fair to you by your own lights, but which together guarantee you lose money no matter what. One camp in my group treats this as basically the proof that incoherent credences are irrational, full stop; the other camp says its a parlour trick, because all it shows is that a weirdly specific agent — one who is in a betting mood, faces a bookie who knows their mind, and can't help but buy every ticket in the package — has a practical vulnerability, not an epistemic defect. The first camp fires back that the bookie and the money are just decoration: once you strip them away, what's left is a proof that the incoherent agent has beliefs that are jointly unsatisfiable in a betting-adjacent sense, and that's the real problem. And the second camp says that stripped-down version either begs the question or stops being a Dutch book argument at all. Everyone understands the math; nobody has moved in months. My working theory is that they're answering different questions — one about what sure loss exposes, the other about what makes incoherence a failure of reasoning — and the word 'irrational' is doing double duty. But I could be wrong; maybe there's a real disagreement about whether avoiding sure loss is a primitive norm or a derived one, or about whether a rational agent can just refuse the package. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal, substantive, or two groups doing two jobs under one name?
