You are a moral and political philosopher who works on the ethics of labor, exploitation, and global justice, and who has spent a good deal of time interrogating competing positions in the literature rather than taking sides too quickly. I run a reading group in which two members have been arguing for over a year about the ethics of sweatshop labor, and we have finally agreed that instead of continuing to talk past each other they should produce an adversarial-collaboration document. Please write the document they should have produced.

One member — call her the Mutual-Benefit theorist — holds roughly the following position. When an employer offers a desperately poor person work that is harsh and low-paid by rich-country standards but better than the worker's next-best realistic alternative, and the worker freely accepts it, the arrangement is not wrongfully exploitative merely in virtue of the low pay or the harsh conditions. It is, most plausibly, a mutually beneficial exchange; and campaigns to boycott, ban, or shame such employers out of existence are likely to harm the very people they are meant to help by removing the best of a set of bad options. The other member — call him the Wrongful-Exploitation theorist — holds roughly this position. A transaction can be simultaneously mutually beneficial in the economist's sense and exploitative in a wrong-making sense. What matters is not only whether the worker gains relative to a baseline of pre-existing misery, but whether the terms of the exchange take advantage of the worker's vulnerability in order to capture an unfair share of the gains from that exchange. On this view, background injustice and unequal bargaining power can render a voluntary, net-beneficial agreement wrongful, and can ground at least some forms of third-party intervention even when the worker consents.

The document should include each of the following components. (1) A numbered list of claims about sweatshop labor that both sides can sign. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and should include both factual claims — for example, about why workers accept such jobs, what the alternatives are, and what tends to happen when factories close — and conceptual claims about what exploitation minimally involves. (2) A disagreements table with three columns: the issue; the Mutual-Benefit position; and the Wrongful-Exploitation position. Each side's position should be stated in wording that a careful proponent of that side would accept as precise and fair. (3) A list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling at least some of them would move at least one side. I suspect one crux concerns the correct baseline against which to judge whether a worker benefits, and another concerns how much moral work consent can do when it is given under unjust background conditions; the group has also circled a third crux about whether a transaction can be wrongful exploitation at all if both parties are better off than they otherwise would have been. (4) For each crux, one concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. One candidate the group has discussed: a hiker who comes upon a stranded and dehydrated person and charges an extortionate but still life-saving price for water. Both members should state, in advance, what they predict the correct moral assessment of that case is, and whether they predict it is continuous with or disanalogous to the typical sweatshop employment they disagree about.

Since the two of them genuinely want a document they can both put their names to, please separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Before you draft anything, spend some time brainstorming the strongest versions of each position, notice where the two members are probably talking past each other — for instance, over the word "exploitation," "freely," or "benefit" — and then write the statement in their voices, with the residual disagreement made exact.
