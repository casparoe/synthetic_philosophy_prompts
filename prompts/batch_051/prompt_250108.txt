I'm in a markets and morals class and we've been on the anti-commodification stuff — the idea that some exchanges are wrong not because they're coercive or unfair but because they corrupt the meaning of the thing being exchanged, like votes or kidneys. I tried to write out the corruption argument in premise form and now it looks like it proves way too much, and I can't figure out where it goes wrong.

(1) If the proper valuation of a good is partly non-market — the right way to value it involves attitudes like love, trust, civic respect, or bodily integrity, not merely a price — then exchanging that good for money expresses the wrong attitude toward it and thereby corrupts (or at least degrades) its proper valuation.
(2) Almost every good we actually trade has some non-market dimension to its proper valuation: childcare involves love and trust, therapy involves emotional attunement, military service involves sacrifice and honor, blood donation involves a kind of bodily giving, ordinary labor involves craftsmanship and dignity.
(3) So by (1), markets in childcare, therapy, military service, blood, and ordinary labor all corrupt the goods being exchanged.
(4) But it would be crazy to say all of those markets corrupt their goods in the way that would justify blocking them. Some might be morally messy, but not corrupt in the vote-selling or kidney-selling sense.
(5) Therefore (1) is false, and the corruption argument against kidney sales and vote selling collapses with it.

I want to keep (1) in some form because I really do think selling votes is different from selling childcare. But I can't see what principle seperates the two cases without just appealing to "we're used to one and not the other." Is the problem in (1), or in the move from (1) to (3), or is (4) what I should give up? If I have to choose, I'd rather give up (4) than (1), but I want to know what that costs.
