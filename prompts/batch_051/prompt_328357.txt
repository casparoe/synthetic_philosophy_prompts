i run a small podcast with a mostly rationalist/EA-leaning audience, and i've been trying not to let guests just run their best hits. next week's guest is a political philosopher who defends the position that sweatshop labor in poor countries is usually a case of mutually beneficial exploitation: the jobs are often awful by rich-country standards and the background poverty that pushes people into them is unjust, but taking the job is still usually better than any feasible alternative, so the transaction itself is permissible and even good, while most outsider responses (boycotts, shaming brands, labor standards that price the jobs out) are feel-good mistakes that leave the workers worse off. his stock reply is that critics are confusing exploitative with wrong, or confusing a duty to fix background injustice with a reason not to transact.

i want eight to ten interview questions that make him do real work. two requirements: (1) at least one question each on: how confident we can be from outside that the job is really better, given that revealed preference ('they took the job') may not carry much weight when the alternative is bad enough; the move where he says a consensual, mutually beneficial exchange cant be made morally worse than not engaging at all — press whether the same move would license buying a kidney from a desperate person or lending at 400% to the unbanked, and whether anything at all counts as wronging someone while benefiting them; and how his view separates what's permissible for a consumer from what's permissible for a multinational employer from what's required of a government. (2) for each question, include the follow-up keyed to the answer you expect him to give, so if he runs the stock deflection the next question pins down what would have to be true for the deflection to actually work.

then add three prep sections: a steelman of the strongest version of his position, a section on the sharpest criticism he'll have the hardest time answering, and a section on what evidence or conceptual moves would let him actually update. format it like this: Warm-up questions (3 questions, roughly 60 words each), Core pressure questions (5 questions plus follow-ups, around 90 words each), The steelman (~150 words), The knife (~150 words), What would move me (~120 words).
