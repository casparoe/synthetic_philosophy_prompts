I'm supposed to give an introductory presentation to a philosophy of physics reading group on the question of whether the wavefunction is real, and I'm trying to summarize the ψ-ontic versus ψ-epistemic debate for people who have seen some quantum mechanics but haven't followed the interpretation literature. A magazine explainer I found frames the debate like this:

'Physicists disagree about whether the wavefunction is something real in the world or just a compact way of recording what an observer happens to know about a system. The ψ-ontic camp says the wavefunction is a concrete physical ingredient of reality: if a complete description assigns a system a wavefunction, then that wavefunction is part of the furniture of the universe. The ψ-epistemic camp says the wavefunction is more like a weather forecast, a summary of information, so two observers with different information could assign different wavefunctions to one and the same underlying state. People used to think this was pure philosophy, but a recent no-go theorem purportedly proves the epistemic reading wrong: if the wavefunction were merely information, then distinct quantum states could overlap in the underlying physical states that realize them, and the theorem shows — without any special assumptions — that they cannot. So the wavefunction is real, and the only question left is how to interpret the wavefunction as a physical field.'

Before I build my presentation around this, I'd like an accuracy audit. Please go through it sentence by sentence and separate what a foundations-minded physicist or philosopher would accept from what they'd wince at. I'm especially unsure about three things: what the no-go result actually assumes and rules out, and whether 'no special assumptions' is defensible; whether the ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic distinction is exhaustive in the way the explainer implies; and whether the final inference to 'the wavefunction is real' — let alone to a physical field — survives once the earlier claims are corrected.
