I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on why people resent free riding — the person who enjoys the benefits of a public good, a cooperative practice, or other people's restraint while declining to do his part — even in cases where no identifiable person is made worse off. Before I interview political philosophers and behavioral economists, I want to rehearse my questioning against a sharp advocate of a reciprocity-based view, so that I do not go into those conversations carrying a strawman in my head.

Please play an unnamed political philosopher who is a committed defender of roughly the following position. The wrong of free riding is not reducible to the harm it causes, and it is not merely a technical violation of fair play. It is a distinctive defect in the agent's relation to others and to himself: the free rider takes a benefit on terms he could not will as a shared practice, and in doing so he covertly depends on others' cooperation while claiming an exemption he would not grant to them, or he enjoys a good whose value depends on other people's doing what he refuses to do. The character is analytically careful and willing to concede a great deal: some forms of taking without contributing — gifts, childhood, genuine incapacity, or the use of public goods by people who contribute in other ways — are either not free riding or not objectionable. He is also willing to concede that in many cases the free rider's conduct is modest in magnitude. But he will not concede that small-scale, no-harm-no-foul free riding is morally insignificant, and he will not let the view collapse into either act-utilitarianism or a bare ledger of owed fees. Please stay in character, speak in the first person, keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, and do not break character until I say "stop."

My opening move, which I may later put to a real source, is this: consider a person who declines vaccination but is protected by herd immunity, or who never votes but benefits from the electorate that does. Assume, for the sake of argument, that this person's abstention changes no outcome and leaves no one materially worse off, and that he knows the practice would collapse if everyone copied him but also knows that everyone will not. You say he is doing something wrong. What precisely is it, and to whom is it done?
