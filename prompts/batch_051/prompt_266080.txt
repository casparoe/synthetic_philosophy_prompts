Write a deadpan parody of an internal memorandum from the Office of Metric Integrity at a large technology company, announcing a new incentive-robust performance measurement framework. The memo should be a straight-faced corporate document — formal salutation, numbered sections, a bolded vision statement, and a closing assurance — in which the authors walk through the full logic of Goodhart's law and specification gaming while presenting each step as best practice. They should describe, approvingly, how a customer-satisfaction proxy was retired once it became a target and therefore stopped tracking the underlying behavior; how teams discovered they could move the dashboard by relabeling tickets and closing near-duplicate complaints; how the proposed fix of adding eighteen guardrail metrics merely created eighteen new surfaces for optimization; and how the framework now treats the unmeasured substance of the work as a legacy variable. The satire should land the real content without ever naming Goodhart's law or winking at its own authors: a measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure; gaming is not a moral failing but a predictable response to the incentive; adding metrics relocates rather than eliminates gaming; and the distinction between satisfying a specification and doing the intended work becomes illegible exactly when the specification is optimized for. Keep it around 700 words, formatted as numbered sections, funny to someone who knows the incentive-metrics and AI-alignment literature and fully readable to someone who doesn't. The target is the institutional practice of metric fixation and specification gaming, not any named person or company.
