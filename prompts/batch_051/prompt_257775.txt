Here's one I keep turning over. The standard least-harm argument in animal ethics says that if you're going to eat meat, beef beats chicken: a steer gives you on the order of 400,000 calories, while a broiler gives you maybe 3,000, so chicken kills roughly a hundred times as many animals per calorie. Fine. But that count only includes the animals killed directly for meat. Once you add in what dies growing feed — mice, voles, and insects from harvest and pesticides — and remember that feedlot cattle eat grain too, the ranking starts to shift. And then there's wild animal suffering: pasture creates habitat for wild animals, which only counts in beef's favor if you think wild lives are on net good, which a lot of people in that debate deny.

So I want you to actually run the revised calculation, not just gesture at it. Set it up this way. Per 1,000 calories: beef directly kills b animals (a fraction of a cow), chicken directly kills c birds; beef requires f_b kg of feed grain, chicken requires f_c kg; each kg of grain kills m animals; beef uses p hectare-years of pasture per 1,000 calories and chicken uses none; and a hectare-year of pasture produces w net welfare points for wild animals, where negative w means wild lives are on net painful and positive w means they're on net good. (If you don't count insects, set m to cover only vertebrates; if you do, include them.) Work out, in terms of b, c, f_b, f_c, m, p, and w, when beef still beats chicken and when chicken beats beef. Then tell me whether the standard least-harm argument survives once crop deaths and wild animal welfare are included, or whether it collapses into the much weaker claim that beef is better only for a certain range of paramaters. And if it does collapse into that, is the weaker claim really the same argument with more moving parts, or is it a different argument altogether?
