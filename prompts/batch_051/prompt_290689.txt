Take Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" and the passage I'm pasting below. I want you to build me a thought experiment where the standard no-trade theorem says a market should stay silent — no trade happens, because any offer would leak enough information to make the other side back out — but Hayek's epistemic picture of markets says the market can still do its job and get some trade going. If that direction won't work for you, you can flip it: no-trade theorem says trade is possible, Hayek's picture says it won't happen. Just say which one you did.

Contraints: ordinary-life setting (used-car lot, hiring decision, small business buying a machine, something like that), no perfect predictors or omniscient planners, agents can be shrewd but not superhuman, no irrationality or different priors as the source of the disagreement. The disagreement has to come out of the passage — meaning the relevant knowledge really is not given to any single mind in concentrated form, and the only ways it gets communicated are whatever actions or institutions the scenario actually supplies. Don't just stipulate the verdicts; make them fall out of the set-up.

After the scenario, walk through what the no-trade theorem says each party should do and why, what Hayek's picture says they can do and why, which feature of the case is doing the work, and how a defender of the losing side would try to resist your case. End by saying whether removing that single feature collapses the disagreement back into agreement.

Use American English.

Here's the passage:

"The various ways in which the knowledge on which people base their plans is communicated to them is the crucial problem for any theory explaining the economic process, and the problem of what is the best way of utilizing knowledge initially dispersed among all the people is at least one of the main problems of economic policy—or of designing an efficient economic system."
