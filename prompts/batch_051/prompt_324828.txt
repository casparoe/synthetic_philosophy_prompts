I am a software engineer, and my exposure to philosophy is mostly confined to what I read outside work, so I want to check whether I have stated a once-heard objection correctly. I have been reading about Daniel Dennett's method of heterophenomenology for the scientific study of consciousness. As I understand it, the method says we should collect subjects' first-person descriptions of their experiences, transcribe them, and treat the resulting text as the primary data. We then try to explain the production of that text while remaining neutral about whether the text truthfully describes a private inner world to which only the subject has access: the report is evidence of what the subject believes or claims to experience, not an authoritative description of what the experience is.

That methodological caution seems obviously right to me. But a colleague who works on scientific instrumentation raised an objection I have not been able to get out of my head. Reconstructed, it runs as follows.

(1) Heterophenomenology requires that a subject's report of an experience be treated as a text: something to be explained, but not accepted as a true report of private facts.
(2) The rationale is that no one else can check such a report against the private experience it purports to describe; the experience is not publicly accessible, so the report cannot be verified or falsified from outside.
(3) But every observational report made by a scientist or engineer is, in the same respect, a report of that observer's private perceptual experience. When someone writes down that the oscilloscope reads 5 V, or that a scan shows a lesion, what has been recorded is something the observer saw; nobody can enter the observer's head to check the seeing itself.
(4) Therefore, if (2) is the rationale for bracketing first-person reports, the same rationale applies to every observational report whatsoever: all such reports should be treated as texts whose truth is bracketed.
(5) A science that brackets the truth of every observational report cannot use any observation to constrain its theories; it degenerates into the study of its own paperwork.
(6) That consequence is untenable, so the bracketing rationale in (2) — and with it the method stated in (1) — must be rejected.

I would rather keep (1) than accept (6), because abandoning the method seems to leave the science of consciousness with no honest way to treat first-person reports at all. But I cannot see how to keep (1) while resisting the slide to (4). Which step should be rejected instead, and what does rejecting it cost?
