I am a novelist, and I serve as the lay member of the Deployment Review Board of a technology nonprofit that builds language-model assistants for public-sector services. We meet on Thursday to decide whether to approve Lumen, a system that drafts responses to people seeking help with housing-benefit appeals. The service it would join is overwhelmed: applicants often wait months for a human reply, and during those months some lose their housing. Lumen's average-case performance is strong. In a controlled evaluation on twenty thousand past inquiries, caseworkers rated its draft replies helpful and legally sound about ninety-two percent of the time, a rate comparable to junior staff. But the lab's red-team and adversarial testing found a stubborn tail: on roughly 0.3 percent of adversarial prompts, Lumen confidently invents statutes and case numbers. In a human-review simulation, reviewers caught these fabrications only about two-thirds of the time, and some test users acted on the invented law to their detriment. The lab proposes approval with human review of a random twenty percent of Lumen's output, arguing that the expected effect is a large reduction in harm compared with the status quo and that no functioning system can offer a guarantee against every possible failure. Several board members oppose deployment altogether: they argue that a system known to produce harmful legal confidences in a foreseeable set of cases should not be used even if its average benefit is high, because it guarantees that some identifiable people will be wronged. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, treat the losing side's strongest consideration seriously, and reach a decision the rest of the board can actually act on.
