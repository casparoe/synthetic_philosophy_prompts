Two people in my board game group got into an argument the other night, and I can't get it out of my head. It started because Kat mentioned she'd rather give to a fund that pays for one stray dog's emergency surgery than to a project that would spare something like forty thousand mice a few minutes of sharp pain after they get caught in a pest-control trap. She said she didn't even think it was close. Ben said that can't be right if you actually believe awareness comes in degrees. His line: if a dog is maybe a few hundred times more awake-to-the-world than a mouse, and the mouse's pain is maybe a tenth as bad and lasts a tenth as long, then each mouse's pain is a tiny sliver — but forty thousand slivers is not a sliver. If you refuse to ever let the multiplication tip the scale, then "more conscious" and "lasts longer" stop meaning anything when you're actually deciding what to do. Kat said no, multiplication only works inside a single stream of experience: you can't staple forty thousand dim points of view together into one large stabbing; a thousand cups of lukewarm water dont make one burn. Ben said that argument proves too much, because then forty thousand people with moderate injuries would never justify more urgency than one person with a horrible injury, and we obviously do add across separate people all the time. Kat said that's fine — addition across separate people is legitimate once each one is a full person; the problem is that mice sit so far down the gradient of awareness that a huge stack of almost-nothings doesn't add up to a something. Ben asked where exactly on the gradient between a mouse and a dog she draws the line. She said she doesn't have to draw a sharp line, because "bald" doesn't need a hair count and it's still a real distinction. Ben said the line sure matters if she's going to let the animals on the low side count for literally zero no matter how many of them there are, and that's a stronger claim than she's admitting. They called it a night there, friendly-ish, and I honestly could not tell who had the better of it.

Please referee this move by move: what each person gets right and wrong at each turn, which specific claims hold up, and who is more right overall. I'm less interested in the dog-versus-mice conclusion itself and more in the structural question of whether capacity for awareness, intensity, duration, and number combine by multiplication, or whether there's a floor below which they count for zero.
