Ok, I've been going back and forth with a friend about Carroll's tortoise, and I want to settle something. Let's define what it is to genuinly go along with a modus ponens argument. Say a person counts as following the argument from 'P' and 'if P then Q' to 'Q' iff they satisfy all of:

(a) they believe the premises;
(b) they see that the premises are meant to support the conclusion;
(c) they accept the inference rule in play (modus ponens);
(d) they have no independent defeater for Q—nothing else they believe counts against it.

Now consider the tortoise, made concrete. The argument is: 'If it's raining, the game is cancelled; it's raining; so the game is cancelled.' The tortoise accepts both premises. It also grants, quite cheerfully, that if those two premises are true then the conclusion is true—and it's happy to write that conditional down as a third premise. But it won't move to the conclusion until you do. And once you've written it down, it asks for a fourth premise: that if the first three are true, then the conclusion follows. Then a fifth, and so on. It isn't being obtuse about the facts; it just keeps treating each 'rule' you offer as another premise.

Does the tortoise satisfy the criteria? Go through them one at a time, and where the verdict is murky, say so instead of forcing a clean yes or no. For any criterion it fails (or might fail), say whether thats the tortoise's problem or the criterion's. Finally: would you amend the criteria? I'm especially curious whether we need something like 'the person treats the rule as a licence to move, not as an extra premise,' or whether putting it that way just begs the question against the tortoise.
