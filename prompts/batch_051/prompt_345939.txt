I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and Moore's paradox has been eating my brain for a piece I'm working on. You know the shape of it: the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining" feels impossible to say sincerely even though it isn't logically contradictory—people sit in rain all the time not believing it's raining. I've written out my gut reactions to a pile of variants, and I can't reverse-engineer the priciple. I'm hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, generates my verdicts, and then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

For each one, imagine I say it to you, sincerely, right now:

1. "It's raining, but I don't believe it's raining." — absurd. I can't get the words out without it landing as a joke or a misfire.
2. "It's raining, but you don't believe it's raining." — perfectly fine.
3. "It's raining, but I didn't believe it was raining until five minutes ago." — perfectly fine.
4. "I believe it's raining, but it isn't raining." — just as absurd as 1.
5. "I believed it was raining, but it wasn't." — perfectly fine.
6. "It's raining, but I'm not sure it's raining." — fine, though it's the most nervous of the fine ones; I'd say it if my evidence was strong but short of certain.
7. "It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining." — uneasy, mildly wrong, but not the crashing absurdity of 1.
8. "It's raining, but I wouldn't bet on it." — fine.
9. "It's raining, but I don't believe the sentence 'it's raining' is true." — absurd in the same way as 1.

So the badness isn't simply first-person-against-third-person, because 6, 7, and 8 are first person and much less bad than 1 and 9. And 4 shows the badness can sit on the second half of the conjunction. I need to know what's really doing the work. Is the rule about what an assertion of the first clause expresses? About what "I don't believe" commits me to? Something about an utterance undercutting itself? Here's the bit I keep goig back and forth on: what would the principle say about "It's raining, but I don't fully believe it's raining" and "It's raining, but I have no evidence that it's raining"? Give me the sharpest version of the principle you can, and then tell me which of my nine reactions survives it.
