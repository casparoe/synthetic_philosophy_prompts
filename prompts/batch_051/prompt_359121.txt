ok so I've been collecting my gut reactions about when mathematics actually explains something in nature rather than just describing it, and I want to know if there's a principle that gets all of them, or close, and which ones I'd have to give up.

Here are the cases:

- Cicadas with 13- and 17-year life cycles. The usual story is that a prime period makes it hard for predators with shorter cycles to sync up with them. I want to say the fact that the period is prime — not just the specific number but the primeness — is doing real explanatory work in the biology.
- Honeycomb cells are hexagonal. I think the fact that a hexagonal tiling minimises material for a given area really does explain why bees build that way, given the functional/evolutionary story.
- A pendulum's period scales as the square root of length over gravity. I feel like the maths is describing a regularity rather than explaining it, but I can't say why this is different from the honeycomb case.
- A shuffled deck is almost always disordered because the number of disorderly arrangements is vastly bigger than the number of orderly ones. That feels like a combinatorial fact doing real explanatory work, but it also seems importantly different from the cicada case because nothing in nature is using the combinatorics.
- A queue took forever because there were seven people in it. I don't think the seven explains the wait, even though 7 is prime. It's just a count.
- Dropped toast tends to land butter-side down, and the story involves the height of the table and the rate the toast rotates. Maths shows up in the details, but I wouldn't say a mathematical object is doing the explaining.
- A bridge stays up because the forces balance, which is Newton's laws plus calculus. Here the mathematics feels constitutive of the physical story, not something added to it, though I dont know if 'constitutive' is just 'explaining' under another name.

Is there a single account of mathematical explanation that gets all or most of these verdicts? Which ones does it force me to drop? And what would it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: sunflower seed spirals showing Fibonacci numbers, with the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers approaching the golden ratio — is that mathematics explaining the sunflower, or just us spotting a pattern? Also, does any of this tell us whether the old indispensability argument is right that mathematics being indispensable to science gives us reason to believe in mathematical objects, or do explanation and ontology come apart here?
