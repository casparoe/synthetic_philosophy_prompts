Here's something that's been bugging me. Wikipedia, which is supposed to be this giant collaborative project for organizing knowledge, has a rule that you basically can't build an article from primary sources, even when you've read them yourself. If you actually sit down with Thucydides and notice something in his text that the secondary literature hasn't said, and you write it up, it gets reverted as original research. The allowed unit of evidence for a claim about ancient history isn't the ancient text itself but what some published scholar has said about it. Meanwhile the same project is pretty happy to use primary-source reporting for current events. So what view about how we know the distant past does that policy presuppose? Does it presuppose that historical knowledge is essentially social or testimonial—that an individual's direct contact with an ancient source isn't a way of knowing, no matter how carefull they are? What rival views does it quietly rule out, and is the current-events exception consistent with the policy's general picture? Then tell me what would actually have to change in how Wikipedia handles ancient history if we thought a careful, methodical reader could know the past directly from the sources.
