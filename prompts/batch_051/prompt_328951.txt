So i spent most of last night at the pub going round and round about rights with a mate, and I'm less sure what we were actually arguing about than when we started. She's broadly consequentialist and kept saying that 'rights' just pick out things that normally do the most good: we respect bodily integrity because respecting it nearly always works out better, not because there's some further moral fact in play. I pushed back with the usual cases — the transplant surgeon, the ticking-bomb torture scenario, the one where you can't shove a large person off a footbridge to stop a trolley killing five — and said that the whole point of calling something a right is that it isn't just another interest to be traded off. She came back with the three-way split: either a right trumps welfare, or it acts as a side constraint that rules certain actions out whatever the good they'd do, or it's just an instrument, a useful rule of thumb or commitment device for keeping deliberation and institutions honest — and on all three readings, she said, the word 'right' does no real work once you've built the underlying theory. I don't think that's right, but i couldn't articulate what the disagreement actually is.

What I'd like is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. The trumps / side-constraints / instruments split has been knocking around for decades without much movement, and my hunch is that the impasse itself is the interesting part. Is it a verbal dispute about whether to use the word 'rights'? Is it a substantive dispute about which trade-offs are permissible — say, torturing one to stop a bomb, or killing one to save five? Is it a disagreement about what shape a moral theory has to have, for instance whether side constraints can be motivated from inside a broadly consequentialist view or only from outside it? Or are the sides answering different questions under one heading, one giving an account of what rights are and another of when rights-talk is useful? And maybe the deeper hidden issue is just whether we need the concept of a right at all, with everyone quietly smuggling their answer to that into the other questions. Given whatever diagnosis you end up defending, tell me what would actually count as progress, and whether you think the thing is settleable at all as opposed to a standoff that no argument will move. Also be honest about your own reasoning: which argument or case would change your diagnosis, and where is it most likely to fail? And write it in British English, please.
