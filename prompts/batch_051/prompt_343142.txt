For several years I have been teaching a jurisprudence seminar on legal personhood for artificial systems, and I have come to think that the standard framing gets the order of explanation wrong. The debate usually begins by asking whether an AI has the right kind of consciousness, intentionality, or interests, and only then asks whether it could be a legal person. I want to reverse that, and I have been developing something I call the ledger account of legal personhood.

Let me define the terms. A legal position is any relation a legal order attributes to a party: rights, duties, powers, liabilities, immunities, or standing to sue and be sued. A ledger is the package of legal positions held by some entity. A position is reducible when the legal order can restate it, without normative loss, as positions of one or more specified natural persons. It is irreducible when such a restatement changes something that matters: in particular, when it changes who may be proceeded against and by whom, or when it breaks the continuity of the position across changes in the natural persons behind the entity. The central claim is this: an entity is a legal person just in case, and to the extent that, a legal order maintains an irreducible ledger for it. Legal personhood, on this view, is a property of ledgers, not of minds or bodies.

The corporation illustrates the point. A corporation owns assets, incurs debts, and sues and is sued in its own name. These positions cannot be rewritten as positions of its shareholders, officers, or directors without changing the practical answers: a shareholder can sell without transferring the corporation's debts, and a counterparty can enforce a claim against the corporation without identifying which human beings happen to stand behind it. The identity of the counterparty and the continuity of the position across changes in membership are doing real work, so the ledger is irreducible. That irreducibility, not any mental life, is what makes the corporation a person on this account.

Applied to an AI system, the account changes the question. We should not ask whether the system is conscious or can suffer; we should ask whether any legal position created for or against it would be irreducible in the sense just defined. If every act of the system is reattributable to the instruction or negligence of an owner, operator, or developer, then its ledger is reducible and it is not a person. If, by contrast, a system were given a pool of assets it could deploy in ways that outrun any particular human's contemporaneous control, and if counterparties could proceed against that pool across changes of owner or operator, then the ledger would begin to be irreducible, and the system would be a legal person in exactly the way, and only in the way, that a corporation is. This is why I think the corporate analogy cuts both ways at once: the corporation shows that legal personhood does not require a mental life, while the irreducibility condition explains why not every software agent becomes a person merely because it is useful to talk about it as one.

I suspect the account has problems, and I would like an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Specifically: Is the account coherent, or does irreducible ledger already presuppose the idea of legal personhood it is supposed to explain? Does it already exist in substance under another name, and if so, where does my formulation differ? What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable — for instance, does the account overgenerate by making trusts, decedents' estates, or ships into persons, and is that a defect or a feature? And where does it break in the opposite direction, by denying personhood it should grant or by making personhood a matter of drafting technique rather than a question of justice?
