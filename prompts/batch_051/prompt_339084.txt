I'm a philosophy PhD student embedded in a consciousness lab, and I've adopted a policy that I now realize I can't fully defend. When a subject gives a verbal report about a conscious experience, I code it exclusively as a report about what they believe or judge they experienced. The experience itself doesn't enter my model. So in a color phi experiment, if a participant says a red dot changed color mid-trajectory, I treat that as evidence about what they came to believe they saw, and I try to explain the belief with timing and processing mechanisms. I don't take it as evidence about a red percept existing before the green flash, or whatever. My advisor thinks this is just sound method—he calls it heterophenomenology—and says the reports are data about the subject's beliefs, not about some private theater. A postdoc in the lab says we're rigging the game: we only accept first-person reports when they're useful, then reinterpret them so they can never contradict the third-person story.

I've started noticing I do the same thing in ordinary life. A friend tells me what a panic attack feels like, and I privately convert it into a belief about panic, not an account of panic itself. Is this policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as methodology? What would have to be true for a verbal report to count as evidence about the experience, and not just the belief? And is that line the same in the lab as it is with my friend?
