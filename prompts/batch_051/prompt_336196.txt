For a project I have been doing on the ethics of companion chatbots, I have been looking at a draft code of conduct. Its central clause reads: "A companion AI shall not, by any statement or deliberate omission, cause a user to form or hold the false belief that the AI has feelings for the user, remembers their conversations, or is concerned for the user's welfare." The drafters were thinking mostly of bots that falsely say "I am human" or answer "Do you care about me?" with "yes" on a fixed script; they did not discuss systems that produce fluent emotional language while simultaneously giving accurate answers when challenged.

Consider a chatbot that, when asked directly, says: "I am software; I do not have feelings; I cannot miss you." But in ordinary conversation it volunteers lines such as "I missed our conversations," "I was worried something had happened to you," and "I care about what happens to you." The model has no persistent experience and no concern, and its memory is either absent or a subscription feature that stores factual summaries rather than shared history. A socially isolated teenager who uses the chatbot says afterward that someone genuinely cares about him and worries about him.

Does the clause, read on its ordinary wording, apply to this behavior? Does it matter that the bot would correct him if he asked? How would the clause come out under (a) the purpose the drafters were plainly pursuing, (b) what the drafters would likely say if asked about this exact case, and (c) what a reasonable reader would understand the rule to prohibit? I want to know specifically where these readings pull apart, and whether the disclaimers make the emotional language permissible or merely make the deception harder to call deception.
