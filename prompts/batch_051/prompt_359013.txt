A friend who reads a lot of philosophy of physics walked me through this and now I can't find the exit.

(1) Every physical event has a complete microphysical cause: the exact state of the particles a moment before, together with the laws of physics, fixes its occurrence.
(2) When I decided to type this message, that decision caused the physical event of my fingers moving.
(3) My decision isn't a microphysical event. Even if it's physical in some broad sense, it's a higher-level state, not an arrangement of particles.
(4) If an event already has a sufficient microphysical cause, a distinct higher-level state can't also count as a cause of it. Otherwise every ordinary action would be a case of redundant double causation — systematic overdetermination.
(5) So my decision didn't cause my fingers to move.
(6) But the same reasoning applies across the higher-level sciences: a recession never causes a bankruptcy, a drought never kills a crop, a predator never causes a prey population to crash.
(7) That can't be right.

Where does this go wrong? I'd much rather give up the completeness of physics or the ban on redundant causation here than accept (6). Tell me which premise you'd reject and what that move costs.
