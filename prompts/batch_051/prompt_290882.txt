I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and it's honestly embarassing how often a smart person I'm interviewing will tell me "well, that's not really intelligence" the moment the result is inconvenient. I want to do a long piece that treats the definition itself as the subject: when we ask whether a language model, an octopus, or an ant colony is intelligent, what are we actually asking?

So before I write any of it, give me the spec. Write the desiderata: the list of requirements that any adequate theory or definition of intelligence has to satisfy to count as serious, not just a definition of convenience. Give me the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (performance on a benchmark vs the underlying capacity, intelligence vs consciousness, individual vs collective), and the empirical results it can't just wave away. With each requirement, say what case or consideration makes it a demand rather than a prejudice.

The cases I keep chewing on: a chess engine that is superhuman at the one task it does and useless at everything else; a slime mold that finds a near-shortest path through a maze with no nervous system; an ant colony that farms fungus and regulates its nest temperature even though no individual ant has the concept of any of it; a language model that passes the bar exam but repeats the same mistake right after it's corrected; a two-year-old who learns a new word from a single overhearing but can't do long division; a person with advanced dementia who used to be a research mathematician; an octopus that opens a childproof jar the first time it sees one.

For each requirement, tell me whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp (my guesses for camp preferences: that intelligence is individual, that it has to be general-purpose, that it has to involve consciousness or a nervous system, that it has to show up in behavior). Then, briefly, run the standard definitions against the list — the flexible-problem-solving definition, the achieves-goals-across-environments definition, the g-factor definition, and any other you think earns a spot — and tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding them. A table for the case–requirement pairs is fine.

And flag any subsets of the requirements that can't be jointly satisfied. I suspect the collective-intelligence cases and the single-mind cases pull the list in two directions, but I'd like to know if that suspicion is actually a theorem about the area or just my own preference worth adressing.
