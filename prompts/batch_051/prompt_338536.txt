I'm a journalist who covers decision theory and the philosophy of mind, and I run a monthly ideas workshop for readers who enjoy formal puzzles. I'm planning a 75-minute session for about 16 adults with no formal training in game theory, and I want a structured activity built around the absent-minded driver problem. The setup is this: a driver on a two-exit highway cannot tell the two exits apart; exiting at the first exit pays 0, exiting at the second exit pays 4, and continuing past both exits pays 1; before the trip he must commit to a single probability of exiting at whichever exit he is currently at. The point I want the participants to feel, before it is named, is the pull between the plan that seems best before the trip and the choice that seems best once an exit is in front of them, because their beliefs about which exit they have reached depend on the very strategy they are evaluating. Design a runnable game with roles, dice or cards, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money: participants should commit to a plan in advance, then be placed at an unrevealed exit and forced to decide whether to stick with it or deviate. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, the rules and materials, a debrief that maps what happened onto ex ante planning versus acting under imperfect recall, and an honest note on which feature of the real problem a staged game cannot reproduce. Also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I run it and how I should recover.
