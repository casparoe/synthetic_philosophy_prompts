I recently joined a working group in my city's transportation department that is evaluating an algorithmic system for allocating road maintenance and traffic-calming requests. Whenever someone raises the worry that the system might systematically favor wealthier neighborhoods, several of my colleagues return to a familiar line: a tool carries no values, only its users do, so our job is to build the thing competently and regulate its uses, and any claim that the system itself is biased or political rests on a category mistake. I have since been reading the older debate over whether artifacts themselves can have politics, and I keep coming back to the contested example of the Long Island parkway overpasses that were supposedly built low enough to keep buses, and by extension the people who rode them, away from certain beaches. My colleagues' position strikes me as principled, but I suspect the tool comparison is quietly doing most of the argumentative work.

I would like an audit of that underlying analogy: that technological systems, including algorithmic ones, are just tools, neutral instruments whose politics enter only through the intentions and choices of the people who wield them. Please make the mapping as explicit as possible, what is supposed to correspond to the tool, the user's hand, the task, the craftsman, the decision to use it well or badly, and then identify where the correspondence holds and where it breaks down. In particular, I want to know which features of the source domain a hammer or a screwdriver silently imports into the target but a technological system may lack or pervert, for example the way a hammer does not reorganize the menu of things it becomes sensible to do. Once the disanalogies are on the table, tell me whether the conclusion my colleagues want to draw, that our evaluation should scrutinize uses rather than the system's design, still survives, and what a better analogy would be if the tool analogy cannot carry that weight.
