I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a piece on how much confidence it is rational to place in metaphysical claims that sound ordinary until they are pressed. Adopt the role of a professional philosopher of modality and personal identity. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the sub-questions in your own scratchpad before you commit to any number.

The proposition to assess is this: it is metaphysically impossible for you, the very individual now considering this sentence, to have originated from a different sperm or a different egg — or both — than the particular gametes whose union produced the zygote from which you developed. Treat "different" as meaning a different gamete from the same two biological parents; if allowing different parents changes the question for you, say so in one sentence and state which reading your number is for. If you think the sentence as stated is too shifty to have a determinate truth value, say exactly what would need to be disambiguated, then commit to a disambiguation and a number anyway.

Please give me:

1. A single credence, from 0 to 1, in the proposition as you have disambiguated it.

2. A decomposition of that credence across at least the following terms, each with a numerical value and a one-sentence justification:
   a. Determinacy: your probability that the question has a determinate, context-independent answer rather than being vague, merely verbal, or only true relative to a chosen counterpart relation.
   b. Criterion of identity: conditional on determinacy, your probability that what fixes "you" across counterfactual possibilities is biological and origin-based rather than psychological, functional, narrative, or some other non-origin-based continuity.
   c. Granularity of origin: conditional on an origin-based criterion, your probability that the identity-fixing origin is the particular zygote — and therefore the particular gametes that formed it — rather than something coarser, such as the parentage, the genome, or the broad circumstances of conception, that a different sperm or egg could in principle preserve.
   d. Modal epistemology: your probability that intuitive, case-based reasoning about the necessity of origin is reliable enough to settle this concrete human case, given the biological oddities involved — for instance, that a different sperm could carry a different sex chromosome, and that monozygotic twins show the same zygote does not by itself pin down a single person.
   Then add any further term you think belongs in the decomposition.

3. The combination rule: show how the terms combine to produce your final number, or explain the dependence structure if the relation is not a simple product.

4. Sensitivity: identify the single term that moves your final credence most, and say what case, argument, or evidence would move that term the most for you.

I am less interested in the precise number than in the map of sub-questions it forces into the open. Wherever you suspect two thoughtful philosophers would place the decimal in different places, say so, and say which term the disagreement most likely lives inside.
