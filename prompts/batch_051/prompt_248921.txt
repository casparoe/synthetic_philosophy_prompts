I'm trying to pin down what I actually mean when I say someone understands a proof, and my gut reactions on cases are pulling in different directions. Here they are.

A student can reproduce the standard proof that sqrt(2) is irrational line by line, but when I ask why we set p/q in lowest terms, he just says "because the proof needs it." I don't think he understands it.

A different student can't do the epsilon-delta details of the chain rule proof, but she can draw the picture and say the two little errors are each small, and the leftover is a small thing times something bounded, so it dies. I do want to say she understands the idea, though maybe not the whole proof.

A mathematician has checked every case in the Appel–Haken four-color proof (the famous computer one) and can vouch for each step, but she can't give me the shape of the whole argument. I'm torn: does she understand it? Does anyone?

A grad student can give three different proofs of the same compactness theorem and say which one would generalize to a non-compact setting and why. Clearly understands it.

Someone who can state a theorem and apply it correctly in new domains but has never worked through a proof: I'm fairly sure she understands the theorem but not why it's true, so those come apart.

A colleague answers every "why" question I ask about a proof, but slowly, and I get the feeling he's rebuilding it from scratch each time rather than recaling it. I think he understands it at least as well as someone who recites it fast.

An undergrad who memorized a proof and can't do it if you change the variable names: doesn't understand it.

My working theory is that understanding a proof is having a compressed skeleton of it — a representation that keeps the dependency structure (which step needs which lemma, and why) and lets you regenerate the details on demand. But I'm not sure it gets all of the above.

So: is there a principle — mine or a better one — that captures these verdicts, or close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? What does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: an undergrad who can fill in every gap in a proof when you tell her where the gaps are, but can't find the gaps herself — does she understand the proof? And if you can think of a case that would split your principle from my skeleton view, tell me what each says about it.
