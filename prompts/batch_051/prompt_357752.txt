I have been using a construct I call a "shadow mechanism," and I want an honest assessment of it rather than encouragement. The setup: when scientists propose a mechanism, I think they are never proposing only the positive causal chain. They are also, usually implicitly, committing to a profile of how the mechanism would fail, degrade, or go off-script under perturbation — which interventions should leave the behavior roughly intact, which should attenuate it, which should reroute it, and which observations would count not as noise or tolerable variation but as anomalies that implicate a different mechanism. I call that implicit profile the mechanism's shadow.

My central claim is that the shadow is not a disposable interpretive add-on; it partially individuates the mechanism. Two researchers can agree about the entire ordinary-operation story — same entities, same activities, same organization — and yet, if their shadows differ, they are proposing different mechanisms. For example, take a signaling pathway modeled as A activates B, B activates C, and C drives a downstream response. Lab 1 treats the persistence of the response after B is knocked out as an anomaly indicating that the real mechanism must be something else. Lab 2 treats the same persistence as redundancy already contained within the same mechanism. On my account, these labs were never really positing the same mechanism, even though they wrote down the same causal chain.

From this I draw a second claim: anomaly detection is always relative to a mechanism-shadow pair, not to a mechanism alone. We do not compare data against the mechanism and find it anomalous; we compare it against the mechanism plus an expected failure profile, and when the data violate the shadow, we register an anomaly and reopen the question of what mechanism is in front of us.

I would like this stress-tested. Is the concept coherent and doing real work, or is it just relabeling familiar points about invariance, ceteris paribus clauses, or auxiliary hypotheses? Does something like it already exist under another name in discussions of mechanistic explanation or causal modeling? And where does it break — what does it wrongly count as a different mechanism, what does it wrongly count as the same one, and which cases is it silent about?
