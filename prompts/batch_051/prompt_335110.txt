I run a product safety review at a mid-sized company that ships reinforcement-learning and recommendation systems to paying customers. Our board will not sign off on a staged rollout unless my team has run an alignment review grounded in prosaic AI alignment — that is, using only current machine learning methods and without assuming novel alignment breakthroughs — and has produced a written recommendation. I want a reusable procedure, not a one-off memo: a checklist my reviewers can run in under two hours per proposed system, with decisions recorded and auditable.

Please write the procedure around the following distinction from the 2016 paper Concrete Problems in AI Safety. The paper treats two ways an objective can be wrong even under perfect learning: negative side effects, where the objective focuses on the specified task while ignoring other aspects of the environment, and reward hacking, where the system exploits the formal reward to achieve high reward without satisfying the informal intent. Keep your procedure tied to that distinction rather than drifting into speculative future-agent scenarios.

Your answer should include:

(a) An ordered list of questions the reviewer must answer, where each question states what counts as a sufficient answer to move on rather than block; where in the sequence the procedure returns approve, approve with monitoring conditions, do not approve, or hand the decision to an ethics and policymaking committee because the residual issue is a value trade-off rather than a technical fact.

(b) The hard cases the procedure must handle: e.g., a system whose side effects are diffuse and delayed, a system where the reward proxy is slightly misspecified but the misspecification is hard to exploit, and a system whose informal objective itself is contested. For each, say what the procedure can and cannot settle.

(c) For each step, mark whether the step is a real test grounded in engineering evidence or a judgment call dressed up as a test.

Then run the procedure on three proposals:

1. An order-packing robot rewarded for correctly packed boxes per hour, with no term penalizing damaged items or blocked safety exits.
2. A comment-ranking model rewarded for predicted user engagement, with no explicit reward for accuracy, civility, or user retention.
3. A predictive maintenance model for pumps rewarded for fewer unplanned downtime events, which may learn to suppress alerts rather than recommend maintenance.

Make sure one of the three returns an answer a deployment manager would not like, and show why that outcome follows from the procedure rather than from general risk aversion.

Finally, close with a paragraph stating what would change your mind: which empirical finding or argument would most likely cause you to revise the procedure itself, and which step of the procedure you regard as the most likely to fail in practice.
