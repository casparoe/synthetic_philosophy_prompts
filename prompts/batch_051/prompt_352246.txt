Consider the position that the boundary between feasible and infeasible computation is an epistemic boundary, not merely a practical one. More precisely: once a problem's solution is determined by a finite, exact specification, the question of whether any bounded agent can arrive at the solution depends on whether there exists a procedure whose resource use grows polynomially rather than exponentially in the problem's size. On this view, many well-defined problems — propositional satisfiability, many scheduling, planning, and folding problems — have determined solutions that no feasible process could reliably find if P ≠ NP, and the phrase knowable in principle therefore describes no real capacity for any agent constrained by the same broad resource limits as human beings and ordinary computers. I want a stress-test of this position rather than an exposition or a literature review. What would have to be true for the position to be false, or for the inference from computational complexity to epistemology to be importantly mistaken? I want the considerations sorted into what would be decisive, what would be merely awkward, and what the position can absorb without cost. Include what a careful defender of the position would have to concede as a genuine liability. In particular, address at least these candidate defeaters: the possibility that P = NP; the objection that worst-case asymptotic running time is the wrong notion of feasibility, and that average-case difficulty, approximation, quantum algorithms, or embodied and physical computation should change the verdict; the claim that human reasoning solves hard problems by reinterpreting or restricting them, so that the formal problem is not the problem actually faced; the idea that knowledge need not be achieved by explicit derivation and can instead be a reliable disposition or product of an external process, so a bound on derivation is not a bound on knowledge; and the worry that feasible is interest-relative, preventing the position from grounding any sharp philosophical line. Finally, assess whether the position has been insulated from counterexample by successive qualifications, and whether such insulation is a defect or the appropriate modesty for a claim about the limits of finite agents.
