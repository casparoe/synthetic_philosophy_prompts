I am comfortable with species concepts, reproductive isolation, and the contrast between anagenesis (change within a single lineage) and cladogenesis (splitting into separate lineages). I would like you to restate, in exactly that vocabulary, the historical-linguistics position that Latin never became Italian at any datable moment: the change was gradual, the two are labels imposed on a continuous tradition, and the search for a boundary between them is therefore misleading. For each component of that position, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no expression in the biological framework at all. Then give me a residue report: does the untranslatable remainder reveal a real disanalogy between languages and species, or does it show that the linguistic claim itself is less precise than it sounds? In particular, I want to know whether mutual intelligibility can do the work that reproductive isolation does for species boundaries, and whether casting the Latin-to-Italian transition as anagenesis rather than cladogenesis quietly assumes the very identity claim it is supposed to clarify.
