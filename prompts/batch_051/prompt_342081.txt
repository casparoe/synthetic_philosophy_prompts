I've been going back and forth with my housemate about whether we should be doing anything about insect suffering, and I'm trying to figure out whether the shortcut I use is actually defensible or just a way to keep myself from having to overhaul my life. My rule is: if I'd put the probability that some animal is sentient below about one in ten thousand, I treat it as zero for practical purposes. If I'm genuinely unsure whether aphids or fruit fly larvae can feel anything, but I think the odds are more like one in a million, I don't let it effect my choices about pesticide, what I eat, whether I buy silk, etc. My housemate thinks this is exactly backwards: he says there are on the order of ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, so even a one-in-a-million chance that a cricket can suffer means a pesticide application or a crop choice might be causing tens of billions of expected-suffering-equivalents, and you can't just round that to zero. He keeps saying the probability has to be multiplied by the scale before I decide whether it's too small to care about.

I can do the arithmetic, but I also feel like a rule that says "tiny probability times astronomical number, always take it seriously" has no stopping point—you get Pascal's-mugged by every fever-dream possibility with a big enough multiplier. So: is a probability floor like mine a defensible epistemic habit, or a bias dressed up as a policy? When would a threshold like that be justified, and if you wanted to keep the benefits of the floor without ignoring the scale argument, how should you set it or what should replace it (smooth discounting, a prior over sentience, a rule about which low-probability scenarios are even estimable, something else)?
