Next year my history teacher is trying an experiment and I cant tell if its brilliant or kind of dishonest. For the whole spring semester our only sources are supposed to be things made by or about ordinary people, and nothing by or about "elites or states." So no presidential speeches, no treaties, no generals' memoirs, no government reports, no big newspapers; instead we read letters home, diaries, gravestones, court records of petty disputes, shop ledgers, recipes, folk songs, parish birth-death registers, stuff like that. He says the point is to spend one semester seeing what history looks like from below before going back to the normal textbook, like a controlled diet.

Last week he ran a one-day trial on the French Revolution this way, and it honestly made me nervous. Half the class came out thinking the revolution was mostly about bread prices and local grudges, because we only read what shopkeepers and farmers wrote, and nobody had read the actual laws or what the assembly was doing. It felt less like "the real story" and more like a different selection bias, except now the bias is called authenticity. But I dont know if that's just a bad one-day version.

So treat the rule as a mechanism, not a slogan: walk through what a whole semester of this would do to what our class ends up believing about the past, which topics would become easy to study and which would quietly disappear, how students and the teacher (and whoever curates the excerpts) would game it, and what happens to the idea that this is more "real" than textbooks. Is there a version of the rule that keeps whatever is good about it without turning into a folk-history bias? Sorry this is long, I just keep going back and forth.
