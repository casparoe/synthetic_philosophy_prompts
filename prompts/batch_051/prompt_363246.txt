I serve on an ethics committee for a hospital system that is piloting a protocol the administration calls validated consent. Under the proposal, for any procedure above a risk threshold, the treating team may describe and recommend the procedure, but the formal consent process may not be handled by anyone involved in the patient's care. A full-time consent officer—salaried, with no compensation tied to how many patients proceed, and rotated across specialties—conducts a scripted information session and records it. At least one week later, a second recorded session administers a short oral quiz. The patient must state the procedure's purpose, two major risks, one alternative, and the expected result of doing nothing. If the patient cannot do this, consent is invalid, even if the patient insists on going ahead. The treating team cannot override the officer, though the patient may retake the quiz. Emergency exceptions exist, and the one-week delay may be waived only after a brief cognitive screen. If a patient later challenges the validity of consent, the recordings are entered as the primary evidence, but the patient may still testify to deception or pressure that occurred outside the recorded sessions.

I am not asking whether the protocol is ethical. I want a behavioral analysis of how it would actually play out. What would it incentivize for patients, physicians, consent officers, and hospital administrators? Who would be filtered in or out, and through which mechanism? Where does it get gamed first—by coaching, by threshold-shopping, by trivializing risk disclosures, or somewhere else? What second-order effects should we expect in doctor-patient trust, litigation, scheduling, treatment delays, and access for patients with low literacy, limited English, mild cognitive impairment, or severe anxiety? Which of the likely failure modes could be fixed without gutting the validation function, and which are intrinsic to the design?
