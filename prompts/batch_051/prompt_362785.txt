A colleague has argued himself into a conclusion I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the faulty step. (1) If a consideration counts in favor of having an attitude, then it is a reason to have that attitude. (2) The fact that a foundation will pay a fortune to anyone who sincerely admires a mediocre painter is a consideration that counts in favor of admiring the painter. (3) So the payment is a reason to admire him, and a sufficiently large payment is a decisive reason to admire him. (4) But admiration has a standard of correctness: it is correct only if its object is genuinely admirable, and no payment to an admirer makes the painter admirable. (5) Therefore there can be a decisive reason to have an attitude that is, by the attitude's own standard, incorrect; he concludes that appeals to an attitude's correctness are confused. I most want to keep (4), and I also find (1) close to a truism, yet I cannot identify which earlier step I am entitled to reject. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
