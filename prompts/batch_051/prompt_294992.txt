In my reading group on anthropic reasoning, we have spent several weeks stuck on a dispute that has, as far as I can tell, been running in the literature for decades without either side moving. The two positions are usually called the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). Very roughly: SSA says that when you are uncertain which observer you are among those your evidence does not distinguish, you should reason as if you had been randomly drawn from the set of all observers who actually exist, and then update on your specific evidence in the ordinary way. SIA says instead that your own existence as an observer is itself evidence: other things equal, hypotheses that predict more observers are confirmed, roughly in proportion to the extra observers they predict. The clash is sharpest in the presumptuous philosopher case, in which a theory that merely posits vastly more observers than its rival comes out strongly favoured before one has examined any ordinary evidence — a result one side treats as an unavoidable consequence of taking one's own existence seriously and the other treats as a reductio of the whole approach.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really computing the same quantity: one is asking how an external bookkeeper should distribute credence across possible worlds without using the fact that the reasoner exists, while the other is asking how I should update when my own existence is an indexical fact I cannot treat as a random draw. If that is right, the debate is partly verbal and partly a standoff between two rival rules for setting priors, rules that neither side can defend without begging the question. But I worry this diagnosis is too tidy, and that there is a hidden formal or empirical question after all — perhaps about whether the number of observers a hypothesis posits is a legitimate kind of evidence, or about whether SIA-style updating would be better or worse calibrated in some long-run sense if one could stand outside a large ensemble of worlds. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a survey of the arguments or a verdict on who wins. Give reasons for the diagnosis, say what each side is treating as non-negotiable, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a theorem, a novel case, an agreement about terminology, a calibration argument, or an honest verdict that the question is ill-posed.
