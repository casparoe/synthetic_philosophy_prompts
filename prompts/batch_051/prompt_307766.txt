I'm working on a paper about how people evaluate colonial infrastructure, and I keep coming back to one episode I'd like you to analyze through the Ferguson-versus-Tharoor balance-sheet lens. By Ferguson-style I mean the kind of ledger that weighs empire mostly in terms of the markets, legal order, and administrative capacity it delivered; by Tharoor-style I mean the kind that weighs it mostly in terms of extraction, destroyed local industry, and broken indigenous institutions. I know those are caricatures, but treat them as the two poles the episode has to be run between.

Here's the episode. The first big wave of railway construction in British India, roughly the 1850s and 60s. Take these facts as given, since I mostly need the analysis. The lines were built mainly by private British companies under a government guarentee: the risk was carried by Indian revenues, so investors got their return even when a line lost money. The earliest routes connected ports like Bombay and Calcutta to interiors that produced cotton, indigo, and grain. Over the following decades the network became an integrated internal market, moved troops quickly, and by independence was infrastructure an Indian state had simply inherited.

I'm not after a history report—I know the broad outines. What I want is a case analysis that uses this episode to stress-test the very idea of a balance sheet of empire. So: what would a Ferguson-style ledger put in the credit column here, and what would a Tharoor-style ledger put in the debit column, and where exactly do they talk past each other? Be disciplined about the machinery: who counts as a stakeholder, what is the counterfactual baseline (no railways at all? railways built later by an independent Indian state? railways on different terms?), and where should something like the guarantee scheme go, since it is neither exactly loot nor exactly development. Then tell me which features of the episode each style of ledger captures well and which it has to shove aside or count twice, and whether the case comes out as one for Ferguson, one for Tharoor, or as a case that shows the whole ledger approach was asking the wrong question.
