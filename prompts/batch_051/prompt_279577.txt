I am a student in a course on collective agency, and there is a phenomenon I keep noticing that I do not know how to label. Sometimes a consideration does the work of a reason for a position, decision, or pattern even though no individual in the situation actually holds it as his or her reason. Let me give three instances.

First, in committee meetings, a group can arrive at a decision that no member individually favors, and yet members will afterwards offer an explanation—"we chose her because she had both the technical background and the managerial experience"—even though no single member weighed both factors; the members who cited the technical background had themselves opposed her management record. The rationale existed only at the committee level. Second, people say things like "the market is pricing in the probability that the ceasefire will hold" or that an insurance premium "reflects a flood risk," attributing a rationale to an aggregate pattern even though no trader or actuary reasoned from that premise to that conclusion. Third, in appellate courts, different judges sometimes join an opinion on mutually incompatible grounds, so that the "reason for the holding" is something no single judge accepts as sufficient.

Is there an established term for considerations like these—reasons or rationales that remain in force even though no mind actually has them? I am not satisfied with a bare label. I want to know how to carve the phenomenon: how it differs from ordinary cases of several people sharing a reason, from merely hypothetical reasons (what a reasonable person would think), and from functional explanations in biology or economics. Is the "reason" here genuinely normative, or only a manner of speaking? Where are the edges—what makes something a rationale that is genuinely nobody's in particular, rather than a summary or an average of individual reasons?
