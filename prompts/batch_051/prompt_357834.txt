I'm taking a language endangerment seminar this term and we keep going over the usual reasons to care — identity, oral literature, culture, maybe the biodiversity analogy — but I've been chewing on a more structural idea and I want someone to be brutal about it. I've been calling it the keystone language principle. The thought is that some languages are to the study of possible human grammars what a keystone species is to an ecosystem: they matter way more than their speaker count suggests. A language with 300 speakers somewhere might be the only attested case of, say, an evidential system that forces speakers to mark eight different ways of knowing something, or an absolute spatial system where everything is north/south/up/down rather than left/right. If it dies undocumented or half-documented, a whole corner of grammatical design space disappears, and linguists lose data they can't reconstruct becuase no other surviving language instantiates that corner. So the principle says: when we triage revitalization money, we should weight languages by structural distinctiveness — how much of the attested feature-space would vanish with them — not just by speaker numbers or how rich their oral literature is.

My flatmate says this is either cold-blooded or just the biodiversity analogy with extra steps, but I don't think it's just a metaphor; I think the epistemic value of a language's grammer is a real kind of value, and triage is already happening anyway, just implicitly and by accident. So I want an honest assessment, not validation. Is this a coherent notion or just a metaphor? What are the nearest things people have already worked out, and where exactly does my principle break? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: the strongest reconstruction of my principle, the nearest existing ideas, the best thing it gets right, the strongest objection, and whether that objection is fatal or just calls for a patch.
