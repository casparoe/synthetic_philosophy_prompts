Recently I have been reading about the distinction between knowing how and knowing that, and I have repeatedly encountered the same brisk formulation of it. An introductory survey of the philosophy of mind I was working through summarizes the matter as follows:

"Ryle's central insight in The Concept of Mind was to separate knowing how from knowing that. Knowing how, for Ryle, is simply a matter of having the relevant ability: to know how to ride a bicycle is to be able to ride one. Knowing that, by contrast, is the possession of a true, justified proposition. Since an ability is not the sort of thing that can be true or false, knowing how cannot be a species of knowing that. That is why someone who can state all the mechanics of balance and steering may still fall off a bicycle on the first attempt. Later attempts to defend propositionalism—treating knowing how as knowledge of a fact about a way of doing something—collapse for the same reason. Ryle's distinction is therefore now widely regarded as settled, and philosophical attention has mostly moved on to other questions about skill."

I am a retired physicist, and I have learned to be wary of textbook summaries that sound a little too tidy; but I do not yet know this literature well enough to locate where the tidiness is doing the work. Could you audit the passage? In particular: (1) whether identifying knowing how with the possession of an ability is an accurate rendering of Ryle's own view, and whether it survives the standard cases in which ability and know-how appear to come apart; (2) whether the bicycle example establishes what the summary claims, and whether the same consideration really defeats the more sophisticated propositionalist accounts; and (3) whether the closing claim about the debate being settled is right. My aim is to know which parts I can carry forward as reliable and which parts I should treat as the summarizer's own gloss.
