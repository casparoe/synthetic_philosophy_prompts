I have been turning over in my mind a pattern I keep noticing in the way rich countries' institutions relate to animal products, and I cannot work out what it commits them to. At home, in places like Western Europe and North America, there is a fairly mainstream ethical discussion about meat, dairy and eggs: animal suffering is treated as a serious moral problem, and public bodies, canteens, schools and charities increasingly frame reducing animal products as the conscientious thing to do. But when donor governments and international agencies turn to food insecurity — in emergency feeding, in school meals, in programmes for malnourished children, and in livelihood projects that hand out goats or chickens — animal-source foods such as milk powder, eggs and fish are treated as straightforwardly good, even as the single most valuable thing you can give. Part of the reasoning, I gather, is that this is the very setting in which the plant-based alternatives available in rich countries are scarce or unreliable: fortified products, year-round legumes, supplements and the like are difficult to supply, whereas a small amount of animal-source food can close a serious nutrient gap. The ethical unease about using animals that is so prominent in the domestic conversation seems simply absent.

I am not trying to settle whether either side is right. What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit view of moral value that this contrast seems to presuppose. Does the humanitarian practice presuppose that animal welfare counts for nothing when set against human hunger and child malnutrition? Or is it better understood as a kind of emergency weighing, in which animal welfare does count but is simply overwhelmed by the human stakes? Is the practice actually committed to any one theory at all, or could several quite different moral outlooks all make sense of it? Is the overall pattern — unease at home, confidence abroad — coherent, or does it amount to compartmentalising the same question in two contexts? And if the practice were brought under a view on which the suffering of a goat or a hen matters as much as the suffering of a human being, what concretely would have to change in the design of food-aid and nutrition programmes, and what would be lost?
