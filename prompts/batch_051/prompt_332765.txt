I keep noticing that international bodies—the UN, human rights NGOs, groups like Amnesty—talk as if history has a direction. They say a government is on the wrong side of history when it resists same-sex marriage or fails to act on climate, and school curricula often present the expansion of rights as a kind of unfolding process. I don't think they're just making a prediction about future opinion; they're making a moral claim about where history is going. What philosophy of history does this institutional practice presuppose? In particular, Kant in his 1784 essay Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose says something like (I'm paraphrasing) that the antagonism among human beings—what he calls unsocial sociability—is nature's device for developing our rational capacities, and that this conflict ultimately pushes the species toward a lawful civil union. Is the UN/NGO rhetoric committed to something like that, or to a Hegelian cunning of reason, or can it get by with a purely counterfactual reading of wrong side (if the rights consensus expands, those resisting will have turned out to be wrong, but no historical necessity)? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if these agencies dropped the teleological claim and spoke only about present norms and concrete commitments.
