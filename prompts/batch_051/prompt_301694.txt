I keep coming back to this claim from the deepfake debate: audiovisual recordings are supposed to be an epistemic backstop for everyday disputes. The thought is that a tape doesn't just give you more or better evidence; it has a different social-epistemic role—it can settle disputes, license the impulse to just check the tape, and shift the burden of proof in ways ordinary testimony does not. I want to see whether that claim can be tested rather than just asserted.

Design the best experiment you can to test the claim that ordinary people treat recordings as having this distinct backstop role, as opposed to treating them as merely very reliable evidence. Pick a design that makes different predictions on those two hypotheses, and be explicit about how you would operationalize the notion of settling a dispute without building the answer into the task. Say what a clean positive result would show and what it would not show, and which versions of the backstop claim are simply not reachable by behavioral evidence. Also keep the settled/contested line clear: it is settled that recordings can be faked; I want to know where people who work on deepfakes and testimony actually disagree, and don't present a contested position as consensus.
