People reach for the Sphex wasp when they want a shorthand for mechanical rigidity: it has a fixed routine—check the burrow, drag the cricket in—and if you nudge the cricket while it's inside, it just redoes the routine from the top rather then improvising. The usual moral people draw is that free agents aren't different from sphexish systems because they're undetermined; they're different because they're flexible, able to redirect means on the fly. I want to know if that's actually a testable claim or just a slogan. Design a concrete experiment that would distinguish an animal whose flexibility is genuine (say, it recombines pieces of behavior it's never emitted together before, in a way that's appropriate to a novel problem) from an animal whose apparent flexibility is just a very large lookup table from environmental cues to fixed routines. Tell me what you'd present, what result counts as the live-flexibility result and what counts as the hollow-flexibility result, and what measure—latency, error pattern, response to the problem being modified mid-stream, something else—would do the separating. Then say what a positive result would and wouldn't settle: if the animal passes as genuinely flexible, have we learned anything about whether it acts freely, or does 'free' still point to something the test never touches? And if you think the contrast between genuine flexibility and a big lookup table is ill-posed at the behavioral level, say so outright and tell me what I should have asked instead.
