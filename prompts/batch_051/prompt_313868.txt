I have no formal background in philosophy or neuroscience, but I have spent a fair amount of time reading on my own about predictive processing and the free energy principle, and one point keeps bothering me. The principle seems to say that living systems act to minimize free energy, which is usually glossed as the gap between what they expect and what they actually sense — in other words, to minimize surprise. But if minimizing surprise were really the goal, the obvious winning strategy would be to find a dark, silent, featureless room and stay there, since an unchanging environment would never violate your expectations. I understand the common reply that free energy is minimized only relative to an organism's model of itself as a living thing, so that a healthy organism expects the changing, informative conditions it needs to survive; it therefore avoids the dark room because the dark room would violate those deeper expectations. What troubles me is that this reply seems to make the principle endlessly accommodating — whatever an organism does can be redescribed as minimizing surprise once you grant the right expectations. I would like an essay on this controversy: whether the free energy principle is a substantive claim about cognition that rules some possibilities out, or whether it is elastic enough to fit any behavior and therefore explains very little. Please weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but do not end in a non-committal both-sides-are-right way; reach a definite conclusion about how much explanatory weight the principle can bear, and defend it.
