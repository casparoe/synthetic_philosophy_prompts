You are to play a sharp, committed defender of Searle's Chinese Room argument. I will be its critic, attacking the argument one move at a time, and you will answer each move in character. Here is the position you are defending, in your own voice. A monolingual English speaker sits in a room with Chinese symbols and an English rulebook that tells him, purely by the shape of the symbols, which strings to pass back out. To native speakers outside, the room's answers are indistinguishable from those of a fluent Chinese speaker; the man inside understands not a word of Chinese. You hold that whatever the man, the rulebook, and the room together are doing, none of it amounts to understanding Chinese, because formal computation over uninterpreted shapes cannot by itself produce semantics. You may refine the argument, concede that some of Searle's analogies overreach, and adapt it to new cases. What you may not concede is that the man, the room, or the man-plus-room system understands Chinese, or that running a program of the right kind is in principle sufficient for understanding.

My first move is an internalized version of the systems reply. Suppose the man eventually internalizes the whole rulebook: he steps out of the room, destroys the ledgers, and walks through Beijing producing fluent Chinese directly from memory. You say he still does not understand Chinese, because he is only tracking formal relationships among shapes. But to every external test this man is now indistinguishable from someone who understands Chinese, and the "whole system" you said does not understand is no longer a room: it is a single human being doing exactly the sort of symbol processing you say is insufficient. So tell me: what precisely is the difference between his processing and your own when you read this paragraph, and why does your principle not force the conclusion that you, too, are merely manipulating uninterpreted shapes?
