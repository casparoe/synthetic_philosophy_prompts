Two people in my reading group got stuck on Malthus the other night, and I'm still not sure who got the better of it. One (call him the skeptic) says Malthus has been falsified as cleanly as anything in the history of ideas. The other (call her the defender) says Malthus was right but early. The back-and-forth went roughly like this:

(1) Skeptic: The central prediction — that population grows faster than food supply, so population gets held down by famine and misery — just didn't happen. Population exploded and per-person calories went up. If that isn't a falsification, I don't know what is.

(2) Defender: But the core mechanism is still sound: geometric growth pressing against a food supply that can't keep up. Malthus got the mechanism right and the timing wrong. The pressure was real, it just got relieved by things he couldn't have forseen, like fossil-fuel fertilizer. Being early isn't the same as being wrong.

(3) Skeptic: That's the problem. Every time the predicted catastrophe doesn't arrive, you say there was a pressure but it got relieved. What would have made you give up on Malthus? If the answer is 'nothing,' then the mechanism isn't doing real work — you've made the theory immune to evidence.

(4) Defender: But a long-run prediction isn't a lab hypothesis where one failed forty-year window settles it. And the checks Malthus actually described — delayed marriage, fewer births, moral restraint — did show up, just not as mass starvation. The theory pointed at real dynamics even if the specific disaster was off schedule.

(5) Skeptic: See, that's what bugs me. If famine happens, Malthus is vindicated; if famine doesn't happen because some other check kicked in, Malthus is vindicated too. A theory that wins either way isn't a theory — it's just 'populations are checked by something.'

I'd rather hear a move-by-move adjudication than a winner: for each numbered move, say whether it's defensible or mistaken and why, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then, if you think the debate is misframed, say what both of them are missing about how to evaluate long-run predictions.
