I'm in a high school philosophy elective, and we have been reading about Wittgenstein's *On Certainty*. A handout from my teacher summarizes the book as follows: all inquiry rests on "hinge" propositions such as that the world has existed for more than five minutes or that there are other minds. These hinges cannot be justified, because any attempt to justify them already presupposes them, but that does not mean they are shaky opinions; rather, they are neither true nor false, they are held with absolute certainty, and they are grounded in universal human instinct. When someone doubts a hinge, the correct response is not to offer evidence but to stop arguing. This, the handout says, shows that skeptical doubts are not false but literally meaningless, because they try to question the very rules that make meaning possible. Finally, the handout concludes that since hinges are just ungrounded commitments that we cannot justify, any set of hinges is as good as any other, so there is no rational way to say that a scientifically minded person's hinges are better than a conspiracy theorist's.

The handout also paraphrases a passage from *On Certainty* — it is not given to us as a direct quotation — as saying that when we give grounds for something, the chain of justifications eventually reaches an end, and what we find at the end is not a proposition that strikes us as true, but our acting, which lies at the bottom of the language-game. The teacher then writes that this passage shows justification bottoms out in brute, non-rational action rather than in truth.

I suspect this summary is mostly right but that some of its conclusions overreach. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and separate accurate claims, common simplifications, and outright misreadings. In particular, is it fair to infer from the passage about grounds coming to an end that hinges are never true or false and that any set of hinges is as good as any other? The final step seems to treat "cannot be justified" as meaning "cannot be rationally criticized," but I think those might be different. Please say what a careful reader of *On Certainty* would accept, what they would object to, and what in the handout is subtly changed. Also include a note on what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you to a different verdict about the handout, or where your reading of the passage is most likely to fail.
