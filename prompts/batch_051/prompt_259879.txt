Me and my friend Dana got into this long argument over Thanksgiving about what grown children owe their parents, and I can't figure out the rule behind her opinions. She has a bunch of settled verdicts and insists they're consistent: a woman whose mother was warm, present, and worked overtime to send her to college, and who now talks to her mother every day, owes her mother substantial care — Dana says it's an ongoing obligation, not just a debt to be discharged. A man whose father left when he was five and never contacted him again owes that father nothing, beyond the help we'd owe any stranger in an emergency, when he shows up elderly and broke. A woman whose parents were decent and reliable, paid her way through college, and now keep up a steady but not especially warm relationship with her — Sunday calls, help with their taxes, occasional visits — owes them real help if they need it, but not the kind of sacrifice you'd make for your own child. And a man who was raised mostly by his grandmother while his mother was alive but rarely around, and who is still close to the grandmother now, owes the grandmother far more than he owes his mother.

Here's the case I want to predict: a woman had a genuinely loving, self-sacrificing mother for her first twenty years — the mother worked a second job when she was sick and paid for college — and then drifted into total estrangement after leaving home. There was no abuse and no big fight; she just feels no bond, doesn't call, and has built a life elsewhere. Now the mother is old, alone, and needs regular care. If Dana wants to stay consistent with her other verdicts, what would she say the daughter owes? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and what principle is she actually running — a pay-back-for-your-raising picture, a depends-on-the-current-relationship picture, or some third thing? If her previous cases underdetermine the prediction, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each possible answer, and what further case would settle which one she holds.
