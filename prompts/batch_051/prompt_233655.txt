I'm a science journalist working on a piece about why people keep trying to ground morality in biology, and after interviewing a moral philosopher I reconstructed what she told me into an argument that now seems to prove way more than she intended. I'm sure I've garbled something, but I cant locate the slip.

(1) The is-ought gap says you can't validly move from premises that are all descriptive to a conclusion that's evaluative; somewhere in the premises there has to be an ought.
(2) So every serious argument for a moral conclusion has at least one evaluative premise.
(3) If the argument is supposed to justify its conclusion, that evaluative premise can't just sit there; it needs support.
(4) The support for an evaluative premise has to be either evaluative or descriptive — those are the only options.
(5) If it's evaluative, we're off on a regress: each evaluative premise needs another evaluative premise, forever.
(6) If it's descriptive, the is-ought gap rules it out, since that would be deriving an ought from an is.
(7) So every moral argument either bottoms out in an unsupported evaluative premise or regresses forever.
(8) Therefore no moral conclusion is ever genuinely justified.
(9) But that's too much: the is-ought gap is usually invoked to show that evolutionary or neuroscientific accounts of morality overreach, not that no moral belief is justified. And in fact it seems we are justified in some moral beliefs — like that arranging for a child to be tortured for fun is wrong.

I'd like to keep the is-ought gap and keep (9), because I'm sympathetic to the idea that science can't just read values off the facts, but I also don't want every moral argument to end in a bare "you just have to accept this premise." Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
