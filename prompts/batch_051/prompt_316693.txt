In my democratic theory class we've spent two weeks on the doctrinal paradox and I've talked myself into a position I can't quite shake. Here's the argument I keep making:

(1) Under majority rule, the group's view on any issue just is whatever a majority of its members hold.
(2) If a group is going to have a view at all, it should be logically consistent—it can't accept breach, accept valid contract, and reject liability all at once, given the doctrine says liability iff breach and valid.
(3) But the usual three-judge case shows that can happen: Judge A has breach and valid, Judge B has breach and not-valid, Judge C has not-breach and valid. Majority for breach (A, B), majority for valid (A, C), majority rejecting liability (B, C).
(4) So majority rule on each single question and the demand that the group's overall view be consistent cannot both be guaranteed.
(5) Therefore the only defensible fallback is that we have to choose between what the group thinks premise-wise and what it thinks conclusion-wise, and either choice silently gives up something that looked non-negotiable.

I half-suspect the jump from (4) to (5) is covering for something, but I can't tell wether one of the earlier steps is actually the weak link. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected version would be. If (1)–(4) are sound, then tell me whether the forced choice in (5) really is forced, and what a third option would look like if it isn't.
