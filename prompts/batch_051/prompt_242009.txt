I sit on our city's charter review commission, and next week I have to pitch the rest of the commission on a proposal I've been pushing for months — I call it sortition when I'm with people who won't roll their eyes. The idea is a standing citizens' assembly of around forty residents chosen by lot, paid a real stipend, matched to the city's age, gender, and neighborhood makeup, serving staggered two-year terms. It would get two real powers: it can commission one independent audit of any city department each year, and it can, by a two-thirds vote, force a public hearing and a formal council re-vote on any ordinance it dislikes. Technically that second one is just a recomendation, not a veto, since the council can re-pass the same thing, but I think the political cost of overriding a random cross-section of the city is the actual power. I keep arguing this is more democratic than what we have now, where the only people at every meeting are retirees and developers' lobbyists, and that a room full of randomly selected people is harder to capture than a room full of people who had to raise campaign money. Last night I had it out with my friend who teaches high school civics, and she said I want government run by people who didn't apply for the job and won't know anything, that only cranks and the retired will say yes, and that everyone else will just defer to the city manager or whichever staffer talks last. A couple of her points landed, and now I'm second-guessing my opener.

The commission I'm presenting to isn't hostile to the word democracy, but they are accountability people: three former councilmembers, a land-use attorney, and a political scientist who studies local government. They think elections at least filter for some basic competence, and they suspect a random body will either get steamrolled by staff or be dominated by whoever has the most free time and the loudest voice. Draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections in roughly the order this group will actually raise them, phrased the way they'd put it, each with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is shakiest. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
