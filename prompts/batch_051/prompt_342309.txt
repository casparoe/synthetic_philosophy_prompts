I have been working through the ethics of Peter Abelard, and I keep getting stuck on what his view does with difficult cases. As I understand him, Abelard holds that sin lies entirely in consent—the moment the soul says yes to what it knows to be wrong—and that the outward deed that follows adds nothing to the agent's guilt or merit: considered apart from consent, the deed is morally indifferent. I find this elegant but hard to keep stable. If someone fully consents to a wrong and is stopped by circumstances at the last moment, Abelard seems committed to treating that person as exactly as guilty as the one who succeeds. But then why do we treat attempts differently from completed wrongs in law and in ordinary judgment? And what should be said about negligent or reckless conduct, where real harm comes about without anyone having consented to it?

Write me a dialogue set after a graduate seminar on medieval ethics, between two students who disagree about precisely this. One defends Abelard's position that the sin is in consent alone and that the outward act adds nothing; the other defends the view that the outward act carries its own moral weight, so that a completed wrong is worse than the same wrong merely intended, and that some conduct can be blameworthy even without a prior evil consent. Make both speakers equally competent and well-informed; neither should be a strawman, and they should answer each other's actual arguments rather than delivering set speeches. Push them into the hard territory—the failed attempt, harm done through negligence, and the question whether divine judgment could diverge from human punishment. Do not leave the disagreement unresolved: by the end, one speaker should have made the more defensible case, and the other should make a genuine concession. After the dialogue, add a short paragraph in which you take a definite position on which view comes out ahead and why, and flag one way in which your reading of Abelard might be contested by scholars.
