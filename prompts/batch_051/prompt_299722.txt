I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and over the past year I have been reporting on a proposal that keeps resurfacing among nonprofit newsrooms and the foundations that fund them. The proposal is an editorial mechanism, not a style preference: a written knowledge rule of assertion governing every factual sentence the outlet publishes. Under the rule, a reporter or assigning editor may publish a factual assertion only if they are prepared to say, on the record to the standards editor, what they know and how they know it — where "know" is meant literally, not as shorthand for "strongly believe on good evidence" or "have confirmed with two sources." A retroactive enforcement arm accompanies this: every printed correction goes into a public assertion ledger with bylines attached, and any reporter whose published assertions are corrected more than twice in a quarter is removed from the reporting desk for retraining in source assessment. The funder behind the proposal insists that the rule be written in terms of knowledge rather than in terms of truth ("assert only what is true") or justified belief ("assert only what your evidence makes reasonable to believe"), claiming that the philosophical dispute over the norm of assertion is settled in favor of knowledge. I want to understand the proposal as a mechanism, not as an occasion to adjudicate that philosophical dispute.

My question is how this would actually play out if adopted by a midsized science and general-news desk. I want to know what it incentivizes among reporters, assigning editors, sources, the standards office, and the funding foundation; where it would be gamed first; how it changes what gets covered and how it gets written; whether the knowledge wording rather than the truth or justified-belief wording makes a practical difference and where that difference is largest; and which failure modes could be fixed by better auditing, thresholds, or appeals without abandoning the scheme. One structural detail I especially want examined: the enforcement trigger is a correction — a later finding of falsity — not a finding that the reporter asserted beyond their epistemic position at the time of publication. A thoroughly justified assertion that turns out false would count against the reporter, while a reckless assertion that happens to be true would not. Analyze the incentives that mismatch creates.

Act as a professional philosopher of language with a working knowledge of the norms-of-assertion literature and a newsroom operations analyst's feel for how rules get gamed. Work in three passes. First, brainstorm every actor with a stake and every incentive the rule changes. Second, turn those into concrete failure modes and gaming strategies, including the failure mode nobody proposing the rule wants to notice. Third, assess which failure modes are structural — built into the rule's wording — and which are mere implementation details that could be fixed without abandoning the idea.

Then write the analysis as a calm, precise memo. After the prose, give a structured summary table with exactly these columns: Dynamic; Actor affected; First-order incentive; Likely second-order effect; How it would be gamed; Severity (1–5); Fixable in principle (yes/partly/no); What would fix it. Fill in each row with concise entries, not full paragraphs.

Write in American English.
