I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about whether a work's moral failings, or its maker's, bear on how good the work is as art, and my gut verdicts on particular cases keep refusing to line up. Here are the cases and the verdicts I'm drawn to.

A director who committed a serious assault makes films that do not touch on it. The films are formally inventive and deeply moving, and I still call them great; the assault changes my view of the man, not of the films.

A painter holds bigoted views, but his still lifes and landscapes contain no trace of them. I find the paintings excellent, and his views leave them untouched.

A writer who, so far as anyone can tell, has done nothing wrong produces an immaculately constructed novel whose entire comic energy is devoted to making cruelty toward a despised group feel witty. I cannot name a formal flaw, and still I cannot call it good art, all things considered.

That same painter also made a series that subtly glorifies the persecution of the people he hates. As sheer technique, it is the finest work he ever produced, but I refuse to call it his best art.

A composer who is violent toward his wife writes for her a cycle of love songs whose tenderness is unmistakably real on the page. I think the songs are excellent, and knowing the biography makes them stranger and more affecting; but I would not say it makes them worse. If anything, the fact that a man like this could write tenderness like that is part of why they hold me.

A gifted director made for a murderous regime a short film whose individual images — crowds, monuments, harvests — are each innocuous, but whose design as a whole makes mass killing look like civic renewal. The film is cinematically flawless, and I want to say: brilliant filmmaking, but not a great film. Yet the defect I sense is not located in any shot; it seems to lie in what the film is for, which is a fact about why and for whom it was made. And in the first two cases I was perfectly willing to keep facts of that kind out of the verdict.

The rule that seems to fit most of this is: the artist's moral character and the circumstances under which a work was made are irrelevant to the work's value as art; only what is in the work itself can count for or against it. That rule handles the first two cases, and it handles the cruel novel and the painter's series. But it sits badly with the love songs and the propaganda film.

Then there is the case I keep turning over. A war photographer takes a formally masterful photograph of a child in mortal danger, and does nothing to help. Nothing in the image records the photographer's inaction; examined purely as an image, it is extraordinary. I cannot decide whether the wrongness of taking the photograph makes the photograph itself a worse photograph, or whether that is a separate judgment about the photographer.

Is there a principled account of what makes a work good as art that captures these verdicts, or close? What does it say about the photograph? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I drop rather than hanging on to a principle that is just a list?
