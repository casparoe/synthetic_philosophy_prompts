I'm a philosophy professor who's been embedded with an ML interpretability lab, and I've ended up with a pile of intuitions about when two "anomalies" in trained networks count as the same mechanism, but no principle that ties my verdicts together. By an anomaly I just mean a stable, reproducible failure or weird activation on a circumscribed class of inputs — e.g., a classifier that reliably mislabels a particualar kind of image. I'd like you to take my verdicts below and tell me whether there is a single account of "same mechanism" behind them, or whether one or more of the verdicts has to go. I suspect this is really the old problem of type-individuation for mechanisms in a new setting: are mechanisms individuated by internal parts, by the upstream cause, by the function computed, or by something more contextual?

Here are my cases:

1. Two small convnets, different random seeds, same training set. Both reliably say "picnic table" for any photo that has a red-and-white checkered cloth draped somewhere in the scene, even when there is no table. Linear probes show that in both models the same early layer computes a red/white grid feature and passes it to the table class. I'm very confident this is the same mechanism.

2. A convnet and a vision transformer, trained on the same data, both misclassify the same set of "red car in shadow" photos as "fire hydrant." Saliency maps suggest the convnet is tracking saturated red pixels, while the transformer is tracking the hard boundary between the shadow and the car. My gut: different mechanisms, even though the behavior is identical.

3. I take a model that misclassifies rotated hexagons as stop signs, fine-tune it on a different road-sign classification task, and it still misfires on rotated hexagons. A probe finds the same early "red plus hexagonal outline" feature driving the error before and after. My gut: same mechanism before and after fine-tuning.

4. Two models both predict "clock" for any image with a small gray circle in the upper-left quadrant. In model P, you can linearly decode "clock" from layer 3 right after the circle appears; in model Q, the decision depends on a longer chain of layers that integrates the circle with other scene features. My gut says these are different mechanisms, even though both are shortcut-y.

5. A convnet and a vision transformer are trained on the same corrupted training set, where every guitar photo contains a faint yellow bar at the bottom from a labeling-tool glitch. Both end up misclassifying clean guitar photos as pianos whenever a yellow strip appears anywhere. The convnet uses the yellow strip as a color feature; the transformer folds it into an object-boundary representation. My gut says: same mechanism, because the shared upstream cause is the same training artifact. But I can't realy square this with case 2, where different internal pathways made me say different.

6. An adversarially trained model and a plain model both fail on pictures of cows taken through a chain-link fence; probing suggests the adversarially trained model is using the fence texture, while the plain model is using a "cow" feature that misfires on fence occlusions. My gut: different mechanisms.

And one I keep going back and forth on: a captioning model and an independently trained classification model both mislabel close-up photos of partially occluded pinecones as hedgehogs; one uses texture, the other shape. I have no settled verdict there.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures these verdicts, or as many of them as possible? Please state it precisely, connect each clause to the case that motivates it, and tell me honestly which of my gut verdicts, if any, must be overturned. Also say what the principle predicts for the pinecones case.
