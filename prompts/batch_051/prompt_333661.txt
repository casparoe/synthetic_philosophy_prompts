Been mulling over something for a while and I'd like an honest take. I've noticed that when people talk about living simply, they only seem to treat it as morally impressive if the effort is visible. Someone who keeps a worn coat going for years is seen as frugal, but someone who just doesn't buy much in the first place barely gets noticed, because there's nothing to point to. I've started calling this the sight-cost account of asceticism: the moral weight we give a simple life tracks how much self-denial is on display, not how little the person actually consumes. Growing your own food is legible, so it counts; not having a car is just an absence, so it doesn't. That would explain why quite demanding forms of restraint get no credit, while fairly comfortable versions of simplicity that involve visible labour get a lot. I know it sounds as if I'm saying all ascetic ideals are just performative. I don't think that's quite what I mean, but maybe it's where the idea lands. Is this a real position someone has worked out under a proper name, or am I just retreading something obvious? What would you say are the three strongest objections, and do any of them kill the account rather than just narrowing it?
