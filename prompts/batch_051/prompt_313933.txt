I spent most of a long evening arguing with a friend about Locke’s account of property, and we got stuck on what looks like a conflict inside Chapter V of the Second Treatise. In the passage where Locke first derives property from labor, he says that when someone removes a thing from the common state of nature, he has mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property—and he qualifies this with the condition that it holds “at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others.” Later in the same chapter, though, he seems to add a different constraint: a person may take only as much as he can use before it spoils, and whatever exceeds that is more than his share and belongs to others, since God made nothing for man to spoil or destroy. My friend insisted these are just two ways of stating one proviso about not leaving others worse off. I think the spoilage constraint is stronger than the enough-and-as-good condition: someone could leave plenty of equally good land for others and still take far more perishable fruit than he can ever use, which the later passage forbids.
