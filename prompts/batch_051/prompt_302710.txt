i'm a high school junior and i've been going down a history-of-math youtube rabbit hole, and its bugging me because now i can't stop noticing this thing. whenever people talk about old science they talk like the past was just an unfinished draft of right now. Democritus gets a gold star because his atoms kinda match Dalton's, but Aristotle gets called wrong or confused because he thought heavy things fall faster, as if he was failing at being a modern physicist instead of doing his own thing. Euclid's proofs get called gappy or informal because he moves shapes around instead of writing algebra, and Newton is always described as not quite having Cauchy's definition of a limit, like he was half-reaching for it. the pattern seems to be: if an old idea looks like a modern idea, the person was almost there, a neglected genius; if it doesn't, the person was blocked and couldn't make the jump to us. either way, the present is the finish line.

i know "anachronism" is a word but that feels too broad, i mean specifically this grading-old-science-by-how-close-it-got-to-us habit. does that have its own name? and are the credit version and the blame version really the same thing, or two seperate moves? also there's the subtler version where someone says "what Euclid was really doing was..." or "in modern terms they were basically doing..." — is that the same habit or something else? i'm not asking for a reading list, i just want to know if this thing has a name and how a historian would carve it up, like where its edges are and whether it's ever actually the right way to talk.
