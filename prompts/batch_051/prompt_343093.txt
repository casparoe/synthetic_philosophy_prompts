I am a retired atmospheric scientist, and I have only recently begun to read the philosophy of science with any seriousness. I would like to put the two backgrounds together: I want a case analysis of a concrete historical episode, examined through the lens of chaos, determinism, and the limits of prediction.

The episode is the Great Storm of October 1987. On the night of 15–16 October, an unusually severe extratropical cyclone struck southern England and northern France, causing extensive damage and a number of deaths. The storm began as an unremarkable feature in the Bay of Biscay, intensified far more quickly than the operational guidance captured, and crossed the English Channel as a deep low. The event entered public memory largely because, the previous evening, a prominent television forecaster made an on-air remark widely taken to dismiss the possibility of a hurricane; the exact wording and context are contested and need not detain us.

Please analyze the 1987 storm through the concepts of deterministic chaos, ontic determinism, epistemic predictability, and the forecast as a product. In particular:

(1) Distinguish sharply between the claim that the atmosphere is deterministic under the relevant classical equations and the claim that it is predictable, and explain what sensitive dependence on initial conditions does and does not imply about each.

(2) Offer a disciplined reconstruction of what the forecast failure actually was. To what extent was it a matter of initial-condition error, given the sparse observations over the Atlantic; of model resolution and unresolved physical processes; of data assimilation; or of how the available guidance was interpreted and communicated? How far can these contributions be disentangled at all, given that attribution itself becomes difficult in a system with a short predictability horizon?

(3) Use the episode to think about responsibility and blame for failed predictions. If a forecast is best understood not as a deterministic guarantee but as a probabilistic or ensemble product, what exactly is the forecaster responsible for? Does the public memory of the storm rest on a confusion between a technical claim about one kind of storm and a plain warning about severe weather, and what would an adequate ethics of forecasting say about that confusion?

(4) Finally, say what the episode does and does not show about the philosophical standing of determinism. Does chaos undermine determinism, or does it only constrain predictability? And was the operational response over the following decades — heavier investment in high-resolution and ensemble prediction and in the communication of uncertainty — better read as a scientific correction, an epistemic correction, or a moral one?

I am not interested in a historical narrative for its own sake, nor in a literature review. What I want is a disciplined mapping between the episode and the concepts: chaos, determinism, the predictability horizon, ensemble forecasting, model error, and the ethics of prediction. Where the record is ambiguous, I would rather you say so than force the theory to speak.
