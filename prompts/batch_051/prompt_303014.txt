ok so i’ve been reading darwin (i’m not a biologist, just a person trying to think straight about group selection), and in The Descent of Man, in chapter 5, right when he’s talking about how the moral faculties develop, there’s this passage where he basically says something like: it’s fine that within a tribe the more virtuous or self-sacrificing members might not have the most kids, because each new generation still inherits the tendency to be good to their own group. But then he says—and by the way, the passage is about natural selection acting on tribes, not individuals—that a tribe with a lot of members who are ready to give aid and sacrifice for the common good would be victorious over most other tribes; and that would be natural selection. The exact line is: “At all times throughout the world tribes have supplanted other tribes; and as morality is one important element in their success, the standard of morality and the number of well-endowed men will thus everywhere tend to rise and increase.” Something like that. ok here’s why I’m stuck. I used to think “group selection is a confused idea” because genes are what get inherited, and a gene for self-sacrifice gets snuffed out within a group even if groups with lots of self-sacrificers outcompete other groups. The folk version is just bad math: an altruist dies to save two brothers, but the whole group thrives, yet the gene still loses relative frequency inside its own group. So I’d nod along and say Darwin here is just being pre-genetic and loose. But now I don’t think that’s what he’s doing. He doesn’t talk about genes at all, and he doesn’t pose it as one level against another. He talks about what he calls a “standard of morality” spreading, and about well-endowed men. And when you read the whole chapter he seems to be giving an account of how a disposition—not just a bit of behavior—becomes more common in a population, where the mechanism is group replacement and also imitation and reputation within the group. So here’s the thing: is his argument actually vulnerable to the selfish-gene objection, or is that objection aimed at a different target? I mean, if you drop gene-centrism and just ask whether a heritable disposition can spread because groups differ in their frequency of having it, is there any formal problem left? Like, if the group effect is strong enough, it can overcome the within-group effect, but does that require the groups to be like super isolated and constantly going extinct? Or is “standards of morality” doing work that genes can’t do?
