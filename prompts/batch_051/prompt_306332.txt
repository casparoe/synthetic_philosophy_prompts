I'm taking a seminar on formal epistemology, and after two weeks on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox I've become convinced that I can run the two cases through a single argument—and that the result is much stronger and more unpleasant than either paradox alone. I've written the argument out below, and I'd like to know which step goes wrong. If it doesn't go wrong, I'd like to know which premise is least costly to abandon, because I don't see how to keep everything I want.

(1) Take a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. For any ticket you care to name, the evidence that it will lose is extremely strong—probability 0.999. If the strength of a proposition's evidential support is 0.999, then you may rationally believe that proposition. (2) Rational belief is closed under conjunction: if you may rationally believe P and you may rationally believe Q, then you may rationally believe P and Q, and so on for any finite list. (3) Applying this to the lottery, you may rationally believe of each individual ticket that it loses; by closure, you may rationally believe that all 1,000 tickets lose. (4) But you also know the setup, so you may rationally believe that some ticket wins, which is the direct negation of the claim that all tickets lose. (5) So, from apparently innocent principles, you may rationally believe an explicit contradiction, which should be impossible. (6) Therefore at least one of the two principles—high probability suffices for belief, or closure under conjunction—must be given up.

Now consider the preface case. An author has gone through every sentence of her book one by one, checking each carefully; she rationally believes each sentence. She also rationally believes, from long experience with how errors survive careful checking, that at least one sentence in the book is false. This combination seems perfectly reasonable. Yet the set consisting of her belief in each sentence plus her preface belief is already inconsistent: the individual sentence beliefs jointly entail the negation of the preface belief, even if she never explicitly combines them. This suggests that the problem isn't really the high-probability rule, since the sentence beliefs needn't be merely probable; and it suggests that giving up closure, which looked like the natural way out of the lottery, would itself be a serious blow—ordinary deductive reasoning constantly relies on combining premises we individually accept. But if we refuse to give up closure and instead tighten the high-probability rule, that still leaves the preface author unexplained. It seems, then, that no single principled account can preserve ordinary deduction, the preface author's rationality, and the lottery player's ticket-by-ticket judgments all at once. The argument appears to prove that our notion of rational belief is either covertly context-sensitive in a way we cannot state, or simply incoherent.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to choose, I would most like to keep closure, because rejecting it seems to pull the floor out from under all deductive reasoning; but I suspect that is exactly where the pressure is. What step or premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
