Here is a running log of a colleague's verdicts on choice problems. I have never managed to extract the rule from her, and now I need to predict what she will say about a new case. Please work through the rule-following, not just the labels.

Newcomb's problem. A predictor who has been right on 95 of the last 100 trials has already made his prediction. One box is transparent and contains $1,000; the other is opaque and contains $1,000,000 if and only if the predictor predicted that the chooser would take only the opaque box. She takes only the opaque box.

Twin prisoner's dilemma. She and an exact counterpart—someone she has never met but who is guaranteed to reason as she does on the same evidence—each choose cooperate or defect in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. Payoffs to each: both cooperate, $5; both defect, $1; unilateral defection, $10 to the defector and $0 to the cooperator. She says cooperate, since her counterpart will make the same move.

Death in Damascus. Death has predicted with near-perfect reliability whether she will spend tomorrow in Damascus or in Aleppo, and will meet her in the predicted city. If she is in that city, she dies; if she is in the other, she lives. She slightly prefers Aleppo, other things equal. She says the problem has no stable answer: each choice, once made, looks like bad news, so the honest thing is to flip a coin and stop pretending either option is better.

Now the new case. A study with every confound controlled finds a genotype that causes both a pleasant response to a certain stimulant and a higher lifetime risk of a heart condition. The stimulant itself is causally inert: it does not cause the condition and does not change the genotype. A cheap genetic test is available, but she declines it. She has never tried the stimulant, likes the idea of trying it, and is deciding whether to take it.

What will she do, if she is applying the rule implicit in her record? Please answer in the following sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths.

1. The surviving rules (100–140 words). State the two or three principles that fit all three logged cases, glossing each in a clause (for example, evidential, causal, functional, ratifiability-style), and name one ruled-out principle together with the logged case that rules it out.

2. The new case under each rule (80–120 words). For each surviving rule, give her predicted verdict on the stimulant case and the one-sentence rationale she would offer under that rule.

3. Best-guess prediction (60–100 words). Say which verdict you would predict, or say honestly that the log underdetermines a confident answer, and identify the feature of her record that carries the most weight in choosing among the surviving rules.

4. The closest analog (30–60 words). Say which of the three logged cases is most like the stimulant case, and in what respect.

5. The case that would settle it (60–100 words). Propose one further decision problem—described concretely enough that someone else could run it—on which the surviving rules disagree, and state what each predicts there.

6. Residual ambiguity (30–60 words). Note any pair of surviving rules that even your proposed test would still not separate.
