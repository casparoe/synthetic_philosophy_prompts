In my upper-level free will seminar, my professor keeps compressing the debate over Frankfurt-style counterexamples into the line "the principle of alternate possibilities treats what could have happened as a proxy for who was in control." She used it last week to set aside a student's worry that determinism removes the ability to do otherwise, and therefore removes moral responsibility. I can tell the line is doing real dialectical work, but I cannot tell exactly what claim is being asserted. What are the distinct things that slogan could be claiming? For each reading, lay out precisely what it says about alternative possibilities, about the ability to do otherwise, about moral responsibility, and about what Frankfurt cases actually show; say whether the reading is trivial, substantive and defensible, or too strong; and say whether that reading would actually support setting aside the determinism worry or simply change the subject. I would also like the readings distinguished by what "could have done otherwise" is taken to mean—an unconditional ability to choose, an ability to act on a choice, a conditional analysis, a sensitivity to actual-sequence features, or something else. I lean incompatibilist, but I am not asking you to defend the slogan; I need a menu of possible precisifications so I can ask my professor which one she means.
