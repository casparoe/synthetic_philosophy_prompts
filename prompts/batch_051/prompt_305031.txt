I sit on the board of the Sparing Ways Foundation, a small private foundation whose governing document directs it to encourage thrift, resourcefulness, and freedom from waste. For thirty years the board has treated this as a demand on the foundation's own conduct. We operate from a borrowed one-room office, employ four people, keep administrative costs below eight percent of disbursements, and fund modest neighborhood programs: repair cafés, tool libraries, home-economics classes, a matching fund for household weatherization. Over the years we have declined professional event planners, paid fundraising consultants, and rebranding proposals, on the stated ground that an institution chartered to promote frugality should not purchase the things other institutions purchase to enlarge themselves. Our mode of operating has become, in effect, the main way the foundation expresses its mission.

A new executive director has put before us a concrete proposal: a five-year, twenty-two-million-dollar public campaign, designed along public-health marketing lines, using paid media, community organizers, and a communications firm to shift household consumption habits across our four-state service area. The director's case, which we accept as methodologically sound, is that this campaign would reduce downstream household consumption by substantially more than the same money disbursed as small grants, and would reach people the current programming never reaches. But approving it would mean that for five years the foundation is among the largest purchasers of advertising in the region, administrative costs rise to roughly a third of outlays, and the same small staff that runs thrift workshops is approving invoices for billboards, focus groups, and media placements.

The board is split, and the split is genuinely philosophical. One camp reads the charter as naming a state of affairs to be maximized: less waste in the world. On that reading, the foundation's own expenditure is simply an input, and refusing the campaign because it feels un-frugal would be a kind of moral vanity, protecting the appearance of frugality at the cost of the thing itself. The other camp reads the charter as imposing a constraint on how the foundation may act, not merely a target for what the world looks like afterward. On that reading, an institution that uses lavish means to persuade people to consume less is enacting the opposite of what it teaches; frugality is the kind of value that must be exemplified in one's own conduct, and the mission is partly constituted by the means chosen. The strongest version of this position is not that the campaign would fail as an instrument, but that it would succeed in the wrong way.

I need a written memo from you that I can circulate to the full board before our vote. Recommend a course of action—approve, reject, or approve with specified conditions—and give your reasons at the level of principle, not just projected outcomes. The memo must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with full seriousness and explain why it does not carry the decision. End with a recommendation the board could act on at the meeting.
