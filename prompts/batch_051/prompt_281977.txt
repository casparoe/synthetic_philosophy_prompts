I'm a backend engineer who wandered into AI safety discussions, and I keep seeing the word "control" used for basically anything you do with a model you don't fully trust. I've been trying to split it into three buckets, and I'd like someone to tell me where the taxonomy is wrong or if it already has a name.

- Cage controls: just cap what the model can do (no internet, no tools, sandboxing, short context window, kill switch). These don't care what the model wants.
- Leash controls: change what the model wants or what's instrumentally rational for it (RLHF, reward shaping, penalties for bad outputs, constitutional AI). My take: these only work if the model ends up the kind of thing that cares about your leash, so they're really alignment attempts wearing a control costume.
- Blindfold controls: let the model do real work but keep it from knowing whether it's in training, an eval, or actual deployment (honeypots, randomized monitoring, withheld context), so even a model that wants to misbehave can't tell when misbehaving would count.

The claim I want tested: cage and blindfold are the only real control measures, because they're the only ones that still work if the model is misaligned and stays misaligned. Leash is control-shaped alignment, and it quietly assumes the thing it's supposed to be checking.

Is the cage/leash/blindfold split actually carving anywhere real, or am I just renaming something people already say more carefully? And does blindfold collapse once the model assigns some probability to being in deployment and starts defecting probabilistically? I realize this might be totaly off, but give me the strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
