I'm a novelist, not a lawyer. I write a series set in a fictional mid-sized American port city — call it Tern Harbor — where law is a recurring plot engine. Every book has some dusty statute or old ordinance suddenly mattering: a blue law used by a developer to smother a competitor, a forgotten shoveling law enforced against exactly one person, a city attorney dusting off a 70-year-old rule to get leverage. My problem is I've been making up how all of this works on the fly, and the two books I've published so far contradict eachother. In the first, a judge called a Sunday-closing law "dead but still on the books" and threw the case out. In the second, an even older sidewalk law was upheld against a single shopkeeper, and the court didn't blink. My editor noticed, and she's right, and now she has to sign off on the legal logic before the book goes to copyedit.

So I want a reusable procedure — a checklist, or an ordered set of tests — I can run on any law in Tern Harbor's code to decide whether it has fallen into desuetude (gone unenforced so long that it's no longer treated as effective) and what follows for the plot. Give me the questions in the order I should ask them, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and make the procedure stop with a clear verdict where it can: "dead letter," "dormant but revivable," "fully live," "live but selectively enforced." Flag the points where the real-world doctrine is unsettled or where the decision is mine as the author rather than something the logic settles.

Fix the constraints honestly: I'm not a lawyer, I'm not going to research jurisdictions, and this has to be runnable in an afternoon when I already know what the scene needs to do. I need it to tell me not just what a court would do, but how different characters would treat the law — a formalist judge, a beat cop, a city attorney looking for leverage — because my plots run on the gap between what the code says and what people actually do with it. And part of my problem is that I often start with the outcome I want and backfill why the law was never enforced; the procedure should tell me what backstory would actually support an outcome, and when no plausible backstory will, so the plot has to change instead of the law bending to the scene.

Then run the procedure on these three invented statutes from the current draft:

(1) An 1890 ordinance requiring every shop on Harbor Street to keep a brass hitching post in working order. It hasn't been enforced since the 1920s, and a heritage district now wants to use it to block a bike shop from taking over an old storefront.

(2) A 1955 ordinance making it a misdemeanor not to clear and salt the sidewalk in front of your business within three hours of a snowstorm. It's still occasionally enforced, but overwhelmingly against businesses on the north side of town and almost never on the south side.

(3) A four-year-old statute making it an offense to sleep in any public park between midnight and 6 a.m. The council passed it expecting the police to leave it alone, and they did, until a new police chief began enforcing it against the encampment outside the library last month.

For each, tell me what the procedure gives, where I'll have to make an authorial call, and make sure at least one result is one I'm not going to like — where the procedure says my plot premise has to change rather than the law bending to the scene.

End by saying what the procedure can't do: the cases it will misfire on, and the questions where the underlying philosophy of law genuinely doesn't settle it, so I shouldn't pretend Tern Harbor has an answer just because I invented the city.

Please write the whole answer in American English.
