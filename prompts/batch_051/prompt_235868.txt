Act as a senior philosopher of mind who works on phenomenal consciousness and is also comfortable with the psychophysics of color and the recent brain-interface literature; think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the possible objections before settling on your final assessment.

I am a PhD student in an interdisciplinary cognitive neuroscience and philosophy program, and I have been trying to make sense of experiments that use direct cortical stimulation or related interfaces to induce colors that subjects describe as lying outside the normal human color space—so-called forbidden or impossible colors—and, more ambitiously, to generate qualia with no ordinary sensory correlate. I have been developing a position I tentatively call the Interpolation Constraint on Novel Qualia. The core claim is that a brain interface can complete a subject’s phenomenal space, but it cannot open an entirely new dimension of simple qualia. My inspiration is the missing-shade passage in Hume’s *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding*, where Hume, having argued that every simple idea is copied from a prior impression, grants that a person who had seen every shade of blue except one could still supply the missing shade from the neighboring shades. I read this as an early and underappreciated recognition that the mind has the constructive power to fill gaps inside an already acquired qualitative dimension, while remaining unable to create a simple idea with no experiential anchor. On my view, the experiments with so-called novel colors work only because hue is a tightly structured quality space with adjacent positions that can be interpolated; the subject is really being given a determinate interior point in an existing space. But we should not expect an analogous result for magnetoreception, echolocation, or any putatively alien modality, because there would be no neighboring experienced anchors from which the new qualia could be generated. The brain interface is thus, in my picture, a tool for interpolation, not for the invention of new simple qualia.

I am aware this sounds like an empirical claim dressed in eighteenth-century vocabulary, and my labmates are split between those who think it is trivial and those who think it is obviously falsified by neural plasticity. I would like an honest assessment. Please do the following: reconstruct the strongest version of the Interpolation Constraint. Tell me whether the distinction between simple and non-simple qualia is doing real work or smuggling in the conclusion. Identify the three or four most serious objections, including at least one that a philosophically trained audience in my department would raise and one that a sensory neuroscientist would raise; for each, say whether it is fatal, merely uncomfortable, or strengthens the view once accommodated. Note the points where my reading of Hume is most likely to be attacked—in particular, whether his missing-shade concession really supports constructive completion rather than just imaginative recombination of memory images. Finally, tell me whether this is already a named position in the literature under a label I have missed, and whether the proposal fails or stops being distinctive at a specific point.
