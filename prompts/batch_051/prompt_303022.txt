I am a novelist preparing a near-future novel in which a small research group presents a government agency with an algorithm for ranking policy interventions by expected social welfare. The group's economist derives a social objective that is a weighted sum of individuals' utilities, and the engineer then sets all the weights equal. A regulator objects that the equality of the weights was never derived, and the scene turns on whether that objection is correct and, if so, where precisely the proof leaves off and the moral assumption enters. I want the economist's argument reconstructed fairly, so that if the regulator is right, she is right for a definite reason rather than by casting vague doubt on the whole enterprise.

Here is the economist's argument as I have reconstructed it:

(1) Every affected individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the standard expected-utility axioms, so each person can be represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function that is unique only up to a positive affine transformation.
(2) The social ordering of lotteries satisfies the same expected-utility axioms, so it too has an expected-utility representation.
(3) The social ordering respects unanimity: if every individual weakly prefers one lottery to another, so does the social ordering; and if every individual strictly prefers it, so does the social ordering.
(4) By the standard aggregation theorem, it follows from (1)–(3) that the social expected-utility function can be written as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, possibly with an additive constant.
(5) But each individual utility function in (1) may be independently rescaled without changing the individual preferences it represents, and such independent rescalings change the weights in (4).
(6) Therefore the weighted-sum result in (4) does not by itself determine a unique weighting scheme; setting all the weights equal is an additional moral assumption, not a consequence of the proof.

I am unsure whether (5) really licenses (6), or whether a defender of equal weights can reply that the conditions in (2) and (3) were already intended to pin the weights down. Tell me whether the argument is valid and, if it is not, exactly which step fails and what a corrected step would say; if it is valid, say what further assumption would be needed before equal weights actually follow, and why that assumption is not already contained in (1)–(4).
