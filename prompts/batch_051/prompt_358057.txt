You are a professional philosopher who specializes in Kant’s practical philosophy and in contemporary debates about moral status. Please work through this problem in the spirit of careful analytic philosophy: first restate the relevant criterion precisely and identify which case you expect to be hardest, then test the criterion against each case one by one, and only then issue a final verdict.

Consider the following broadly Kantian criterion of direct moral considerability. A being can be wronged directly — that is, one can have a duty to that being itself, rather than merely a duty regarding it — only if the being is an end in itself by virtue of having a rational nature: the capacity to set ends, to act on principles it gives itself, and to be subject to the moral law. On this reconstruction, merely sentient nonhuman animals are not ends in themselves; they may be used as means, and whatever duties we have concerning them are indirect, grounded in duties we owe to rational beings. Species membership as such carries no moral status; it matters only insofar as it tracks the relevant capacities. Now consider three cases at the edge of this criterion.

(a) A nonhuman primate raised in a long-term research program who has learned several hundred arbitrary signs, combines them in novel ways, can communicate about absent objects, and at least sometimes appears to delay gratification according to self-imposed plans. (b) An adult human being with a congenital condition that leaves them with profound cognitive impairments: they feel pleasure, pain, and attachment, but they cannot deliberate, form long-term intentions, or hold themselves to rules, and they are not morally responsible for their actions. (c) A hypothetical nonhuman animal bioengineered to possess the neurological prerequisites of practical reason — foresight, impulse control, means-end planning, and something like a sense of obligation — yet having no human ancestry and no human cultural formation.

For each case, decide whether the being qualifies as an end in itself and therefore as an object of direct duties, and say why. Where the answer is underdetermined, say whether the difficulty lies in the facts of the case or in the criterion itself. Then address whether any of these cases shows that the criterion needs revision — for example, that sentience rather than rational nature should ground direct duties, or that species membership is doing hidden work, or that moral status should be scalar rather than all-or-nothing. End by stating what you think a consistent Kantian ought to say about the moral status of animals, even if that requires departing from Kant’s historical position.
