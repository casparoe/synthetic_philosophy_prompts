In Perpetual Peace, Kant develops a distinctive argument in the First Supplement: that nature itself, through human beings' unsocial sociability, through the plurality of languages and religions that divides peoples, and through the commercial spirit that gradually takes hold of nations, supplies a kind of guarantee for the possibility of perpetual peace, even among agents who do not directly intend it. I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar for PhD students in economics and theoretical computer science. They are fluent in mechanism design, credible commitments, common knowledge, and evolutionary game theory, but have not read Kant. Restate Kant's claim as faithfully as possible in that vocabulary — as, for example, a claim about how self-interested local dynamics might converge on a peaceful equilibrium without any agent aiming at it. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the argument translate cleanly, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no counterpart in the students' framework at all. I am especially interested in whether Kant's appeal to nature's purposiveness, and his insistence that the resulting peace is a moral task rather than merely a useful equilibrium, survive the translation; if they do not, tell me whether the loss is due to a limitation in the target vocabulary or to an assumption in Kant's argument that the framework rightly refuses.
