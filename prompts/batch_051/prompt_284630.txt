An argument has been bugging me since a seminar, and it seems to prove too much and I cant find the exit. (1) Sentience is a matter of degree: a honeybee has some, a crow has more, a dog has alot more, and a thermostat is at zero — it responds to the room but there's nothing it's like to be one. (2) Moral status tracks degree of sentience: how much an entity can be wronged ramps up as sentience does. (3) If sentience is a continuum that hits zero somewhere between the honeybee and the thermostat, then there is no non-arbitrary point on that continuum where status switches from "none" to "a little". (4) A merely arbitrary line can't carry moral weight, so there can't be a real moral difference between the entity just above it and the entity just below it. (5) So either the thermostat has a sliver of moral status — which seems absurd, smashing one isn't even mildly a wrong to it — or the honeybee has none — which also seems absurd, pulling the wings off one looks wrong. (6) Therefore sentience can't be graded in a way that matters morally; there must be a sharp line somewhere after all, even if I can't say where.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), but I have a sinking feeling some step around (3)–(4) is doing all the work and I can't name what's wrong with it. If I reject that step, am I saying moral status floats loose from the underlying facts, or just that "graded" applies to the judgements we're entitled to make rather than to some cutoff in nature? And if I keep (3) and (4) both, which of the earlier claims am I really stuck giving up, and what does that cost?
