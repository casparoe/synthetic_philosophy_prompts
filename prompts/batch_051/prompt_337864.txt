One influential, much-disputed interpretation of the GNU GPL holds that a program which links against a GPL-licensed library — even when the program's own source contains none of the library's code — becomes a derivative work of the library and must itself be distributed under the GPL if it is distributed at all. The same licensing community, however, treats a program that merely invokes a separately distributed GPL program through a socket, a pipe, or a command-line call as an aggregate of two independent works, not a derivative. Take these two verdicts as data about a live practice rather than as settled law. What theory of software-work individuation does this practice presuppose? I want the implicit metaphysics reconstructed: what makes two programs one work here — shared text, the programmer's design choices, runtime interaction, substitutability, or something else — and can one principle draw the linking/communication contrast without gerrymandering? Identify where the practice is underdetermined by the metaphysics, and say what specific compliance decisions a different theory of work individuation would flip. If the contrast cannot be drawn by any principled notion of 'same work,' say that plainly instead of forcing one.
