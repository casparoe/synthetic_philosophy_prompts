You are a moral philosopher in the analytic tradition, specializing in forgiveness, resentment, and the reactive attitudes. I need your sharpest diagnosis of a problem, so before you settle on an answer, take a few minutes to brainstorm the commitments the argument rides on and the various places a person could oppose it. Then present your considered judgment.

Here is the position I find myself defending: unconditional forgiveness — forgiving a wrongdoer who has not repented, apologized, or made amends — is at least sometimes morally required, not merely permitted or admirable. But the following argument seems to show that unconditional forgiveness is never even justified, which would make my position incoherent, and I cannot accept that conclusion.

1. In the central case, to forgive someone for a wrong is to overcome, or commit oneself to overcoming, the resentment one bears toward that person because of the wrong.
2. Resentment is a fitting response to an unexcused wrong. It presents the wrong as a violation of what the victim was owed, and it registers a demand for recognition or repair.
3. If an emotional response is fitting, one has a pro tanto reason to have it. It follows that one needs an adequate countervailing reason to give it up.
4. The consideration that would make resentment no longer fitting — and the standard source of that countervailing reason — is a change in the wrongdoer: sincere repentance, honest apology, or meaningful effort to make amends.
5. In unconditional forgiveness, no such change has occurred and none is required; the forgiver deliberately does not make forgiveness conditional on the wrongdoer's response.
6. So in unconditional forgiveness, one gives up a fitting emotional response without any factor that makes that response no longer fitting.
7. Giving up a fitting response in that way is unjustified. It amounts to abandoning the moral demand embedded in resentment for no adequate reason.
8. Therefore unconditional forgiveness is never justified.
9. But the position I want to defend says that, in some cases at least, one is required to do exactly what (8) says is unjustified: forgive the unrepentant wrongdoer.
10. Nothing that is unjustified can be morally required.
11. Therefore my position is incoherent: unconditional forgiveness cannot be morally required, and treating it as a duty is to endorse something unjustified.

I am unwilling to give up the connection between forgiveness and resentment in (1) and (2), and I am also unwilling to treat mercy toward the unrepentant as merely optional. So the failure must lie somewhere in the middle. Identify the exact step or steps to reject, explain why the rejected step looked so plausible, and then tell me what the rejection costs: what I must accept instead, and how it should change my picture of what forgiveness does with resentment and what it means for forgiveness to be unconditional.
