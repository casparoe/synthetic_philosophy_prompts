Principle 15 of the 1992 Rio Declaration says: "In order to protect the environment, the precautionary approach shall be widely applied by States according to their capabilities. Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation." I've been turning over what this actually commits a state to in a case the drafters pretty clearly werent picturing. Say it's 2035 and a coalition of mostly equatorial countries announces it will begin a modest stratospheric aerosol injection program: a small fleet of high-altitude planes spraying sulfates to shave roughly 0.4 degrees off global average temperature, because heatwaves are killing tens of thousands of their citizens a year and they've lost patience with the pace of emissions cuts. Their own modeling suggests the program could weaken the South Asian monsoon and cause crop failures in countries that never consented, and there's the feared termination-shock problem: if the spraying is ever halted abruptly, temperatures could snap back within a decade or so. So here's what I want to know: does Principle 15, read on its own terms, require, permit, or forbid this — either for the spraying states or for outsiders trying to stop them? Walk through the readings systematically: the plain words, the purpose behind the principle, what a reasonable reader would take the text to mean, and what the drafters would probably say if you could ask them now. I especially want a close look at whether the phrase "cost-effective measures" does real work in the case, and whether the principle pushes toward deploying because of the heat deaths being prevented or against deploying because the intervention itself threatens serious or irreversible damage. If the readings diverge, which one should carry more weight, and why? And does the fact that the countries most exposed to the monsoon risk didn't consent change what the principle demands? For the response, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter.
