I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar in formal epistemology, 14 students, one 80-minute session. We've spent the last two weeks on precise Bayesianism, and I want to run a game that makes them feel the pull of imprecise probabilities — the idea that some bodies of evidence don't pick out a single rational credence, only a set (or interval) of them. I don't want to open with the theory; I want them to stumble into it and then have it named in the debrief.

Design the game for me. I'm picturing something with urns or paper bags and colored chips: a first round of Ellsberg-style choices (one bag with a known 50/50 mix, another with an unknown mix of red and black), then a second round where they have to do something more committal, like post odds or set prices that other students can bet against, so the gap between their buying and selling prices has to be explained. But if you've got a better setup, use it.

Give me exact rules, materials (including how many chips and how the points economy works so no real money changes hands), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the distinction between precise and imprecise credence. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, students turning the pricing round into a game of reading each other rather than reporting their own uncertainty — and how to handle it.

Be frank about what the game cannot capture. In particular, I worry it can't distinguish between a genuine imprecise-credence state, mere indecision, and ambiguity aversion as a taste, so say whether that's true and how to handle it in the debrief.

Finally, tell me which design choice you're least confident is doing real work rather than manufacturing the effect, and what a student would say or do during the debrief that would make you conclude the game is misleading and should be scrapped and replaced with a straight lecture. American English, please.
