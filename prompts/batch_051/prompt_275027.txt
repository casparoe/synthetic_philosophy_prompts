I have recently come to hold a moderately realist version of the New Hume reading of Hume on causation, and I want to know how exposed that position really is. By this I mean: I read Hume as accepting that there are real necessary connections in nature—hidden powers or ultimate principles that are wholly concealed from us—while holding that the only idea of necessary connection we can form comes from experienced constant conjunction, together with an impression of determination that arises in the mind when it passes from an observed event to its usual attendant. On this reading, Hume's repeated denials about necessary connection are denials about our cognitive access, not denials that the world contains such connections. I am not attracted to the strongest unqualified realism, nor to a version on which the hidden connection is an idle posit; I hold the moderate view that the New Hume reading gets the basic shape of the position right without my needing to endorse every use its defenders make of it.

Before I build my own work on this, I want the case against it, organized by how much it should move me. What would have to be true—textually or philosophically—for this reading to be wrong, or for a nearby rival to be clearly better? In particular: which considerations would be fatal rather than merely awkward, e.g., a decisive argument that Hume's theory of ideas rules out reference to powers of which we have no impression, whether by analogy, by relative idea (a conception that fixes reference without supplying positive content), or by any other route? How much weight should I give to the strongly regularity-sounding passages, such as the two definitions of cause in the first Enquiry? Which apparent defeaters count only against a cruder version of the New Hume reading, and can be set aside by someone who holds the moderate version I have described? For each, tell me whether it pushes toward the plain regularity reading, toward a projectivist or quasi-realist reading that explains away realist-sounding passages as something other than assertion of hidden powers, or toward something else, and say what a defender of my position should in fairness concede rather than dismiss.

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated so that no textual evidence could in principle overturn it; if it has, is that a defect in an interpretive thesis of this kind, or is it simply what a well-built exegesis looks like? This question has recently become personally relevant because I reworked a course on causation and found that this reading made a number of passages cohere that I had previously skipped over; that very coherence is part of why I want to know what would count against the reading before I trust it.
