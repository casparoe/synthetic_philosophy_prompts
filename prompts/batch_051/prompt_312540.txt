I write about science and ideas for a living, and I have been trying to think through a line of reasoning I heard from a philosopher while reporting a story on deceptive communication. She begins from a passage in Augustine's *On Lying*, where he writes: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." From that passage she constructs the following argument, which she admits has an unsettling sweep.

(1) On Augustine's definition, the wrong of lying is located in the speaker's state of mind: one lies only if there is a mismatch between what one believes and what one deliberately utters or signals.  
(2) A person who carefully confines herself to statements she believes are true, while arranging her words so that the hearer draws a false conclusion, has not produced that kind of mismatch.  
(3) So, by Augustine's definition, such a person has not lied.  
(4) If she has not lied, then whatever she has done wrong is not the wrong of lying.  
(5) But misleading someone through true statements can be just as manipulative, just as destructive, and just as much an abuse of another person's trust as an outright lie.  
(6) Therefore either Augustine's definition mislocates the wrong, or our ordinary willingness to blame people for misleading by true statements is a moral mistake.

She thinks the argument shows that the familiar contrast between lying and misleading is less stable than moral common sense assumes. I find the steps hard to resist, but the conclusion feels like it proves too much: I do not want to give up either the intuition that outright lying is distinctively wrong or the intuition that careful misleading can be culpable. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Is the weak point the move from Augustine's words to the claim that exploiting an audience's likely inference is not a "sign" of what one has in mind, or the move from "not a lie" to "not the wrong of lying"? Or is the real difficulty elsewhere? Please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you are least sure of.
