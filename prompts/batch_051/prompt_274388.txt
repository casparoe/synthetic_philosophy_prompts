I've been going down a rabbit hole reading the ergodicity economics stuff, and I keep noticing a structural parallel I can't quite bring into focus — I'd like a real verdict on whether it's deep or just a loose analogy. The critique of expected-value reasoning there goes roughly like this: an ensemble average is computed across many players at one time, but an actual person lives a time average over repeated gambles, and when wealth compounds multiplicatively those two come apart. A gamble can have positive expected value while the repeated player's wealth trends toward zero with probability one, because the right growth rate is the geometric (time-average) one, not the arithmetic ensemble mean. The standard illustration is a biased coin-flip bet where expected wealth grows but the typical trajectory shrinks.

I can't shake the feeling that the same shape shows up in a debate in education research about tutoring interventions. A randomized trial reports an average treatment effect measured once at follow-up — that's an ensemble average across students at t=1. But a student actually experiences a time series: a kid who falls behind in year one gets less out of year two, the gap compounds, and there are absorbing-ish states like 'checked out of math forever.' In my head the mapping is: cross-sectional ATE = ensemble average, a single kid's multi-year path = time average, cumulative learning = multiplicative compounding, and disengagement = an absorbing state. That mapping is doing all the work, so if it's wrong the parallel collapses.

So: is this the same mathematical object — non-ergodicity with multiplicative growth and absorbing states — or am I just relabeling treatment-effect heterogeneity? If the analogy held, would the implication actually be that we should optimize each student's geometric mean growth rate rather then the arithmetic mean gain, or does the analogy predict things that are false before it can generate that?
