I have been using a distinction in my own thinking about suffering-focused ethics and want it checked before I lean on it any further. I call the relevant items locking harms. A locking harm is an experience whose disvalue cannot be offset by later positive welfare within the same life, not because the pain is lexically more important, but because the experience changes the subject's evaluative standards in a way the subject would have refused had the offer been made in advance. The central claim is that standard welfare accounting treats well-being as fungible across time within a life, and that this is false for the subclass of harms that operate by replacing the evaluative self that would otherwise perform the accounting. Example: someone is given a drug that permanently lowers her standards, after which she is genuinely content under conditions she would previously have found intolerable. An ordinary total-welfare view says the intervention was good for her if the contentment lasts long enough. On my view, there is an unoffsettable negative entry for the loss of the evaluative self, no matter how much later contentment follows. I do not take this to establish negative utilitarianism; I take it to give suffering-focused views a principled location for their asymmetry without committing them to the claim that no amount of pleasure ever offsets any amount of pain.

What I want is an honest assessment, with the strongest version of the view on the table before you attack it. Specifically: (1) Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse once you notice that many ordinary experiences reshape evaluative standards to some degree? (2) Is it already in the literature under another name? If so, give me the nearest existing view and say precisely where mine departs from it; I care less about the label than about whether the underlying move is sound. (3) Give me the two strongest counterexamples or failure modes: cases where the view would give a clearly wrong verdict, and cases where the unoffsettable entry is just ordinary disvalue relabeled and the concept does no work. (4) Does the view actually deliver the asymmetry it promises, or does it end up licensing a paralyzing conservatism about any intervention that changes preferences, including medical treatment and education?
