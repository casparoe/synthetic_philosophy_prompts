Here are the criteria I take to define a court rather than a mere private mechanism for ending disputes: (1) an independent tribunal, whose decision-maker does not owe her position, pay, or future work to either party; (2) resolution under public, general rules rather than the decision-maker's preferences or a one-off bargain; (3) a meaningful hearing in which each side can present its evidence and test the other's; and (4) decisions that are public and reviewable for error. Now consider a jurisdiction in which every employment and consumer contract requires arbitration. An arbitrator is chosen from a state-approved roster and paid by the company; the individual may challenge one candidate for cause. The arbitrator applies ordinary statutory law, but discovery is limited and awards are confidential. A court may set aside an award only for corruption or evident partiality, not for a mistake of law. The individual may not go to court first. Is this system a court in every sense that matters? Go criterion by criterion; where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say so; and then say whether the case shows that one of the criteria is not doing real work, or that this is a different kind of institution with its own standard of legitimacy.
