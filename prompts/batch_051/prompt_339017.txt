The exam question was:

In The Subjection of Women, Mill argues that the subordination of women cannot be justified by an appeal to what is natural. Reconstruct the main steps of this argument, and clarify the relation among three claims: that the subordination of women is customary, that it is artificial, and that it is wrong.

One student wrote the following answer:

Mill's target is the idea that the subordination of women is justified because it is natural. His first move is to point out that every system of domination has seemed natural to those who benefit from it: slavery, aristocracy, and male rule were each once taken to be simply the way things are. This shows, Mill thinks, that what gets called natural is usually just what is customary; the label "unnatural" is, in most cases, only a way of marking something as unfamiliar or contrary to custom. Since the subjection of women is a universal custom, any departure from it will naturally appear unnatural to those used to it, so the appeal to nature is circular: it takes for granted the very arrangement it is supposed to justify. Mill also insists that the mere fact that an arrangement is long-standing and universal gives it no presumption in its favour: custom cannot by itself justify the subjection of women, and its defenders must produce independent grounds for the inequality. Mill then adds that we cannot even say what the nature of the sexes is, because women have never been allowed to develop outside the system of subordination. What is called the nature of women is really a product of that system, which has suppressed some capacities and cultivated others; under equal institutions the supposed differences might shrink or disappear. From this Mill concludes that the subordination of women is unnatural, and since it is unnatural it is unjust. The fact that it rests on custom rather than nature shows that it has no legitimate basis, and Mill's positive view is that the natural relation between the sexes is one of equality. He also thinks the appeal to nature fails because it slides from how things are to how they ought to be: even if a trait really were natural, that would not by itself make the practices built on it right, which is why defenders of the subordination have to smuggle in the normative conclusion.
