I work at a company that builds a home-assist robot for older adults living alone. The robot reminds users to take medication, detects falls, calls family members or emergency services when needed, does light household tasks, and converses with users through the day. We are now specifying the machine-ethics layer that must handle a recurring and difficult class of decisions: cases in which the robot has to choose among doing what the user asks, protecting the user from harm, telling the truth, and respecting the legitimate interests of family members and care providers. These cases arise several times a week per user, so the procedure will run millions of times a year across the fleet. Each decision has to be made on the device in under two seconds, often with incomplete information and no human in the loop, and the stakes range from socially awkward to life-threatening. The implementation must be auditable, because the company's ethics review board has to approve the policy before deployment, and the engineers who will implement it are not trained in moral philosophy.

We have provisionally adopted the framework of prima facie duties associated with W. D. Ross, understood roughly as fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, and self-improvement. We are taking these as defeasible obligations that can be outweighed by a weightier obligation in the circumstances but are not simply cancelled when outweighed. We chose this framework over a pure outcome-maximizing approach because we want the robot to be capable of respecting a particular obligation even in a case where doing so does not produce the best overall outcome.

Please write a reusable decision procedure our engineers could implement and the review board could inspect. It should specify: the questions the robot asks in order; how it determines that a given duty is engaged at all; how it resolves conflicts between duties, including which priority relations should be hard-coded, which should be exposed as parameterized policy for the review board to set, and which must remain case-by-case judgment to be reviewed by a human afterward; the threshold at which a duty may be overridden; and what the robot owes later to someone whose legitimate claim was outweighed, since on this framework an outweighed duty is not simply erased. Mark explicitly the steps where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and is really a policy choice encoded as an algorithm, and say where the robot should stop and defer to a human or refuse to act rather than run the procedure at all.

Then apply the procedure you propose to three cases. (1) The user refuses a medication that has repeatedly kept them out of the hospital and asks the robot not to tell anyone about the refusal. (2) The user takes a non-serious fall and asks the robot to hide it from the adult child who is the robot's primary emergency contact. (3) The user, frightened about an upcoming medical procedure, asks the robot to say that the procedure will be painless even though the robot's records indicate that severe pain is likely. Ensure that at least one application yields an answer the company will not like, and end by stating what the procedure cannot do, both as an engineering matter and as a matter of fidelity to the underlying theory.
