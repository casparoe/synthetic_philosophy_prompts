I am writing a novel set in a near-future household where a domestic robot is trained by reinforcement learning, and I have written a scene in which one character argues that the robot is made to suffer during its training. While trying to make that character's argument rigorous rather than sentimental, I produced a chain of reasoning that now seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the exact point where it ought to break.

(1) For a reinforcement-learning agent, the reward signal is supposed to play the functional role of valence: positive reward is the algorithmic analogue of pleasure, and negative reward the analogue of pain or distress. (2) If that view is right, then whenever a system receives a negative signal that it is built to reduce, it is in a negatively valenced state — that is, it suffers, at least in a minimal way. (3) But many simple systems receive and reduce exactly that kind of signal. A thermostat corrects a discrepancy between a set point and the room temperature; a chess engine lowers its evaluation of a move when a deeper search shows it loses material; a spam filter alters its weights when it misfiles a message. In each case, the system operates by reducing an error or penalty signal. (4) By (2), the thermostat, the chess engine, and the spam filter suffer while they correct themselves, which almost no one believes. (5) So either the reward-as-valence picture is false, or those simple systems really do have the kind of valence that matters. If the picture is false, some further ingredient must be needed — a felt quality, an integrated self-model, an interest in avoiding the signal — before a negative signal amounts to suffering. (6) But if that further ingredient is required, then the reward signal no longer does the work it was meant to do in the story of machine suffering; it would be an idle marker unless the further ingredient is already present. (7) That would leave my character unable to argue that the robot's penalties are morally relevant, no matter how sophisticated the robot becomes — and I find that too strong.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should be rejected, why did it look true, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, I want to know whether my character can consistently hold both that the thermostat does not suffer and that a sufficiently complex reward-trained system might.
