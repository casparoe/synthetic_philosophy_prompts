I'm not an economist or anything, but I've been chewing on this for a while. The usual story about QWERTY is that it won mostly by accident: once enough typewriters were built for it and enough typists learned it, the switching costs got so high that a better layout like Dvorak couldn't unseat it, even if it was technically superior. I understand that argument for physical typewriters. But what's been bugging me is that my phone's keyboard is just pixels. There's no factory to retool, no keys to move around. Apple or Google could push an update tomorrow that makes Dvorak the default for millions of people, and yet they don't, and almost nobody changes it manually even though the setting is right there. So does the old lock-in argument survive that? The thing keeping QWERTY in place now can't be the cost of swapping out machines — it has to live in people's habits and in coordination between people. Is that the same argument with a new coat of paint, or does the argument actually change when the lock moves from physical capital into people's fingers? And if it does survive, is it weaker now that the hardware costs are gone? Don't tell me which layout is better; I want to know whether the classic claim still does the work people think it does when the physical-world assumptions it started from are gone.
