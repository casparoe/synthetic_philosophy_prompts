Ok so I just came back from a long arguement with my freind about whether you literally see your grandmother when you look at a photograph of her, or whether you just see a flat marked surface and infer that she looked like that. We kept going in circles because we were using different criteria for what counts as "seeing through" a picture, and I want to work this out with a genuinely hard case.

Here are the criteria I've been running with. They're cobbled together from the argument, not from any official source, so just take them as given:

1. Counterfactual dependence: if the scene had been different in some visible way, the image would have been different in a corresponding way, because the scene itself forced the change.
2. Belief-independence: the causal route from scene to image doesn't go through anyone's beliefs or intentions about how to depict the thing. A drawing fails this because the artist decides what to put where; a plain photo passes because optics and the sensor do the work.
3. Perceptual informativeness: the image answers the same kind of open-ended visual questions a direct view answers—what's left of what, what occludes what, whether two things would line up if you moved your eyes, how bright something is compared to its surround.
4. Real particular: the image is of an actual thing or scene at some time, not of a fictional or merely possible object.

Now the case. There's a remote-sensing practice called clear-sky compositing: a satellite images the same patch of ground over and over for days or weeks, the cloudy pixels get masked, and the system stitches together a single cloud-free image. A newer version uses a deep learning model to fill the patches where every pass was occluded. The filled pixels aren't produced by light bouncing off the ground; they're reconstructed from surrounding pixels plus a learned prior about what terrain and buildings tend to look like. The output looks seamless. Most of the pixels really do come from the scene on some date, but in a few patches the model has quietly invented a plausible-looking access road or a building outline that was never actually there, and nobody has verified this because the clouds never cleared during the collection window.

So an analyst looks at the composite and, pointing at one of those filled patches, says "there's a road through the valley." Does the analyst see the road through the image, the way you'd see it through a telescope or a window? Or do they see an image that partly records and partly predicts the scene?
