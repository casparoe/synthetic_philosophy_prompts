Two years ago covid took out most of my sense of smell, and coffee — which used to be my whole personality, basically — now smells like wet cardboard mixed with exhaust. The other night at dinner I mentioned this to two friends, Maya (she's in a neuroscience PhD) and Leo (he did a lot of philosophy of mind). They immediately started arguing about what's actually going on with me. Here's the back and forth, as close as I can remember it.

(1) Maya: It's obvious the experience itself changed. You're getting the same molecules, but the coffee no longer has that round, chocolatey character. It's not that you used to enjoy that character and now you dont — the character is simply gone. That's a change in qualia, not in preferences.

(2) Leo: Too fast. The sensory signal might be perfectly intact and the damage is downstream, in the reward and palatability circuits. Same experience, but the liking response got knocked out. "It tastes fine but I no longer want it" and "it tastes bad" are different things, and you're just assuming the first is wrong.

(3) Maya: But from his point of view those two readings are completely indistinguishable. If you tell him "same taste, new dislike" and I tell him "new taste," nothing in his experience lets him arbitrate. And if no first-person fact settles it, the whole "raw taste" versus "reaction to the taste" split is doing no work.

(4) Leo: "He can't tell from the inside" doesn't mean "there's no difference." You could tease them apart in principle — dissociations between olfactory cortex and palatability pathways, or tests of detection thresholds versus liking ratings. Not being able to introspect a distinction isn't the same as the distinction being unreal.

(5) Maya: But now you've changed the subject to physiology. Qualia were supposed to be the thing that's directly given to the subject. If the subject can't separate a pure quale from his attitude toward it, then the pure quale is a posit you can't individuate — it's Dennett's Chase-and-Sanborn coffee tasters again: one says the brew changed taste, the other says his taste for the brew changed, and neither can tell his own case apart from the other's, which is exactly why Dennett says qualia are incoherent as usually understood.

(6) Leo: That move is too strong. "He can't say which" gives you at most unreliable introspection; it doesn't show there's nothing it's like for him. I agree there's a hard epistemological problem, but you're dressing up a failure of discrimination as an ontological conclusion. And honestly Dennett's coffee example just shows introspection gets the boundary wrong sometimes, not that experience is a myth.

After that it sort of fizzled out because the food came, and I'm left wondering which of them was right about what — and whether they were even arguing about the same thing.
