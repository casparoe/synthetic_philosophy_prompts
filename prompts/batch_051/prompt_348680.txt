I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I may be using some of these terms imprecisely; correct me where the distinction matters. At my company we are designing a system that reads telemetry and incident reports from a production service and produces evaluative judgments: what went wrong, whether a given action was justified in the circumstances, what the responsible party should have considered, and so on. One module, which I'll call the judgment module, is deliberately isolated from the rest of the system. Its output is written to an append-only store that no actuator, scheduler, or downstream service is permitted to read, and for any of its judgments to affect the world a human must manually copy a judgment out of the store and decide to act on it. That quarantine is a deliberate safety property, not an oversight.

Around this design the same argument has been recurring for months without moving. One camp says the system plainly is not a moral agent, and arguably not an agent at all, because a moral agent must be able to act on its judgments, to intervene or refrain or at least try, and this system is architecturally prevented from doing any of that; it is a moral spectator at most. A second camp says this confuses moral agency with causal effectiveness: the system forms judgments about what ought to be done, which is the hard part, while the ability to execute those judgments matters for whether we blame or punish the system, not for whether it is a moral agent in the first place, just as a person with locked-in syndrome remains a moral agent even when unable to move. A third camp says the argument is an artifact of the word "agent," which we inherited from human cases in which perceiving, judging, and acting come bundled; once an engineered system unbundles them, the word simply has no settled application.

I am not asking which camp wins. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal or substantive; about the facts of the system, about standards for applying a concept, or about which capacities matter for moral agency; or perhaps several camps answering different questions under a single heading. Given that diagnosis, what would count as progress? A distinction that dissolves the dispute, a modification to the system that would test one side's claim, or an argument that no progress should be expected. My own untutored hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about where to draw the boundaries of "agent," but I would like that hypothesis examined rather than ratified.
