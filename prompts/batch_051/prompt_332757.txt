I cover ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I keep tripping over the phrase "the wrong kind of reasons" in metaethics without really getting what the "wrong" is doing. Give me a working definition of what makes something a reason for an attitude — belief, admiration, fear, amusement — and run it through these cases: a structural engineer's report that the bridge is unsafe (surely a reason to believe it's unsafe); a pill that would make you believe the bridge is unsafe with no evidence at all; a tyrant who offers you a fortune to admire him and threatens to shoot you if you don't; Pascal's wager, considered as a reason to believe in God; and a friend who says he'll be hurt unless you geniunely find his joke funny. My hunch is that "reason" is doing two jobs here and that the "wrong kind" ones aren't really reasons, but I can't articulate what the two jobs are. Give me the definition, apply it to all five, and tell me which of your verdicts you're least confident about.
