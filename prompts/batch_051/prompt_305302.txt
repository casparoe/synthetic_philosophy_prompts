Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading my way into philosophical theology, and I find myself returning to a question that seems undertreated: what would a theory of scriptural inspiration have to do to make sense of the fact that communities have treated translated texts as inspired, rather than as merely imperfect stand-ins for an inspired original? The historical data pull in several directions at once. The Septuagint, the early Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, appears to have functioned as scripture for Greek-speaking Jewish communities, and New Testament writers frequently take up the older texts in forms that align with it rather than with the Hebrew textual tradition as later standardized. At another pole, some traditions treat a particular vernacular translation—most prominently the King James Version, in what is often called KJV-onlyism—as itself the inspired or authoritative text, while other traditions regard every translation as derivative and defeasible. Meanwhile, the philological side of my old scientific habits keeps reminding me that every translation is already an interpretive act, that languages do not carve up meaning along the same joints, and that the manuscript traditions behind any putatively original text are themselves plural and editorial.

What I want is not a survey of existing positions but the specification: what must an adequate theory of inspiration achieve if it is to handle translated texts without collapsing into either pure textual positivism, on which only a particular wording is inspired, or pure content-ism, on which only a detachable meaning is inspired and the linguistic form is incidental? Please lay out the desiderata such a theory must meet, and for each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, marking which are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of particular theological camps. In particular, identify the distinctions the theory may not collapse—for instance, between translation as mere transmission and translation as a new act of composition; between the authority of a text and the authority of a particular linguistic form; and between what a text says and what a community in fact received across centuries. Then tell me which pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement would do the most work in excluding the main available proposals. A table for the requirements is acceptable, but the reasoning behind each entry is what I actually want.
