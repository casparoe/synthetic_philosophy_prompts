I'm running a small reading group on decision theory, and we got stuck last week on Pascal's mugging. The framing we kept returning to: a mugger says, "Give me $10 right now, or I will use powers I claim to have to make 3↑↑↑3 people (a number so large it is beyond astronomical) each live through a lifetime of torture; if you pay, he'll spare them." Since the claimed harm is so enormous, even a vanishingly small probability that the mugger can deliver seems to swamp the certain loss of $10, and that feels crazy.

Our group has been collecting objections to taking the mugger seriously, and I want a more thorough list then the usual ones. So far I have:
- The complexity-prior objection: a rational prior is not independent of the size of the claim. A hypothesis this baroque deserves a prior so small that it undercuts the huge payoff.
- Bounded utility: the disvalue of suffering doesn't grow without limit the way the mugger pretends, so the threatened outcome never actually yields the advertised amount of utility.
- The many-muggers problem: by the same logic you should pay any of infinitely many muggers making incompatible demands, so the policy gives no real guidance.
- The escalation problem: paying now doesn't close the book; the mugger can immediately come back with an even bigger number, so you're policy never terminates.

These all feel right, but I want one more objection that is structurally distinct — not a rewording of bounded utility (so "risk aversion" is out), not a special case of the complexity-prior point (so "just set the probability to zero" is out), and not a combination of any two items above. It also shouldn't be a general complaint about expected utility theory; it has to bite specifically at the mugging. Then tell me honestly whether the new objection is actually any good, or whether it's clever but doesn't really stop the mugger.

Please organize your response as:
- "The new objection" (200–250 words): state the objection fully.
- "Why it's not a variant" (100–150 words): explain how it differs from each of the four listed objections.
- "Honest assessment" (100–150 words): say whether it's a genuinely independent problem for the mugging or mostly a rhetorical reframe.
