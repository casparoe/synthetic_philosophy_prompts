I teach a Saturday enrichment workshop on competition, character, and cooperation for high-school students — usually 16 students, ages 16 to 17 — and I want to fill a 75-minute block with an activity rather than a talk. The students arrive with a canned view: competition either builds character (grit, excellence, fair play) or corrodes it (cutthroatness, cheating, envy). I want them to feel, before they hear it, that this question is underspecified — that the same people, in the same room, doing the same task, will behave quite differently when the scoring structure changes from a positional contest to a non-positional one. Design a runnable activity with at least two phases. In the first phase, the scoring should be zero-sum or rank-based in a way that makes helping a competitor straightforwardly costly. In the second phase, keep the task, the groups, and the personnel identical, but change only the scoring rule so that being useful to others, sharing know-how, or raising a partner's score is also the best way to raise your own. Present the phases as rounds of a single game, so the shift in the students' own behavior — not my framing — becomes the evidence. Include full rules and materials; a payoff scheme that does not use real money (if food rewards, make them optional and give non-food alternatives); a timeline within 75 minutes; debrief questions that first have students report what they did and how they felt before any vocabulary is introduced; then a mapping from observed moments to the distinction between rivalrous or positional competition (where the good is relative rank or scarce reward) and non-rivalrous or non-positional competition (where the good is improvement, skill, or joint success at the activity); and finally a statement of the view I want to test on them — that one familiar kind of competition can be internal to a practice and cultivate virtues, while another kind makes the practice an instrument and can corrode virtues — a contrast close to Alasdair MacIntyre's internal versus external goods, though I will introduce his name only afterwards. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (for example, students refusing to win at others' expense, or gaming the second phase), how to handle each failure without blunting the point, and be explicit about which part of the broad question whether competition builds or corrodes character the activity cannot settle and what I should say myself to close that gap.
