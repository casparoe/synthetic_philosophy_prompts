After close to a decade reading the EA Forum, LessWrong, and the adjacent rationalist-adjacent blogosphere, I have been asked to give a working-paper talk to the research staff of a small effective-altruism-aligned grantmaking foundation, and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a general defence of my view.

The paper defends a version of the institutional critique of effective altruism, centred on earning to give and measurability bias. My thesis is that the movement's core epistemic practices — explicit expected-value modelling in cause prioritisation, and the continued status given to earning-to-give pathways — jointly select for interventions whose benefits are individually attributable, monetisable, and legible on near-term planning horizons, and select against structural, capacity-building, and norm-shifting work whose contribution can only be assessed through trained judgement. The move I most want to defend is that this is not a measurement problem to be fixed with better data, another parameter, or a term for upside; it is a constitutive feature of a method that treats unmeasurable contribution as unrankable, and unrankable as not choiceworthy in practice. I therefore argue that explicit expected-value estimates should be demoted from a gatekeeping role to an input, and that a substantial class of grant decisions should instead be made through structured expert judgement and adversarial review.

The audience is the reason I am asking for help. It is not a general academic seminar. The room will be roughly ten researchers from the foundation: mostly economists and philosophers who do explicit cause prioritisation for a living, two longtermist researchers working on AI safety, and the director, who came up through global health. They have read the standard outside critiques — that the movement is technocratic, that it ignores structural change, that it is captured by particular kinds of money — and largely regard those critiques as aimed at a strawman or at the movement of 2015. They already think in terms of expected value of information, worldview diversification, hits-based giving, and moral uncertainty. They will not be impressed by the claim that quantification is difficult; they believe they have already internalised that and built around it.

Give me the objections I will actually get from this audience, in roughly the order they will arise, ordered by the mix of likelihood and damage, each phrased the way one of those researchers would put it. For each objection: its real force, what I should concede immediately, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply, with a note on where the reply is soft. Before the objections, give me the single concession I should make in the first two minutes to stop the cheap versions of these objections from eating the question time. And name the one question I am hoping nobody asks, along with the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

I am most worried about two moves in my argument, so make sure the objections and replies address them. The first is the move from the claim that the method is biased to the proposal to replace or constrain the method: my audience will say that the proposed replacement is at least as biased, but less transparently so. The second is the charge that earning to give is now a minor part of the movement, so my argument reduces to a local point about one subcommunity rather than an institutional critique.
