I'm a novelist. My current book is set in a town where the local "founding day" pageant turns out to have been invented in 1962 by the Chamber of Commerce, but at a town meeting somebody waves that off with "doesn't matter, it's tradition." It got me interested in the bigger version of the same sentence: people say "our nation is ancient" or "we've always done it this way" and treat that as an argument about borders, authority, who belongs. I want a scene where another character actually takes the sentence apart instead of just rolling their eyes. So here's what I need. When someone says "it's tradition" or "we've always been this way," what are the distinct claims they could be making—about how old the practice is, whether its age gives a reason to keep it, whether it's genuinely ours, whether it makes us who we are? For each reading, I'd like to know if it's false, trivially true, unfalsifiable, or a defensible reason, and which of those readings would actually support the work the slogan is being made to do. A menu of readings with verdicts would be ideal; I dont need one right answer.
