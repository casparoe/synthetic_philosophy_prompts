I have spent enough time in political philosophy seminars and in the surrounding literature on Rawls's later work to notice that the debate about his treatment of religion has reached a peculiar impasse, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of first-order arguments. The two camps, as I understand them, run roughly as follows. Defenders of Rawls maintain that his view does not require religious citizens to hide their deepest commitments. What public reason restricts is only the kind of justification that may be offered for coercive political decisions in the public political forum, and it applies even-handedly to secular comprehensive doctrines as well as religious ones. Moreover, the proviso is meant to be accommodating: citizens may introduce religious considerations into public political debate provided that suitable public reasons are eventually offered in support of the position. Critics respond that this is still a demand for translation into an idiom that secular citizens will accept, that the symmetry is merely formal because secular reasons are treated as the common currency while religious reasons are always on probation, and that the entire framework presupposes a contestable ideal of democratic citizenship rather than anything both sides share. Both camps seem to agree on the main points of what Rawls actually says, and both claim to care about fairness to religious citizens and about the stability of a pluralist democracy, and yet neither side has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about any of those surface issues but about the baseline: which kinds of consideration in a religiously plural democracy should be treated as needing justification, and which as the default. But I am not confident, and I can imagine other diagnoses. Is this a substantive moral disagreement about what civic respect or fairness requires, a verbal or taxonomical disagreement about what should count as a religious reason or a public reason, two camps answering different questions, one exegetical and one normative, under a single heading, or a dispute that is ultimately empirical, turning on claims about what institutional arrangements actually preserve stability and mutual trust? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
