Hey — I'm a novelist, and I'm trying to work out the emotional logic of a scene before I write it. Could you write me a short story, maybe 800 to 1,200 words, that dramatizes the sunk cost fallacy, but not the usual just-walk-away-the-money's-already-gone version?

Here's the character I keep coming back to: an old woman, a once-respected shipwright on a shrinking island, who has spent most of her life and her late husband's insurance payout on a wave-powered fishing boat she was sure would change the harbor. Its never worked for more than a few days at a time, and the town treats her as a cautionary tale. Now her sister has offered her a room on the mainland, and a scrapyard will take the hull for almost nothing. Ordinary sunk-cost thinking says she should sell and leave, because the past costs are gone either way.

But I want the story to push against that, or at least complicate it hard. Ive been seduced by the thought that honoring sunk costs isn't always irrational: sometimes a sunk cost works as a commitment device, because if you always quit once a project dips, you can't bind your future self to anything expensive, and no one around you takes your promises seriously when it gets hard. And sometimes the past investment is load-bearing for your identity — the cost isn't really sunk because it's still doing work as the person you became. I don't fully trust these ideas, so the story should stress-test them rather than just illustrate them.

Make her self-aware enough to argue both sides in her own head — she knows the textbook line and still can't act on it — but do reach a real ending, not a shrug. I care less whether she stays or goes than that the ending makes a genuine case one way or the other.
