I'm writing an essay about what actually vanishes when a tight-knit community comes apart — a neighborhood, a congregation, a union local, that kind of thing. The standard account says people lose belonging, or a sense of identity, or simply having people around who care about them. I've been trying to push past that. My current thesis is that the deeper loss is something I'm provisionally calling tacit coordination: the background of shared habits, local knowledge, and unspoken expectations that lets people cooperate without constantly negotiating, monitoring, or formalizing things. In a stable community a lot of cooperation just runs on default settings — you don't have to ask whether a neighbor will grab your mail while you're away or whether a small favor needs to be repaid on the spot. When the community dissolves, that infrastructure disappears, and my worry is that it can't simply be rebuilt by joining new voluntary groups, because voluntary groups usually need explicit rules and trust-building in a way older communities didn't.

What I'm stuck on is this. A friend read my notes and said the framing makes community sound like a mere instrument — social capital, as if the only reason to miss it were that it made life cheaper and more conveniant. I want to resist that, but I'm not sure I can. My move so far has been to say that the feeling of belonging is partly constituted by having that tacit infrastructure: you belong somewhere precisely when you can act on those unspoken expectations without second-guessing yourself. But then the essay may end up with no real distinction between belonging and tacit coordination, just two descriptions of the same thing.

Is that a problem, or should I just own it and make it the point? Also, does the tacit coordination framing leave out something people actually mourn when a community dies — like being known as a particular person with a history, not just a reliable node in a network? And where do you think the weakest step in the argument is, as I've described it?
