I am a working evolutionary biologist, and the machinery I am most comfortable with is that of inclusive fitness, coefficients of relatedness, reciprocal altruism, costly signaling, and multilevel selection. I keep returning to a debate in moral philosophy that I half-understand and would like to see translated into my own terms. On one side, there is the claim that morality, or at least the kind of impartial justification that matters, demands that we not weight our own kin, friends, or comrades more heavily than strangers when deciding who gets helped. From that point of view, even a parent who saves his own child instead of two other children he could have saved is, at best, indulging a pardonable bias. On the other side, there is the claim that this gets things exactly backwards: that particular attachments are not noise around the edge of the moral point of view but are part of what gives us reasons in the first place, and that a person who treated his spouse or his friend as merely one interchangeable bearer of interests would be failing precisely as a spouse or a friend. The partialist side tends to insist that the impartialist is asking us to perform a kind of alienation from the very relationships that make a human life worth living.

What I would like is a translation, not a summary. Restate this dispute in the vocabulary I actually use, kin selection, reciprocal exchange, group-level pressures, evolutionarily stable strategies, and the distinction between proximate and ultimate explanation, as faithfully as you can. Do not pretend that every philosophical claim has a tidy biological analog. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the argument translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which parts you had to fudge or distort to make fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all. Finally, tell me whether that residue is pointing to something important that my framework cannot say, or whether it mostly marks spots where the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it looks.

I want the response written as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
