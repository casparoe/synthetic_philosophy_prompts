I have been turning over the question of how to assess scientific models whose assumptions are known to be unrealistic, and I find myself pulled between a position I find compelling and an intuition I cannot get rid of. The view I have in mind is the instrumentalist position often linked with Milton Friedman’s essay on positive economics, the idea sometimes called the F-twist: since every model abstracts, the realism of a model’s assumptions is beside the point, and the only relevant test is whether the model yields sufficiently accurate predictions about the phenomena it targets. On this view, asking whether households really solve infinite-horizon optimisation problems or whether firms really maximise profit is a category mistake; models are tools for prediction, not descriptions of mechanisms. The view has real virtues: it provides a single, non-arbitrary criterion for choosing among models, it explains why rival models of the same system can both be useful, and it refuses to let science sink into interminable disputes about whether assumptions are plausible.

At the same time, I cannot shake the intuition that something is lost when a model’s central assumptions are false in ways that matter to the case at hand. Suppose a macroeconomic model represents an entire economy as one representative agent, with no money, no default, no heterogeneous firms and no coordination failures, and then uses that model to explain why a financial crisis occurred and to recommend policy. I can accept that such a model might forecast an aggregate series reasonably well. But I find myself unable to accept that it explains the crisis, or that its forecasts license policy, because the mechanisms it attributes to the world are not the mechanisms that actually operated. The intuition is that prediction and explanation are different achievements, and that predictive success cannot compensate for a false story about how the world works when an explanatory or practical conclusion is being drawn from it.

I would like you to present both positions at their strongest and tell me which should yield, or whether there is a reading on which both survive. The instrumentalist will say my intuition is a demand for picturable truth and that explanation should itself be understood instrumentally, as unification, compression or a reliable rule for intervention. The intuition insists that a model which gets the causal structure wrong has not explained anything, however well it tracks the data. I want to know what would justify each move: what would it take for the instrumentalist theory to be amended or abandoned, what would it take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, and whether one can draw a principled line between acceptable idealisations that isolate a real factor, such as frictionless planes in physics, and useful fictions that replace the target system altogether. Is it coherent, for example, to separate contexts of forecasting, explanation and policy so that both commitments survive? Please answer in British English.
