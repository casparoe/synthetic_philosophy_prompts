I recently noticed what looks like a structural parallel between Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion and the debate over whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering. In Parfit's puzzle, total-welfare aggregation seems to require preferring an enormous population of lives barely worth living to a smaller population of very good lives. Conservation practice, by contrast, tends to treat more habitat, and therefore more wild animals, as close to an unqualified good. But if many wild lives are on balance worse than nonexistence — short lives dominated by predation, starvation, disease, or exposure — then the aggregative logic appears to reverse: more habitat would mean more lives barely worth avoiding, and habitat destruction would prevent those lives. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I merely pattern-matching across the word population? If the parallel is real, what would have to be true about the sign and distribution of wild animal welfare for it to hold, and where does it first break? Give me one concrete claim the parallel seems to predict that you take to be false, and say whether that breaks the analogy or merely complicates it.
