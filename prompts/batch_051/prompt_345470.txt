I am organizing a graduate seminar on moral worth, and I want to avoid the usual situation in which each account is assessed mainly by the one case it was designed to handle. Before we adopt any account of moral worth, I want the specification of what such a theory actually has to get right. By moral worth I mean the distinctive credit an action earns when the agent does the right thing for the right reasons, rather than credit owed simply because the action turned out to be right. Please give me a full requirements list: the cases any adequate theory must classify correctly, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the psychological or epistemic constraints it must respect. To seed the list, consider at least these four cases: the prudent merchant who gives correct change because it is good for business; the person who does the right thing while sincerely believing, on the basis of general principles, that the action is wrong; the dutiful agent who acts only out of concern for the action's rightness and not out of concern for the person helped; and the agent whose helpful act is overdetermined by both the right-making motive and a self-interested one. For each requirement you propose, state the case or consideration that forces it, indicate whether it is a genuine desideratum or merely the preference of one family of views, and mark it as negotiable or non-negotiable. Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating the standard accounts of moral worth, and say whether any two of the requirements are in tension with one another. A table for the requirements is welcome, as long as the reasoning behind each entry is explicit.
