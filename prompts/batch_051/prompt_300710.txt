I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'll state this in terms I know. For about a year a reading group of engineers and scientists I'm in has kept returning to an argument that never moves. The question is why some fields accumulate reliable technique while others stall, and specifically whether tight feedback loops explain the difference.

One side — mostly the engineers — argues that fields advance when a claim can be routed quickly to an unambiguous failure signal. A compiler rejects a type error before the code runs; a test suite turns a regression into a red build; an experiment either matches a predicted measurement or it doesn't. The cost of being wrong is paid immediately and locally, so bad ideas die fast and good ones compound. Fields without that, where essays never "fail" in any mechanical sense, drift into schools, prestige, and claims that can never be shown wrong.

The other side says this has the causality backwards. You can only build the type checker, the test, or the experiment once the domain has already been formalized enough that you know what a failure looks like. The feedback loop is thus a product of progress, not its engine. They add that feedback can be locally clear and globally misleading: tests pass while the system rots along an untested dimension, metrics get gamed, A/B tests optimize a proxy while the real outcome drifts. And they point to fields with little empirical feedback that still progress — mathematics through proof, and even philosophy by making distinctions stick. So clear feedback is, they claim, neither necessary nor sufficient for progress.

A year in, nobody has moved. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who wins. Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'progress' — accumulated technique versus explanatory understanding? Is one side reasoning in a circle, since clear feedback both causes and is caused by formalization, so their examples are preselected to fit? Are the two sides drawing on different time scales or different base rates — decades of software versus centuries of science — so they are hardly talking about the same cases? Is there a hidden empirical question about how a field gets its first spec before it can have a checker, and is that question even answerable? My own working hypothesis is that the two camps mean different things by 'feedback' — one an automated check against a spec, the other contact with reality — and that much of the standoff is people talking past one another. I'd like that hypothesis tested, not assumed.

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually says, and then say what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a comparison of fields, a counterexample, or an argument that the question is ill-posed. Finally, tell me what would change your diagnosis — which consideration or evidence would make you switch to a different account of what kind of disagreement this is, and where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
