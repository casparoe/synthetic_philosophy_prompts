For a unit in my sociology class on how groups handle conflict, we have been working with three ideal types that come up in nearly everything we read: honor cultures, in which people experience slights as attacks on their standing and feel bound to answer them personally; dignity cultures, in which people are taught that their worth is independent of others' opinions, so insults should be absorbed, ignored, or handed to an impartial authority; and victimhood cultures, in which people respond to harm by seeking public recognition of their suffering and validation from third parties rather than by direct retaliation. Our assignment is to analyze a concrete proposal the way a policy designer would, tracing incentives and failure modes instead of simply arguing for or against it. The proposal comes from our student council and would replace ordinary discipline for student-to-student conflicts with an online grievance ledger.

Under the proposal, every student receives a monthly allowance of twelve points. To make an accusation, a student must post a statement naming the accused and stake at least three points. A panel of eight students, chosen by lot each week, hears both sides and votes in secret on whether the grievance is significant. If a majority says it is significant, the post stays on the public board for the rest of the term, the accused must publish a one-paragraph acknowledgment that cannot contest the facts as described, and the accuser keeps the stake and receives a bonus of three times the staked points. If the panel votes it down, the post is removed and the accuser loses the stake. The accused cannot file a separate grievance about the same incident but may spend points to mark the post "disputed," which halves its visibility on the feed. Points cannot be bought, sold, or transferred, but the five students with the highest totals each term get first choice of courses and an exemption from study-hall duty.

I want to understand how this mechanism would actually behave for three different students, one whose instincts are honor-based, one dignity-based, and one oriented toward victimhood. What does the ledger push each of them to do in the first week, and how would their behavior change after a year of living under it? Where is it most likely to be gamed first, and by whom? What second-order effects would it have on friendships, social groups, and the school's broader norms about conflict? And does the way the system plays out give any reason to think one of the three cultural frameworks is morally superior, or does it mainly show that each is adapted to different conditions and that any fixed rule will tend to be captured by one of them?
