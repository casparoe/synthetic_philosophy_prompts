I run model validation at a mid-size insurance company. Every time one of our teams updates a catastrophe-risk or pricing model, I'm the one who has to decide wether the model's behavior can be explained in plain English to our board and the state regulators, or whether we're basically saying "we ran a simulation and that's the answer." I keep hitting the same wall: some models have a short, clean story — a few drivers, maybe a formula — but others only make sense as the whole interacting mess, and any summary we write either misleads or is just a restatement of the code.

A colleague pointed me to the idea of computational irreducibility — roughly, that some systems have no shortcut: to know what they'll do, you have to effectively run them, so no compact explanation exists even in principle, not just for us. That reframed the problem, but I don't know how to apply it day to day.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure for my team. When a model only makes sense as a simulation, how should we decide whether to keep hunting for a compressed explanation, accept that the simulation is the explanation, or flag the model as something we can't sign off on? The procedure has to be usable by analysts who don't care about philosophy, it has to take under an hour per model, and it has to hold up when regulators push back — my boss signs off on whatever I write, but she won't read anything long.

Give me exactly seven numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make the items the procedure itself: the tests to run in order, then two worked mini-cases — make one of them a case where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like — and end with what the procedure can't settle. Use American English.
