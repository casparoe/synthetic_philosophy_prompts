What is your actual credence in the following proposition, and how does the number arise from its parts?

Proposition. Suppose a two-year league is run in which adult novices are randomly assigned to argue for or against contested factual or policy claims shortly before each match. Matches are judged by trained lay judges using a rubric that awards points only for: accurately restating the opponent's strongest case before attacking it; answering the strongest objections actually raised rather than evading them; attaching explicit probability ranges to the factual claims one relies on; and conceding a sub-claim of one's own once the opponent has challenged it successfully, rather than continuing to defend it. Winners are ranked on a skill ladder, and opponents are matched by rating. A comparison group of similar adults spends the same hours discussing the same questions informally, without scoring or ranking. The proposition is that, after two years, the league group scores substantially higher than the comparison group on a battery of new, unannounced questions measuring calibrated reasoning: accuracy of probability assignments, responsiveness to evidence, and the ability to state the strongest argument against one's own view.

I want the number built up in public, not a bare guess. Decompose the proposition into the sub-questions it depends on—including at least the following, which you may modify or supplement:

a. That the rubric as specified rewards truth-conducive argumentative behavior rather than the familiar package of rhetorical skill and conversational dominance.
b. That trained lay judges can apply the rubric reliably, without being systematically swayed by confidence, fluency, or likeability.
c. That two years of scored practice produces a durable, transferable improvement in reasoning on new questions, rather than a narrow skill at the game or no effect.
d. That the competitive incentives—being assigned a side by coin flip, caring about winning, defending positions one does not hold—do not cause participants to reason worse outside the game, for example by bending their reasoning toward whatever side serves them or by making their prior views more extreme, to a degree that cancels or outweighs the gains.
e. That the comparison group does not spontaneously acquire much of the same improvement, so that the between-group difference survives.
f. That the operationalization of improvement—calibrated accuracy, evidence responsiveness, and owning the strongest counterargument—captures what actually matters in the claim that argumentation-as-sport improves reasoning, so that the proposition is not settled by a definitional choice.

Assign each a rough probability with a line or two of justification, show how you combine them into a final credence in the proposition, and then give the final number. Then state which term your final number is most sensitive to, what evidence would move you most on that term, and where you would locate a disagreement between someone at 0.35 and someone at 0.70 on the proposition. For calibration: I put myself around 0.35, while a colleague I respect is near 0.70, and I want to know not so much who is right as which hidden sub-question we are really disagreeing about.
