A friend has been pressing the following argument on me, and I cannot find the flaw even though I dislike where it leaves us.

(1) The aesthetic value of a work is fixed by what a suitably informed perceiver can see in it; facts about its history matter only insofar as they show up in the work's perceptible features.
(2) A perfect forgery is, by hypothesis, indistinguishable from the original under any perceptual examination: it presents exactly the same appearance.
(3) Therefore a perfect forgery has exactly the same aesthetic value as the original.
(4) The original and the perfect forgery differ only in their histories: who made them, when, and with what intentions and achievements.
(5) If that historical difference could make an aesthetic difference, then two works could present identically and yet differ aesthetically, which contradicts (1).
(6) So the preference for an original over a perfect forgery cannot be an aesthetic matter; it must rest on non-aesthetic concerns such as provenance, rarity, investment, or snobbery.

I am not sure I can accept (6), because the argument seems to prove too much. It would force me to say that learning that a painting I admired is a forgery can never change my aesthetic judgement of it, only my estimate of its price or pedigree; and that the originality of an artist's achievement can never itself count as an aesthetic merit. Yet when I imagine learning that a work I love is a fake, it does not feel as though merely market value has been lost.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If I must choose a premise to reject, tell me which premise to reject, why it looked so plausible, and what the main positions on fakes and originals each have to pay for their preferred rejection. If instead you think the argument is sound, explain why I should accept those costs.
