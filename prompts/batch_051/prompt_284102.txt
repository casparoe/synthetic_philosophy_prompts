I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and for the past few months I have been reporting a long feature on institutional memory: who gets into the archive, who falls out of it, and what the gaps themselves tell us. The term I keep reaching for is archival silence, but it is doing too much work, and I need to force it into some discipline before I build an 8,000-word piece on it.

I do not want a summary of the scholarly literature; I want a working definition that can survive being run through the cases I am actually weighing. The tension pulling on the term comes from two directions. Sometimes an archival silence seems to mean that something was never recorded because the people with the power to record did not think it worth recording. Other times it seems to mean that something was recorded and then omitted, suppressed, destroyed, or made unfindable. A third set of cases sits between those: the person appears in the archive, but only through a hostile or indifferent administrative grid. I am not sure one definition covers all three.

Here are the cases.

1. A mid-19th-century farmworker kept detailed journals and correspondence through decades of local strikes and early cooperative organizing. After her death, a relative offered the papers to a county repository. The board declined on the grounds that she was not a person of historical importance, and the papers were later thrown away.

2. A 15th-century city chronicle omits the reign of a deposed prince after the new council demands the omission. A single copy of the earlier account survives in a monastery library elsewhere in the same city, so the events were not lost to the city as a whole.

3. In a 1960s psychiatric hospital, each patient appears as a line in a complete admission ledger: name, date, diagnosis, discharge or death. The ledger records nothing the patients said, wanted, or understood.

4. In the 1990s a newspaper digitized its back issues but skipped the women's pages because the company considered them low-value filler. A 1950s women's-page column that contains one of the few local accounts of a housing-desegregation campaign is now absent from the digital archive; the microfilm exists in a subbasement and can be consulted in person.

5. A security service compiled a thick file on a 1970s activist, built from informants' reports and surveillance. The holdings about her are abundant but written entirely by people watching her.

6. A community has maintained a century of its history in ceremony, story, and song; the state archive holds almost nothing about it.

7. A city hall's building permits from 1923 vanished when the basement flooded. The loss was hydraulic, not a policy: no one selected those records for destruction.

I have fixed views only at the ends: the first is an archival silence in any useful sense of the term, and the last is not one. The middle five are where the term gets away from me, though I am inclined to say the surveillance file is not a silence even if it is something else that is wrong.

So: propose a working definition of archival silence, run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, revising it or defending the surprising verdict as you go. Finish by saying which of your verdicts is least secure.
