Last term I took over as moderator of our university's interdisciplinary seminar on minds and machines, which mixes computer scientists, philosophers, and a steady stream of members of the public. I have settled into a screening policy. When someone sends the list an argument that a human capacity—mathematical insight, creative understanding, moral judgment—lies beyond computation in principle, I decline to circulate it unless it either rests on a formal uncomputability or complexity-theoretic result, or states the claim precisely enough to be tested. In close cases I lean on the Church-Turing thesis as part of the background: any procedure that is effective in the relevant sense can be run by a Turing machine, so the burden falls on the person who denies computational realizability to say where the halting problem or a hardness result enters. A colleague, a philosopher of physics, tells me I am misusing the thesis. He says the Church-Turing thesis concerns the coincidence of an informal notion, the effectively computable function, with a formal one, the Turing-computable function; it does not state that the brain, or any other physical system, is limited to Turing-level computation, and it does not settle which human capacities are effective procedures in the first place. I can see his point, but I cannot referee every submission, and the policy has kept the list from being overrun. Is my shortcut defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as methodological hygiene? If it is not defensible as it stands, is there a nearby rule that preserves the filtering benefits without smuggling in a stronger claim than the thesis supports?
