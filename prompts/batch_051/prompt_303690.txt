At a meeting of our decision-theory graduate seminar, I found myself defending an argument that now seems to me to prove far too much. I cannot locate the slip, and I would like to know which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A gamble is rationally evaluable only if it has a well-defined expected value, and two gambles can be rationally compared only when both have well-defined expected values.

(2) The expected value of a gamble is its probability-weighted average over the possible states: EV = Σ_s p_s v_s. For countably many states, this is the limit of the partial sums in the standard ordering of the states.

(3) In the Pasadena game, a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the player receives (-1)^(n+1)(2^n/n) dollars. The states are n = 1, 2, 3, ..., with probability p_n = 2^{-n}. The expected value is therefore Σ_n (2^{-n})((-1)^(n+1)2^n/n) = Σ_n (-1)^(n+1)/n.

(4) That sum is conditionally convergent: in the natural ordering it converges to ln 2, but its positive and negative parts each diverge, so its terms can be rearranged to converge to any real number or to diverge.

(5) Therefore the Pasadena game has no unique expected value determined by its probabilities and payoffs alone; any proposed value is an artifact of the ordering of the states.

(6) Now consider the Altadena game, which is exactly like Pasadena except that in every state it pays one dollar more. Since Altadena pays $1 more than Pasadena no matter when the coin first lands heads, Altadena must be strictly preferable to Pasadena.

(7) But by (1) and (5), Pasadena has no well-defined expected value, so expected-value reasoning cannot license any comparison between Pasadena and Altadena.

(8) Hence the argument forces us either to deny that Altadena is strictly preferable to Pasadena, to abandon the principle that rational comparison requires well-defined expected value, or to revise the account of infinite expected value given in (2).

The first option strikes me as absurd; I would sooner keep (6) than (1). But I want to know exactly which premise or step goes wrong, why it looked correct, and what a repaired decision theory would have to say about conditionally convergent expected values. In particular, can dominance comparisons of this sort be retained without restoring a unique expected value for Pasadena?
