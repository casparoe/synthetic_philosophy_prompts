I have been rereading Descartes’s remarks about animals, and I keep running into what looks like a real tension between two texts. In Part Five of the Discourse on Method he argues from the human capacity for open-ended, appropriately responsive speech to the conclusion that animals do not think at all. Even a disturbed or defective human being, he says, can arrange signs and respond to what is said, whereas no animal does any such thing; so the apparently clever behavior of animals must be traced to the disposition of their organs, just as a clock’s behavior is traced to its wheels and springs. That sounds like the notorious doctrine that animals are mere machines, with thought absent rather than merely unknown.

Yet in a 1649 letter to Henry More he seems to soften this. There he grants that, just as we cannot prove that any thought is present in a brute, so the lack of such proof does not establish that none is present, since the human mind cannot reach into an animal’s interior. The first text seems to warrant a positive claim about the nonexistence of animal thought; the second seems to leave the question open.

Are these actually in tension? Give me the strongest candidate reconciliations: a change of mind, a distinction between demonstrative certainty and practical or moral certainty, a distinction between thought in the strict sense and the mechanically explicable sensations Descartes was willing to grant beasts, or a reading on which the Discourse conclusion is more modest than it looks. For each candidate, say what it buys and what it costs. Then give your verdict on which reading requires the least special pleading, and defend it.
