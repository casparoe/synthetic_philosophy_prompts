I retired a few years ago from a career in biochemistry and have been reading political theory in an unsystematic way. A recurring arguement in my local reading group is whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal country. People bring in completely different things: one person means drug policy and free speech, another means taxes and how easy it is to start a company, a third means how much the state intrudes into family life and religion, and someone else insists the real question is openness to immigration. The odd thing is that each of them can make their own ranking sound obvious, because each has quietly chosen a different meaning of 'liberal' and then found a measure that fits it.

I've decided to write a longish essay (or a talk for the club) on what would actually settle the comparison, rather than which country wins on any one measure. I have a few months and no appetite for a fifty-page policy audit. My own working list of questions that might need answering is roughly: (1) should economic and personal liberty be scored separately or collapsed into one index; (2) should we compare de jure rules or de facto experience, since a law and its enforcement can diverge; (3) how to weight the dimensions once separated, especially when the neighbours plausibly trade off against each other; and (4) whether 'liberal' should mean classical liberal, American-style social liberal, or the Nordic social-democratic tradition—in which case the whole exercise might be circular.

I'm not after a survey of the literature, and please no citations or reading lists. I want your defended judgement: if I can only settle two or three of these (or better ones you'd replace them with) before the ranking can be defended, which should they be, and why? Rank them and defend the ordering—what makes a question upstream of the others, tractable for someone with my background, and likely to actually change the final answer, and what sort of evidence would move a question up or down. Also tell me which ones are overrated or can safely be put off. British English throughout, please—I find American usage distracting in this kind of writing.
