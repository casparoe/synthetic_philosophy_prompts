I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of cognitive science course, now in a unit on modularity, and I have been asked to run a 75-minute session for our 14-person seminar on Fodor's modularity thesis and the massive modularity debate. The other students have done the reading, but the contrast between modular input systems and non-modular central systems stays abstract for them: they can list Fodor's criteria but have not felt why the distinction matters. I want an activity that makes them run into the distinction in their own cognitive behavior before it is named or lectured about.

Please design an activity around a two-phase pair structure, though you may depart from it if you can defend something better. It should open with a brief, unannounced individual timed task — something like a color-word interference task in the spirit of Stroop — in which each participant discovers a fast, mandatory process inside them that they cannot voluntarily switch off and that they cannot correct from the inside even when they know the correct answer. Then, in phase one, pairs divide into a "responder," who must produce rapid outputs under time pressure and restricted information, and an "analyst," who has full access to the materials and may advise but may not slow the responder down. Include a lightweight scoring system (accuracy and speed, with a penalty for errors) so the conflict between the two demands is real, without using money. In phase two, the same pair faces an open-ended problem that requires drawing on arbitrary background knowledge — for example, judging whether a proposed university policy is fair — where the responder's fast, automatic processing is now useless and the analyst's slow, cross-domain deliberation is indispensable.

The debrief should map phase-one moments onto Fodor's features of modular input systems — mandatory operation, speed, informational encapsulation, shallow outputs, limited central access — and phase two onto his picture of central systems as slow, isotropic, and Quinean. Please be careful not to overclaim: a Stroop-like effect demonstrates automaticity and mandatory operation more directly than informational encapsulation, and the debrief should say explicitly what each phenomenon does and does not show. The debrief should also raise the question the activity cannot settle — whether phase two supports massive modularity or instead shows why central systems must be non-modular — and should point out where the analogy between the pair roles and real cognitive subsystems breaks down. Finally, the debrief should leave participants seeing the mind as containing at least two quite different kinds of information-processing organization, without letting them infer that cognition is either wholly modular or wholly unencapsulated.

Please provide concrete rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief script in conversational form, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, participants squinting or blurring the display to neutralize the interference, or the analyst taking over the responder's task) and how to handle each failure, and an honest list of features of Fodor's claim that a classroom of undergraduates cannot instantiate at all (such as fixed neural architecture and characteristic patterns of acquired breakdown).

You are an expert philosopher of cognitive science who also designs active-learning classroom exercises. Please think carefully before committing: start by brainstorming three or four candidate formats and briefly say why you reject or keep each, then present the final design at an operational level of detail so I can run it directly.
