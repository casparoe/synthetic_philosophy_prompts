Read The Merchant of Venice through the lens of money, liquidity, and wealth, taking the play's commercial texture seriously without reducing it to an economics fable. The action opens with a solvent merchant who cannot pay cash: Antonio's fortune is tied up in ships at sea, so to fund Bassanio's courtship he signs a bond with Shylock whose forfeiture is a pound of Antonio's flesh — a thing that is neither fungible, divisible, nor liquid, and whose extraction would destroy the debtor and with him the value of the claim. I would like a reading that brings in the distinction between solvency and liquidity, between wealth as a stock of real resources and wealth as a spendable flow, and between money as a medium of exchange and the non-monetary things the play repeatedly puts in its place: caskets, daughters, faith, flesh. Where does the play show contracts and enforceable claims creating wealth, and where does it show the opposite — that some apparent assets lose their value the moment one tries to realize them in spendable money? Where do the casket test and the courtroom scene resist the idea that everything can be made commensurable in money? I am writing an essay about whether the play can be taught not only as a drama of mercy and revenge but as a critique of credit and of the strange metaphysics of money, and I want the philosophical distinctions used honestly, with a willingness to say where the play resists the reading.
