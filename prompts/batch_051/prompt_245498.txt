As a journalist who covers law and ideas, I kept coming back to an argument the defense pressed in several forms while I was covering an extortion trial, and I still cannot find the step that fails. The defendant had obtained a true and embarrassing story about a city official — the sort of story a newspaper could lawfully have published — and told the official he would publish it unless he was paid. Assume the defendant had no duty of confidentiality and was legally free to publish. The jury convicted him of blackmail anyway.

The argument that troubles me runs as follows:

(1) Publishing the story would have been lawful.
(2) Accepting money from the official would have been lawful.
(3) If it is lawful to do A and lawful to accept payment, then it is lawful to announce that one will do A unless one is paid.
(4) So it was lawful for the defendant to announce that he would publish unless he was paid.
(5) Announcing that one will publish unless paid is exactly the threat that constitutes blackmail.
(6) Therefore blackmail is lawful, and the conviction was unjust.

I am certain (6) is false, but I cannot see which earlier step to give up. Each of the earlier steps looks right: (1) and (2) simply record two permissions the law grants, and (3) seems to be the principle that makes ordinary bargaining possible — a seller may announce that she will keep her car unless you pay her asking price, and nobody calls that extortion. Yet the same reasoning appears to license a witness saying that she will testify truthfully against you unless you pay her, which plainly is extortion. So the argument proves too much. Which step is false, why did it look so plausible, and what do we give up by rejecting it? In particular, what principled line separates the car sale from the blackmail threat without simply assuming the conclusion?
