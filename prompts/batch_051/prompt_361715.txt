I keep bouncing off the nuclear deterrence literature because every piece I read is built around a handfull of favorite cases (Cuban Missile Crisis, the 1980s war scares, some false-alarm incident) and one favorite mechanism, and I want the full spec before I read any more. Write the desiderata for a theory of nuclear deterrence: what it has to get right to count as adequate for judging whether any actual doctrine — mutual assured destruction, no first use, flexible response, escalation dominance — does what its advocates claim. I mean the paradoxes a theory has to dissolve rather than wave at: why a threat to retaliate is supposed to be credible when carrying it out would destroy you too, why making retaliation more automatic can make war more likely instead of less, why a rational actor can rationally threaten to let things slip partly out of their control. The distinctions a theory has to respect: deterrence vs compellence, credibility vs resolve vs capability, a threat being believed vs being rational to carry out. The empirical regularities it can't contradict: decades with no direct nuclear exchanges but plenty of proxy war, near-misses, and arms racing. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding the standard views, and whether any pair of desiderata looks jointly unsatisfiable in a way that would explain why the field keeps re-trading the same arguments. Skip the scorecard, I'll do that myself; the incompatibilities are the part I actually want.
