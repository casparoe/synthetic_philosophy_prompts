You are preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on nineteenth-century philosophy, and the students have just read the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Write a diagnostic quiz, of the sort one might find in a serious philosophy magazine, entitled "Which of Nietzsche's three kinds of history do you actually practise?" The quiz should sort respondents into monumental, antiquarian, or critical history on the basis of their judgements about concrete cases, not on the basis of which label they would claim for themselves.

The quiz should consist of eight to ten questions. Each question should present a short, concrete scenario — for example, a proposal to remove a Victorian statue of a philanthropist whose family profited from the slave trade; a local historical society's campaign to preserve an unremarkable eighteenth-century terrace; a national curriculum commission deciding how to balance the achievements of a country's founders against their documented wrongs; a person deciding what to do with a family archive; a revolutionary movement deciding what to do with the monuments of the old regime — and should offer two or three response options corresponding to the different pulls the three modes of history would exert. No question may use the words "monumental", "antiquarian", or "critical", nor cognates such as "monument"; the options must be phrased in ordinary language. Your purpose is to sort people by what they actually find compelling in the cases, not by which Nietzschean term they think describes them.

After the questions, provide a scoring key that maps each response option to one or more of the three modes, with fractional scores where an option plausibly expresses more than one mode. Then give three interpretive summaries of roughly 120–180 words each, describing what a respondent of each predominant type values and what that type is in danger of: the monumental respondent treats the past as a source of exemplary peaks that inspire present striving, at the cost of distorting the past into a gallery of selectable models; the antiquarian respondent treats the past as an inheritance whose details are to be preserved and honoured, at the cost of a mummifying piety that resents genuine novelty; the critical respondent treats the past as something from which one must win one's freedom by judging and breaking with it, at the cost of sawing off the roots that sustain one's own capacity to judge. These summaries should be in your own words; do not quote or half-quote any translation of the essay. If you judge it useful, you may add a fourth, explicitly named "unhistorical" outcome for respondents who resist having any settled orientation towards the past at all, but the three core types must remain the principal results.

Conclude with a section headed "Where the quiz cannot separate the positions", in which you identify two or three places where the modes converge or diverge in ways a scoring key can only imperfectly represent — for instance, the fact that a monumental and an antiquarian respondent may both vote to keep a controversial monument but for different reasons, or the fact that a person may be critical about one communal past and antiquarian about another. The audience is philosophically able undergraduates who have read the essay and would appreciate being caught out by their own answers; the tone should be serious but not solemn.

Write the entire quiz, the scoring key, and all surrounding prose in British English, using British spelling and punctuation throughout.
