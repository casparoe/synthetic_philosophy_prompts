Write a poem in three numbered movements about the difference between what a text-to-image model does when it produces a striking image and what a human artist does when she makes one. The first movement should be assembled almost entirely from stock phrases, familiar idioms, and ready-made collocations, cut together in surprising ways; it should be good in the way an apt collage is good, but none of its verbal material should be new. The second movement should adopt a strict, self-chosen form — a meter, a stanza shape, and a rhyme scheme — and should aim for a genuinely fresh move within that form: an image or turn the form makes possible but does not dictate. The third movement should break that form, and the break should itself carry the poem's claim about which of the three movements counts as creative, or whether all three do. Without ever naming them, the poem should track a familiar philosophical distinction among three kinds of creativity: combining familiar elements in unfamiliar ways; exploring a structured space to find an unoccupied niche; and transforming the space itself so that new kinds of moves become possible. A philosopher who knows this taxonomy should recognize exactly which movement enacts which kind of creativity, while a reader who has never encountered the taxonomy should still find the poem coherent, pointed, and worth rereading.
