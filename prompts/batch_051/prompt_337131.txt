I am a high school student, and I have been trying to think through Dennett's idea of the intentional stance after reading about it in my philosophy club. As I understand it, the argument is that when a system is too complex to predict by working through its physical parts or its engineering specifications, we can predict it by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires; the classic example is the chess-playing computer, where supposing that the program wants to win and thinks its queen is threatened lets us anticipate its moves without reading its code. The claim that does the real work is that the intentional stance is a predictive tool, and it earns its keep only as long as it makes better predictions than the alternatives.

What I am wrestling with is whether that argument survives the way today's chatbots are actually built. A large language model is not handed a goal like winning a game or navigating an environment; it is trained to predict the next word over an enormous body of human text, and its outputs are produced by a process that, at least at inference time, does not select actions in the world or update itself in response to consequences. Yet people, including people who know exactly how such systems work, routinely say that a model thinks, knows, intends, and wants, and they treat those attributions as both explanatory and predictive. So the thing the intentional stance was originally supposed to predict appears to have changed: goal-directed behavior in an environment is missing, while the stance itself has never felt more natural to take. Does Dennett's argument survive this development, or does it need repair, and in doing so become weaker, stronger, or different in kind? I would especially like to know which premise of the original argument the development puts pressure on, and whether a repaired version still tells us anything about what we are doing when we attribute beliefs and desires to a next-token predictor.
