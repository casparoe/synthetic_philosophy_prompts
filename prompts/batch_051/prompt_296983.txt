Question: In the hitchhiker case, Parfit wants to show that the self-interest theory can be indirectly self-defeating and, relatedly, why it might be rational to have a disposition that causes one to act contrary to one’s immediate self-interest. Explain these claims and state precisely what the hitchhiker case does and does not show about the truth of the self-interest theory.

Student answer: In the hitchhiker case, a stranded man promises to pay you a reward if you rescue him. Once he is safe, he has no self-interested reason to pay, so a straightforward self-interest maximizer will break the promise; and if you know that, you will not rescue him. To avoid this, his best option is to make himself trustworthy by developing a standing disposition to keep promises even when keeping them costs him more than breaking them would. This is what Parfit calls rational irrationality: it is rational to have a character trait that sometimes causes one to perform irrational acts. The lesson is that the self-interest theory tells him to stop being a case-by-case maximizer and instead follow a rule like always keeping his promises, because following that rule will maximize his long-term self-interest. Since this rule tells him to act against his self-interest in particular cases, the hitchhiker case shows that the self-interest theory is false.
