Ok, here's one for the demarcation problem. I keep collecting objections to Popper's idea that falsifiability is the thing that separates science from non-science, and I want to check whether there's a real objection I've missed. I dont need any of the following restated, and by new I mean not just a rewording, a special case, or a combination of them:

(1) Duhem–Quine holism: a theory never has observational consequences all by itself; it gets them only together with auxiliary assumptions about instruments, background conditions, and other theories. So when a prediction fails, the failure can always be blamed on an auxiliary rather than on the theory, and no single result ever logically forces you to give the theory up.

(2) Ad hoc rescue: even setting Duhem–Quine aside, a defender can just tweak or add an auxiliary to absorb a failed prediction. Unless the criterion comes with a rule against ad hoc patches, falsifiability alone never bites, and once it does come with such a rule, the rule is doing the work rather than falsifiability.

(3) Actual scientific practice: scientists treat one failed prediction as an anomaly to investigate, not as the death of a theory. So falsificationism either misdescribes what scientists do, or it has to soften into a methodological convention about when to declare something falsified, which isnt the clean logical criterion Popper wanted.

(4) Probabilistic statements: the claim that this atom has a fifty percent chance of decaying in the next hour is not falsified by any finite run of outcomes, and science is full of claims of that kind. Popper's attempts to handle them are notoriously strained.

(5) Too weak: lots of things we call pseudoscience contain perfectly falsifiable claims. What makes astrology or creationism pseudoscientific looks more like how their defenders handle a failed prediction than like the logical form of the claims. Falsifiability is not sufficient.

(6) Too strong: some legitimate science is hard to give a falsifier for. String theory is the standard example, but broad existence claims and models with many free parameters also fit. Falsifiability may exclude parts of science we have no independent reason to expel.

(7) Theory-ladenness of observation: the observation reports that are supposed to serve as falsifiers are themselves soaked in theory and background expectations, so there is no neutral observational tribunal in front of which a theory can be made to cleanly fail.

(8) Self-application: falsificationism itself is not falsifiable. If the criterion is meant to be applied to theories generally, it seems to demote itself; if it is exempt because it is a methodological proposal rather than an empirical theory, then Popper has quietly changed the subject from a logical criterion to a normative stance.

Given that list, give me one more objection to falsificationism, genuinely different from those eight and not a variant of any of them. Then be straight with me: is it a real problem for the criterion, or is it only embarrassing on an uncharitable reading of Popper?
