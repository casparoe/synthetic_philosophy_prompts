I'm a charge nurse on a hospital palliative floor. About once a week we hit the same wall: a patient wrote an advance directive years ago — no ventilator, no feeding tube, keep me comfortable — but right now either they're asking for treatment, or they can't ask and the family says the old them would have wanted to fight, or the person in the bed seems fairly content even though the paper says to let them go. The doctors mostly look to me to sort out whose wishes count before they'll sign the orders, and I've started thinking of it as a contest between the person who wrote the paper and the person in the bed now.

Write me a procedure I could actually run at 2am when theres no ethics consult available: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says follow the paper, where it says the person in the bed wins, and where it has to stop and say no checklist can settle this. Then run it on these three cases, and make sure at least one of them comes out in a way I won't feel good about:

(1) A man with ALS wrote "no ventilator" five years ago, and tonight, unable to breathe and in a panic, he is fully oriented and begs to be put on one, saying he's changed his mind.

(2) A woman wrote "no antibiotics, no feeding tube" twelve years ago, and now with advanced dementia she doesn't recognize anyone, but she eats when someone feeds her, laughs at the radio, and when she gets pneumonia she doesn't seem distressed; her daughter says the woman in the bed still enjoys her days.

(3) A man wrote "if I'm ever like my father was, don't keep me going," and now he's minimally conscious, the doctors can't say whether he's like the father, and his wife and brother disagree about what he would have meant.

Also be honest about where the procedure stops being a procedure and turns into a guess.
