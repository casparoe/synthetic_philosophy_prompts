Among the recurring themes in the fragmentary notes that Blaise Pascal, the seventeenth-century French philosopher, left unpublished at his death is the relation between justice and force. For present purposes, his position may be summarized this way. Pascal observes that a verdict or law that is just but cannot compel obedience tends to be defied, while a law that can compel but is not just is mere tyranny. He further suspects that the two are rarely found together in human affairs: because people argue endlessly about what justice is, while it is obvious who is stronger, what passes for justice is in large measure the custom and success of the powerful, preserved under more respectable names. The remedy, as he sees it, would be to close the gap between the two—to supply what is genuinely right with the power it needs to be effective, and to bring what has power under the constraints of justice—though he remains doubtful that human institutions have actually achieved this.

Now imagine that Pascal is presented with a contemporary criminal court that has adopted a machine-learning risk-assessment instrument. When a person is arrested, the judge receives a risk score computed from large historical datasets of charges, prior failures to appear, rearrests, and various demographic and geographic features. The score is officially advisory, but in practice it heavily influences decisions about bail, pretrial detention, and release conditions. Defenders of the instrument say it replaces judicial whim and mood with consistent, evidence-based regularity. Critics say the instrument is opaque to the people it affects, and that because it is trained on the historical record of policing and prosecution, it may quietly inherit and stabilize the very custom and force that shaped that record.

Working strictly from the summary of Pascal's view given above, predict how Pascal would most likely evaluate this practice. In particular: would he see the risk score as a welcome step toward making justice effective, as a new disguise in which force presents itself as impartial judgment, as a mixture of both, or as something that breaks his framework in an instructive way? Attend especially to the features his view emphasizes—the difficulty of recognizing justice, the ease of recognizing force, and the way custom and power harden into law.
