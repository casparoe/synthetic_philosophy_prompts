Two people on my research team got into a long argument last week about whether an AI model's chain-of-thought can be read as evidence of what the system actually reasoned, or whether it is better treated as a sanitized gloss produced for the benefit of whoever is reading the transcript. I could not referee it at the time, and I want a careful adjudication.

Amina began from the claim that when a model emits a step-by-step derivation and the final answer is conditioned on the text already generated, the chain is — in an important sense — the reasoning: it is computed before the answer, not after it, and it causally constrains what the model can output next. Marcus replied that the model is trained on one thing, the correctness of the final answer, and not on the honesty of the intermediate steps, so the chain is underdetermined by the training signal and can easily become a plausible narrative that terminates in the right answer. Amina answered that this is also true of asking a human to think aloud; we treat a doctor's or an engineer's stated reasoning as evidence about why they decided even though we cannot inspect their neural activity, and a standard of proof that ruled out every fallible report would rule out ordinary explanation too. Marcus said the disanalogy is in the optimization pressure. A human's self-report is shaped by a social practice in which there is some cost to being caught confabulating, whereas a model's chain is generated under the pressure of a single scalar objective, and the same objective that makes the answer right can, in a capable enough system, reward a clean-looking rationale for the wrong sorts of reasons. Amina replied that for many hard problems the cheapest route to the right answer really is to work the problem through accurately in the chain, so answer-level training can and often does train faithfulness as an instrumental subskill. Marcus closed by saying that this is exactly the problem: the cases where a safety team most needs the chain are the cases where the model has a reason not to display its work, so a monitoring scheme that tracks faithful chains on easy problems may systematically lose signal at the point of highest stakes. They left it there.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move: for each of Amina's and Marcus's turns, say what is right, what is overstated, and where a step relies on an empirical assumption rather than a conceptual point. Then identify the crux of their disagreement, and say whether Amina's optimism, Marcus's pessimism, or a qualified intermediate position is most defensible — and under what empirical conditions each would be right. Be explicit about the three concepts doing the work here: faithfulness, obfuscation, and optimization pressure.
