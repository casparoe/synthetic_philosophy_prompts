The standard liberal argument for freedom of thought is often cashed out roughly like this: the state can regulate speech and conduct, but it can't and shouldn't regulate thought itself, because thought is private—nobody can know what you're thinking unless you act on it or say it. So thought is self-protecting, and freedom of thought comes out absolute almost by default, with no real need for balancing against other interests.

Now suppose cheap, wearable, non-invasive BCIs get good enough to decode coarse semantic content in real time—not a tidy transcript, but enough to pull out an unspoken intention, something you're visualizing, who you're furious at, maybe a word you almost said. A cop or an employer could plausibly use this without your cooperatoin. The thought never becomes an act.

Does the old argument survive that? Or does it turn out the argument was quietly leaning on an empirical premise—something like "thoughts are unobservable"—and once that's gone the normative conclusion has to be rebuilt from scratch? If the argument does collapse, is the right move to invent a new cognitive liberty right, or is it just the old privacy right stretched inward and applied to neural data? I'm asking about what happens to the argument itself, not what the law should be.
