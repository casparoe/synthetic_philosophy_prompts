Give me a working definition of abandonment as applied to works of authorship: the condition in which a work is no longer meaningfully tied to its owner, such that it becomes reasonable for third parties to preserve, adapt, or take over the work without the owner's active permission. I want a definition that gets these six cases right:

1. A novel published in 1965, whose author died in 1994, whose publisher no longer exists, and for which a library's two-year good-faith search identified no living heir.
2. A desktop application last updated in 2009 but still sold and supported by the company that wrote it; releases are rare, but the helpdesk answers tickets and the company depends on the income.
3. An open-source library whose sole maintainer has committed nothing for six years and does not answer issues or email; the license permits copying and modification, but the official version, project name, and release process remain under the maintainer's control.
4. A 1993 video game for hardware no one still makes; it is not sold anywhere, but the publisher still exists and occasionally sends takedown notices when people share copies online.
5. A niche scientific software package written in the 1980s by an author who is alive, replies to email roughly twice a year to say an update is coming, has released nothing in over a decade, and refuses to let anyone fork or maintain the code.
6. A film made by a production company that dissolved in the 1980s; no one knows who, if anyone, inherited the rights, and no one has exploited the film since.

I am confident that case 1 counts as abandoned and case 2 does not; I can see arguments both ways on the rest. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it surprises you. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts, and identify which features of the cases are doing the real work.
