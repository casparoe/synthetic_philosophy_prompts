I teach an intro philosophy of language class where I've been sneaking NLP stuff in on the sly — 16 undergrads, one 75-minute session. I want them to *feel* the distributional claim that a word's meaning is mostly its position relative to other words, not a definition they learned or a thing they point at. Design an in-class game where the students are tokens in a 2D embedding space: each one gets a word on a card, and over a series of rounds they physically rearrange themselves on a floor grid according to constraints (I'm imagining something like: every time I read a sentence, the content words in that sentence take a step toward each other, and every round everybody drifts apart a little). I don't care exactly what rules you choose, as long as by the end clusters and polysemy show up in the room itself — like "bank" getting tugged between a money cluster and a river cluster. Give me rules, materials (a tape grid? cards? how big a room?), a round structure that doesn't use real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto actual embedding geometry: dimensions, distance, clustering, vector arithmetic, why a polysemous word is one representation pulled in multiple directions. I don't want a lecture up front, and the phrase "word embedding" shouldn't be said until the debrief, so their not just play-acting a metaphor they already know. Then tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time (what if someone's word barely appears? what if they start talking and negotiating instead of moving?), and be honest about what a 2D floor physically cannot show about real high-dimensional embedding spaces. If the whole game is a bad way to make the point — if it rigs the outcome or quietly smuggles in the distributional conclusion — push back and tell me what to run instead. I'd rather get a candid list of weak spots than a polished lesson plan.
