I have been collecting a friend's verdicts about truth, tact, and the small untruths people tell in the service of politeness, because I cannot reconstruct the principle behind them. She is not an absolutist in either direction. Here are the judgments I have recorded.

A doctor tells a frightened patient, without euphemism, that the cancer has returned and that the next round of treatment is unlikely to cure it. She says this is right. She adds that the same doctor's gentle "you will be fine" would have been seriously wrong.

A guest at a dinner party says, without being asked, that the meal was wonderful, although the anxious host had served an over-salted and almost inedible dish. She says the guest said what one should say.

A friend of hers from Osaka declines a low-stakes invitation by saying that the day is a little difficult, and never states the refusal outright. She says this is a perfectly good way to decline: the hearer will take the point, and a flat "I do not want to come" would have been needlessly wounding.

A colleague from Rotterdam tells another colleague in a project meeting, in front of the rest of the team, that her section was confusing and that this is why the client pushed back. She says this is right, perhaps obligatory.

A professor tells a student, plainly, that the student's conference paper is not ready to submit. She says this is right, however anxious the student is.

An acquaintance at a party says, unasked, to someone who has just finished telling a story, that the story was boring. She says this is wrong, even though it was true.

The case I cannot settle is this. A man has just delivered the best-man speech at a wedding and turns to a close friend in the reception line. He asks whether it was all right, because he was nervous. The close friend thought the speech was meandering and flat in places, but the moment is over, no similar speech is planned, and the speaker is exhausted and relieved. What would my friend say the close friend should answer: the truth, or something warm and reassuring that he does not fully believe?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the rule behind these verdicts. Present the most plausible rules that fit the track record, including at least one that would require honesty in the best-man case and at least one that would permit or require the reassuring answer; identify the closest analogue among her earlier verdicts; and then commit. Which rule is she most likely running, what does it predict for the best-man case, and what further case would best settle whether you are right? If your chosen rule forces you to treat one of her recorded verdicts as anomalous or as needing reinterpretation, say so.
