I have been trying to think clearly about pleasure, and I find that my own judgements about particular cases are pulling me in different directions. This matters to me because I want to know whether hedonism — the view that pleasure is the only thing good in itself — is even a well-formed view; if 'pleasure' has no unity, perhaps the view cannot be stated in the first place. I would like you to help me find the principle, if there is one, behind my verdicts.

Here are the cases as I find myself judging them.

1. Tasting a ripe peach on a hot day is a paradigm pleasure. If anything is a pleasure, that is.

2. The quiet satisfaction of finishing a long, difficult piece of work is also a pleasure. It does not feel like the peach, but I would not hesitate to call it pleasant.

3. The relief I felt when a worrying medical test came back clear was, I want to say, a kind of pleasure — even though it was mostly the disappearance of dread rather than the arrival of any positive feeling.

4. Schadenfreude: when a colleague who has been unkind to me suffered a minor professional embarrassment, I felt a small glow that I am not proud of. I count it as a pleasure, though a discreditable one.

5. Sitting in a warm room listening to rain, feeling nothing very much, is pleasant. But I struggle to point to any particular feeling that is 'the pleasure'. It seems more like a way the whole situation is coloured.

6. Being absorbed in solving a puzzle: while I am doing it, I am not aware of enjoying myself — I am aware only of the puzzle. Yet afterwards I say it was thoroughly enjoyable, and I would be surprised to be told that I had had no pleasure at all.

7. Scratching a bad itch is intensely pleasant, but I hesitate to put it in the same category as the others. It seems too automatic, almost reflexive, to count as a pleasure in the sense that matters.

8. An addict's fix: there is an unmistakable felt rush, but I am reluctant to call it a pleasure in the same sense either — though I cannot tell whether that is a conceptual judgement or just moral distaste.

So I seem to think that pleasure cannot simply be a distinctive sensation: the peach, the satisfaction, the relief, the schadenfreude, the rainy afternoon and the absorbed puzzle-solving all feel different, and in the puzzle case there is no felt pleasure at all while I am doing it. But my hesitations about the itch and the addict's fix pull the other way: they have the felt quality, yet I want to withhold the word.

The tentative principle I have been working with is this: a pleasure is a feeling with a particular positive quality — a kind of inner glow — which varies in intensity and duration but is otherwise the same wherever it occurs. That principle fits the peach and perhaps the schadenfreude, but it seems to fail elsewhere.

What I would like from you is this. Is there a single coherent account of pleasure that captures most of my verdicts, or at least explains why the troublesome ones are troublesome? What would such an account say about cases I have not listed — for instance, taking pleasure in something one knows to be false, or a drug that produced the 'glow' in a person with no object or thought attached to it? Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, give the principle's verdicts on the further cases, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which would have to be revised or abandoned.
