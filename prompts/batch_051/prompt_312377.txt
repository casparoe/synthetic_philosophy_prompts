Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the epistemic status of Solomonoff induction. The statement is the document the two camps would sign after a patient attempt to locate exactly where they part ways.

Define the shared background neutrally as follows. Solomonoff induction (algorithmic probability) assigns probability to finite binary sequences by mixing over all programs on a fixed universal machine, with each program weighted by an exponentially decaying function of its length, so that short programs receive more prior weight. Two mathematical facts are agreed background. First, the invariance theorem: changing the universal machine changes Kolmogorov complexity by at most an additive constant and changes the resulting Solomonoff prior by at most a multiplicative constant. Second, a dominance result: for any computable probability measure, the Solomonoff mixture dominates that measure up to a positive multiplicative constant depending on the measure, which implies that, in the limit, predictions made from the mixture converge to the truth under that measure.

Camp A holds that Solomonoff induction is the correct formalization of Occam's razor and a genuine ideal of inductive rationality; actual methods, on this view, should be judged by how well they approximate it or its convergence behavior. Camp B holds that Solomonoff induction is a formally illuminating object but that the machine-relativity constants, the asymptotic character of the convergence results, the uncomputability of the mixture, and the restriction to computable measures prevent it from bearing the normative weight Camp A assigns to it.

Write the document they would produce, with the following structure.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of nontrivial claims both camps can sign, covering the formalism, exactly what the mathematical results do and do not show, and which questions the dispute actually turns on.

2. Disagreement table. Columns: (a) the question at issue; (b) the precise claim Camp A will sign; (c) the precise claim Camp B will sign, phrased so that each side accepts the wording for its own position; (d) whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, with a note when it is verbal.

3. Cruxes. A table with columns: (a) the crux, stated as a sub-question such that settling it would make at least one side revise its view; (b) why it matters; (c) a concrete argument, case, or computational result both sides agree would bear on the crux; (d) Camp A's prediction for that test; (e) Camp B's prediction for that test.

Do not stop at the observation that the two camps emphasize different goals. Make each formulation precise enough that changing a single clause would cause one side to refuse to sign. In particular, the document should address at least these points: the significance of additive versus multiplicative constants in finite samples; whether convergence in the limit can justify an inductive rule for finite agents; whether an uncomputable rule can be a rational ideal; whether restricting the hypothesis space to computable measures is a principled modeling choice or an unjustified restriction; and whether Kolmogorov complexity is best viewed as an explication of Occam's razor or as one simplicity measure among others. Where the collaboration finds that part of the dispute is verbal, identify the differing terms and restate the substantive core.
