I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and over the past year I have repeatedly encountered a gloss on Van Fraassen's Reflection principle that I suspect is doing more work than it can bear. I would like it audited rather than simply accepted or rejected. The principle, as I understand it, states that my current degree of belief in a proposition should equal my current expectation of the degree of belief I will hold in that proposition at some later time. A colleague of mine defends the principle by analogy: my future self is to be treated as an expert, in roughly the way one treats a better-informed informant, so that just as I would set my credence to the expected judgment of a panel of experts, I should set my present credence to my expected future credence. The work this analogy is meant to do is substantial: on this view, violating Reflection is not merely a matter of exposing myself to a diachronic Dutch strategy or some similarly formal defect; it is the same kind of epistemic failure as ignoring a better-informed expert because the expert's report is inconvenient.

I would like a careful audit of this analogy. (1) Set out the correspondence as charitably as possible — a table is fine: what maps to the client, the expert, the expert's private information, the deference rule, and the expert's track record. (2) Identify where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that are idle. (3) Say which features of genuine deference to an expert the analogy quietly imports that a future self may lack — such as forgetting, anticipated irrationality or bias, or information I will later have but cannot now anticipate. (4) Then tell me what survives of the colleague's conclusion. If the analogy fails, does the Reflection principle fail with it, or does the principle remain justified on some other and perhaps weaker basis, such as diachronic book-avoidance or expected-accuracy considerations? In other words, is Reflection best understood as a deference principle at all, or is the expert-deference gloss merely a heuristic that should be laid aside once the principle's real basis is identified?
