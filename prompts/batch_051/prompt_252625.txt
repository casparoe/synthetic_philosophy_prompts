In his essay The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity, Daniel Dennett proposes that a self is not a thing inside the brain or an immaterial substance but an abstraction, something like a center of gravity. A center of gravity is not an atom located somewhere in the object, yet it is not nothing: it does real explanatory work when we predict how a chair will tip or how a body will fall. Dennett suggests the self is like that—a spatiotemporal focus of a web of narratives that a person and others tell, revise, and negotiate across a lifetime.

I notice an asymmetry in how we treat such narrative centers. For an individual human being, we are willing to say that the narrative center is the real person—the thing that owns its history, makes promises, is answerable for acts done long ago under very different self-understandings, and is a proper target of praise and blame. But consider a long-running fictional character written by dozens of authors across generations, or an institution that keeps a continuous official voice, issues apologies, makes commitments, revises its story about itself, and is even treated by law as a person. Many people will say those collective narrative centers are only useful fictions, and that the moral reality lies with the underlying authors or officeholders—even when there is no single author and no single human who persisted through the whole story. Is there a principled difference that makes the individual center of narrative gravity morally deep while making the collaborative or institutional one merely metaphorical? Or should we either demote the individual self to a convenient fiction that cannot properly be the target of moral judgment, or promote some sufficiently robust collective narrative centers to full selfhood? Tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and test your answer against a difficult nearby case: a person whose life story is itself radically fragmented or largely narrated by family members, caregivers, and clinicians.
