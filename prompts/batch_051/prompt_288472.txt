I have been reading Origen's On First Principles, Book IV, and a passage there has unsettled my sense of what is really contested in the old fight between allegorical and literal readers of Scripture. In that passage, Origen argues that the Spirit deliberately wove into the biblical text certain stumbling blocks: historical impossibilities, ritual instructions that cannot be carried out, and sayings about God that would be morally or conceptually unworthy if taken at face value. His claim is that these features are placed there as cues, almost as traps for a too-quick reading, directing the reader to look for a spiritual meaning beneath the letter. What strikes me is how close this is to what a modern fundamentalist might say about the same phenomena, with the valence reversed: the hard sayings and apparent absurdities are there to test obedience, and the faithful reader submits to the literal word even where it offends autonomous reason.

I have watched this same argument recur in different vocabularies: Alexandrian allegory versus Antiochene historical reading, the medieval fourfold sense versus the reformers' insistence on the plain sense, and now the hermeneutical disputes between critical scholars and theological literalists. Both sides, it seems, can agree that certain passages are, on their face, scandalous, and both sides accuse the other of smuggling in a prior philosophical picture of what a divine text must be. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not best described as a disagreement about what the texts say, but about which criterion governs interpretation: internal textual cues versus an inherited rule of faith, or a prior doctrine of Scripture's nature. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement about the content after all?
