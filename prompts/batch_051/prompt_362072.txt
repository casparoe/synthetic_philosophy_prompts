I'm currently taking a philosophy of biology course, and I'm trying to turn the standard reply to the tautology objection into a usable definition of 'fitness'. The problem, as our instructor framed it, is that if fitness is defined as actual survival and reproduction, then saying that the fittest survive becomes empty; the standard reply is to define fitness as a probabilistic propensity rather than an actual outcome. I'm convinced that reply is on the right track, but I keep finding cases where the obvious versions come apart, and I want a definition that survives them.

Here are the cases I'm using as my test battery, with my tentative intuitive verdicts. I don't believe all of these verdicts can be right together.

1. Two genetically identical aphids on the same plant. A ladybug eats one before it lays any eggs; the other lays forty. I lean toward saying the two have the same fitness, because they have the same intrinsic dispositions, even though their actual output differs.

2. A sterile worker ant that rears many of its queen's offspring, several of which found new colonies. I want to say the worker has fitness, but only through the reproduction of close relatives, so direct and indirect fitness have to be distinguished.

3. A plant genotype that produces ten thousand tiny seeds. In dry years most survive; in wet years most drown. A second genotype produces ten large drought-resistant seeds every year. I have no idea how to rank them without specifying an environment or a run of years; maybe neither is simply fitter.

4. A mutation that speeds up development in a prey species. Right now it is advantageous because slow developers are eaten by a newly arrived predator, but it makes adult wings fragile, and the advantage may disappear as the predator shifts its search image. I think fitness has to be relativized to a selective environment over a specified period, but I worry that this is a hedge rather than a definition.

5. A trait in a small population that has increased in frequency entirely by genetic drift and confers no average reproductive advantage on its bearers. I lean toward saying its bearers are not fitter, and that the definition must avoid counting every actual increase in frequency as fitness.

6. A bird with a mutation that improves a courtship display but is linked to a gene that halves fertility. The mutation spreads anyway because the display doubles mating success. I think this may still count as fitness at the level of the gene or the trait, but I'm not sure how to say that precisely.

7. A type that in each generation has a 0.5 chance of leaving no offspring and a 0.5 chance of leaving twenty, set against a type that reliably leaves six. The first has the higher expected output; I'm genuinely unsure whether that makes it the fitter type, since it often leaves zero.

8. A woman who has ten children, none of whom survive to reproduce, set against a woman who has two, both of whom have many children. I'm sure the second has the higher Darwinian fitness, which makes me think a definition built only on counting offspring is too crude.

Please work through this as a professional philosopher of biology would. Before proposing a definition, brainstorm the constraints it needs to satisfy. Then give me the best working definition of 'fitness' you can defend — or, if you judge that no single definition does all this work, give me a small family of related definitions and explain how they fit together. Run the definition through all eight cases, say where it yields a surprising or ambiguous result, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, tell me which of my tentative verdicts is least secure and why. Please separate what is settled in the field from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus. Write in American English.
