I keep hearing a version of this claim in discussions of training language models: process supervision (scoring each intermediate step as it's generated) doesn't just improve final answers — it teaches the model a transferable way of working a problem, while outcome supervision (scoring only the final answer) merely selects for answers that look right on the training distribution. I want to know wether that's a testable claim or just a slogan.

Here's a concrete setup to keep the answer from floating away. A model is trained on multi-step arithmetic word problems. Every problem has a unique valid chain of three to five steps and one final numerical answer. One run gets process supervision: each step gets a correct/incorrect signal as it's produced. Another run gets outcome supervision: only the final answer is scored, with training compute and the frequency of successful full chains in its rollouts held roughly equal. Both runs end up with the same accuracy on held-out problems of the same type. In that setup, what could it even mean to say the process-supervised model has learned to reason while the outcome-supervised model has merely learned to score well?

Your job: design the best test you can to distinguish 'the model is actually running through the steps and using them to reach the answer' from 'the model emits step-shaped text because that's what got rewarded, while the final answer is produced some other way'. Be concrete about the probes or interventions you'd run at test time, and say for each what result would push you toward each interpretation. Then step back and say what behavioral or representational evidence of this sort can never settle, and where you'd be relying on a contested notion of "actually reasoning." I've seen this distinction asserted as if it were obvious, so please explicitly separate the parts that are settled from the parts people who work on this genuinely disagree about, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
