Here is a passage from Peter Suber, The Paradox of Self-Amendment (1990), Section 3, "The Dilemma":

"The authority to change legal rules is provided by a special group of legal rules which we may call (after Hart) rules of change. Rules of change permit and structure the process of enactment, amendment, and repeal. Can the rules of change permit and structure the process of their own change? More pointedly, can they authorize their own change into forms inconsistent with their original forms? Can they make their self-change irrevocable?"

Suber is writing about law, but the same structure recurs in any system that changes its own rules, from a written constitution to a game such as Nomic, in which the principal move is the amendment of the game's own rules. I want a working definition of a rule in force in such a self-amending system: a criterion that determines which rules are actually binding on the participants at a given time, as opposed to rules that are merely proposed, aspirational, customary, or superseded.

Please give me a definition that counts the following as rules in force:

(a) a written amending clause in a small association's bylaws requiring a two-thirds vote to amend the bylaws;
(b) a standing rule of the association specifying the procedure by which new standing rules are enacted.

Counts the following as not in force:

(c) a drafted but never adopted proposal to replace the amending clause;
(d) a preamble stating that the association's rules should remain adaptable.

And gives a definite answer either way for each of the following:

(e) an entrenched clause that requires a two-thirds vote to amend itself and also states that this same clause may never be repealed;
(f) a consistent unwritten practice by which the members amend the bylaws by consensus even though the written amending clause specifies a simple majority vote;
(g) an amending clause that is amended through its own procedure into a form inconsistent with the original clause, for example by lowering the very voting threshold that made the amendment possible;
(h) a game rule stating that any player may amend that very rule at any time and in any way.

Propose a definition, run it against (a) through (h), say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the apparently surprising verdicts. If you revise it, show that the revised version still gets the earlier cases right. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the definition as a whole, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
