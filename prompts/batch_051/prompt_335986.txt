I'm in a graduate decision theory course, and one session on the Death in Damascus case left me with an argument I can't defuse. It begins from a scenario everyone in the room seems to accept as coherent, and it appears to show that decision theory simply has nothing to say in such cases — which can't be right. I'm hoping you'll locate the exact step where the argument breaks, and give me a sense of what I'd give up by rejecting that step rather than one of the others.

The setup is this. Tonight you must choose between staying in Damascus and fleeing to Aleppo. Death — or, if you prefer, an infallible predictor with a long and perfect record — has already predicted which city you will be in, and will be waiting in exactly that city. If you are in the city where Death is waiting, you die; if you are in the other city, you live. You know all of this, and you know the prediction has never once been wrong.

Here is the argument:

(1) A rational resolution of a decision problem must at least be an act that survives reflection: after you have settled on it, you should not be left with conclusive reason that another available act is strictly better. Call an act that survives this test "stable."

(2) If you were to settle on staying in Damascus, you would — given the predictor's perfect record — be certain that Death is waiting in Damascus. But conditional on Death being in Damascus, fleeing to Aleppo is strictly better than staying: it saves your life where staying loses it.

(3) So staying is not stable. Settling on it makes the alternative demonstrably better.

(4) By parity of reasoning, fleeing is not stable either. Settle on fleeing, and you should be certain Death is waiting in Aleppo; conditional on that, staying is strictly better.

(5) Staying and fleeing are the only pure acts available. So no pure act is stable.

(6) If no pure act is stable, then breaking the tie by a coin flip or a hunch does not make the chosen act rational; it merely performs one of the unstable acts anyway. But rationality cannot have nothing to say in a two-option, life-and-death problem. At least one of the acts must be permissible.

(7) So something in (1)–(6) must be false, and whichever premise goes, it seems to take a widely held principle of rational choice down with it.

Where does this go wrong? My own commitments, for what they are worth: I would rather not reject the predictor scenario outright, since Newcomb-style cases are usually put forward precisely to show that perfect predictions are worth taking seriously rather than declaring incoherent. And I am tempted to reject (1), on the grounds that stability feels like an aesthetic requirement rather than a normative one, but I can't see whether the argument would survive without it. I'd like the candidate repairs priced, not just named.

Please answer in the following four titled sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. The diagnosis (about 150 words): state exactly which step or steps fail, and say whether the flaw is inferential or whether the argument is valid and a premise must go.
2. Why the plausible steps look forced (about 250 words): for each innocent-looking premise, explain why it seemed undeniable, and why the step you identify as the failure initially looked like harmless bookkeeping.
3. What each repair costs (about 300 words): assess the main live repairs — rejecting stability, restricting dominance reasoning when the relevant states depend on acts, weakening or rejecting the perfect-predictor setup, admitting mixed acts, or any other candidate you take seriously — and price each in terms of what must be revised elsewhere in decision theory.
4. The bottom line (about 150 words): say what the agent in Damascus should actually do, and state the reformulated principle of rational choice, if any, that survives your diagnosis.
