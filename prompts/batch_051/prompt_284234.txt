I am trying to pin down what I actually think about when relying on a cognitive tool erodes a person's understanding of a domain, as opposed to merely changing how they exercise that understanding or delegating a peripheral skill. I have collected my gut verdicts on a series of cases, and I want to know whether there is a single principle behind them, or a small set of candidates, and which of my verdicts would have to go.

Here are the cases.

A radiologist now reads studies by starting from an AI system's highlighted findings and suggested diagnoses. After several years, she is still good at catching the system's false positives, but it takes her much longer than it used to, and she no longer notices the subtle patterns she once picked up on her own. Her overall performance with the system is better than her old unaided performance ever was. My verdict: her diagnostic understanding has eroded.

A physicist who once did the long symbolic derivations in his papers by hand now runs them through a computer algebra system. He could still explain every rule the system applies and could reconstruct a simple derivation from scratch if asked, though it would take him a while. My verdict: his understanding of the physics has not eroded.

A chess player analyzes nearly all his games with an engine. His tournament results have improved, and he articulates positional concepts such as piece activity, pawn structure, and anticipating the opponent's ideas far better than before, but his ability to calculate long variations unaided has measurably declined. My verdict: his chess understanding has increased, not eroded, though he has lost something.

A coastal sailor who once navigated by charts, tide tables, and dead reckoning now sails with a chartplotter and autopilot. He can no longer sketch the coastline or predict a current without the plotter, though he is objectively safer with it than he ever was without it. My verdict: his navigational understanding has eroded.

A lawyer who spent a year relying on an AI legal-research tool now finds she cannot remember how to build a Boolean search or trace how a case has been treated by later courts without the tool, though her command of the underlying law is as good as ever. My verdict: her legal understanding has not eroded; she lost a research knack, not understanding.

A physician now follows a hospital care-pathway algorithm for common admissions. When a trainee asks why step 3 comes before step 4, she says that is what the algorithm says. She catches gross errors but cannot reconstruct the rationale for most of the steps. My verdict: her understanding of the treatment pathway has eroded.

Someone who once spoke a second language decently now uses a real-time translation app for nearly every conversation in that language. Her comprehension is intact, but her active production has collapsed. My verdict: her understanding of the language has eroded.

A student uses an AI tutor that asks Socratic questions about a chapter of mechanics. At the end of the term she explains the material better than before, and she has also become better at generating her own questions when the tutor is taken away. My verdict: her understanding has increased.

A programmer who once wrote code from scratch has, after two years of AI pair-programming, lost the ability to start a project from a blank file without the assistant. She can still read, review, and repair the assistant's output well, and she can explain what the code does. My verdict: my gut says her programming understanding has eroded, but I keep going back and forth on this one, precisely because she can still read and repair code.

The radiologist and the chess player are the pair I cannot get under one heading: both lost an unaided capacity and are better with the tool than they ever were without it, yet I call the radiologist's case erosion and the chess player's case growth.

My half-formed principle is something like this: understanding is eroded only when what gets delegated is a capacity whose exercise partly constitutes understanding of the domain in question, and it is not eroded when the delegated capacity is merely instrumental to that understanding. But the sailor and lawyer cases make me distrust this principle, because the constitutive/instrumental line seems to do the work in exactly the places where I am least sure how to draw it.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, and for each clause or component say which case or cases motivate it. Then say what the principle predicts for the programmer case, and for a further case I have not listed: a mathematician who once proved theorems by hand and now can only check machine-generated proofs, though she checks them carefully and catches the machine's gaps. Finally, be honest: which of my verdicts does the best simple principle force me to drop, and which would survive only if I complicated the principle enough that I should probably distrust it?
