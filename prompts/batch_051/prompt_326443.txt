I chair a state parole board. We decide roughly three hundred release cases a year, each on a short docket, and we vote on whether to grant release to people who have completed their minimum sentence. Every case file contains two kinds of material. There is the individual story: the person's testimony at the hearing, their disciplinary record, the programming they completed, a release plan, any victim or survivor statement, and the impressions board members form from all of it. And there is the actuarial category: a risk class that places the person in a reference class of people with similar offense history, age, and other static factors, together with that class's historical rate of new offenses. By the 'inside view' I mean the attempt to understand this particular person's trajectory and predict them as an individual; by the 'outside view' I mean anchoring on what happened to broadly similar people in the past.

The recurring problem is that the two views pull in different directions. Board members are regularly moved by a composed, well-told account even when the actuarial category says people who look like this reoffend at a high rate; but we are equally capable of applying a base rate mechanically to a person who is genuinely not a member of the reference class the score assumes. Assume the actuarial categories are reasonably calibrated and lawfully adopted; the question is not how to audit the instrument, but how the board should reason with it and with everything else in the file.

I want a reusable decision procedure, one that a board member with no formal training in probability or decision theory can run in about twenty minutes on a single case, that tells us how to combine the inside and outside views rather than simply defaulting to one of them. Write that procedure. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to deny or defer, where it hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than one of prediction, and where it requires the full board to hold an individualized hearing. It should include a rule for selecting a reference class, and for what to do when two defensible reference classes yield materially different base rates and a board member is tempted to choose whichever one fits a prior lean. It should also say explicitly which distinctions must survive simplification and which can safely be dropped in practice.

Then run the procedure on three stylized cases. First, a person whose actuarial risk category is low but whose hearing was evasive and whose release plan has concrete, obvious gaps, including a housing commitment that no longer exists and no verified employment. Second, a person whose actuarial risk category is high but whose file contains an unusual, documented, case-specific fact that the instrument cannot see: during a year of work release they built a small business that now employs two family members and holds a lease with a year remaining. Third, a person for whom two reasonable reference classes point in opposite directions. At least one of the three applications should produce an outcome a veteran board member would resist. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do, and the conditions under which following it would make our decisions systematically worse rather than better.
