I'm a retired physicist, and since I stopped going into the lab I've been reading a lot of moral philosophy to keep busy. Last week two of my old labmates came over for lunch and got into it about a mutual friend of ours. This guy, Greg, drives alone to work every day in an enormous pickup truck, through two residential streets with a school crossing, and he's bad about glancing at his phone at stoplights. None of us is a saint either, but Greg was the one they were arguing about. I mostly just listened, and I genuinely still don't know where I come down. The argument went something like this, more or less as I remember it:

(1) "What Greg does is a small but real chance of killing somebody, and he picks that risk entirely on his own. That's exactly the sort of thing you need someone's consent for. It doesn't become fine just because the probability happens to be low."

(2) "Then none of us could ever cook with gas, ride a bike, or walk a dog, because all of those impose some tiny risk on other people without asking them. His commute is a negligible addition to the background risk we all live inside and all quietly accept."

(3) "Accepting background risk isn't consenting to Greg making it worse. The pedestrian on that sidewalk didn't opt into the extra two tons of steel, and the downside is all hers — if something goes wrong she's dead and he's a little late. It's avoidable, it's lopsided, and 'small' is not the same as 'zero.'"

(4) "So where's your line, then? Which risks are too small to count? You can't draw that line without it being arbitrary, and once it's arbitrary you're back to 'wrong whenever I'm sufficiently upset about it.' Expected-value reasoning at least doesn't pretend to have a bright line: it counts everyone's risks and benefits the same, and if the expected harm of Greg's truck commute were greater than the benefit, it should stop."

(5) "The line doesn't have to be a number. It's set by what a person could reasonably refuse to have dumped on her by someone else. Nobody can reasonably demand a zero-risk world, but she can reasonably refuse to carry the downside of Greg's convenience when most of the benefit is his. Small risks matter when they're not fairly distributed."

(6) "But 'reasonably refuse' is just as squishy as 'too small to matter' — it lets any nervous person veto any activity. And the benefit isn't all Greg's: the pedestrian gets the whole world of paved roads, ambulance access, groceries, schools. The tiny risk of a passing truck is the price of that deal. If we each had to give consent every morning, we'd have none of it."

Adjudicate this move by move. Rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific points, and what one or both of them are missing that would seperate the strong parts of their positions from the weak ones.
