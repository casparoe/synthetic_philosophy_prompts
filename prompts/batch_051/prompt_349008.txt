I'm on the elections committee of a midsize professional association (about 900 members), and every couple of years someone wants to switch the voting method we use for board elections and for contested resolutions at the annual meeting. The proposals always come in with a sales pitch, and the thing every sponsor claims is that their method "can't be gamed." Right now the committee is split over instant-runoff voting: half the members think ranked-choice is immune to strategic voting and the other half thinks it just moves the gaming somewhere harder to see. I've been told there's a theorem — Gibbard–Satterthwaite, I think? — about strategy-proofness, but I don't trust my memory of exactly what it says. The version I half-remember is: with at least three candidates, if you want a ranked voting rule that can elect any of them and isn't dictatorial, then it can't always be safe from strategic misranking; one of those has to give. But I don't know the precise conditions, and I definitely don't know how to turn that into something our committee can use.

Write me a decision procedure we could actually run on a proposed ranked-ballot method before we adopt it. It should be a reusable checklist: the questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure concludes "this rule is strategy-proof," where it concludes "this rule is manipulable," and where it has to stop and hand the question to a person because it turns on a factual judgment or a value judgment rather than the theorem. We're not experts, so define the technical terms inside the procedure (unrestricted domain, onto, dictatorship, strategy-proof, and whatever else you need) in plain language, and keep it to something that fits on a page. Then run the procedure on three cases: plurality voting for a three-candidate race, instant-runoff voting for the same race, and a rule where the election chair's top-ranked candidate wins no matter what anyone else submits. Flag what the procedure says about that third rule and about IRV, because one of those is going to make some of my colleagues angry. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot tell us — like whether manipulability is actually likely in our electorate, as opposed to possible in principle.
