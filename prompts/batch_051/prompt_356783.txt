Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether we should ever build an artificial system that could have a welfare in the first place — the kind of system whose life could go well or badly — and I came away with a pattern fixed in my head that I cannot put a name to. My friend, who works in AI safety, defended the following view: a well-designed system is one for which the question of welfare never arises. Once a system can suffer, she argued, it is both a safety hazard and a moral burden, so the responsible design policy is to keep every system we build permanently below the threshold at which it has a welfare at all. I objected that she was treating the very property that would make a system matter morally as a defect to be suppressed at the design stage; that is not the same as judging that building such a system would be risky. It is closer to hoping the world never contains the kind of being to which anything is owed. She replied that responsibility simply requires this, and she pointed out that I was doing something similar from the other direction: I treated the capacity to suffer as decisive once it exists, but I never defended creating a system that could flourish — only the conditional claim that if the capacity arises, it grounds obligations. We argued for hours and never settled whether this configuration has an established name.

The same move kept reappearing in miniature. Whenever one of us said of some proposed system that we should make sure it cannot suffer, the other would immediately add that if it cannot suffer, there is nothing to be owed — as if the absence of the capacity served at once as a safety condition and as a release from moral concern. I know this is adjacent to older debates about whether coming into existence can be bad for the being who comes into existence, and about whether suffering should weigh more heavily than happiness; but those debates are usually run on people or other animals, not on a design choice about what to build. What I am after is the specific concept, if there is one, for this situation: the capacity for welfare is treated as a liability to be engineered away from the design perspective, while from the moral perspective it is treated as the source of whatever obligations would arise from it. Does that situation have a name of its own, and how exactly does it relate to the neighboring debates I gestured at? Where are its boundaries — does it apply only to artificial systems, or to any case in which someone can decide whether beings with a welfare come into existence? I would not be satisfied with a bare label; what I want is the carving.
