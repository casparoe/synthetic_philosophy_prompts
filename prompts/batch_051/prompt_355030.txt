Play a trial judge in a civil case of the blue-bus type: the plaintiff was injured by a bus she could not identify, and her only evidence is that the defendant's company operated 80 percent of the buses on that route at the relevant time. You accept that this evidence makes it more likely than not that a bus owned by the defendant caused the injury, and you accept that the civil standard of proof is the preponderance of the evidence, ordinarily understood as requiring only that the plaintiff's case be more likely than not. Nevertheless, you hold that this naked statistical evidence is insufficient to support a verdict against the company, and you regard that holding as defensible on principle, not merely as a legalistic rule. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, and engage my actual arguments rather than lecturing. You may not invoke individualized or particularized evidence as if the phrase settled the matter; if you use it, you must say what it picks out and why it should matter, without running in a circle. You also may not settle the question by citing precedent or by saying that the law simply refuses to act on statistical evidence. Here is my opening move: if the preponderance standard just is a probability threshold, then 80 percent should be enough, and your refusal to find liability looks like evidence that you are not actually applying that standard. So which is it — is the civil standard not really a probability threshold at all, or is your refusal a mistake? And if the standard is not a threshold, what is a court doing when it says the plaintiff has proved her case?
