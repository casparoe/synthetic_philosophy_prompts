In a translation workshop, a disagreement about a memoir passage ended with someone citing the old proverb "traduttore, traditore" — "the translator is a traitor" — as if it settled things: every translation betrays the original, so it makes no sense to call one version more faithful than another. I doubt the proverb can carry that much weight. What distinct precise claims might it be expressing — about what is lost or distorted in translation, whether that loss is inevitable or merely common, what "fidelity" is fidelity to (sense, style, force, the reader's experience), and whether "betrayal" is meant literally, morally, or as a metaphor? Lay out the readings as a menu; for each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; and identify which reading, if any, would actually support the use to which the proverb was put in the workshop.
