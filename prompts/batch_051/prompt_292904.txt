I'm drafting a short position statement on knowledge and practical interests for an epistemology workshop, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is where I've landed.

(1) Fallibilism. A subject can know that p even when her evidence does not entail p.

(2) Epistemic contextualism, as a semantic thesis. The proposition expressed by a knowledge ascription such as "S knows that p" can vary with the ascriber's context — in particular, with which error possibilities are salient and what practical interests are in play. My version is ascriber-driven: it is the speaker's context, not the subject's, that sets the epistemic standards.

(3) The familiar bank-case verdicts. In the low-stakes bank case, the subject's self-ascription "I know the bank will be open on Saturday" is true. In the high-stakes version, with the same evidence but a costly error if she is wrong, the subject's "I don't know whether the bank will be open on Saturday" is true and the positive self-ascription is not. I also accept the third-person contextualist result: a disinterested observer in a low-stakes context may truly say of the high-stakes subject, "She knows the bank will be open on Saturday," even while the subject's own high-stakes "I don't know" is also true in her context.

(4) The knowledge norm of assertion, biconditional. A speaker is well enough positioned to flat-out assert that p if and only if the speaker knows that p, with "knows" evaluated according to the assertor's context.

(5) The knowledge norm of practical reasoning, sufficiency direction. If a subject knows that p, then she may legitimately rely on p as a categorical premise in practical deliberation, even when a great deal rides on whether p.

(6) A rejection of pragmatic encroachment. I do not think practical stakes directly alter a subject's knowledge state itself; what stakes do is raise or lower the standards in the ascriber's context. I also take it that whether a person may rationally rely on p in action is settled by her own epistemic position and practical situation, not by the context of some third party describing her.

Which of these actually pull against one another, and which only appear to? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and for each tension say which commitment would have to give and how costly that revision would be. Then tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence while preserving as much of the package as possible.

Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
