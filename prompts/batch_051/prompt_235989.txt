I'm a journalist preparing a feature on how comparative psychologists police the boundary between physiological and mental explanation. For the piece, I want a dialogue that turns on a single methodological rule from C. Lloyd Morgan's An Introduction to Comparative Psychology (1894). Morgan there proposes, in substance, that an animal's behavior should never be interpreted as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty when it can be interpreted as the outcome of one lower in the psychological scale. I am deliberately paraphrasing rather than quoting. Write a dialogue between two equally competent researchers: one who regards this principle as an indispensable parsimony constraint, and another who argues it has functioned as a bias against attributing genuine cognition to nonhuman animals. Have them work through a concrete case, such as a raven that appears to maneuver another raven away from a hidden food cache by leading it to a decoy location. The exchange should not be a strawman; each should concede what is strong in the other's position. The central disputed question should be whether the phrase can be interpreted does real methodological work, or whether it smuggles in a prior commitment about where the psychological scale bottoms out. End with each character stating the observation or experiment that would, in their view, move the issue forward.
