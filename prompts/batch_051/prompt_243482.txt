I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, so please explain any technical terms you introduce. I'm troubled by an inconsistency between my company's written policy for handling bugs and outages and what we actually do, and I want help reconstructing the view our actual practice implicitly assumes.

The written policy is a severity-scoring system. Every bug or outage gets a numerical score based on how many users it affects, how severe the harm is (data loss, downtime, degraded performance), and how long it lasts. In principle, engineers work tickets in descending score order. In practice, something else happens. If one identifiable customer—usually a significant account—is actively affected, we open a war room and pull senior engineers off scheduled fixes, including fixes that by our own scoring would prevent hundreds or thousands of smaller outages across the user base, until that one customer is satisfied. The same pattern appears with vulnerabilities: an exploit being actively used against one prominent customer receives immediate attention, while a cluster of lower-impact vulnerabilities affecting far more users can sit unaddressed for months because no single affected party is conspicuous enough to force action.

So the written rule treats the right priority as roughly severity multiplied by the number of people affected, while the actual practice behaves as if that product is not what matters. What view of how harms and benefits aggregate does the actual practice presuppose, as distinct from the written rule? Does it presuppose that many small harms cannot simply be added together until they outweigh a serious harm to one identifiable party? Does it presuppose that a harm concentrated in one person, who can complain about it specifically, has a kind of weight that the same harm spread across an anonymous aggregate does not? Does it presuppose that the relevant test is not the total harm prevented but whether the decision can be justified to each affected person individually? Or is the practice better explained by non-philosophical considerations—retention incentives, contractual obligations to premium accounts, fear of one vocal client—that merely resemble such a view?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with. Identify places where the practice is underdetermined, in the sense that several rival theories would license the same behavior, and say whether the written rule and the actual practice presuppose consistent views. Finally, tell me what would have to change in our triage and escalation procedures if we replaced the implicit view with a thoroughly additive one, on which harms and benefits simply sum across individuals, and whether anything of moral importance would be lost.
