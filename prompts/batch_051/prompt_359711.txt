I'm leading a discussion of Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," and I want to read it through the lens of ritual substitution and the transfer of guilt. The story gives us a city whose entire happiness is made to rest on one child kept locked away in filth and misery. Everyone old enough knows how the arrangement works: the child's suffering is treated as the price of the general flourishing, and while most citizens accept this and work it into their conscience, a few walk away in silence. I would like you to interpret the story with the categories of the scapegoat and the sacrificial victim kept clearly in view, and to press on the differences among them.

In particular: does the child actually function as a scapegoat in the traditional sense, in which a community's guilt or impurity is transferred to a victim and carried off, or is this instead a story about a community assigning costs to someone who cannot refuse? The child is never accused of a crime and is not exactly blamed or expelled in a moment of collective violence; the child is kept, deliberately, as a standing condition of the good. Does that make the child a sacrifice rather than a scapegoat, and if so, does the sacrifice work? Where, in your reading, does the guilt live: in the child, somehow transferred there; in the citizens who stay and know; in the structure of the arrangement itself; or in the reader who finds the bargain almost persuasive? The people who walk away seem to reject the deal, but they do nothing for the child beyond leaving. Does the story treat that as the only honest response, or as another evasion?

I want a committed reading, not a survey of options. Take a definite position on what the child most fundamentally is in the story and on whether the mechanism of substitution succeeds or fails, and defend it by working through the story's central details: the child's condition, the way the citizens learn about it, the ones who stay, the ones who walk away, and the narrator's stance toward the reader. Use paraphrase rather than quotation and do not assign page citations. Say explicitly where the story resists or complicates the theoretical idea of the scapegoat rather than merely illustrating it.
