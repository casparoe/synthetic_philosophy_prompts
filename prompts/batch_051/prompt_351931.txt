I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I'm planning a feature on whether increasingly autonomous systems leave us without anyone to hold responsible when things go badly wrong. For my own thinking, I'd like you to write a short dialogue between two philosophers set during a public inquiry into a fatal crash involving an autonomous shuttle. Their disagreement is this. The first holds that the crash exhibits a genuine responsibility gap: the engineers observed reasonable standards, the operator's oversight matched the vehicle's design, the system itself cannot be an apt target of blame, and yet the loss feels not merely tragic but normatively unanswered, so something real is missing from our practices rather than merely unassigned. The second holds that this is an artifact of a backward-looking, retributive picture of responsibility: responsibility is better understood as a family of forward-looking practices, including liability, insurance, licensing, monitoring duties, and conditions on whether and where such systems are deployed, and the correct response is to redistribute those practices so that the costs of the system's failures settle on the parties who chose to field it, rather than to hunt for a culpable individual and call the failure to find one a gap.

The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth: each character should engage the other's strongest point rather than a strawman, and they should not talk past one another. Have each character, in closing, name the one point on which the other came closest to changing their mind. After the dialogue, add a short note stating how confident you are in your rendering of the two positions and singling out the point in the exchange you are least sure of.
