Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether there is a morally significant difference between lying and deliberately misleading someone without making a false statement. His recurring move was to invoke the saying, "A lie of omission is still a lie," as though that settled the matter: because someone who withholds or omits relevant information is lying, misleading by omission deserves the same moral condemnation as asserting a known falsehood. I am fairly sure the saying is doing more work than it can bear, but in the moment I could not articulate the distinct claims it might be expressing. Could you take that one-liner apart for me? I would like the different precise theses it could stand for laid out as a menu: for example, claims about whether omissions are literally lies, claims about whether omissions are equally culpable, claims about whether they violate the same conversational or moral norms, and any other readings you can identify. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and plausible thesis, or false (or so imprecise as to lack a determinate truth value), and indicate which of the readings would actually support my friend's conclusion that misleading by omission is morally on a par with lying. I am less interested in which single reading is "correct" than in seeing the field of possible readings and what each one would actually license.
