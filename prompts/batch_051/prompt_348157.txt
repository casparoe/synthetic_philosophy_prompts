You are a professional philosopher with a side specialty in designing active-learning exercises for philosophy classrooms. Think through the design carefully and from scratch rather than reusing a familiar activity.

Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in normative ethics (16 students, 80 minutes) that makes participants feel the force of consequentialist cluelessness: the problem that if an act’s rightness depends on its total consequences, then even mundane acts ramify into further consequences—including effects on complex social chains and on which people will later exist—that no finite agent can enumerate, let alone assign probabilities to, so the agent cannot know what consequentialism actually recommends. The central constraint is that this problem must emerge from what participants do, not from a framing lecture. Do not use the terms “cluelessness,” “consequentialism,” or “expected value” before the debrief.

Think in particular about small real choices the students could actually make during the session, a structured way to pass those choices through several rounds of consequence-tracing, and rules that systematically multiply or conceal information so that the cascade becomes unsurveyable in the students’ hands.

Your deliverable should include: (1) the setup and materials, including a concrete small-stakes payoff mechanism that requires no real money; (2) the rules and round structure, written tightly enough that another instructor could run the exercise; (3) a cold open that gets them playing within minutes; (4) a timed plan for the full 80 minutes through the debrief; (5) a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the philosophical structure—for example, where consequences could still be listed, where probabilities could no longer be assigned, and where identity-ripple effects appear—and that then lays out the main philosophical responses, such as retreating to expected consequences over knowable outcomes, distinguishing objective from subjective rightness, or giving up consequentialism; (6) a list of predictable failure modes and how to handle them; and (7) an honest closing note on which features of real consequentialist cluelessness the exercise cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must supply. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanisms for the activity, then choose and defend the strongest one.
