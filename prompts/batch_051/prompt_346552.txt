I'm a retired chemist and I've only started reading epistemology proper in the last few years, so forgive me if I'm trampling over distinctions people have already made. The standoff I keep coming back to is in the epistemology of conspiracy theories. On one side you have the generalists: people who think there's something wrong with conspiracy thinking as such — that such theories are self-sealing, or unfalsifiable, or that the prior on any given one should be very low because most don't pan out. On the other side are the particularists, who point out that real conspiracies do happen (Tuskegee, the tobacco industry burying what it knew, Volkswagen's emissions cheating) and say each theory has to be judged on its own evidence like any other empirical claim. This debate seems to me to have been going in circles for decades without either side moving an inch. My working theory is that the two sides are calculating the base rate over different reference classes: generalists are sampling from the population of all accusations that ever circulated, including the crazy ones, while particularists are sampling from only the accusations that eventually got investigated and confirmed. If that's right, then the real disagreement isn't about rationality at all, it's about what population you're conditioning on — a sampling problem. But I'm honestly not sure. Alot of it might also be a verbal fight about the word 'conspiracy theory,' or just two sets of bedrock attitudes toward official institutions that evidence isn't going to budge. Diagnose the standoff for me. What kind of disagreement is it actually, and given the diagnosis, what would count as real progress — or should I just expect it to stay stuck? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question. American English, please.
