So I'm writing a novel set in a near-future country where health care is entirely public and everything hinges on the official Schedule of Diseases. The country tried to settle the old fight about what counts as a disease by making the baseline purely statistical: a condition gets listed as a disease iff the relevant trait is more than two standard deviations below the population mean on some species-typical function and that deviation is correlated with lower survival or reproductive success. No values allowed in at the ground floor — 'disease' is supposed to be a purely descriptive, biostatistical category, like being underweight or hypertensive.

But they knew pure naturalism would leave alot of people angry (deaf people who don't want their condition called a disease, infertile couples who do want theirs funded, and so on), so they bolted on a popular override. Once a year, any citizen can propose adding a condition to the Schedule or striking one off; if a proposal gets a hundred thousand signatures it goes on the ballot, and the three proposals with the most votes take effect immediately, regardless of what the statistics say. The agency's own statistical revision only happens every five years, and at that point it can quietly put things back.

I want to make sure the book doesn't just use this as set dressing. Walk me through how this thing actually behaves. What gets added and removed first, who organises around it, how do the statistical baseline and the yearly vote pull aginst each other, and where does the whole notion of 'disease' start to drift? Where does it get gamed, by patients, doctors, pharma, or activists? And are the failure modes the kind you could patch with a better rule, or is this two-track system just re-creating the naturalism-vs-normativism argument inside an institution?
