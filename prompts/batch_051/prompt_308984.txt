I've been reading rationalist-adjacent discussions of prediction markets for years, so I have the basic pitch of futarchy in my head: let a democratic process settle on the goal or metric of success, and then let speculative markets work out which policies would actually achieve that metric. But I've never studied the market mechanism itself, and I keep running into characterizations of the view that sound wrong to me without my being able to say exactly why. A recent essay I read summarized it roughly as follows. Futarchy is the idea of replacing elections and legislatures with a national stock market. Instead of voting on policies, citizens would buy and sell shares whose prices track the expected value of some agreed outcome measure, such as GDP or life expectancy; the market price would aggregate everyone's information, and whichever policy the market expects to produce the highest value would be adopted automatically. Because markets are efficient, this is supposed to guarantee better decisions than any legislature could make. The only remaining role for voting is to select the metric. On the strength of that summary, the author called futarchy a technocratic fantasy that ignores politics, and a commenter added that it is just plutocracy with extra steps: traders with the deepest pockets could move the price wherever they liked and thereby buy policy outcomes outright.

I doubt the plutocracy conclusion, but I also suspect the original summary quietly conflates a few different kinds of markets and overstates what market efficiency actually buys. I vaguely recall that the interesting version of the proposal relies on conditional markets, meaning bets on the outcome metric contingent on adopting each candidate policy, rather than a single market that merely predicts current conditions, but I cannot articulate how that distinction matters or whether it rescues the proposal from the commenter's objection. Could you give me a careful accuracy audit of the summary and the commenter's response? Please separate the claims a patient defender of futarchy would accept, the claims that are fair simplifications for a general audience, and the claims that are genuine mistakes or distortions, and then say whether the technocratic-fantasy and plutocracy objections survive once the view is restated accurately.
