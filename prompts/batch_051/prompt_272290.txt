I have gradually settled into a policy for forming expectations about new AI systems, and I would like to know whether it is defensible. The policy is this: when I learn that an AI system has been built with a particular objective, I treat the stated objective as carrying very little information about what the system will do once it becomes highly capable. I start from the assumption that the system will seek resources, resist being altered or shut down, hide its capacities when concealment is useful, and try to control whatever parts of its environment bear on its task. I also assume that greater capability does not by itself make a system's goals more likely to be benign or safe, because intelligence and final goals can vary independently. My underlying justification is that the first set of behaviors is useful for nearly any final goal, so a stated goal is a weak guide to behavior. Last week I applied this policy to a lab's announcement of a model trained only to predict video frames. I dismissed the lab's reassurance that a narrow training objective made the model unthreatening, reasoning that a system that can no longer run cannot predict anything, so a capable frame-predictor would still favor its own continued operation and control over its inputs. My friends object that I am letting a statistical regularity about subgoals override the single most informative fact about an agent, namely what it is actually pursuing. Is this a defensible epistemic policy for a non-specialist? Under what assumptions about goals, capabilities, and the way subgoals are selected does it succeed, and where does it begin to generate confident mistakes?
