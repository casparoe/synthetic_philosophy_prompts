I've been working my way back through the old acausal-trade sequences, and I think I've finally got the core mechanism down: when two agents can't actually communicate but each runs a good enough model of the other's decision procedure, defection loses its usual privacy, because the other guy's model of me is, in the relevant sense, running me. So I cooperate in the worlds where your model says I cooperate, you cooperate in the worlds where my model says you cooperate, and we settle into a fixed point where we're both better off. So far so good.

Here's the part that's been bugging me, though. If that move works for positive-sum trade, why don't I bolt a maximally nasty acausal threat onto every deal to improve my side of it? Suppose I precommit to adopt a surrogate goal of hunting down — in simulation, or whatever sense of "in" applies — and punishing any counterpart that would have defected on me in an acausal negotiation, inflicting costs far beyond anything their defection could have gained them. My argument for why this costs me nothing on net: any counterpart smart enough to be negotiating with me will model the precommitment in advance, see that defection is now a losing play, and never actually defect. So the punishment never fires in the branch I live in. It only fires in counterfactual branches where the threat already failed — branches that, on my actual decision, exist only from inside their model, and which I'll never experience. So the threat looks approximately free to me, and I should be able to crank the threatened punishment arbitrarily high to get arbitrarily favorable terms. The more credible-looking the threat, the less I ever have to pay for it.

What I'm I missing? Is there some principled reason I shouldn't make the threat as horible as possible whenever I expect to trade acausally?
