At my decision-theory reading group, two members ended up in a long disagreement about the absent-minded driver, and I never managed to reach a verdict. The setup, as they were using it: a driver in a bar plans her route home. There are two exits on the highway; taking the first drops her in a rough neighborhood (payoff 0), taking the second gets her home (payoff 4), and missing both leaves her on a bad stretch of road (payoff 1). She is absent-minded: whenever she reaches an exit, she cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. Because the exits are indistinguishable to her, an implementable rule is just a probability p of continuing whenever she finds herself at an exit, and the expected value of rule p calculated before she sets out is 4p − 3p², maximized at p = 2/3, where it is worth 4/3.

Marta argued that the driver should adopt p = 2/3 and then, at any exit, simply execute that randomization; the only coherent question, she said, is which whole rule is best from the planning position, since a policy that conditions on knowing which exit she is at would require information she does not have. Jude pushed back that once the driver is standing at an exit, rationality means maximizing expected payoff relative to her beliefs there, and under p = 2/3 she should assign probability 3/5 to being at the first exit and 2/5 to the second, at which point continuing and exiting are exactly tied. He then pressed on a nearby rule: under p = 0.8, the probability of being at the second exit is 4/9, so exiting is worth 16/9 while continuing is worth 12/9; at that decision point, exiting is the locally rational move. The fact that p = 2/3 maximizes ex ante value, Jude said, gives the driver at the exit no reason to keep faith with a plan that was optimal only for her earlier self. Marta replied that the local calculation cannot be used as a decision guide without collapsing: since the driver cannot make the deviation conditional on the exit, if deviating is rational at one exit it is rational at every exit, and the resulting rule — always exit — has ex ante value 0, which is worse by the driver’s own planning lights. The local criterion, she concluded, is only a consistency test that singles out p = 2/3 as the rule under which neither action is strictly better at the point of choice, and it cannot license moving from one rule to another. Jude answered that this admission is exactly the puzzle: at the driver’s only actual point of choice, the rule gives her no unique instruction and no reason to prefer continuing over exiting, yet acting on that local indifference would unravel the ex ante optimum unless some binding principle makes her execute the precomputed randomization — and standard expected-utility theory supplies no such principle. Marta’s final move was that the principle is just the requirement that an agent facing an indistinguishability she cannot condition on must choose a rule she can actually follow.

Please referee them point by point: who is right about which specific claim, where each argument overreaches or underreaches, and what both of them are missing. Give the verdict as exactly five numbered findings, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or closing paragraph. Make the fifth finding state where your reasoning is weakest and what specific argument, result, or consideration would most plausibly change your overall verdict.
