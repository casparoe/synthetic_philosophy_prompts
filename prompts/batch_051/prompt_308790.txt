I'm a student in an upper-division philosophy of social science course, and my study group is preparing a handout for our unit on social categories. I'd like you to write a decision tree that classifies positions on whether generational cohorts—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and similar labels—are real kinds, or merely convenient ways of slicing up a population.

Please design it as follows. The audience is other undergraduates in the course, and the tree should be something a person can work through in about ten minutes. Aim for six to nine branch questions and five to eight leaves.

Build the tree from the logical space of claims rather than from a list of named positions. Start from the shared starting point that generational labels are not pure noise: people use them, and at least some apparent differences between cohorts survive a first glance. Each branch node should be a concrete claim or short case with a yes/no answer, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Among the questions, make sure to probe things like: whether the differences between cohorts remain after controlling for age, historical period, and life stage; whether there is a fact of the matter about membership for someone born on a cusp, such as the last day of 1964 versus the first day of 1965; whether the relevant regularities would have existed if the label had never become popular; and whether being classified as, say, Gen Z feeds back into how members of that category understand themselves and behave.

Please make sure these positions appear as leaves, each with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment: a natural-kind realism on which cohorts share a formative essence or stable dispositions; a homeostatic property-cluster view, on which a real kind is a stable cluster of co-occurring properties held together by some causal mechanism; an interactive or looping-kind view, on which the act of classification changes the people classified and thereby helps make the kind real; a nominalist view on which the intervals are conventional and do no genuine explanatory work; and a cohort-effect realism on which what is real is a continuous age-period-cohort variable, while the generational kind itself is an artifact of coarse-grained labels.

For each combination of answers that no live position occupies, note the empty region and say briefly whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because the resulting view is unattractive, or because the view is merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot distinguish, and say what further concrete case would separate them.

I don't need citations or a bibliography; the value is in the diagnostic structure rather than a literature review.
