In most legal systems, a chair left on the curb with a sign offering it to any passerby is abandoned property: the owner's rights come to an end, and whoever takes it may use it, alter it, sell it, or destroy it. By contrast, when the sole maintainer of a piece of useful software disappears — no forwarding address, no successor, no explicit license — the code remains under copyright for many decades, and anyone who copies, patches, or redistributes it without permission is technically infringing; orphan works such as books, films, and photographs whose rightholders cannot be located sit in the same position. In both sorts of case an asset is, in practice, ownerless, yet the physical object may pass into a new owner's hands while the intellectual work stays locked away. Is there a defensible principle that justifies this asymmetry, or is it largely an accident of legal history? Give the strongest justification you can construct, test it against the case of abandonware still embedded in devices people depend on, and end with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or narrowed.
