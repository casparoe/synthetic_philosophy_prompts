Take the nineteenth-century development of non-Euclidean geometry and analyze it through Douglas Hofstadter's concept of jumping out of the system, the move he calls JOOTSing and illustrates with the MU-puzzle in Gödel, Escher, Bach. I want the analysis to run in both directions: the concept should illuminate the history, and the history should expose what the concept gets right, what it gets wrong, and what it leaves out.

Here is the setup I want you to work with. In the MU-puzzle, a solver starts with the string MI and a small set of string-rewriting rules, and tries to derive MU. Working strictly inside the rules only produces endless thrashing; the puzzle yields when the solver steps outside the formal system and reasons about it, for instance by finding an invariant property shared by every derivable string, a fact about the number of I's, that MU lacks. Hofstadter's point is that the decisive move is metalevel: treat the system as an object rather than continuing to operate within it.

Now apply this to the history. For roughly two thousand years, Euclidean geometry functioned as the system: its first four postulates were treated as fixed, and the fifth postulate, the parallel postulate, looked more like an unproved theorem than an axiom. Geometer after geometer tried to derive the fifth postulate from the other four, working inside the system. The breakthrough came when Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky stepped outside that project and asked what geometry follows if the parallel postulate is denied; Bolyai and Lobachevsky developed an alternative geometry, and later model-building by Beltrami, Klein, and Poincaré showed that the new geometry was consistent relative to ordinary Euclidean geometry, which settled the old derivability question from outside.

Run both directions of the analysis. First, be disciplined about the mapping: what in the history corresponds to the formal system, the starting string, the target string, the invariant, and the jump itself? Is the jump a single moment, a single person's achievement, or a distributed shift in a research program? Second, identify where the analogy under-describes or misdescribes the episode. The historical case was not a token derivation in a well-bounded formal system; it was a millenia-old tradition in which geometry was understood as the science of physical space, and treating a postulate as negotiable was not merely a clever metalevel move but a change in what geometry was taken to be about. Spell out where the JOOTSing picture captures genuine structure and where it papers over the social, institutional, and conceptual work that made the breakthrough possible.

Third, let the history talk back to the concept. Does non-Euclidean geometry confirm JOOTSing as an account of conceptual change, or does it show that the concept is mostly a vivid relabeling of ordinary abstraction and metamathematics? What does the episode reveal about the limits of JOOTSing, for example the assumption that the system has crisp boundaries, that one can simply choose to step outside, and that the hard work is the jump rather than the construction that comes after it? Conclude with what a more adequate concept of escaping a governing framework would need to add.
