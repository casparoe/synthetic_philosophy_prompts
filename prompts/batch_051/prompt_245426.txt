At a recent reading group, a participant kept cutting off a debate about whether modern uses of certain words are "corruptions" by repeating one line: "The original meaning is the true meaning." His examples were that "decimate" properly means to reduce by one tenth, so a city "decimated" by a storm is a misuse unless exactly a tenth was destroyed; that "philosophy" means love of wisdom, so someone who does it only for a paycheck is not really doing philosophy; and that "virtue" comes from the Latin for manliness, so the concept of virtue carries an ineliminably masculine standard. I find each of those conclusions suspicious, but I cannot tell whether the problem is the slogan itself, the examples, or the move from one to the other.

I would like you to treat the slogan as a philosopher would: lay out the distinct precise claims that "the original meaning is the true meaning" could be used to express. For instance — though do not limit yourself to these — it might be a thesis about what words literally mean now, about what they ought to mean, about the continuity of a concept across time, about who has authority over a word, or about a residue of a word's history that still colors its present use. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and whether it actually gets the speaker to any of his three conclusions or whether those conclusions require an extra premise. Then say which reading or readings people who reach for this slogan typically mean, even when they have not distinguished them. I am less interested in a dictionary verdict on "decimate" than in the map of possible claims and where the slogan's apparent force comes from. One further wrinkle: I have since learned that a favorite etymology of his, that "sincere" comes from sine cera, "without wax," is probably a folk etymology; I would like the analysis to say what happens to each reading of the slogan when the etymological premise itself is false.
