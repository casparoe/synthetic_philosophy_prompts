At the opening of C. S. Peirce’s paper The Fixation of Belief, he writes: “Few persons care to study logic, because everybody conceives himself to be proficient enough in the art of reasoning already. But I observe that this satisfaction is limited to one's own ratiocination, and does not extend to that of other men.” I have turned this observation into a working policy. When I disagree with someone who seems as competent and informed as I am, I default to treating my own reasoning as though it were first-person evidence, while treating their reasoning as something I need not accept until I can independently reproduce a flaw in my own thinking. In practice this means I rarely give ground in a live dispute, even when I cannot point to a specific mistake in my interlocutor’s argument; at most I will take the disagreement away and quietly redo my reasoning alone. I recognize that a conciliationist might say I owe my interlocutor’s reasoning the same weight as my own, and that my policy looks like a double standard. But Peirce’s observation suggests my asymmetry is not idiosyncratic; it may be built into how first-person and third-person reasoning appear to a mind. Is my policy defensible under any version of the peer disagreement debate, or is it a rationalization of egocentric bias? What facts about the first-person/third-person asymmetry would need to hold for the policy to be justified, and is there a more modest version I could adopt without simply deferring to others?
