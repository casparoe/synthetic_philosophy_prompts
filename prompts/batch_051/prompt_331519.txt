I'm in an environmental political economy module and we've just read Mill's 'Of the Stationary State' (Book IV, Chapter VI of the Principles of Political Economy, 1848). The passage that's stuck with me is this one:

"It must always have been seen, more or less distinctly, by political economists, that the increase of wealth is not boundless: that at the end of what they term the progressive state lies the stationary state, that all progress in wealth is but a postponement of this, and that each step in advance is an approach to it."

Mill then turns round and says the stationary state would be a chance to fix distribution and stop chasing endless accumulation. But the thing is, we've also covered carbon taxes as the main policy tool, and the whole pitch for carbon taxes is that they let growth continue while emissions fall — the 'green growth' claim. If carbon pricing can decouple GDP growth from carbon emissions, then the climate constraint doesn't force the stationary state in Mill's sense, and his premise that all progress in wealth is merely a postponement of an inevitable stationary state looks like it's been bypassed. Does his argument still have force, or does it collapse into a purely cultural point about the rat race? I want a committed answer: tell me whether Mill's argument survives the development of green growth/decoupling, needs repair, or is dead — and identify exactly which premise the decoupling claim bears on. Don't give me a neutral survey.
