I've been working on a theory I call preference decompression. It goes like this. In option-thin environments — poverty, legal restrictions, strong family expectations, a small job menu — occupational choice is compressed: people sort mostly by material payoff and available slots, so small average differences between men and women barely get room to show up. In option-thick environments — wealthy, gender-egalitarian ones — that compression is gone. Now a person has many acceptable options, and small latent differences get much more leverage over the final choice. Then there's a second step, which I call a comparison cascade: when you face many acceptable options, you take cues from same-gender peers about which of them to pick, so a field that tips slightly female can tip further through networks, comfort, and not wanting to be the odd one out.

The upshot: the freer the choice, the more small average differences get magnified into large observed differences. So the gender-equality paradox — e.g., fewer women in STEM in Scandinavia than in some much less equal countries — is exactly what you'd expect even if the underlying average differences are tiny. The separation you measure is mostly amplification machinery, not the size of the original difference.
