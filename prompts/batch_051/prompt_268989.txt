I have been trying to pin down a pattern I keep noticing, and I do not know whether it has a name. At my school, everyone keeps to the right side of the hallway during class changes. The choice is arbitrary — nothing would break down if we had settled on the left — but it is stable because each person keeps right only because they expect everyone else to, and no one treats a left-side walker as a bad person, merely as an inconvenience. Yet I have seen other rules that seemed to start this way and then change character. In my group of friends, whoever proposes a plan generally pays for the first round of snacks; the practice began as a loose convenience, but people now get genuinely indignant when someone fails to do it, and they describe the offender in moral-sounding terms such as "cheap" or "inconsiderate." Similarly, my family has a rule that politics is not discussed at dinner. It started as a practical way of avoiding arguments, and now my parents treat a breach as a failure of respect toward the family itself, not simply as a bad idea. I have read about a philosophical account of conventions according to which a convention is an arbitrary regularity that people follow because they expect others to follow it. That account seems to fit the hallway case well, but it says little about why a regularity would begin to feel binding the way a moral rule does.

So my question is whether there is an established term for this transformation by which a merely conventional regularity comes to be felt as obligatory — as something one ought to do and may be blamed for failing to do. I am not only after a label; I want to know whether the distinction I am drawing is sharp, whether the hallway case and the dinner rule are really two ends of one spectrum or two different kinds of thing, which cases sit at the boundary (etiquette rules such as saying "please" seem to be in between), and what, if anything, must be added to the expectation-based account of conventions to explain the felt obligation.
