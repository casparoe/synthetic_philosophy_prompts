I'm a novelist, and I caught two writers at my retreat going in circles after dinner; I honestly still can't sort out the fall-out. I kept my mouth shut, but now I want someone to tell me who was actually right.

Rasha's novella centers on Marta, a ceramics teacher in Oaxaca who slowly goes blind; the character is partly based on Rasha's late aunt and mostly invented. Daniel started it: "It's true that Marta goes blind, even though Marta doesn't exist, so you can say true things about something that isn't there." Rasha said no: it's true "in the story," but in real life "Marta" picks out nobody, and a sentence with an empty name doesn't say anything true or false. Daniel: "Then 'Marta doesn't exist' can't be true either, and that one obviously is true — it's the whole reason we call her fictional." Rasha: "That one's fine, because there 'Marta' is shorthand for the character, which does exist as a story-role; only the woman fails to exist." Daniel: "So now there are two Martas, and the one you can truthfully talk about is the story-role. But then what makes 'Marta goes blind' true in the story? If the sentence isn't about any object, no fact makes it true." Rasha: "The text asserting it, plus a competent reader tracking the story's conventions, is what makes it true. No object needed." Daniel: "But readers infer unsupported things and miss supported ones, so 'whatever the right reader would infer' can't be the ground. And if you later write a second novella where an elderly Marta never went blind, we'd all call it the same character reimagined. What is 'same' picking out if the name never refers?"

Adjudicate this move by move, from clearly wrong to clearly right, with what each should concede and hold. Don't force a single winner if the honest answer is that each is right about different pieces. Then tell me the subtle issue both are missing — the thing they'd need to get their two pictures compatible, if there is one.
