My sister joined this giving group a couple of years ago and it's turned into a very practical fight about whether I'm a bad person. The group isn't religious — more like a small club that reads cost-effectiveness research and sends money to whichever charity prevents the most deaths per dollar. She now gives away about a third of her income, she canceled coming on our dad's 70th birthday trip because the airfare "could buy something like two hundred bednets," and at Christmas she told my nine-year-old nephew that his new Playstation was about a hundred bednets and he should sit with that for a minute. Everyone in the family thinks she's become insuferable.

But here's the asymmetry. Before this, I used to read profiles of people who live on almost nothing and give the rest away, and I'd think: that's the best kind of person. The local paper literally just ran one of those profiles on her — "saint next door" type thing — and I agreed with every word of it, and then didn't invite her to my own birthday dinner because I didn't want the evening ruined. So the same conduct makes her a saint from a distance and a bully up close. Is their a principle that lets me keep both responses, or is one of them bogus?

I've tried the obvious defense on myself: the problem is the preaching, not the giving. But I don't actually think the quiet version would fix it, because the canceled trip is a rebuke even without the bednet comments. If what she does is genuinely admirable, then it starts to look less like an optional hobby and more like what the rest of us owe too — which would mean the thing I can't stand is just that she's a constant reminder that I'm not doing it. A friend who took exactly one philosophy class keeps calling this "the demandingness objection," but giving a problem a nickname doesn't let me off the hook. What should I actually do with the asymmetry: keep it, reverse it, or give it up — and which side, my admiration for her or my resentment of her, goes first?
