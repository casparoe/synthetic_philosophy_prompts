Quine's argument for the indeterminacy of translation can be reconstructed as follows:

(1) A radical translator has no evidence to work with except the natives' observable linguistic behavior — ultimately, their dispositions to assent to or dissent from sentences under various stimulations.
(2) Two translation manuals can remain empirically equivalent across all possible behavioral evidence while one pairs 'gavagai' with 'rabbit' and the other with 'undetached rabbit part' or 'rabbit-stage'.
(3) Nothing beyond behavioral evidence could ever decide which manual is correct.
(4) If no possible evidence could decide between the manuals, then there is no fact of the matter about which is correct.
(5) Therefore translation between the two languages is indeterminate.

I cannot decide whether this goes through. The inference from (3) to (4) looks like exactly the kind of verificationist move that is usually rejected elsewhere; but if it is rejected, the argument appears to lose its conclusion. Is the argument valid, and if not, where precisely does it fail? In particular, can someone accept (1) and (2) yet deny (5) by holding that the two manuals describe genuine, evidence-transcendent semantic facts — or does that just restate the denial of (4) without addressing the argument?
