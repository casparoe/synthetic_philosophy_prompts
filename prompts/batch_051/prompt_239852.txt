In most democracies, a great deal of political practice is organised around a single left–right scale. Polling firms ask respondents to place themselves somewhere on that scale; commentators describe a party as having moved left or right and explain an election as a shift towards one pole; parties are called centre-left or centre-right; and voters are said to sit on one side or the other. Yet the very institutions that lean on this scale also treat political opinion as multi-dimensional: they ask separate questions about economic redistribution, cultural traditionalism, immigration, and the scope of the state, and they readily observe people who are, say, economically left-wing and culturally right-wing, or libertarian in one domain and authoritarian in another, without treating the whole scheme as broken. I want to know what this practice presupposes, not sociologically but philosophically. Does treating left and right as if they were positions on a single continuum strictly presuppose that the distinction names something real and unified about political disagreement, or is the practice compatible with the view that left and right are merely a conventional shorthand for shifting coalitions? Is it committed to some claim about the underlying structure of values—for instance, that the surface diversity of political views is organised by one dominant opposition, such as equality against hierarchy—or is it equally compatible with a pluralism of irreducibly separate values that merely happen to correlate for contingent historical reasons? And what would the practice have to change if we accepted, consistently, that political ideology has several irreducible dimensions and that any left–right placement is a lossy projection? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and state clearly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
