I'm presenting a paper next month at a joint political theory and intellectual history workshop, and I need a prep sheet for the question period rather than a fresh defense of the argument. The thesis is that Adam Smith's treatment of colonies in the Wealth of Nations is not best read as an early defense of colonial self-government or as a moral indictment of empire in the modern sense. On my reading it is a systems argument about the misallocation of capital and the perverse incentives of chartered monopoly: the colonial trade monopoly holds the rate of profit above the level Smith treats as natural, draws capital away from agriculture and the home trade, and leaves Britain carrying the costs of defending and administering an empire whose benefits are captured by a narrow set of merchants. Smith's remedies settle accordingly—opening the colonial trade, making the colonies contribute to imperial revenue, or a negotiated separation—and he is noticeably uninterested in consent or self-rule as sources of legitimacy for the colonies themselves. My claim is that calling this anti-imperialist flatters our categories and imports normative machinery Smith does not actually deploy; at the same time I want to resist the postcolonial reading in which his reform proposals are just cynicism, because on his own account the reforms would materially change how the empire distributes its burdens and gains.

The room makes this hard. Two historians of political thought read Smith through the Theory of Moral Sentiments and the impartial spectator; they will want to know whether I have suppressed an explicitly moral vocabulary of injustice, oppression, and the resentment of the governed in order to turn Smith into a proto-economist. A historian of economics will press the mechanism itself: whether Smith's claim about the colonial monopoly raising the rate of profit does the work I need it to do, or whether his argument depends on assumptions about capital mobility and returns that a careful reconstruction cannot sustain. A postcolonial scholar will object that treating empire as a distortion of an otherwise natural commercial order idealizes the very commercial society that conquers and dispossesses, and that my reading lets Smith off too easily. The chair is on record as suspicious of any narrative that turns an eighteenth-century Scotsman into a harmless critic of empire.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as those particular people would phrase them, each tagged with its likely source and how damaging it is. For each, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and how to reply in the minute or two available. Then identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope the audience misses, and the objection I should most hope nobody formulates. Be definite about this: rank the objections you think are likeliest to lose me the room, and if the right advice is that the thesis needs one surgical narrowing before the talk, say so and say what it is.
