I am teaching a seminar on forecasting and the planning fallacy, and we keep returning to the distinction between the inside view and the outside view — roughly, between reasoning about a case from its distinctive features and reasoning from the distribution of outcomes across a class of apparently similar cases. As an exercise, I have been compiling the best arguments for the claim that the inside view retains some legitimate role even when we grant, for the sake of argument, that the outside view is usually better calibrated. So far I have four. First, the reference-class problem: the outside view yields a number only after one selects a comparison class, and in contested cases there are several defensible classes that imply very different base rates, so case-specific judgment is smuggled back in at the stage of choosing the class. Second, private information: the outside view deliberately sets aside facts that are unique to the present case, but a unique fact is sometimes the single most diagnostic thing one knows. Third, novelty: for genuinely new undertakings there is no relevant track record, and any reference class is a forced analogy to cases with a different structure, so the resulting base rate is a kind of pseudo-precision. Fourth, selection effects: the naive base rate lumps together all attempts in a class, including the unserious and the foolish, whereas the relevant probability should be conditioned on the case's having survived whatever screening brought it before us — and that conditioning depends on facts about the screening process itself.

I am looking for a fifth reason that is genuinely different from these four, not a restatement of one of them, and not a special case of the reference-class problem or the selection-effect point. If you have a candidate, state it precisely, say how it differs from each of the four, and indicate whether it can withstand the usual outside-view reply that base rates already encode whatever the inside view claims to add.
