I'm interning at a small media-law firm this summer, and my boss has put me on a project for a client who's building an app where users give local plumbers, electricians, and handymen a single 1-to-5 "quality score." No written reviews, no comments, no individual reports—just the aggregate number next to the business's name, computed from customer reports the app doesn't show or verify. The question I've been asked to think through is defamation risk: if a business ends up with a low score and sues, would the score count as a protected opinion or as an assertion of fact? My boss wants me to predict how a particular state-court judge, whose defamation decisions she follows closely, would come out.

Here's the judge's track record as I've digested it. She dismissed a claim over a restaurant review saying the lobster was "rubbery" and the place "overpriced," calling it subjective taste with nothing to prove or disprove. She let a claim proceed over a customer's post saying "I watched the chef plate frozen fish from a grocery-store box while the menu said fresh-caught," because it asserted an objectively verifiable event. She dismissed a securities analyst's "underperform" rating and 12-month price target as a forward-looking judgment that reasonable investors read as opinion, especially with the standard hedge language. She dismissed a "worst professor I've ever had" comment as loose evaluative hyperbole. And she dismissed a claim over a consumer-watchdog group's "D" safety rating for a contractor, saying it was a predictive, multi-factor judgment under a published methodology, not a claim about any specific event.

I can see at least two principles that fit all those decisions, and they point different directions for our bare number. So, acting as an expert on defamation doctrine and the philosophy of language, start by brainstorming the candidate rules the judge could be applying, say what each predicts about our 1-to-5 aggregate score, identify which earlier case is the closest analog, and then give a best prediction of the judge's verdict. I'm also interested in what additional fact you'd add to the app's design or the case—a disclaimer, showing the distribution of ratings, a note that reports are unverified, whatever—that would most cleanly settle which rule the judge is actually running.
