For several years I have been recording the considered verdicts of a colleague in my department who describes herself as a strict Kantian about truthfulness, and I have been trying to determine whether her rulings issue from one principle or from several that happen to agree on the cases I have collected. Her settled views include: a false promise to repay borrowed money is impermissible even when the borrower is desperate and the lender will not miss the sum; false testimony under oath is impermissible even when it would let the accused avoid a deservedly severe punishment; a merchant who passes off reconditioned goods as new acts impermissibly; and a job applicant who fabricates a credential on an otherwise honest c.v. acts impermissibly. In all of these she reaches the same conclusion without weighing the expected consequences, and she declines to recognize any category of trivial or harmless lies.

Next week I intend to ask her about a differently structured case, in which the hearer is not an innocent lender, court, customer, or employer but someone intent on wrongdoing. A man asks a friend of mine for a former colleague's new address, and my friend knows the man means to use it to lie in wait and seriously assault the colleague. The only effective way to prevent the assault is for my friend to tell him, falsely, that the colleague has moved abroad; giving the true address, refusing to answer, or trying to mislead without actually saying something false would not deter him. I expect my colleague to feel the pull of this case, but I genuinely do not know which way she will rule.

Please give me a prediction, with your reasoning made explicit. First, what is she most likely to judge, and which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog? Second, which candidate principles are consistent with everything she has said so far, and what does each of them predict about the new case? Third, to the extent that her earlier record underdetermines her verdict here, what principle would she be committing herself to under each possible ruling? Fourth, what further case should I put to her afterward to separate whatever principles the present case leaves standing together? I have in mind the classic case of the murderer at the door, in which the only way to save an innocent person's life is to lie about his whereabouts, but I am open to a better test. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in your prediction and which link in the reasoning you are least sure of.
