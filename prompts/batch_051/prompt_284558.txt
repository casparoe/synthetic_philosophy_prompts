I am giving a colloquium talk to a secular, analytically trained philosophy department whose consensus view is that the currency of moral judgment is harm, consent, fairness, and autonomy, and that judgments driven by disgust or by ideas of sacredness are either pre-theoretical noise or post hoc rationalizations of taboo and prejudice. I will defend the opposite: a limited class of purity and sanctity judgments — judgments about desecration, degradation, and the wrong of treating something that ought not be used as if it were merely a resource — tracks a genuine normative feature that is not reducible to harm, consent, or convention, even while I grant that disgust is unreliable as a moral test and has a brutal track record when aimed at out-groups. My argument turns on cases in which the sense of a distinct wrong survives after every specifiable harm, rights violation, and convention-based explanation has been subtracted. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as this audience would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on where each reply is soft; include the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
