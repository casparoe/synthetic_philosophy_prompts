You are an expert on formal welfare economics and the philosophical foundations of utility, with a good handle on the history from Jevons through Robbins to Sen and Harsanyi. I’m a PhD student in philosophy of economics, and I’ve got stuck on a debate that seems to have been frozen for decades, at least in how economists talk about it. Work through it carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the possible sources of disagreement before you commit to a diagnosis.

The debate is interpersonal utility comparisons. Jevons in The Theory of Political Economy says something to the effect that every mind is inscrutable to every other, and that there is no common denominator of feeling, so you cannot compare the amounts of utility two different people get from a thing. Robbins later made this the official line: comparing one person’s satisfaction with another’s is not a scientific statement. But a lot of welfare economics and moral philosophy keeps quietly doing it or presupposing it—Harsanyi’s impartial observer needs it, as do most uses of social welfare functions, and Sen’s whole project of comparing capabilities or functionings seems to require saying something about who is worse off. Nobody has refuted the ordinalist point, and yet the discipline hasn’t stopped using interpersonal comparisions either.

My question is not who is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is. Is it a substantive empirical question about whether such comparisons can be made? Is it a semantic point about ‘utility’ meaning preference-satisfaction versus something broader? Or is it a normative disagreement about whether welfare economics should be a predictive science or a guide to policy? If the two sides are answering different questions, what would count as progress—a formal result, a distinction, or a change of question? My hunch is that the debate got stuck because ‘utility’ is doing two jobs at once, but I’m not sure.
