I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about why large language models so often state falsehoods with complete confidence, and I recently read a popular article that applies a famous philosophical essay by Harry Frankfurt to this phenomenon. The article summarizes Frankfurt roughly as follows. Frankfurt distinguishes bullshit from lying: a liar, on his account, knows or at least believes that what he is saying is false and says it anyway, which means the liar has to keep track of the truth in order to evade it. A bullshitter, by contrast, does not care whether what he says is true or false; his attention is directed at the impression his words create and whether they serve his purposes. For Frankfurt, the article continues, this makes bullshit more dangerous than lying, because the liar still acknowledges that truth matters while the bullshitter ignores the question of truth altogether. The author then applies this to chatbots. When a language model confidently asserts a falsehood, the author says, it is not lying, since it does not know that the claim is false and is not trying to conceal the truth. But it is bullshitting in Frankfurt's exact sense, because the system is trained to produce fluent, plausible, useful-sounding text rather than to track whether its claims are true. The article concludes that describing these outputs as errors is therefore a mistake: they are bullshit, and we should treat machine-generated text the way Frankfurt says we should treat human bullshit.

I found most of this persuasive, especially the point that the chatbot is not lying. But the step from not lying to bullshitting strikes me as too quick, because the machine seems to have no purposes, no sense of an audience, and no concern one way or the other in the way Frankfurt's examples require. Since I have no philosophical training, though, I do not trust my instinct here. I would like to know which parts of the article's summary a careful reader of Frankfurt would accept, which parts are overstated or subtly unfair to the original essay, and whether the article's final conclusion, that LLM hallucination is bullshit rather than error, follows from the accurate parts of the summary. If it does not follow, I would also like to know what a fairer description would be of what is happening when a language model asserts a falsehood.
