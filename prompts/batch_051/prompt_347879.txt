My friend and I have been going back and forth about infinite ethics, and I keep noticing a move I don't have a name for. Bostrom's Infinite Ethics paper gets you to the problem: if the world contains an infinite quantity of good and bad, then in cardinal arithmetic adding or subtracting a finite amount doesn't change the infinite total, so a classical aggregative view seems to say it is ethically indifferent whether we prevent a holocaust or cause one. I'm not stuck on that. What I want to identify is the recurring attempt to get out of it by switching from global totals to local comparisons. For example, someone will say that even if you can't change the total, preventing a particular suffering still makes the world better because you can hold the rest of the world fixed and compare the local region where the suffering would have occurred with the same region after the intervention; the region is better, and no other region is worse, so the act is an improvement. Then the opponent says this relies on treating some arbitrary bounded region or ordering of regions as morally privileged, and that in an infinite world the same trick can be run in reverse to make the act look neutral or worse. Is there an established name for this exchange, or specifically for the local-difference move? It's near ideas like finitism or expansionism in the literature, but I don't think it's identical; I'm after what exactly distinguishes it, whether it's a genuine alternative to aggregative ethics or a disguised finite assumption, and I'd like a definite verdict on whether this move actually escapes the paralysis or just relocates it.
