I'm a philosophy PhD student who got roped into writing the final for an upper-level undergrad seminar on distributive justice, and I want one essay question on the equality-of-what debate (welfare, resources, capabilities) that can't be answered by just reciting the usual summaries — you know, "Dworkin says equality of welfare subsidizes expensive tastes, Sen says equality of resources ignores disability." The question should be doable in 90 minutes, and it should force students to work through a specific crux rather than perform the positions. The crux I have in mind: are the expensive-tastes objection and the disability objection really two seperate problems, or are they two places where the same issue shows up — namely, how the metric draws the line between circumstances and choices — and does a capability metric actually solve the expensive-tastes problem or just push it into the choice of which capabilities get put on the list? One way to build the question is to ask them to evaluate a hybrid proposal, something like "equalize capabilities, but only over a fixed, publicly justified list of urgent capabilities," and say what that buys and what it gives up compared to welfare and resource metrics. If you've got a cleaner device for that crux, use it. Then give a model answer of about 800 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The marking scheme should name five or six must-hit moves, and for each one say which common mistake it's there to catch — e.g. the student who thinks capabilities just means adding a disability weight to a resource bundle, or the one who thinks the expensive-tastes objection refutes welfarism on its own rather than forcing a stand on responsibility for preferences. Everything the student needs should be built into the question and the standard positions, no outside readings or citations.
