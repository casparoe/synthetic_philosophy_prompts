Eugene Wigner famously described the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences: concepts developed for aesthetic or internal reasons — complex numbers, non-Euclidean geometry, group theory, Hilbert spaces — keep turning out to be the precise language in which physical reality is written. I have been struck by what looks like the same structure in supervised machine learning. There, a learner restricts itself to some mathematically convenient class of hypotheses — an architecture, a norm penalty, an optimization bias, a simplicity prior — and yet the hypothesis it selects generalizes to observations outside its training set. The mapping I have in mind is this: mathematical structures chosen for elegance or tractability correspond to hypothesis classes and inductive biases chosen for tractability and convenience; physical phenomena correspond to the data-generating process; and the surprising accuracy of mathematical description corresponds to out-of-sample generalization. On both sides, the world appears kind to a specific kind of compact, humanly tractable description: the description we have a principled reason to prefer is also the one that, surprisingly often, is true or predictive.

I want to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure or merely a superficial resemblance. If it is genuine, do results transfer between the two domains? For example, could the generalization literature — no-free-lunch theorems, VC bounds, Solomonoff induction, double descent — be read as sharpening or dissolving Wigner's puzzle, and does the history of mathematics in physics suggest that a given inductive bias works only because our particular world happens to occupy a friendly corner of hypothesis space rather than because of any a priori guarantee? Where exactly does the mapping come apart, and what is a consequence the parallel would predict that seems to be false?
