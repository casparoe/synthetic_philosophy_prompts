I've been reading around Wittgenstein's private language argument and one thing keeps snagging me. As I understand the argument (roughly, and without trying to quote it): a word only means something if there can be a difference between using it correctly and it merely seeming to you that you used it correctly. That difference normally comes from public rules and from other speakers checking you. For a sensation word, the worry is that if only you can know the sensation, nothing outside your memory could ever correct you, so the mark stops tracking anything and just means whatever feels right at the moment. On this way of putting it, a genuinely private language is either impossible or not really a language.

Now a boundary case. A woman has a recurring pain with three or four qualities no clinician has a public word for. She keeps a diary using invented marks for each quality. Over a couple years, she notices the marks correlate with triggers, medications, and times of day so consistently that it looks like real discrimination: the mark isn't arbitrary, and she can be wrong about it (she sometimes writes mark A, then later decides from how things develop that it was really B). Later, in a neurology study, researchers find distinct brain-activity patterns that line up with the qualities she'd been tracking; her marks still refer to the felt qualities, not to the scans. On the criteria above, is her diary a private language? Go through the criteria one at a time, say where they force a no and whether that no is right, and address this: does the brain-scan correlation make the practice public after the fact, or does it just show a practice can be privately meaningful and publicly vindicated later without being a language? I care less about the verdict than about whether the criteria are doing useful work or just drawing a line where its rhetorically convenient.
