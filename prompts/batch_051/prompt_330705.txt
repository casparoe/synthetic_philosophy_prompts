In American copyright practice, the moment a band records a song, the law treats a single act as producing two separately ownable and separately licensable things: the musical work (the composition—melody, harmony, lyrics) and the sound recording (this particular captured performance). The two can end up in different hands: a songwriter's estate can own the work while a label owns the recording; a cover band makes a new recording of the same work; a band that plays the song live from memory uses the work but no fixed recording. What unsettles me is that the same distinction is imposed even when nothing like a separable composition existed before the studio. An electronic track assembled in a DAW, with no score and no distinct prior act of composing, is still divided into an underlying work and a particular fixation of it.

I would like a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes about what a piece of music is. It seems to presuppose that a song is a repeatable, abstract thing—a type—and that recordings and performances are concrete instances or tokens of it. But the law never says this, and I cannot tell whether the institution strictly needs that picture or merely sits comfortably with it. Could the whole system be run by someone who denies that musical works exist as abstract objects and holds that only concrete sound events are real? What would actually have to change—in contracts, licensing, or the flow of royalties—if the underlying ontology were replaced with a nominalism about musical works, or with the view that the recording itself is the work rather than an instance of one? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and mark the places where it is philosophically underdetermined.
