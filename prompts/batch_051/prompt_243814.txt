I retired in 2021 after forty years as an experimental biologist, and I have lately been working through political philosophy with the habits I learned at the bench: before I accept an explanation, I want to know what observation would distinguish it from its rivals. The freedom literature has left me with a three-way distinction—negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-mastery or the capacity for self-government, and republican liberty as non-domination, where a person is unfree in virtue of living subject to another's arbitrary will. A friend of mine, a retired mathematician who has read this literature far longer than I have, gives confident verdicts about whether a person in a described situation is unfree, but she will not state her rule. I have been recording her judgments as a small dataset in order to reconstruct it.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A traveller is locked in her hotel room by a stranger and cannot leave until morning. — Unfree.

2. A subject of an absolute monarchy has never been interfered with by the present monarch, but the monarch may lawfully imprison, exile, or confiscate the subject's property at will; the subject has no vote, no right of appeal, and no legal exit. — Unfree.

3. A citizen of a constitutional democracy is subject to an income tax she campaigned against and still opposes; the tax was enacted through regular procedures, is enforced by an impartial authority, and she regards those procedures as legitimate. — Not unfree.

4. A member of a cooperative is denied a benefit because the board applies a rule she voted against; the rule was adopted by a vote in which she participated, and an appeal procedure she considers fair heard her objection. — Not unfree.

5. A migrant worker's visa is tied to her employer, who holds the legal power to cancel the sponsorship at will and thereby force her to leave the country; the employer has never threatened to use it and treats her courteously. — Unfree.

6. A worker objects to a schedule change imposed under a collective agreement she voted against; the agreement is administered by an elected works council, and her objection was heard and rejected through a grievance procedure she regards as fair. — Not unfree.

The new case I want to reason about before I ask her: A domestic worker has a part-time arrangement under which her employer may lawfully dismiss her at once for any reason or none and may unilaterally alter her duties. The employer has never exercised either power. The worker controls her daily schedule in practice, has substantial savings, could find comparable work within a few weeks, and chooses each month to continue because the work suits her.

I would like you to do four things. Identify the candidate rules that could account for her six judgments, and say which of them the record eliminates. State what each surviving rule predicts about the domestic worker. Then say which rule you think best explains the whole record, and offer your best guess as to whether she will call the domestic worker unfree. Finally, propose one further case, different from all of the above, whose verdict would most cleanly settle which rule she is running if the domestic-worker case leaves room for doubt.
