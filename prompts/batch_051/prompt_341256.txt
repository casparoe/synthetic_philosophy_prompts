I'm putting together a short unit on knowledge and practical stakes for an epistemology reading group, and I keep going back and forth on a version of the usual bank-type case that won't sit still. Basic setup: Ava has checked an investment company's website and has solid but not certain evidence that a particular index fund is safe. In the low-stakes version, she's mentioning it to a friend over lunch, with nothing riding on being right. In the high-stakes version, she's about to roll her whole retirement account into the fund, and being wrong would be ruinous. Same evidence, same proposition. Nearly everyone I've asked says it's fine for Ava to say "I know the fund is safe" in the low-stakes case, but not in the high-stakes one. So far nothing new.

Here's what's bugging me. First add Ben, who overhears both conversations from the next table and has no stake in anything. Is it coherent for Ben to say, of one and the same Ava and one and the same proposition, that she knows it (in the low-stakes conversation) and that she doesn't know it (in the high-stakes conversation)? If yes, that pushes me toward the idea that "knows" itself shifts semantically with context. If no, then maybe stakes are changing Ava's actual epistemic position rather than the word's content — but then Ben looks mistaken in one of the two, and its not obvious how to pick. Neither option lets me keep everything. Then add Cara, Ava's financial advisor, who is only present for the low-stakes lunch conversation. Cara's professional reputation is on the line if she signs off sloppily, though Ava herself has nothing at stake. Several people I've asked now report the high-stakes intuition leaking into the low-stakes scene — they get uneasy about "Ava knows" because of Cara's interests. I can't tell whether this is a point for a context/attributor view, a subject-stakes view, or just noise.

What I want: act as a professional epistemologist who works on contextualism and pragmatic encroachment. For each of the five accounts below, first take a minute to think through how it would handle the Ben and Cara cases specifically — I think that's where they genuinely come apart. Then rate each account from 0 to 10 on four dimensions: (1) handling the basic Ava low/high pattern, (2) handling Ben, (3) handling Cara, and (4) overall semantic plausibility and theoretical economy taken together. I don't care about bibliographies or who said what; I just want the view's internal engine. Then rank all five from most to least attractive overall, say in a sentence or two per account what did the most to put it where it landed, and give me a rough sense of how you weighed the four dimensions. Don't just summarize the views — I want a ranking you'd actually defend.

A. Epistemic contextualism — the content or semantic value of "knows" differs between low- and high-stakes contexts.
B. Subject-sensitive invariantism / pragmatic encroachment — "knows" means one thing, but whether Ava knows can depend on what's at stake for Ava herself.
C. Strict invariantism plus an error theory — "knows" has a single semantic value; the high-stakes denial is a folk mistake driven by something like warranted assertability rather than truth.
D. Assessor relativism — knowledge attributions get their truth values only relative to an assessor's standards, so Ben's two assessments can both come out true.
E. Ambiguity — "knows" is lexically ambiguous, and the stakes cases are really cases of picking out different senses of the word.
