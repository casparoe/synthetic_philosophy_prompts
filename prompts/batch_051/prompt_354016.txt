Each year I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and each year the same sentence appears as a settling move in the session on prior choice: in the limit, the priors wash out. This year it did real work. Two students were reconstructing a dispute between two research groups that fit the same model to the same dataset but, because they started from different priors, announced substantively different posterior conclusions. Another student ended the discussion by saying the difference does not matter because, with enough data, the priors wash out. I want my students to see exactly what is compressed into that sentence before any of them repeats it again.

Please disambiguate the sentence for me. What distinct precise claims could "the priors wash out" be used to state? Lay them out as a menu, from the nearly trivial to the strong and general, with a verdict on each: platitude, true but only under stated conditions, or false as stated. I want at least these readings separated: that a prior's influence on the posterior is small in some particular finite dataset; that as the sample grows the posterior concentrates on the true parameter value; that two agents with different priors will come to assign increasingly similar probabilities to future observations; and that their full posterior distributions over the parameter converge to one another as the data accumulates. For each reading, say what conditions it needs and what happens when they fail—in particular conditions about the model being correctly specified, about the prior assigning positive mass to the right neighborhoods of the true value, about the two agents' priors overlapping rather than living on disjoint parts of the parameter space, about the observations being sufficiently regular or non-stationary, and about the difference between eventual convergence and convergence at a rate that matters for finite samples. Then tell me which of these readings, if any, actually supports the student's claim about the two research groups, and which readings are false or unproven as stated. I want the students to leave that session able to say exactly how much work a five-word slogan can be made to do.
