I'm a linguistics PhD student, partly TA-ing an intro sociolinguistics course, and I've wound up thinking about how prescriptive grammar handles singular they. The other day my supervisor pulled out an internal style memo our department's style commitee drafted in the late 1990s and never revised. The relevant passage says:

"The singular they is permissible when the antecedent is indefinite, as in everyone returned to their seat. When the antecedent is a definite, named individual, the pronoun must be he or she; otherwise the sentence is ungrammatical."

Now suppose a student submits a dissertation chapter that refers to a specific consultant, call them Sam, who uses they/them pronouns, and the chapter contains the sentence: "Sam said that they had recorded the interview." Under the rule as literally written, this gets corrected to "he or she," which is wrong on the facts and at least mildly hostile. The drafters obviously didn't have nonbinary people in mind, but the clause is still on the books. I'd like to know what the rule actually requires or permits in this case on the standard approaches to interpretation: its literal wording, the purpose it was meant to serve, what the 1990s committee would have said if asked now, and what a reasonable copy editor today would take it to mean. Do those readings converge, and if they diverge, which one should govern?

But there's a deeper question I can't quite shake. If "Sam said that they had recorded the interview" is plain grammatical English by every descriptive standard, and the rule still tells us to change it, does the rule misdescribe the language, or just prescribe one register over another? Is this a case where a rule is simply outdated, or one where the very idea of a grammar rule is doing something a descriptive generalization never could? I guess I'm asking whether this single unrevised sentence in a 1990s memo tells us anything about whether there is such a thing as bad grammar, or only such a thing as choices that make people in authority wince.
