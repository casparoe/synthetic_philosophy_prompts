I spent my career as a research chemist at a large university-affiliated institute, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy, especially work on paternalism and the ethics of nudges. A pair of practices I observed in my last decade there now strikes me as philosophically puzzling, and I would like you to help me work through it.

The first practice involved our retirement plan. Every new employee was automatically enrolled at a 5 percent contribution rate, with the contribution rising by one percentage point each year unless the employee actively opted out. The plan administrators were explicit about their reasoning: most employees said they wanted to save more for retirement, but very few ever got around to enrolling, so the default was set to match the savings behavior employees themselves reported wanting rather than the behavior their inertia would otherwise produce. Participation was nearly universal, and almost no one opted out after the first few months.

The second practice involved a very different kind of choice. The same institute ran a clinical research program, and before a researcher could draw a small blood sample from a healthy volunteer, the volunteer had to read and sign a multi-page informed-consent document in the presence of a witness. The form spent much of its length on remote risks—a bruise, faintness, possible infection—and on the fact that the sample might be stored for future unspecified research. Nothing could proceed unless the volunteer personally signed, and the volunteer remained free to withdraw at any time.

My question is what theory of the human agent these two practices jointly presuppose. In the retirement case, the institution seems to assume that what a person actively does, or fails to do, is not a reliable guide to what they really want, and that it is acceptable to arrange the choice architecture so that the easy path tracks the person's own stated goals. In the blood-draw case, the institution seems to assume that only a person's explicit, informed, freely given consent can legitimize even a small physical intervention, and that no third party may substitute a judgment about what would be good for them. The oddness is that the stakes run in the opposite direction from what a simple importance test would predict: retirement saving can shape an entire old age, while a single blood draw is small and usually harmless, yet it is in the small-stakes case that the institution insists on explicit consent. Are these two assumptions reconcilable within a single theory of autonomy, welfare, and competent choice, or does the institution's practice quietly contain two incompatible pictures of the agent? Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories as precisely as you can; distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit with; identify where the difference between the cases is principled and where it is arbitrary; and say what would have to change in one or both practices if the institute set out to make its presuppositions consistent. Please write your answer in American English.
