I run model-risk review at a mid-size lender. Every quarter we validate maybe a dozen models that spit out a number between 0 and 1 — probability of default, probability a loan application is fraudulent, probability the applicant will pass ID verification. The business teams are already multiplying those numbers by dollar amounts, feeding them into Bayesian pipelines, and telling our auditors they're "probabilities." Most of them arent. I'd like a reusable procedure my reviewers can run on any model to decide whether its output is allowed to be treated as a probability, as opposed to a score you can only rank. Reviewers are smart but they don't know measure theory or philosophical logic, and each one has maybe four hours per model per quarter; they can query the model on a held-out set but often can't read the source code. I sign off, but our head of credit and the compliance lead push back, so the checklist has to be defensible in plain language.

I keep coming back to Cox's theorem as the actual test. My rough understanding: if you want your plausibility numbers to behave like common sense — more plausible things get higher numbers, equivalent bodies of evidence get equivalent numbers, all the relevant evidence gets used, and you can't get two different answers by doing the same reasoning two different ways — then the numbers have to obey the ordinary probability calculus. So being a probability isn't a label a vendor puts in a report; it's a discipline the numbers either satisfy or don't.

Turn that into a checklist. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "score only, do not multiply," and where it hands off to a human because the question is a judgment call rather than a computation. Phrase the tests operationally — for instance, how you'd check path-consistency on a held-out dataset without knowing model internals.

Then run the procedure on three of our models:
1. A logistic regression fit on a complete, unbiased sample and used on the same population.
2. A fraud model whose output is a hand-wired weighted sum of expert rules, scaled to fall between 0 and 1.
3. A "consensus" model that averages two sub-models, where one was retrained recently and the other wasnt; the two sub-models sometimes disagree by more than 0.3. This is the one we've been telling the regulator produces probabilities for two years.

For each, say which box it lands in and what the cheapest fix is, if any. And tell me honestly which cases the checklist still can't settle and where a person has to decide.
