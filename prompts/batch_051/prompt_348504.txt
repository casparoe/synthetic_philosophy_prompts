I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, and I keep returning to a debate that, from inside the laboratory, always seemed faintly disreputable and yet obviously important: whether the beauty or elegance of a theory is any sort of evidence that the theory is true. I now see that the affirmative side is not mere physicist romanticism. It ranges from the historical observation that the most formally economical and symmetric theories have repeatedly turned out to be the successful ones, through the rationalist thought that nature herself is parsimonious, to the methodological claim that where data are absent, elegance is a legitimate and perhaps the only guide. The negative side treats elegance as a psychological lure: a projection of aesthetic taste, a standing invitation to confirmation bias, and the reason a sophisticated field can spend decades elaborating beautiful formal structures under no empirical discipline.

I would like to see this debate restated, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary I actually think in: the vocabulary of statistical inference and model selection. I mean likelihoods, priors, posteriors, marginal likelihood, explicit and implicit complexity penalties of the AIC/BIC/minimum-description-length sort, regularization, overfitting, the bias–variance trade-off, cross-validation, identifiability, sensitivity to prior choice, effective number of parameters, and the like. I am fluent in that vocabulary and do not need it explained from scratch. Then give me the residue report. Go through the main claims on each side and tell me which ones translate cleanly into claims about model selection, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. I especially want to know what becomes of three things: the distinction between simplicity and beauty proper (symmetry, economy, the feeling of surprise); the claim that elegance can guide theory choice in the absence of data rather than merely after data arrive; and the suspicion that elegance is aesthetic projection rather than an inferential property. Please also say whether the residues mean the statistical vocabulary is missing something the philosophers have rightly noticed, or instead that some of the philosophical claims are less precise than they look once a translation is attempted.

Finally, I would appreciate an explicit account of what would change your mind about the residue assessment: which further argument, distinction, or example would lead you to reclassify a distorted or untranslatable claim as cleanly translatable, or the reverse, and where you think your mapping is most likely to fail. Please write in American English.
