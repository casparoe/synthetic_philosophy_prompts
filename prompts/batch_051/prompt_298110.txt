Two political philosophers who have disagreed for years are preparing a joint document. One holds a position close to Rawls's difference principle: social and economic inequalities are justified only when they work to make the least advantaged members of society, identified by their holding of an index of primary social goods, as well off as they could be under any alternative arrangement. The other holds a prioritarian view: the moral value of a benefit depends on the absolute level of well-being of its recipient, so that arrangements are assessed by maximizing a weighted sum of well-being with weights decreasing as recipients become better off, and inequality as such carries no independent value. They have repeatedly been told in discussion that they are really saying the same thing, since both give special concern to the worse off and both reject average utilitarianism; each insists, however, that the residual disagreement is substantive and not merely verbal.

They decide to run a good-faith adversarial collaboration and to produce a single document both can sign. Write that document. It should open with a numbered section of agreed claims, phrased so that neither side winces: claims about what average utilitarianism gets wrong, about whether leveling down is an improvement, about the moral importance of the least advantaged, and about the limits of a purely comparative or purely absolute reading of advantage. It should then contain a disagreements table. Each row should isolate one precise sub-question on which they still part ways — at minimum the currency of evaluation (primary goods or some index of advantage versus individual well-being), the structure of the principle (a maximin-style or lexical rule defined over social positions versus continuous weighted aggregation), and whether relative comparisons matter only instrumentally. For each row: give each side's position in wording that side would accept; identify the crux such that settling it would move at least one of them; and specify a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on that crux. Suitable cases include a reform that slightly improves the worst-off position while greatly improving many of the merely well-off, a risky policy that could leave the worst-off slightly worse off in exchange for large expected benefits to others, and a comparison between two societies with the same average well-being but different spreads. For each case, record in advance what each side predicts the correct verdict is, and what each predicts the other side will say.

Finish by stating which parts of their disagreement, if any, the document itself identifies as verbal rather than substantive, and which single residual question each side would most want settled. The document should read as something both could put their names to, not as a compromise that flattens their positions.
