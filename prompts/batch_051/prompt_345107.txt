I have this rule: if someone gives me a probability, I only take it seriously if they can explain the reasoning as a coherent degree of plausibility in the Cox sense—like, obeying the sum and product rules at least in principle. Last week a doctor said there's a 30% chance a treatment will work, and when I asked what would make her say 31% instead, she couldn't answer. I dismissed the number entirely. My friend says I'm using a theorem about ideal logic to reject everyday probabilistic reasoning that works fine in practice, and that a vague 30% from a doctor is better than nothing. But I thought Cox's theorem shows that any consistent way of reasoning under uncertainty has to be isomorphic to probability, so if the number isn't part of such a system, it's not just imprecise—it's not even a probability. Is my rule defensible, or am I overextending the theorem? How should I distinguish between "imprecise but informative" and "not even wrong" when someone gives me a number?
