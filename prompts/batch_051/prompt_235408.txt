I'm in a decision theory class and we just did money pumps, and a friend and I have been arguing about whether the standard argument actually works or is just a rhetorical trick. I want to lay out the version I keep reconstructing, as numbered steps, because I half-suspect it's too strong but I can't say where it breaks.

Suppose someone has cyclic preferences: tea over coffee, coffee over beer, and beer over tea. (1) If a person prefers X to Y, then they'd be willing to pay some small amount—say a nickel—to switch from Y to X. (2) So if this person starts out holding coffee, they'd pay a nickel to switch to tea, since they prefer tea to coffee. (3) Once they have the tea, they'd pay a nickel to switch to beer, since they prefer beer to tea. (4) Once they have the beer, they'd pay a nickel to switch back to coffee, since they prefer coffee to beer. (5) They end up holding coffee again but 15 cents poorer than when they started, even though they could have just kept the coffee. (6) A rational person cannot be led by their own step-by-step choices into a final state that is strictly worse than where they started. (7) Therefore, cyclic preferences are irrational.

I keep thinking step (6) is doing something suspicious, but my friend thinks the problem is earlier, maybe in step (1), because "preferring X to Y" sounds like it's about a straight choice between X and Y, not about paying to get out of Y. Which step, if any, fails? And if the problematic step can be restated in a way that's true, does the argument still get you to (7)? Please write the answer in American English.
