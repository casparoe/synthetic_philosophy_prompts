I have been trying to settle where I stand on the moral difference between lying and misleading, and I have ended up with a theoretical conviction and an intuition that seem to me incompatible. The conviction is that what matters about deception is what the other person ends up believing, or is led to believe without good reason, rather than the particular route by which I caused that belief. Suppose a friend asks me whether Jack came to the party last night. If I know he came and I want the friend to believe he stayed away, I could answer falsely, "No, he did not." Or I could answer truthfully, "He told me earlier that he would be out of town all evening," knowing that my friend will infer that Jack never arrived. On the conviction I am describing, these two answers are morally on a par: the same false belief is deliberately produced in the same person, and the difference between stating a falsehood and exploiting a true statement's natural implication is only a difference in the mechanism of deception, not in what is wrong with it.

But I cannot shake the intuition that the plain lie is worse. It seems to me that when I say something false I make the falsehood part of what I present as true, whereas when I merely mislead I allow the other person to be, in a sense, the author of their own false conclusion, even though I have arranged the materials from which they draw it. I also suspect that the intuition may be tracking a real distinction between the norms that govern assertion and the norms that govern the management of other people's beliefs. A rule of truthfulness, one might think, governs only what I assert and not what I imply, so the truthful misleading answer complies with it, while an ethic of good faith that governs the inducing of beliefs would condemn both equally. Perhaps the intuition is just an artefact of our practice of singling out assertions for blame, and I should discount it. Or perhaps the theory is too coarse: maybe being lied to wrongs a person in a way that being cleverly misled does not, because part of what I owe others is to make my communicative acts themselves, and not only their effects, reliable enough to act upon.

I want to know what it would take for each of these resolutions to be the right one. What would justify giving up the intuition as an artefact of how we apportion blame? What would justify keeping the intuition and amending or abandoning the outcome-based conviction? And is there a plausible reading on which both can survive, for example by locating the moral difference in something other than the wrongness of the act itself, such as the kind of trust that is broken, the degree of blame one deserves, or the secondary duties one takes on? Give me the strongest version of each move, and tell me which resolution you would accept. Do not simply report that one side is right; I want the costs of each move made explicit.
