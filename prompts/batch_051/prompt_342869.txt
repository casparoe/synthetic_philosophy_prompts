I've come around to a view I've been calling moral trade, and I want to know what would actually count against it. The view is that some normally impermissible acts — breaking a promise, running a small deception, bending an institutional rule that exists for good reasons, offloading a modest risk onto a few people — can be morally offset the way carbon gets offset. If you deliberately secure a compensating moral gain elsewhere, one you wouldn't otherwise have produced, the original wrong is cancelled, or at least the act becomes permissible on balance. It's not straightforward consequentialism; the wrongness of the act still counts as a real line you crossed, but you can pay it down by buying an offset somewhere else. I've never been able to extend it to serious violence, so leave that out.

Help me map what would have to be true for this to be false — not just the obvious line about treating persons as fungible. Which cases can't the view describe, and which intuitions does it live or die on? What is it committed to that turns out unacceptable, or that a plain consequentialist and a plain deontologist wouldn't also say? Where does it collapse into one of those rivals? What do debates about aggregation, distributive justice, and dirty hands imply for it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which ones a committed moral-trade theorist should concede as counting against them and which ones they can dismiss as attacks on a straw version. Then say whether the view, as I've qualified it, is so insulated that nothing could touch it — and whether that's a problem or just a sign it's a framework rather then a theory. I'd personally be most moved by a clean case where the offset is fully delivered and the beneficiaries are real, but the person I broke the promise to still has a legitimate complaint. Give me a committed verdict, not an evenhanded tour, and write in American English.
