In my school's philosophy club we have been arguing about how to weigh intuitions for general principles against intuitions for particular cases, and I keep going back and forth on a methodological question. Suppose a principle is very simple, and it lets us organize a large number of otherwise puzzling comparative judgments. Then a single vivid case seems to conflict with it. I am tempted to say that the principle's simplicity and its ability to unify the other cases are themselves evidence that the conflicting case-intuition is unreliable, and that the correct move is to keep the principle and explain away the case. In our discussion, the concrete example was transitivity for "better than." Someone proposed three outcomes where A is only slightly better than B, B only slightly better than C, and C only slightly better than A. Several of us thought that, precisely because transitivity is so simple and useful, the right conclusion is not that betterness can cycle, but that the person describing the case is using their terms loosely, leaving out a relevant respect, or being misled by the way the alternatives are presented.
