I'm retired now—spent most of my career as a field ecologist, mostly working on how populations respond to environmental change—and I've fallen into philosphy late, which is probably why my first essay idea sounds like ecology. I'm trying to write a short piece about whether universities should keep hosting "useless" fields, and I'd like serious comments on the argument before I expand it.

My rough thesis is that most defenses of apparently useless scholarship either claim everything has hidden utility or fall back on knowledge being intrinsically valuable. I want to try a third line: universities' research portfolios are under strong selection pressure from funding, hiring, and publishability, all of which favor problems that pay off quickly and predictably. In ecology, selection for current performance tends to eat away at the standing variation that lets a population cope with a changed environment; that's part of why seed banks and bet-hedging exist. So my proposal is to treat odd, low-yield fields—say, the taxonomy of an obscure invertebrate group, or the study of a dead language—as a kind of epistemic seed bank. Their value isn't mainly the particular facts they produce, and it isn't the promise that each one will someday become useful. It's that they preserve heterogeneous ways of setting up problems, and a system that keeps a fraction of its resources in exploratory, low-yield niches is more robust when the intellectual environment shifts. The analogy I'm leaning on hardest is the conservation-biology argument that we should preserve wild crop relatives and biological diversity for option value, even when no currently useful trait is visible.

What I'd like is serious, point-by-point comments on this idea as it stands. In particular: does the ecological analogy do actual work, or is it just decoration? Am I smuggling together "useless," "unpopular," and "not currently fundable"? Is there a way to distinguish a seed bank from a stagnant field without presupposing the conclusion? Does the argument over-generate, by also defending fields that are sloppy or obscurantist? I realize i may be mangling "option value," which economists use more precisely—push back if I'm misusing it. And if the whole framing rests on a confused premise, or this sort of essay can't be done well in this form, I'd rather hear that plainly than get polite suggestions for polish.
