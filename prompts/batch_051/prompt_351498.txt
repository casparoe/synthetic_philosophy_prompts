I'm in a third-year course on the philosophy of wisdom, and we spent a long time on Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics Book VI, especially the part where he says practical wisdom is about grasping particulars, not just universal rules, and that older or experienced people often see correctly in particular cases because experience has given them an eye for them. Our professor keeps making the point that this kind of wisdom is supposed to be gained through living, not through a syllabus. But then a friend told me about a proposal in our philosophy department, and I'm honestly not sure if it's brilliant or a complete disaster.

The proposal is that the standard intro ethics final paper would be replaced by a "practical wisdom practicum." Students would keep a private weekly decision journal about some non-trivial real-life choice they actually faced that week (whether to confront a friend, transfer programs, report cheating, end a relationship, etc.). At the end of term, each student has a 45-minute interview with a panel of two faculty members and one trained "community elder," like a retired social worker or judge. The panel gives a qualitative grade of "wise" or "not yet wise," based on whether the student shows what the department calls "integrative judgment": naming the relevant moral features, seeing the situation from multiple perspectives, acknowledging uncertainty, and taking responsibility for what they could have done. The journal is confidential; only the student and panel see it. There is no rubric beyond a nine-point list of "signs of practical wisdom" written by the department. The department says this is a real assessment of wisdom, not just ethical knowledge.

I'm a bit skeptical but also kinda curious what would actually happen. Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. You are an expert on virtue ethics and institutional design. Start by brainstorming the likely first- and second-order effects, then work through the incentives this creates for students, for panel members, and for the department itself. In particular, what does it incentivize among students who are strategic, conflict-averse, or genuinely trying to become wiser? How would the panel be gamed, and what biases or power dynamics would enter? What happens when faculty become judges of a student's private life? What failure modes appear, like students inventing dilemmas, performing vulnerability, telling the panel what they want to hear, or panel members disagreeing about what counts as wise? Can the scheme be fixed without gutting it, for example by blinding, using standardized cases, rotating panelists, or pairing the journal with a more traditional exam? And does the mechanism actually teach or measure wisdom, or does it mainly sort students who already had it?
