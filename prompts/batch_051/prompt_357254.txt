I have been working out a view I call the "last-author principle" about advance directives, and I want an honest assessment of it rather than a defense. The principle begins from a distinction between scenic authority and authorial authority. Scenic authority is the kind of control we exercise over present, local experience: what we eat, whom we see, whether we want to stay in bed, what we currently enjoy or find distressing. Authorial authority is control over the shape of a whole life: which commitments count, how the life is allowed to end, what kind of larger project the person is living out. My claim is that when a competent person issues an advance directive, they are exercising authorial authority, and this authority does not lapse when dementia or another condition later strips them of the capacities that made them an author of their own life. The later self retains scenic authority over present comfort, but scenic authority does not extend to overriding the directive, because the later self is not in a position to answer for the life as a whole. To put it bluntly: a contented later self who wants to rescind a refusal of life-prolonging treatment should be listened to the way a patient's report of pain is listened to, not the way a co-author's disagreement is listened to.

Does this mark a real distinction or just a metaphor with conviction? Is the principle already worked out under another name? Give me your candid assessment: what it captures, what it falsifies or distorts, the strongest objections, and whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
