I am a philosophy professor preparing a session on the KK principle and the anti-luminosity argument for a graduate epistemology seminar that several working distributed-systems engineers are auditing. They have deep fluency in their own field — consistency models, linearizability, read-your-writes and session guarantees, monotonic reads, eventual consistency, leases, failure detectors, split-brain, local versus global state — but no formal background in epistemology. I know the epistemology well, and I know their field only well enough to suspect that an analogy in this vicinity is not entirely innocent, which is why I want the translation done carefully rather than decoratively.

Please restate the following package as precisely as you can in the vocabulary of distributed systems: (1) the luminosity thesis, that for a mental condition C, if one is in C one is in a position to know that one is in C; (2) the KK principle, that if one knows that P, one is in a position to know that one knows that P; and (3) the anti-luminosity argument, with its margin-for-error reasoning, for the conclusion that no nontrivial such condition is luminous and that KK is doubtful in much the same way. Be careful not to collapse the technical notion of being in a position to know into actually knowing, since that distinction carries much of the philosophical weight, and flag any place where the distributed-systems vocabulary tempts you to lose it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about most: which claims translate cleanly into claims about replicas, consistency guarantees, and locally detectable state; which claims you had to fudge and exactly how you fudged them; and which claims have no counterpart in the distributed-systems setting at all. In particular, the engineers have a running joke that KK is just read-your-writes consistency and that anti-luminosity is just the observation that reliable failure detection is impossible. I need your verdict on how much of that joke is insight and how much is category error. End by saying directly whether the distributed-systems vocabulary carries the epistemological weight here, or whether the residue is where the philosophy actually lives.
