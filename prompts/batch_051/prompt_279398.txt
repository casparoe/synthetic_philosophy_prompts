I've been rereading Mill for a class on law and paternalism and got stuck on a bit in Chapter V of John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* that I think gets underread. He's discussing restrictions on selling poisons and says: "the restriction of the sale of poisons; all cases, in short, where the object of the interference is to make it impossible or difficult to obtain a particular commodity. These interferences are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer." The position I've ended up with is that Mill's principle gives a stronger and less comfortable case against modern drug prohibition than the usual "prohibition causes crime" argument: it says the primary liberty wrong is done to the buyer/consumer, not the dealer, and it treats restrictions on availability as an interference with personal freedom even before we get to arrest and punishment. I want to stress-test this. What would count against it? I know the obvious responses (addiction undermines autonomy; there are externalities to families and emergency rooms; Mill himself allowed labeling regulations and restrictions on sale to children), but I want them sorted by how decisively they actually defeat the Millian anti-prohibition reading, not just registered. Also: are there modern cases—say, fentanyl, prescription-only opioids, supervised injection sites, Portugal-style decriminalization—that force the Millian to retreat or to distinguish between making something impossible to obtain and making it harder on purpose? Which of the position's components would a committed defender have to give up first? And does the view risk becoming unfalsifiable if it treats every regulation as a liberty infringement while still allowing some "police functions"? I'd like an honest map of where the position is exposed, from fatal to merely awkward, rather than a defense of it.
