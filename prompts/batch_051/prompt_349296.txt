For a small philosophy group I'm in, I've been trying to write down my whole position on hope and optimism in one place before the others take it apart, and I'd like a consistency check first. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Belief is governed by evidence: someone is epistemically criticizable whenever their confidence outruns what there evidence supports, never mind how much better it makes them feel.
(2) Optimism, understood as a standing disposition to expect things to go well, is an epistemic vice when it's maintained against the evidence — even though it can be a very good thing in practice.
(3) Hope is not belief. Hoping for an outcome doesn't mean judging it likely; it means treating the outcome as a live possibility and being emotionally and practically oriented around it. So hoping for a long-shot recovery doesn't require an irrational level of confidence.
(4) Because of that, it can be perfectly rational — maybe even admirable — for a patient with a terminal diagnosis and a small chance of a new treatment working to keep hoping, and a clinician who goes out of their way to scrub away that last bit of hope has done something wrong, even if the clinician's probability estimate is exactly right.
(5) Doxastic voluntarism is false — neither beliefs nor hopes are under direct voluntary control; you can't just decide to hope. But you can cultivate hope indirectly: by dwelling on favorable possibilities, choosing what you attend to, and spending time with certain people. That's why it can make sense to talk about a duty of hope even though you can't do it on command.
(6) At the same time, I think we shouldn't deliberately train our habits of attention in ways we know will make our beliefs drift away from our evidence; that strikes me as a kind of self-inflicted epistemic harm.

I honestly can't tell whether this set hangs together, and I'd rather find out now than in front of everyone. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are. For the real tensions, rank them by severity and say what would have to give. Then tell me what the cheapest single revision would be. I'd rather get a ruthless audit than a flattering one, and I also want to know whether the whole thing is just me trying to keep evidentialism and the consolations of hope at the same time.
