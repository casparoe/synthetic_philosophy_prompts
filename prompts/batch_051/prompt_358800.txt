For an upper-division philosophy course on social ontology or the philosophy of language (around 20 students, one 75-minute session), design an in-class game, playable with slips of paper or cards and no real money, that makes students feel the force of the Lewisian idea that a convention is an arbitrary regularity sustained by precedent and mutual expectations rather than by agreement or sanctions, and that such a regularity can harden into something people treat as normatively binding. The game should be arranged so that the class produces a norm out of sheer precedent: no one proposes it, no one directs it, no one punishes deviations, yet by the final rounds most participants comply, and they can recognize that they complied because they expected others to comply, even though a different regularity would have worked just as well. Include concrete rules and materials, a points-or-status payoff scheme that needs no real stakes, a round structure that shifts the situation so the arbitrariness becomes visible (for instance by bringing together two subgroups that developed different precedents, or by changing the options so the old convention must be reapplied), and a debrief that maps what happened onto Lewis's conditions: regularity in a population, common knowledge of the regularity, conditional preference for conforming to it, and the standing availability of an alternative regularity. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time the game is run and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of Lewis's account a classroom exercise cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply in the debrief.
