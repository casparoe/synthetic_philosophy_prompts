Been reading Spinoza's Ethics and tried to pull a single argument out of it, and I cant tell if I've cheated somewhere. The passage that got me is from the proof of Part I, Proposition 5 (old Elwes translation):

"If several distinct substances be granted, they must be distinguished one from the other, either by the difference of their attributes, or by the difference of their modifications (Prop. iv.). If only by the difference of their attributes, it will be granted that there cannot be more than one with an identical attribute. If by the difference of their modifications—as substance is naturally prior to its modifications (Prop. i.),—it follows that setting the modifications aside, and considering substance in itself, that is truly, (Deff. iii. and vi.), there cannot be conceived one substance different from another,—that is (by Prop. iv.), there cannot be granted several substances, but one substance only."

When I unpack that passage, heres the argument I end up with.

(1) A substance is what is in itself and is conceived through itself; a mode is what is in something else and is conceived through that other.
(2) Two substances cant share an attribute, because an attribute is just what the intellect grasps as the essence of a substance; two things with the very same essence would be one thing, not two.
(3) God, by definition, is the absolutely infinite substance—the substance whose essence contains every attribute that expresses reality. So any attribute a second substance could have is already God's.
(4) God exists (Spinoza argues for that separately).
(5) If there were a substance other than God, it would have to have some attribute; otherwise it wouldn't really be a substance.
(6) By (3), that attribute is already God's.
(7) So the would-be second substance shares an attribute with God, which contradicts (2).
(8) Therefore there is no substance besides God; and since everything is either a substance or a mode, everything else is a mode of God. Monism.

I keep feeling like step (2) is doing more work than the proof lets on, or that the whole argument only goes through because step (4) sneaks in another argument without earning it. So tell me: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say? If it is valid, which premise should a pluralist reject, and what's the strongest version of that premise they could defend? Then tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail—what sort of counterexample, or different reading of "attribute" or "substance," would make you switch from "valid but unsound" to "invalid," or the reverse.
