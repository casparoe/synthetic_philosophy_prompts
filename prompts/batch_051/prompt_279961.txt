I'm a backend engineer and I've been going down a rabbit hole on "low-impact AI" after a coworker sent me a blog post. The pitch is appealing to me as an engineer: instead of trying to perfectly specify what we want, give the AI an objective function thats probably misspecified, and add a penalty for changing the world too much so the mistakes stay small. But when I tried to sketch what that would look like in a toy gridworld, I got stuck. The obvious formalization is to pick a baseline — roughly, what would have happened if the agent did nothing — and then penalize divergence from that baseline. But to compute divergence you need a metric over states, and picking that metric already feels like sneaking in a value system. Is a broken vase more "different" than a vase two feet to the left? Is a room with the lights left on close enough to one with the lights off? The metric does all the philosophical work, and I don't see how to pick it without a full spec of what matters.

I want you to act as an AI alignment researcher with a strong philosophy background and compare two ideas/concepts for me. The first is "low impact" as a formal property: an agent or action is low-impact if it keeps the world close to the no-agent baseline under some chosen distance metric. The second is the ordinary notion of a side effect, which seems to be defined relative to what the agent is actually trying to do and what we care about — a side effect is something that happens that isn't part of the goal, and it's only a bad side effect if it touches something we value. Are these two concepts actually the same thing, or is the formal one just a way of laundering value judgments through a metric and a baseline? Is the value-ladenness of the metric a fatal problem for the formal approach, or is it fine as long as the designers are explicit about it? Can "avoid side effects" even be defined without a full specification of value, or does low-impact AI just relocate the alignment problem rather than solving it? Please think seperately about baselines and metrics, brainstorm one or two concrete toy examples before answering, and then give a clear verdict.
