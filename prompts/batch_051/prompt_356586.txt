I'm a novelist working on a scene set in a walk-in clinic where a practice manager has to reconstruct, months later, exactly what she knew when she countersigned a laboratory results sheet. A friend who teaches epistemology has been trying to convince me that the scene is secretly about Nozick's truth-tracking theory and its denial of the closure principle—that if you know P and you know P entails Q, you must be in a position to know Q. I find the idea dramatically useful, but I don't want the zebra-in-the-zoo or brain-in-a-vat examples; I want an original case that could actually happen in the kinds of institutions I write about, and that carries the weight of the scene.

Construct me a thought experiment of no more than a paragraph with these specifications. An ordinary agent in an ordinary setting—the clinic, say, or some similarly mundane workplace—comes to know P by a specified method. P transparently entails Q, and the agent believes Q by deducing it from P. On Nozick's account, where knowledge is relativized to the method by which the belief is produced and requires true belief plus sensitivity (if P were false, she would not believe P via that method) and its truth-side counterpart, the agent should come out knowing P but not knowing Q. The failure should be generated by the sensitivity condition, not by the agent's doubting the entailment, failing to believe Q, or making a logical slip. Specify the contents of P and Q; both should be ordinary descriptive propositions about the scene, though Q may be a denial of a mundane, low-probability mishap such as a swapped sample, a misfiled page, or an unnoticed cancellation. Do not use brains in vats, Cartesian demons, evil scientists, fake barns, lotteries, disguised animals, or any prop that could not plausibly feature in a realistic version of the setting on an ordinary Tuesday. Specify the methods and the salient nearby possible worlds precisely enough that the verdicts can be checked, and make clear why the nearest not-Q world is one in which she still holds Q, while the nearest not-P world is one in which she drops P.

After the scenario, run the tracking test for P and for Q separately, showing where the divergence appears. Then tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work, why the closure principle comes to look bad here, and how a defender of closure would most plausibly resist—by challenging the closeness ordering, the method indexing, or the way the entailment is described—along with the tracking theorist's best reply. Finally, say what happens if that one working feature is removed: does closure reassert itself, or does the case collapse back into a familiar skeptical dilemma? I want this as a working note for my own drafting, so I would rather have the machinery of the case laid bare than an elegant summary.
