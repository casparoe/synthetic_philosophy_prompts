Two friends of mine got into a dispute at a street-food market and asked me to referee it; I could not settle it to either's satisfaction. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

The choice was between two vendors selling the same dish. Stall A had a long, constantly replenished queue; stall B had almost no line. Mira wanted B: she had just received a message from a local acquaintance who lives nearby, saying B is the best version of that dish she has had in the city and that she would not queue at A. Leo wanted A. He argued that a queue is aggregated information: many customers, over the course of the evening, have voted with their feet for A, and if B were in fact better it is unlikely the crowd would systematically have missed it.

Mira replied that this treats a line as a tally of independent judgments, but a line can be an information cascade. Once a line begins, later arrivals may take the line itself as evidence of quality and join even when their own private information points the other way. In that case, the line is partly self-reinforcing, and its length overstates the amount of independent evidence it contains.

Leo granted that cascades are possible in principle, but he said they are fragile. In a real market a queue is tested repeatedly—by locals, by regulars, and by people who have eaten at both stalls. If A were resting on an arbitrary early choice, some sufficiently confident person or some shock should eventually have broken the pattern. That the line had persisted seemed to him more consistent with genuine quality than with a cascade.

Mira answered that a cascade need not collapse. Each new customer who privately doubts A may still rationally join, because the weight of all previous choices can swamp one private signal. Her message about B is exactly that kind of private signal; it could be swamped even if the line contains little independent evidence about quality. So the line's persistence does not discriminate between Leo's story and hers.

Leo replied that she was now describing rational Bayesians, and rational Bayesians only follow the herd when their own signal is weak and the public history is already sufficiently informative. Her one local acquaintance's message is a single signal; it should not overturn a long sequence of revealing choices unless she can show that the sequence stopped being revealing, which she cannot. To claim a cascade, one needs independent evidence about how the line began and whether later customers were copying rather than choosing.

Mira said Leo was facing the same problem from the other direction. She had positive evidence for B, while the queue only showed what people did, not why they did it. Since Leo cannot rule out that many people joined after seeing the line, he risks counting the same early accident many times over as if it were fresh confirmation.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each substantive move, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partially right, and explain why. Then say who is more right overall, and identify which further considerations about rational herding—beyond what either of them said—would be most relevant to choosing a stall in this situation.
