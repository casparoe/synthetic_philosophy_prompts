I spent most of last night arguing with a mate about Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two people with the same prior, who each update by conditioning on private information and whose posterior probabilities for some claim are common knowledge (each knows the other's, each knows the other knows, and so on), can't agree to disagree. My mate kept saying it's a pretty formalism that dies on contact with real arguments, because in real life you never quite know the other person's reasoning and everyone starts from different priors. My line was the theorem never said otherwise: it's telling you the disagreement has to live in one of those two places. I want to put my students inside that rather than tell them it.

I run an undergraduate PPE seminar, 16 students, one 75-minute session. Design a game or activity where they feel the force of the result themselves. They should each get private information, form a probability for some proposition, then have to announce their probabilities in successive rounds, and the rules and payoffs should be arranged so that the gap between their numbers gets squeezed in the convergence rounds before the phrase 'common knowledge' is ever used. The proposition should have a determinate answer nobody can look up — like the mix of tokens in a bag the facilitator has prepared — so all their information comes from the game. I'd also like one deliberately broken round where the theorem isn't guaranteed to bite, e.g. the two halves of the room are quietly given different priors, so they can feel what actually changes. Don't mention Aumann until the debrief.

Give me rules, materials, a points-based payoff scheme with no real money, a rough timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that seperates the common-prior assumption from the common-knowledge-of-posteriors assumption and maps each round onto one or the other. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the theorem the classroom version can't reproduce. I'd rather you commit to one concrete design than give me three options and hedge.
