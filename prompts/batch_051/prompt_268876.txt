I supervise master's-level counseling trainees at a university clinic, and I've noticed we make the same call differently week to week: when to stay with a client's self-examination and when to nudge them out of it toward action, grounding, or distraction. The folk rule I want them using, but can't quite operationalize, goes something like this: self-examination is productive when it's aimed at a specific question the client can actually answer, about something they have some control over, and when it tends to close—ending in a decision, an action, or a new take—rather than spooling into another loop. Rumination is the mirror: global self-questions ("why am I like this?"), low controllability, no natural stopping point. I also keep running into the fact that some clients use introspection itself as avoidance: they think about the problem precisely so they don't have to do the thing, so an answerable question and a feeling of control aren't automatically enough. The clinic sees mostly anxiety and mood stuff, so the supervision default is "validate and explore," and I suspect that default is itself biasing us.

I'd like you to turn this into a written procedure my trainees could actually run in about ten minutes after a session, when they're deciding how to frame the next session or what to flag for supervision. They each carry eight to ten clients a week, they're relativly inexperienced, and we meet for supervision weekly; the procedure should tell them which questions to ask in order, what counts as a "stay with it" answer versus a "redirect" answer, where it stops with a hard "redirect" or a hard "get supervision," and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a rules call. Also flag the places the procedure is likely to misfire, and be specific about which step misfires and why.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a client replaying a breakup conversation for the third session, asking whether they said the wrong thing and what sending a text to their ex would do; (b) a client with a long pattern of nighttime journaling that always ends at "why do I feel empty," with no action ever following; and (c) a client two months into a bereavement who keeps trying to "understand" why their sibling died and gets more distressed each time. I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me to redirect case (c), and I'm not sure I trust that.

I want the procedure itself as a table with columns: Step, Question to ask, What indicates stay-with-it, What indicates redirect, and Where this step breaks down.
