I am a PhD candidate in normative political theory, currently attached to my city's Office of Democratic Innovation, where I help design citizens' juries and panels. Our office agrees on the principle: sortition breaks the link between political power and social advantage in a way that elections and appointment cannot. The disagreement begins the moment we turn the principle into an actual body. Which issues should go to an allotted body at all, and which should stay with elected officials, experts, or a direct vote? From what population should the lot be drawn when the governed, the affected, and those practically able to serve are not the same set of people? Which exclusions are legitimating, and which quietly smuggle in a competence test under the language of representativeness? When should the body recommend rather than decide? And what should the procedure do about the fact that a large share of those selected decline to serve, with the no-shows skewing younger, poorer, and less credentialed than the seated panel?

I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in lottocratic theory, that a program officer without graduate training can run in half a day before any question reaches legal counsel or the elected council. It should consist of ordered questions with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer, explicit stop conditions, and marked points where the theory runs out and the decision becomes a matter of political values that must go to the council. In translating the theory into procedure, preserve the distinctions that do real work—political equality versus impartiality, descriptive representativeness versus deliberation under conditions that do not track expertise or status, standing to be selected versus standing to be governed—and say honestly which recurring design conflicts the theory cannot settle.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals, showing for each what the procedure decides and at which step it becomes decisive, and including at least one case where the procedure goes against our office's inclination to use sortition:

1. A citizens' panel on the city's ten-year capital-investment priorities, with authority to recommend, but not bind, the council.
2. A citizens' jury on whether the city should adopt wastewater surveillance for public-health monitoring, given the trade-off between privacy and early outbreak detection.
3. A citizens' panel on the redesign of a single arterial road through one neighborhood, where most of the costs and benefits will fall on residents of that corridor.

End with a short account of what the procedure cannot do: the considerations it will systematically miss, and the cases where a competent official's unstructured judgment would likely outperform it. Conclude with a single final line giving a one-sentence summary of the procedure's core rule, preceded by "Summary:".
