I teach a graduate seminar on strategies for doing good—the debate between systems-change approaches and marginal, incremental interventions—with 12 students and a 75-minute session. Design a playable simulation, not a role-play discussion: a game with explicit rules, a fixed decision cycle, and a scoring scheme that does not use real money. The students should manage a small concrete system such as a community health clinic's patient flow, a school's chronic-absenteeism pipeline, or a food bank's intake-to-distribution chain, with visible stocks, flows, delays, and at least one feedback loop. The rules should be arranged so participants first feel the pull of marginal fixes—adding a staff member, extending hours, tightening a screening threshold—and then encounter, through the system's own behavior, the limits of those fixes: a queue that moves rather than shrinks, a target that rebounds, a local metric that improves while an upstream stock deteriorates. The structural-versus-marginal distinction should emerge from play, not from anything I say before the debrief. Provide the rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the distinction, plus an honest note on what the simulation cannot capture and what I will need to supply in discussion, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.
