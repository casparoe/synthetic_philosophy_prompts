Last night a friend and I spent hours arguing about whether mental images are picture-like depictions or something closer to descriptions in a non-spatial code, and I came away convinced that most of our disagreement was really a disagreement about what a theory of mental imagery has to achieve. Before I go further down either side, I want the specification first: what must an adequate theory of mental imagery get right? Write the desiderata, and for each one give the phenomenon, experiment, or consideration that makes it a requirement. Let adequacy mean adequacy for empirical cognitive science: the theory should tell us what kind of representation imagery-based tasks actually draw on, predict the behavioral and neural data, and not merely describe what imagery feels like from the inside.

I know the rough shape of the debate, so you can assume it. The depictive tradition, associated with Kosslyn, holds that imagery uses a spatial, functionally picture-like format. Its favorite findings are the ones where reaction time tracks the metric properties of an image: mental rotation time rising with angle of rotation, scanning time rising with distance, and images behaving as if they have spatial extent, with accompanying size and inspection effects. The propositional or descriptive side, associated with Pylyshyn, replies that these behavioral data are consistent with subjects' tacit knowledge of how they would behave in the corresponding perceptual situation, and that the underlying format could be amodal and language-like, with the 'picture' merely what the process feels like from the inside. My friend and I kept discovering that our disputes about the data were really disputes about what we were entitled to ask of a theory in the first place, so I want those questions made explicit.

In writing the list, work through at least these considerations, and add any others you think belong. Which behavioral phenomena — rotation, scanning, size and inspection effects, selective interference between imagery and perception — are genuine constraints on the format of representation, and which are weaker than people assume? Which neuropsychological findings must a theory respect, and why — for instance, cases where a person loses the ability to form visual images while perception is largely spared, the reverse pattern, and cases where spatial imagery survives while object-like visual imagery is impaired? What exactly must a theory do to avoid conflating the format of a representation with its conscious phenomenology, and to make the depictive-versus-propositional question empirically decisive rather than introspective? What does the tacit-knowledge or demand-characteristics challenge leave standing in the behavioral evidence, and what would a theory have to show to get past it? And where do the known difficulties of reinterpreting a memorized ambiguous figure in imagery fit: is that a genuine constraint on format, or an artifact of the task?

For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or a local preference of one camp. Then flag any subset of the desiderata that you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see — and identify the single requirement that does the most work in separating serious theories from the rest. A brief scorecard of the depictive and propositional families against your list is welcome, but do not let it take over; the criteria are the point.
