I am a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of self-transformation, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people react to transformations that differ mainly in their means and speed. Consider two cases I have been using in my interviews. One person spends years learning a language—classes, flashcards, humiliating conversations, eventually dreaming in it—and people describe this as one of the most admirable ways a person can change. Another person acquires the same linguistic competence in a matter of weeks through a safe, regulator-approved neural implant that reorganizes the relevant cortical networks. The people I speak with tend to say that the first person has remade himself, while the second has merely had something done to him; they call the second outcome hollow, inauthentic, or even a kind of cheating, although they admit the two may be behaviorally indistinguishable. The same informants usually insist that a cochlear implant, an insulin pump, or even psychiatric medication that alters a person’s emotional range is unproblematic and often liberating. So the asymmetry is not a blanket suspicion of technology, of the body, or even of the mind. It is a specific suspicion of using a device to arrive, quickly and without struggle, at a form of excellence that we ordinarily think must be earned.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. The strongest candidate justifications I can construct come from authenticity, the value of achievement, the narrative unity of a life, or a view on which some goods are gifts that are spoiled by deliberate manufacture. But I can also see a deflationary story on which the asymmetry is merely status quo bias, a romantic attachment to effort, or a desire to keep excellence scarce. Test the candidate principles against nearby cases: learning perfect pitch by training versus receiving it as an implant, working through grief versus pharmaceutically deleting the memory, years of meditation versus direct brain stimulation that induces the same states, and draughtsmanship versus a visual prosthesis that confers photographic recall. Please give me the strongest principle you can find, say whether it survives those tests, and render an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Also say how confident you are in that verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.
