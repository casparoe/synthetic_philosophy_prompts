I am taking a course on African philosophy, and we have been studying a relational conception of personhood associated with Ubuntu. On the view we were taught, person is not a biological category: one is human by birth but becomes a person only by degrees, through relationships. Four criteria were presented. A person is someone who (1) is recognized by a community as one of its own and identifies with that community; (2) takes part in the community's shared practices and way of life; (3) stands in relations of reciprocity, discharging obligations to others and able to call on their support; and (4) is gradually shaped through these relations into what the community counts as a good human being, so personhood admits of degrees.

Now consider a case at the edge. Thandi grew up in a close-knit community, married and taught school there, and was widely respected. Thirty years ago, after a bitter dispute, she moved alone to a remote cabin and cut off all contact. She remains mentally sharp and deeply reflective: she spends her days writing careful notes about what she owes the community she left, though she never sends them. She harms no one, helps no one, asks nothing of anyone, and no one asks anything of her. Her former neighbors still remember her by name and say they would welcome her back, but she never answers their letters.

On the criteria above, is Thandi a person—fully, partially, or not at all? Please go criterion by criterion, say explicitly where the criteria fail to settle the matter, and say whether you think the case shows that the relational criteria need revision (for example, by recognizing a purely individual capacity such as rationality or moral self-consciousness, or by treating membership in the human species as sufficient for personhood). Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which further fact about Thandi or which general consideration would most plausibly flip your ruling, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
