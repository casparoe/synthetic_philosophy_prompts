A friend and I argued late into the evening about inverse reinforcement learning and got nowhere, so I want to think through the dispute more carefully. The disagreement is about what an IRL system has actually done when it observes a person's choices in some environment and returns a reward function. I maintained that, when the system works, it has genuinely inferred something about that person's preferences: a representation whose content extends beyond the particular choices it saw and which can be right or wrong. My friend replied that this is a mistake born of treating a modeling convenience as a discovery. Because the observed behavior underdetermines the reward function — many different reward functions make the same policy optimal, and the equivalence class is preserved under positive rescaling and potential-based reward shaping — he says the output is only a compact redescription of the choices, and there is no further fact about the person's real preferences for any test to answer to.

Then be precise about what even a clean result would and would not settle: whether a positive result could show that the system recovered something more than a behavior-equivalent summary, and whether a negative result would refute my claim or simply expose the limits of the test.
