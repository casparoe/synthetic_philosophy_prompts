I am trying to get clear about what I actually believe about the Daoist notion of wu-wei — roughly, effortless or non-forced action — and I have found that I hold five separate claims that I am not sure are jointly tenable. I would like an audit of the set rather than a lecture on the topic. The five, as best I can state them:

(1) The phenomenon is real. Skilled performers — an improvising jazz musician, a driver merging into heavy traffic, an experienced lecturer in full flight — often describe their finest moments as ones in which the action "does itself," and I take this to be more than a flattering way of saying the effort happened to be low.

(2) Wu-wei is best understood not as literal inaction but as cultivated spontaneity: a responsiveness that has become second nature through training, so that proper form arises without forcing.

(3) The state cannot be achieved by aiming at it directly. Someone who watches himself trying to be spontaneous, and tries to relax on cue, is doing an extra thing — performing effortlessness — and the result is precisely the self-consciousness that wu-wei is supposed to be free of.

(4) Yet deliberate practices such as repeated drills, imitating an exemplar's form, and ritualized routines are, in fact, the ordinary routes by which people come to act effortlessly; the person who refuses all such structured practice on the grounds that "trying is the problem" is more likely to end up stiff than fluent.

(5) More generally: if a state is such that aiming at it directly undermines it, then that state cannot be the intended product of any course of action designed to produce it.

I cannot tell which of these merely look as if they conflict and which genuinely pull against each other. Could you go through the five, identify the pairs in tension and the apparent tensions that dissolve, rank whichever are real by severity, and say what the least costly revision would be and what it would cost — without simply assuming at the outset that one particular thesis is the mistake?
