I am a doctoral student in moral psychology, and I keep noticing a pattern in judgements about hard choices for which I have no established vocabulary. Consider two cases. A driver must either hit one pedestrian or swerve and hit several; whatever she does, someone is harmed. A colleague breaks a promise to me because breaking it is the only way to prevent a much worse outcome for someone else. In both cases people often grant that the agent chose the best available option, yet they also insist that the agent now owes something particular to the person let down or harmed: an apology, compensation, full disclosure, or at least an acknowledgement of that person's complaint. This leftover requirement is not the same as saying she ought to have acted differently — that would contradict the judgement that she chose rightly — but it is not a mere feeling either, because it governs what the agent ought to do next and what the complainant may properly demand. Is there an established name for this phenomenon, and how exactly does it bear on whether the original conflict was a genuine moral dilemma? I am not satisfied with a bare label: I want the discrimination between nearest concepts, the conditions under which the phenomenon appears (must a duty be violated, or does it also arise from permissible trade-offs among persons, or from accidental harm?), and the boundary cases where it is uncertain whether the leftover is moral rather than merely epistemic. If no established term fits exactly, say so and propose one with a definition.
