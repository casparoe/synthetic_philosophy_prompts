I've come around to the view that death is bad for the one who dies because it deprives them of the life they would otherwise have had, not because dying hurts or because being dead is some kind of negative experience. The Epicurean line (you can't be harmed by something you'll never be around to experience) just feels too neat to me, and the deprivation account seems to get the obvious stuff right: that dying at 30 is worse then dying at 90, that we think someone misses out on things even if they never know it. But I want to actually stress-test it instead of just talking myself into it. What would genuinely count against the deprivation account — the stuff that should lower my confidence rather than the objections I can already shrug off? Walk me through the cases and arguments it handles least cleanly: the timing problem (when exactly is the harm done?), the fact that there's no subject around to bear the harm, the symmetry with not being born yet, people in permanent comas or vegetative states, maybe the worry that if any lost possible good makes death bad then everyone's death is infinitely bad because there's always some further good they might have had. Sort them from merely awkward to close to fatal, and say which of these a committed deprivation theorist would themselves admit are real costs instead of just biting the bullet. Then tell me whether the view has been qualified so much that it can absorb anything, and if so whether that's actually a problem for a view like this. And if you had to name the single strongest candidate to replace it, what should it be?
