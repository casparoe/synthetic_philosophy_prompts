Write an essay on whether the argument from ultimate harm for moral bioenhancement survives a distinction it tends to elide. Begin by reconstructing the Persson–Savulescu line of thought: our moral psychology is built for small, face-to-face, ingroup-based life, while modern technology gives us powers — nuclear weapons, engineered pathogens, large-scale environmental disruption — whose harms are enormous, delayed, and often anonymous; traditional moral education works too slowly and too weakly to close this gap; and so we should develop and use biomedical means to improve moral motivation. Then weigh the standard objections, including the value of the freedom to do wrong, the contestedness of what "better" moral motivation amounts to, and the worry about coercion or state-imposed pacification. But give special attention to a less-discussed problem: the ultimate-harm argument seems to require improving moral judgment in unprecedented, long-range, coordination-heavy cases, while the interventions usually defended would chiefly alter moral motivation — empathy, aggression, altruism, willingness to act — in the here and now. Take a definite position on whether the ultimate-harm argument can license anything beyond a modest research program into moral bioenhancement, and state exactly what obligation, if any, you think it grounds. Do not end neutrally or say only that more research is needed.
