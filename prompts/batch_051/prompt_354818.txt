I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size startup, and I've somehow become the person who runs our weekly go/no-go meeting where we decide what to spend our limited engineering weeks on. Lately the same shape of argument keeps winning: someone says a project has a tiny chance of an absolutely enormous payoff — a feature goes viral, one whale client signs, the platform takes off — and then any finite cost looks obviously worth paying, because the expected value comes out astronomical. I bumped into the term Pascal's mugging online (as I understand it: a guy asks you for five bucks or else he'll cause some astronomically huge amount of suffering, and if there's even a tiny chance he's telling the truth, the expected value of paying is huge, so a naive calculation says pay up), and it made me think our meetings are quietly running on mugger logic. But some of these bets really are just good bets, and I dont have a way to tell the difference.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure my team can follow, not a one-off opinion about any single project. The room is me, two other engineers, and a product manager, nobody has a philosophy background, and we've got maybe twenty minutes per proposal; the CTO signs off on the bigger ones but wants something she can defend without reading the underlying theory. I suspect part of what separates the legitimate bets from the muggers is the difference between a probability we can actually estimate and a number someone pulled out of the air, and the difference between a big finite payoff and an effectively infinite one — but I need that sharpened into an actual checklist or sequence of questions. Each step should say what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the final call to a person because the remaining question is values rather than math. Then apply it to two hypotheticals: (1) spending $20k on an influencer campaign that has maybe a 0.5% chance of getting picked up by the algorithm and tripling our signups for a quarter; (2) spending three engineer-weeks on a feature that one hard-to-reach enterprise prospect has hinted would make them sign a $2M contract. I'd like one of those to come out 'no' even though the room will hate it — and if the procedure honestly can't do that, tell me why. Also mark the places where the procedure will misfire or where we're smuggling in a judgement call and pretending its arithmetic.
