I keep running into the phrase "a rule of grammar" and realizing I don't know what would actually count as one. Give me a working defintion of a grammatical rule — what it takes for a pattern to be a rule rather than just a generalization you can read off a table — and run it through these cases:

- walk → walked. It extends to brand-new coinages: if I invent a verb "to plish," the past is "plished" and nobody blinks. That's a clear case of a rule if anything is.
- go → went, good → better (suppletion, if you want the jargon). These look stored item-by-item; no rule is building "better" from "good" on the fly. Clear non-cases, I'd think.
- sing → sang, drink → drank, ring → rang. There's a vowel-change pattern covering a bunch of frequent verbs, and it occasionally oozes into new territory by analogy (dive → dove, sneak → snuck; people can even be nudged to accept "splang" as the past of a made-up "spling"). But it isn't the default the way -ed is. Is this a rule, a family of little rules, or just a list whose members happen to rhyme?
- cactus → cacti, focus → foci feels sort of rule-ish, but status → statuses and octopus → octopuses don't follow it, and nobody would make "flactus" into "flacti." Is it a rule over a tiny closed class, or only a pattern?
- Overregularization: a three-year-old says "goed" and "runned." That makes it look like the -ed rule is real for the kid and the adult irregulars are just memorized blockers they haven't learned yet. If a rule is whatever speakers actually compute, is adult "went" an exception that blocks the rule, or is the adult system best described as a rule plus a list?
- Sound change: in the prehistory of English a sound change turned p into f, which is why English father and fish correspond to Latin pater and piscis. Correspondences like that are strikingly regular across the vocabulary, and the apparent exceptions usually have their own story — but no English speaker is running them now. Is that regular-but-unconscious pattern a "grammatical rule," or only a historical regularity?

I suspect the word "rule" is doing two jobs — a descriptive pattern in the data and a psychological thing a speaker actually computes — and these cases are where the two jobs come apart. Propose a definition (or give me two), test it against every case, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and whether you'd rather revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Bonus if it explains why rule-hood feels like it comes in degrees.
