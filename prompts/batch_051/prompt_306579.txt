I'm a PhD student in ML, mostly thinking about reward design and how to train LLMs to reason reliably, and I keep having the same argument with my advisor about the process vs outcome supervision distinction. Our lab's working definition: I count a training signal as genuinely process-based when (i) it attaches to intermediate steps rather than only to the final output, (ii) it says something about the step itself—its validity, relevance, usefulness—rather than just about whether the step happened to lead to a correct answer, (iii) it wouldn't be redundant once you already knew the final answer, and (iv) it stays roughly the same under counterfactual endings (a valid step is still scored as valid even if a later typo ruins the solution). In other words, the signal has to grade the step, not just grade the step's consequences.

Now the borderline case I can't adjudicate: a neighboring group is training a math-solving model with dense per-step rewards, but the reward for each step is computed by Monte Carlo rollout. For a given step, they sample a few hundred continuations and take the fraction that eventually reaches the correct final answer as the step's score, then use the difference between consecutive step scores as an advantage signal. So the model gets a number attached to each intermediate step, and it really does learn intermediate heuristics from it—how to isolate variables, when to factor, stuff like that—but every one of those numbers is completely derived from the final answer. On my checklist this looks like it satisfies (i) but maybe not (ii) or (iii), and (iv) seems wrong because the score of a good step would change if the sampled endings stop arriving at the right answer. But I'm not sure my checklist is drawing the line in the right place, because the signal is being used like process superivsion and the model demonstrably learns process-level skills from it.

Does this Monte Carlo step-score setup qualify as process supervision under my four criteria? Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion. Points 1 through 4 should work through criteria (i) through (iv) honestly, flagging wherever the criterion itself is ambiguous as applied to this case, and point 5 should say whether you think the criteria need revising—for instance, whether process supervision should be defined by what the learner is able to acquire rather than by where the signal's information came from.
