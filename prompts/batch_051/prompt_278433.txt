I've been going back and forth with a friend for weeks about whether Singapore's government is legitimate in anything more than a fear-and-resigned-acceptance sense. My line is that if a regime delivers decades of security, growth, and order, and a large share of its citizens seem to genuinely accept the arrangement on just those grounds, then something normative-looking is doing work that can't simply be explained away as stability or habit. Her line is that performance can buy stability and maybe support, but those are causal or prudential facts — they don't add up to a right to rule, which has to come from authorisation by the governed. It feels like a real disagreement rather than a verbal one, but we've also kept talking past each other, so I want to try this as an adversarial collaboration.

Take the two camps as: (A) performance legitimacy — when a regime reliably produces prosperity and order, that can ground at least a defeasible right to rule even without contested elections; Singapore and maybe China are the natural cases. (B) authorisation legitimacy — performance can make rule tolerable or give people reasons to support it, but only ongoing authorisation by the governed through participation and accountability makes rule legitimate; so Singapore/China show support or stability, but not legitimacy.
