A puzzle that keeps recurring in aesthetics is how a sequence of non-sentient sounds can itself be sad: a piece of music has no beliefs, no desires, no inner life, and yet we say without hesitation that it is sad, as if the sadness belonged to the sounds rather than only to our reaction to them. I want a working definition of 'sad music', or of a piece of music's being sad, that resolves this puzzle and gives a definite verdict on each of the following cases. If you think the ordinary predicate does two jobs, one picking out a property of the sonic structure and one picking out a listener's affective response, give me two definitions and say which cases move between them.

Cases:

1. A slow dirge in a minor key, descending melody, long notes, soft dynamics, no lyrics.
2. A bright major-key advertising jingle in fast 4/4, clipped vocal syllables, no emotional content in the text.
3. A field recording of an adult sobbing, presented by itself as an audio artwork.
4. A jaunty nursery tune in a major key whose lyrics describe the death of a pet.
5. A slow instrumental piece in a tuning system and idiom unfamiliar to the listener, belonging to a tradition that classifies the piece as expressing mourning.
6. A synthetic instrumental track produced by a machine-learning system trained to maximize a sadness classifier's confidence; it exhibits the standard acoustic correlates of sadness, but no human selected those features in order to express anything.
7. A detuned electronic drone with a slow low pulse, no melody and no voice-like contour, that listeners reliably describe as desolate.
8. A two-second descending minor-second figure, in isolation.
9. A concert presentation consisting of four and a half minutes of performed silence, announced as a musical work.
10. A slow major-key instrumental piece performed at a funeral, which listeners describe as unbearably sad in that setting, though the same recording heard on the radio would be called pleasant or bittersweet.

For each case, give a definite yes-or-no classification under the definition, and say whether that classification is secure or forced. Where the definition yields what you regard as the wrong answer or no answer, revise it rather than papering over the failure; where you keep a surprising verdict, defend it. Then identify the single verdict you are least confident about and say what pressure remains.

Finally, spell out how your definition assigns work to four potentially relevant factors: the composer's or performer's intention, the listener's felt response, objective acoustic structure, and surrounding performance tradition or context. You should especially say how conflicts among these are handled: a piece intended as sad but sounding cheerful; a piece sounding sad heard by someone who feels nothing; and a piece whose sadness is supplied entirely by the occasion rather than by the notes.
