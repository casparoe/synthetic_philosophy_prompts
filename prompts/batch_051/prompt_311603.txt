I'm a TA for a course that's half epistemology and half philosophy of law, and the current unit is on legal proof. I'm grading a batch of short-answer midterms, and the question I wrote (the class had just spent a week on base rates and reference-class problems, so I tried to keep this downstream of that) was:

"The Blue Bus and gatecrasher cases are often used to argue that naked statistical evidence cannot support a legal verdict, even when it makes the disputed proposition more likely than not. Explain what makes a piece of evidence nakedly statistical, set out the two cases, and critically compare at least two accounts of why such evidence is felt to be insufficient."

One student's answer, copied verbatim:

"In Blue Bus, a pedestrian is hit by a bus she can't identify, but 80% of the buses on that route are operated by Blue Bus Co. In the gatecrasher case, 1,000 people attend a rodeo while only 499 paid, so a randomly selected attendee is 50.1% likely to have crashed the gate. Naked statistical evidence is evidence that bears only on group membership or base rates and doesn't tie the harm to this particular defendant's conduct.

Both cases clear the preponderence standard if that standard just means 'more likely than not,' since the plaintiff's side is more probable on the frequencies alone. Yet most people share the intuition that neither liability nor conviction should follow. Thomson diagnoses the problem in terms of the public message of the verdict: a verdict resting on a bare frequency treats the defendant as a representative of a class rather than as the person who actually did the deed, so it can't affirm the particular event in the way verdicts are supposed to. Nesson takes the other route, arguing that statistical evidence isn't causally connected to the defendant's conduct, so it can't underwrite the relevant kind of particularized belief about what the defendant did.

I think the actual engine of both examples is that the gatecrasher figure is just barely above fifty percent, so the preponderance standard is doing almost no work there. Once the base rate is much higher, the worry mostly disappears and the frequency becomes just another piece of probabilistic evidence. So the cases are really about threshold calibration, not about a special epistemic defect in statistical evidence."

Please give the answer a grade (letter grade or score out of 10, your choice) and write the point-by-point comments a grader would give, so I can pass them back to the student. In particular, check whether the two named positions have been attached to the right authors, and whether the student's closing argument about the gatecrasher percentage actually follows from the examples.
