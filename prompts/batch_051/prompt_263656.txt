I spent most of my career as a bench biologist before I retired, and I've lately been trying to figure out what people in AI actually mean by "interpretation," because the word seems to cover a lot of things that, in my old life, would not have counted as understanding anything. When we said we understood a system, we meant we had a mechanism: there were parts, they did identifiable things, and if we poked one of them the behavior changed the way our account predicted. So I keep wanting to reserve "interpretation" for that, but the field wont cooperate.

Here's what I want. Give me a working definition of "interpretation" — what it is to interpret an AI system — that gets these seven cases right:

1. A decision tree that has been trained to reproduce a neural network's outputs. It's faithful, but it's a different model; nothing in the tree is a part of the network.
2. A saliency map (a heatmap showing which pixels mattered for a classification).
3. A mechanistic description of a circuit in a transformer, together with experiments showing that knocking out the circuit changes behavior exactly as the description predicts.
4. A person's post hoc explanation of why they made a decision — plausible, sincere, possibly confabulated.
5. A linear probe (a simple classifier trained on a model's internal activations) that reliably tells you whether the model is representing a certain feature.
6. A plain-English summary written for policymakers that correctly predicts what a model will and won't do in new situations, but says nothing about internals.
7. One AI system of roughly equal capability giving a mechanistic account of a peer system, with nobody able to check the account's details.

For calibration, my own current verdicts: 3 is clearly interpretation; 5 and 6 are clearly not, though useful; and 1 is the case that really bothers me. I could be pulled around on 2 and 7.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and tell me where it surprises you or where you think my verdicts are wrong. If the honest conclusion is that "interpretation" does two different jobs — something like explanation-for-intervention and explanation-for-expectation-setting — then say so and give me the two definitions. I'd also like to hear whether the definition changes when the interpreter is an AI rather than a person, because that seems to be where this whole recursive interpretability question lives.

Please answer in American English.
