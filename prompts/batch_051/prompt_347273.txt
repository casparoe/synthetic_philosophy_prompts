I spent my working life as a natural scientist, and only recently have I begun reading philosophy of mind seriously. I find myself returning to Richard Adams's Watership Down, a novel I have known for years. It gives its rabbits speech, foresight, social hierarchy, and a religion, while also grounding the story in their actual ecology and instinctive behavior. I would like a reading of the novel through Morgan's canon, understood as the methodological rule that one should not explain an animal's behavior by a higher mental faculty when a lower one will suffice. Where does the novel respect that rule, and where does it knowingly violate it? Please attend at least to Fiver's premonitions, Hazel's long-range planning, and the totalitarian warren at Efrafa. I am not asking for a verdict about whether the book is merely anthropomorphic. I want to know what a careful Morgan-canon reading of the novel reveals about the burden of proof in attributing cognition to nonhuman animals—whether the fiction exposes a weakness in the canon, a weakness in our ordinary attributions, or both. Conclude by saying what the novel contributes to this debate that an abstract treatment of parsimony cannot.
