I got pulled into a long dinner argument about whether annihilationism or universalism is the only way to make God's justice square with the traditional teaching on hell, and I ended up scribbling the following argument against eternal conscious torment on the back of a reciept. It's been bugging me because I can't tell whether it's actually watertight or whether I've done something sneaky with the word "infinite." I'll lay it out as I'd defend it:

(1) A punishment is proportionate only if its total severity doesnt exceed the gravity of the offense it's imposed for.
(2) Every offense a finite creature commits is finite in gravity: it flows from finite knowledge, finite malice or weakness, and deals a finite amount of harm.
(3) Eternal conscious torment is an infinite punishment: it is literally endless positive suffering, not a very long but finite sentence.
(4) An infinite total severity cannot be matched by any finite gravity — an infinite magnitude outruns every finite one, however large.
(5) So no offense a finite creature commits could merit eternal conscious torment; it would always be disproportionate.
(6) A perfectly just being imposes only proportionate punishments.
(7) So a perfectly just being would never impose eternal conscious torment on a finite creature.

One friend says the traditionalist blocks the argument at (2), because the gravity of a sin is set by who it's against — an infinite being — and not by the finitude of the sinner; but then I worry that every tiny sin becomes infinitely grave, which seems unhinged. Another part of me keeps suspecting that (3) and (4) trade on two senses of "infinite": being endless in time versus having an infinite severity at a moment. So: is the argument valid? If not, which exact step fails and what two readings does it confuse? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of eternal conscious torment reject, and can they do it without collapsing all sins into the same infinite gravity?
