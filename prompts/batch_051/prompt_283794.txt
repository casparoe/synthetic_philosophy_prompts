I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind working on the problem of other minds, and I've been trying to formulate a version of solipsism that shows up less as a metaphysical thesis than as a standing temptation in ordinary social life: the 'main character' intuition, in which other people come to seem less real than oneself, with thinner inner lives, their actions somehow staged, the world arranged around one's own point of view the way a game populates itself with non-player characters. I want to think through this intuition by reading The Truman Show, which literalizes it: within Seahaven, Truman really is the only unscripted person, and the town really is built around him. But the film also complicates the intuition, since Truman's misery comes precisely from being unable to meet anyone as genuinely other, and since the film cuts to viewers and to a woman who was removed from the set for trying to tell him the truth, all of whom treat him as a real person from outside.

I'd like a close reading that works in both directions at once: using the philosophical distinction between an epistemic worry about other minds and a moral stance of treating others as less than full persons, and using specific scenes to test and refine that distinction. Does the film diagnose the main character intuition as a natural but false and ethically costly temptation, or does it partly vindicate it by making Truman's suspicions locally true? Does it equivocate between "I cannot know whether others have inner lives" and "I may treat others as props," and where does it keep the two apart? And does the ending show that what Truman needed was not proof of other minds but the decision to act as if they are real?
