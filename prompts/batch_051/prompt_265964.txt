Ok so here's the situation. I'm giving a paper next month at a joint Scripture-and-theology workshop, and I need Q&A prep, not a defense of my thesis — I've got the thesis covered. The paper argues roughly this: the historical-critical method (source, form, redaction criticism, the whole "read the Bible as an ancient human text" toolkit) is usually defended either as a neutral science that establishes "what actually happened" or as a preliminary fact-check that faith builds on afterward. I want to defend it a third way: as an ascetic discipline. On my view the real payoff of historical criticism is not the reconstructed history you get out of it, it's the training — it makes you set aside your own theological wishes and let the text be strange, be other than you. And that ascetic capacity, not the historical results, is what makes the method genuinely apt for reading scripture as revelation rather than as a mirror.

The audience is two camps and I need the objections from each. Maybe two-thirds are NT PhD students trained to think the only responsible question is the historical one ("what did this text mean in its own setting?") and they'll get twitchy the moment I call their method a spiritual practice or say its main value isn't the history it reconstructs. The other third are Barth-flavored systematic theologians — the kind who think revelation is God's present act of address, a property of the living Word, not something any historical reconstruction can deliver — and they'll hear "indispensable for reading scripture as revelation" as the red flag. There's also one senior liturgist who mostly cares whether any of this matters for reading scripture aloud in worship, but ignore them unless you see a cheap win.

My argument in miniature, so the objections can target it: (1) historical criticism works by a disciplined suspension of the interpreter's own needs and answers; (2) that suspension is structurally what ascetic traditions have always aimed at, even if the content here is scholarly; (3) if revelation involves encounter with something other than myself, then a practice whose whole point is preserving the text's otherness is intrinsically the right kind of training for it — not just a fact-checking preamble.
