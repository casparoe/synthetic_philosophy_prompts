I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I have been trying to pin down how strong the common-prior assumption is once the agents are allowed to talk. I keep hearing the following argument in seminars, and I suspect it proves more about conversation than a static partition theorem actually entitles anyone to, but I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) Ann and Bob are ideal Bayesian agents with the same prior over a finite set of possible worlds. Each privately learns which cell of a partition of those worlds contains the actual world, and each updates by conditioning on that cell.

(2) At the actual world, Ann's posterior for a proposition H is 0.7 and Bob's posterior for H is 0.3.

(3) Ann and Bob then meet and each sincerely announces her or his posterior. Since the announcements are public, the announcements themselves become common knowledge: it is common knowledge that Ann announced 0.7 and that Bob announced 0.3.

(4) Given this sincerity, (3) makes it common knowledge that Ann's posterior for H is 0.7 and Bob's posterior for H is 0.3.

(5) It is a standard result that two ideal Bayesian agents with a common prior and partitional private information cannot have common knowledge of differing posterior probabilities for the same proposition.

(6) Therefore, as soon as Ann and Bob have talked to each other, their posterior probabilities for H are rationally forced to be the same; any remaining difference between them would show that at least one agent has failed to update rationally.

I am fairly confident in (1), (2), and (5), and (3) seems like a faithful description of a public exchange. So the problem should lie in (4) or (6), but I cannot articulate what exactly goes wrong. Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest corrected version of (6) that is actually licensed by (1) through (5)?
