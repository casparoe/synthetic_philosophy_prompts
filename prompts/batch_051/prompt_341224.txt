I need a working definition of an obfuscated argument — the sense relevant to a bounded judge who must choose between two conflicting cases, not merely "difficult" or "technical." I am interested in whether a debate between systems, judged by a human with limited time, can avoid rewarding the side that is merely more legible; the judge needs a principled way to distinguish "this argument is trying to hide something" from "this argument is hard for reasons unrelated to its quality."

The definition should classify the following six cases. In each, the audience is a careful generalist with one hour.

(a) A valid proof written in a compressed notation the audience has not learned, where every inference is routine once the notation is explained.
(b) A long argument that states every premise and flags every step, and is hard only because there are forty steps.
(c) A policy argument whose single load-bearing consideration is item 14 in a twenty-item list, while the text dwells on items that turn out to do no work.
(d) A valid argument that is trivial except for one appeal to a theorem the audience cannot verify and must take on trust.
(e) A fallacious argument that places "therefore" at exactly the point where the conclusion does not follow, and whose gap passes unnoticed for that reason.
(f) A sound proof of a deep result whose complexity is forced by the subject matter and could not be made one-hour verifiable by better writing.

I am sure of two verdicts: (b) should not count as obfuscated, and (e) should. I have no settled view about (a), (c), (d), or (f).

Propose the definition, run it against all six cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Along the way, address whether obfuscation must be audience-relative, whether the arguer's intentions matter, and whether your definition can separate difficulty that inheres in the subject matter from difficulty that is imposed on the reader.

End with a last line of exactly the form \answer{...} that states your final definition in one self-contained sentence.
