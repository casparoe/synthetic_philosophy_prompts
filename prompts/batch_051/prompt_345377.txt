I am pulled in both directions about whether novelty, variety, and adventure are non-instrumental components of well-being. On one side, I find hedonism about well-being compelling: it is simple, unified, and determinate, and it gives a natural account of the everyday value of novelty, since habituation blunts pleasure and boredom is itself unpleasant, so novelty pays off inside the experiential ledger. On the other side, I have an intuition I cannot shake. Compare two lives. The first is highly varied: difficult projects, risks, reversals, and growth, with a lower overall hedonic balance than the second. The second is placid and continuously pleasant, but almost entirely repetitive — the same satisfying day replayed without memory of the repetition, with boredom stipulated away. I judge the varied life better, and I do not think the judgment reduces to a confused estimate of pleasure; the varied life seems to contain something the placid life lacks, something like growth, range, or self-extension rather than felt enjoyment. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes (an imagining-away failure? a narrative illusion? a disguised distaste for how the placid life would look from outside?), what it would take for the theory to be amended (treating novelty or development as non-instrumental goods), and whether there is a reading on which the intuition and the hedonic picture are compatible rather than in genuine conflict.
