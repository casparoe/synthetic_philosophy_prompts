Before I read any more proposals about whether a whole-brain emulation would be me, I want the full specification first. Please write the desiderata that an adequate theory of personal identity for whole-brain emulation must meet: a theory that says whether, and under what conditions, an emulation of a person's brain is the same person or the same mind as the original, and what that identity consists in. I am interested mainly in the identity and fidelity question rather than in engineering feasibility, though the two will make contact, so the list should also include any requirements that must be met if the emulation is to count as a faithful continuation of the original's mind.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say why a theory that misses it has gone wrong in a specific way. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. Then flag any subsets of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular views. The purpose is to have a standard against which competing philosophical proposals can be assessed in a seminar, not to make a practical decision about whether to upload.

At a minimum, make sure the list handles the following, but do not let my list limit you if you think there are further requirements that are genuine:

- destructive scanning versus non-destructive scanning, where the original survives and there are two candidates for being the person;
- halfway and gradual-replacement cases, including the question of when, if ever, identity transfers during a slow neuron-by-neuron substitution;
- branching and teletransportation-style cases, including what the theory should say about uniqueness and about multiple emulations run in parallel;
- the distinction between preserving detailed neural structure, preserving functional or computational organization, and preserving the mind or psychology;
- whether the theory should require the emulation to be conscious, and what structural or behavioral facts would decide that;
- consistency with what is known about ordinary biological change over time: molecular and synaptic turnover, sleep and coma, and childhood-to-adulthood change, all of which the original plainly survives;
- the relation between emulation and the cases any theory of personal identity must already get right, so that the emulation verdict does not force a revision of settled cases unless the theory says explicitly why.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. Conclude by briefly marking which requirements the main families of views — for example, biological continuity, psychological continuity, functionalist or patternist, and copy views — fail most cleanly, but spend most of the answer defending the list itself. Write the response in American English.
