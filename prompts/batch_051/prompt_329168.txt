I find myself pulled toward the Neogrammarian principle that sound change is exceptionless: for any apparent exception to a sound law, there must be some further conditioned environment, an analogical restructuring, or a borrowing, such that the underlying phonetic rule holds without residue. I value this principle because it makes historical phonology something more than a catalogue of accidents. It imposes a discipline on explanation by forbidding ad hoc appeals to irregularity: if a correspondence between sounds looks messy, the theory tells us to keep looking for the hidden regularity rather than settle for a list of exceptions.

At the same time, I cannot seriously deny that a speaker's synchronic grammar contains suppletive forms such as go/went and good/better, where no regular morphological rule maps one member of the pair onto the other. I regard those forms not as failures of grammatical description or as mere defects, but as structured parts of a speaker's competence: they are learned item by item, yet they are integrated into the paradigm and produced automatically. If a child acquires go and went, the child does not seem to be applying a general rule; the relation is arbitrary and has to be stored. That seems to me a firm linguistic fact, not a glitch in my data.

Those two commitments now appear to be in tension. Why should I accept that the history of sound systems is scrubbed clean of arbitrary exceptions if the mental grammar itself contains arbitrary item-specific correspondences that speakers simply memorize? Does the diachronic claim really conflict with the synchronic one, or only with a stronger claim that language in general is a system of exceptionless rules? If they do conflict, which should yield: the exceptionless sound-change thesis, or the belief that suppletion belongs to core grammatical competence rather than to the periphery? Or is there a reading on which they are compatible because sound change and grammatical knowledge are governed by different kinds of explanation?
