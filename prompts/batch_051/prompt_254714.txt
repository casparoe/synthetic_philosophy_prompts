I am a high school student in a philosophy club. Two members have been arguing for a year about moral reasoning: a moral generalist holds that right and wrong are governed by principles we can state in advance and that apply across cases, while a moral particularist holds that whether a feature counts for an action, against it, or matters at all depends on the whole context, so principles are at best rough summaries of past judgments. They have agreed to write an adversarial-collaboration document instead of continuing to talk past each other. Write that document.

Begin with a numbered list of claims both can sign, precise enough not to be vacuous; move any disagreement that is merely verbal into this section. Then give a disagreements table with three columns: the point in wording both accept as neutral, the particularist's own formulation, and the generalist's own formulation. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side. For each crux, give one concrete, ordinary case both sides accept as relevant to settling it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case shows. Make sure one crux concerns whether a single feature can switch valence between contexts, counting in favor of an action in one situation and against it in another, and another concerns whether a principle can state its own exceptions well enough to serve as a decision procedure rather than a retrospective summary.
