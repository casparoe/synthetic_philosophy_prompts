So I cover science and ideas for a living, and for the last few weeks I've been going back and fourth on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The rough version: to be truly responsible for an action you'd have to be responsible for the kind of person you are, because your action flows from the way you are — your desires, beliefs, temperament, all of it. But you can't be responsible for the way you are: to be responsible for that, you'd have to have chosen it, and that act of choosing would have to flow from some deeper way you already were, and now you're off on an infinite regress. Nothing can be the cause of itself, so nobody ever manages to be the ultimate source of anything, and true moral responsibility is impossible. The kicker is, this doesn't even depend on determinism; adding randomness just adds luck, not authorship.

I run a monthly ideas discussion group — adults, mixed ages, none of them philosophers, usually about 14 people, 90 minutes. I want to run one session on this idea, but the rule for my group is no lecturing up front: the idea has to come out of something they actually do, and we only name it afterwards. I want them to actually feel the force of the argument — specifically the way the "but did you choose that?" question just keeps going until it bottoms out in stuff nobody chose — and I want them to feel it while they're in the middle of doing something like judging a person, not while they're sitting back evaluating an argument.

Design me a session. The deliverable should be a runnable activity: roles or rules, a timeline for the 90 minutes, whatever materials or prompts I'd need (a short case they react to, sentence starters, a board, whatever), a debrief that maps what happened onto the premises of the argument — the "actions flow from the way you are" premise, the "you couldn't choose the way you are" premise, the regress — and an honest note on which part of the argument the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to course-correct. The thing I most want them walking out with is the difference between an ordinary excuse (bad childhood, drunk, whatever) and the radical claim that nobody, not even the person who had a perfectly good childhood and chose on purpose, is ultimately responsible — and that the radical claim doesn't depend on determinism being true. Indeterminism isn't a loophole.
