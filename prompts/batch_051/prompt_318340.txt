My friend and I have been going in circles about whether a society's abundance has moral worth over and above the welfare it produces. She has an argument that it doesn't, and it's been bugging me because each step sounds right but the conclusion feels like it's flattening something. It goes like this:

(1) The value of any stock of goods, tools, and knowledge has to show up somewhere in the world.
(2) The only places it could show up are: (a) someone uses or enjoys the stock, (b) someone knows the stock is there and feels more secure or pleased because of it, or (c) the stock is available to be drawn on if need arises.
(3) Each of (a), (b), and (c) is just a contribution to someone's welfare—actual, expected, or option value.
(4) So all the value of a stock is welfare value.
(5) Therefore a society's wealth has no moral worth beyond what it buys in terms of welfare, and talk of abundance mattering for its own sake is confused.

To back this up she has this thought experiment: imagine a dead civilization left behind an enormous, perfectly intact storehouse full of everything it knew and made—records, libraries, tools, artworks—on a planet no one will ever visit. No one uses it, no one knows it's there, and no one will ever be able to draw on it. If abundance itself had value over and above welfare, she says, that storehouse would have to be valuable. But it plainly has no value. So abundance only gets value through its actual or potential uses for someone.

I can't tell whether this is actually valid or whether she's pulling a fast one somewhere. If it's invalid, I'd want to know exactly which step fails and how it should be restated. If it is valid, then I guess the conclusion follows, even if it's unflattering to how I'd like to think about prosperity.
