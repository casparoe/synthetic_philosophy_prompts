The claim is roughly this: children arrive at a grammar from input that is too sparse, too noisy, and too short on negative evidence to determine the grammar on its own, so any successful learner must bring a language-specific prior, constraint, or hypothesis space to the problem. People call this the poverty of the stimulus, and they use it to draw opposite conclusions about whether LLMs tell us anything about human language acquisition.

I want you to treat this as a design problem, not a survey problem. Help me build the strongest feasible test of that claim using resources I would plausibly have: synthetic languages, controlled training corpora, ablations on dataset size and noise, and comparisons of model classes with different inductive biases. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need something I could prototype.

First, tell me which reading of the claim the test targets, because I suspect the claim packs at least three different jobs into one sentence.
(a) An input-distribution claim: the relevant real-world input lacks a usable signal for some grammatical rule.
(b) A learner claim: no domain-general learner we know how to build can recover the grammar from that distribution, while a learner with a language-specific bias can.
(c) A conceptual claim: the disagreement is really over what counts as evidence that a learner has acquired a rule.

Then design the single strongest test you can, targeting the reading you judge most decisive. If a fair test of the other readings requires genuinely separate setups, add at most two secondary rows, but make clear which row is the one I should actually build. After the design, say plainly what even a clean result would leave unsettled, in particular whether a positive result would show that the bias is specifically linguistic, that it is innate rather than acquired by some other route, or that humans actually use it.
