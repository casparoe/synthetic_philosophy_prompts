In my philosophy of economics seminar the same argument goes round and round every year and nobody moves. One camp says prosperity is valuable only as a means: it matters because it buys health, education, leisure, security, freedom from drudgery. The other camp says that can't be the whole story — a society that has actually solved the problem of scarcity and produces abundance on a huge scale is good in a way over and above the goods that get consumed, maybe as an achievement, or as a collective capacity, or as slack that makes experimentation and generosity possible. The weird thing is everyone concedes the standard facts: diminshing marginal utility of income, adaptation, the environmental costs of growth, that past some point extra stuff doesn't make people happier. Yet neither side moves. The instrumentalists accuse the abundance people of aestheticizing wealth or smuggling in status; the abundance people accuse the instrumentalists of a monkish refusal to look at the social world as a whole.

I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What is the disagreement actually about? My own suspicion is the two camps might be answering different questions — one about what makes an individual life go well, the other about what makes a society admirable or free — but I'm not sure. Maybe it's a substantive fight about whether non-welfare values like achievement or mastery can count at all, or a hidden empirical disagreement about where our prefereces come from and whether they should be trusted once they've adapted to abundance. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a deciding case, a reformulated question? Or is this just a clash of bedrock intuitions that no argument will move?
