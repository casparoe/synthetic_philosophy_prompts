I am a software engineer at a company that builds route-planning software. I have been asked to give an eight-minute talk at our quarterly engineering all-hands, making the case for why our team has deliberately chosen to ship a route-finder that stops when it finds a route within a predefined threshold of the best estimated travel time, rather than continuing to search for the single fastest route. The audience will be mixed: senior engineers who will want a principled justification, and sales and marketing colleagues who are uncomfortable telling customers that the product is explicitly good enough rather than optimal.

Recently I read Herbert Simon's article “Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment” (Psychological Review, 1956). One passage in it has stayed with me. I cannot quote it verbatim from memory, but the gist is this: Simon describes a decision-maker, or an organism, moving through a sequence of available alternatives in an environment; because its information and computational capacity are limited, it searches until it reaches an aspiration level that is satisfactory, and it does not need to establish that no better alternative exists. He suggests that rationality in such an environment is a property of the match between the organism's choice mechanism and the environment's structure, not a property of finding the global optimum.

I want the talk to be built around that passage, interpreted for our situation. The speech should make the case that shipping a satisficing route-finder is not a failure of standards but a different, and possibly more appropriate, standard for an environment where exhaustive optimisation is costly and users care about reliability and speed of response. It must not lapse into philosophy jargon, and it must not pretend the word “satisficing” explains itself; it should give the non-technical half something concrete to hold on to, while giving the engineers a real argument they can defend. Write the speech.
