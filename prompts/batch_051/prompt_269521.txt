You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral and legal philosophy, with a particular interest in the normative significance of time. Below is a disagreement I witnessed between two members of my reading group; I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can. Please adjudicate it.

Begin by brainstorming the distinct issues in play before writing your verdict: the difference between the persistence of a past wrong and the state's present reason to punish; the purposes of criminal law; the moral status of repose and finality; the difference between a principle and an institutional proxy for it; and whether the passage of time could matter in itself rather than merely as evidence of something else. Think carefully at each step, and do not simply pick a side.

The group had been discussing a proposal to abolish limitation periods for serious offences such as serious assault and large-scale fraud. Two members, Mariam and Daniel, ended up arguing about whether the passage of time has any moral significance of its own.

Mariam began: "The wrongness of an act does not decay. If someone beat or defrauded another person, that act remains wrong twenty years later, and the victim's standing to make a claim against them does not lapse simply because the calendar has moved. Time changes our evidence; it does not change the moral facts. So a limitation period looks like an administrative convenience dressed up as a principle: we stop being able to prove things reliably, and we call that 'justice'. For serious offences, we should recognise the victim's continuing claim and abolish the time bar."

Daniel replied: "You are conflating the persistence of the wrong with the proper occasion for punishment. Punishment is not read straight off the badness of a past act; it is a practice directed at a person now, and it serves forward-looking ends — restraint, reformation, the community's relationship with the offender. Thirty years after an offence, the person facing punishment is seldom the same in the ways that matter: they may have changed their conduct, raised children, become dependable. Finality is not mere comfort; social life depends on the ability to treat old matters as settled."

Mariam: "Your appeal to forward-looking ends doesn't do the work you need it to. If reformation matters, the reformed offender should be spared and the unrepentant one punished; a limitation period spares both alike. It is a crude proxy for the very considerations you invoke. And the claim that the offender is 'no longer the same person' proves too much: if we accepted it generally, every promise, debt and obligation would lapse with time, yet the law still holds you to a contract decades old. The victim, meanwhile, has lived with the wrong the entire time; their claim has not gone anywhere, so why should the offender's?"

Daniel: "The promise and debt analogy actually cuts the other way. Promises and debts are voluntarily undertaken and forward-looking; criminal liability is imposed. I am not asserting a strong metaphysics of personal identity. I am saying that the state's authority to respond can have temporal limits without anyone denying that the original act was wrong. And notice the asymmetry you dismiss: the victim's suffering may persist, but letting it set the state's licence indefinitely turns criminal law into a vehicle for private grievance. Revenge survives time as well as injury does. Part of the point of law is to give reasons that are not merely the victim's."

Mariam: "Now you are sliding from moral argument to institutional caution. It is one thing to say victims should not run the criminal law; I accept that. It is another to show that a bright-line time bar is the right institution. If evidence is the worry, allow the delay to be weighed, as courts sometimes do when deciding whether a delay was reasonable. If reformation is the worry, assess it case by case. If the concern is the offender's autonomy, that cuts against all retrospective liability, not merely old ones. I see no reason why time itself — as opposed to these proxies — should extinguish liability."

Daniel: "The bright line is not a mere proxy; it is the only workable way to honour the value of repose. Case-by-case assessment of someone's life over decades invites the state to rummage through their entire history and makes every life permanently provisional. A society that can always interrupt you to answer for something from long ago never fully lets you back in. Repose is a moral achievement: it is the community's acknowledgement that, over time, a person has been allowed to become eligible for ordinary standing again. Without limitation, the state holds a standing threat over everyone, and that changes what it is to be a citizen."

Mariam, more quietly: "Then you are conceding that some serious wrongs will never be answered for at all. That is a cost — often borne by the victim — and costs need justification, not a name. Calling it 'repose' tells me why the offender benefits, which I already knew; it does not tell me why the victim's claim should give way."

Daniel: "All law involves costs borne by wronged parties; the state sometimes declines to pursue a wrong because pursuing it would damage values we are not willing to sacrifice. A limitation period is that kind of judgement made visible. What you call a mere convenience is a judgement that even justified punishment has a sell-by date. If you really think no period of time could ever weaken the state's reason to punish, I think you owe us an account of what punishment is for such that time is irrelevant to it."

I could not referee them, so I would like you to. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each of Mariam's and Daniel's contributions from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and in what respects — or whether they are right at different levels and talking past each other. Finally, identify what both of them are missing. Be willing to distinguish the moral claim from the institutional question and to say where the disagreement is substantive rather than merely verbal.
