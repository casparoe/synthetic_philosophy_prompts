I have recently started working for a small foundation whose board asks me to justify each grant by reference to its long-term consequences, and I keep discovering that I cannot honestly do so: for any serious project, I can tell a plausible story in which it does great good over fifty years and an equally plausible story in which it does great harm, and I have no principled way to set one story against the other. This has pushed me into the philosophical literature on consequentialist cluelessness, and I would like a careful comparison of two responses I keep finding there. The first response, characteristic of act-consequentialism, distinguishes the criterion of rightness from a decision procedure: an act is right just in case its actual consequences are best, but agents may permissibly use rules of thumb, since trying to calculate ultimate consequences is usually self-defeating. The second response, characteristic of rule-consequentialism, changes what gets evaluated: candidate codes of rules, rather than individual acts, are assessed by their consequences when generally accepted. Please compare these two responses as answers to the cluelessness worry. Are they genuinely distinct, or does the first collapse into the second once the choice of decision procedure itself has to be justified? Does either do more than relocate the problem—the first to the selection of a decision procedure, the second to the selection and acceptance-effects of rules? It would also help to consider how each fares on the distinction sometimes drawn between simple cluelessness, where the possible downstream effects can be listed but not assigned probabilities, and complex cluelessness, where the space of relevant downstream effects is not even surveyable. Throughout, please separate what is settled in the literature from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
