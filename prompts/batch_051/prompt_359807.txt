A person who emigrates — renouncing one citizenship and taking up another — is generally seen as exercising a legitimate, even admirable freedom, and international human rights law affirms a right to leave any country. But when a group attempts to exit by creating its own jurisdiction — buying or leasing land in a poorer country to build a charter city governed by its own rules, or launching a seastead outside any state's territorial waters — the reaction is much more likely to be suspicion, obstruction, and accusations of evasion or neocolonialism. Both acts are attempts to leave an unwanted political order; the difference is that the first joins an existing state while the second builds a new one. Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry in judgment? Test any principle you propose against at least these cases: an entire community that emigrates together and joins an existing state; a charter city that pays substantial fees to its host state and accepts its criminal law while writing its own commercial and regulatory rules; and a seastead in international waters that no state currently governs. Say which cases the principle handles correctly, and whether the asymmetry should be kept, abandoned, or reversed.
