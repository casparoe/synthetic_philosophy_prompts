I've read most of the sequences and lurk on the rationalist decision-theory forums, but I've never actually sat down and worked through evidential cooperation in large worlds systematically. I host a small rationalist-adjacent podcast, and I've got an episode coming up with a guest who defends the view that evidential cooperation is the correct default for agents in large worlds — roughly, that one-boxing in Newcomb's problem isn't a weird special case but the thin end of a wedge, and that rational agents should act as if they're coordinating with all the agents whose decision procedures are correlated with theirs, even when there's no causal channel between them. She's been writing about this for ages and is very good at deflecting the usual objections, so I need questions that force her past her standard moves.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions for her, meant to be asked in sequence. I don't want the usual "what drew you to decision theory" fluff; I want the questions to come out of actually knowing the view. In particular, I'd like at least: one on how Newcomb's problem is supposed to generalise to cooperation in large worlds, including how she'd answer a causal-decision-theory listener who says the one-boxing intuition is just a misframed causal graph; one on the standard objection that the view commits you to paying off evidential blackmailers and Pascal's muggers; one on how you choose the reference class of agents you're evidentially cooperating with, given that similarity can be carved up in arbitrarily many ways and the recommended act flips depending on the carve; and one on what the view says, in a world where every action is predicted by someone, that stops it collapsing into a rationalisation of whatever you were going to do anyway. For each of those, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — the sort of follow-up that would make her stop and say "good question", not the sort that just asks her to restate herself.

My real worry is this: I'm not sure the view has any empirical content left once the replies are in. So I'd like the last question or two to press on what observation about other agents, or about ourselves, would actually count against evidential cooperation. If the answer is that nothing would, because the view is a story about what rationality consists in rather than about expected observations, then ask whether she's happy with that, and why a self-described naturalist should be. Keep the questions colloquial enough for a podcast but not dumbed down; the audience can handle terms like Newcomb's problem and updatelessness, and I'll gloss Pascal's mugging on air if it comes up, so you don't need to avoid the term.
