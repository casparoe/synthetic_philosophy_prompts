I help run a discussion group for technically literate adults—working programmers, engineers, and data scientists, few with real biology training—and next month I'm teaching a session on heritability. The goal is not to give them a list of cautions but to make the quantitative-genetic concept itself do the work, so the standard misuses come to feel like category errors rather than tribal errors. Don't explain heritability to me; plan the session I should run in about ninety minutes with a whiteboard.

The audience reads blogs and knows heritability is a culture-war-adjacent topic; their epistemics will be worse than usual unless the sequence itself disarms the issue. I suspect the right order starts with the point that heritability is a statement about variance in a population, not about an individual or a trait taken in the abstract, but I want the dependency structure made explicit rather than asserted. What component has to be in place before the others, and what does each later component depend on?

Then the carrying example. I'm inclined toward height, because the audience already knows it and because it doesn't carry IQ's political charge, but I want to know whether height is actually the right carrying example: what it must have and what it must lack. If height fails in some way I haven't noticed, tell me what example carries the whole session instead.

I also want a misconception map for this audience, with the errors in the order they're likely to be held rather than in conceptual order. My guesses: (a) "highly heritable" means the environment barely matters; (b) "lowly heritable" means genes barely matter; (c) heritability is a fixed fact about a trait, a proportion of the cause that applies to each person; (d) a trait's heritability tells you how much an intervention or a changed environment could alter it; (e) when a trait is highly heritable within every group, between-group differences are probably genetic too. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off rather than corrected afterward, what the headed-off version is, and how to do it without the session becoming a string of "actually, no" corrections.

Be explicit about what to leave out at this level. I suspect narrow-sense versus broad-sense heritability belongs in the omissions, along with most of the missing-heritability literature, but I want the list with reasons and a way to say so that doesn't make the audience feel I'm hiding something.

Finally, give me a check for understanding at the end: a question or two that would distinguish someone who has actually acquired the concept from someone who has only learned to stop saying the forbidden sentences.
