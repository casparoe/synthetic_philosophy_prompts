I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether deplatforming and public shaming campaigns should count as punishment. His position was that they cannot: no state, no verdict, no due process, no formal deprivation—just private people deciding whom to associate with, however severely they express that decision. I kept pushing back that this lets the phenomenon escape too cheaply, since the person who loses a platform or a job over a public shaming undergoes hard treatment, public condemnation, and exclusion that the community plainly intends as a penalty. Around midnight I found myself reaching for The Scarlet Letter to make the point, and I would like to turn that gesture into something more careful before standing behind it.

I am writing an essay that reads the novel through the contemporary debate over deplatforming and social sanction as punishment. Hester's official sentence—the scaffold and the letter—is imposed by magistrates, but most of what she actually suffers is the town's ongoing social enforcement: the watching, the gossip, the refusal of ordinary fellowship, the treatment of Pearl, the way the mark becomes the lens through which her whole life is read. The novel seems to track how an informal economy of shaming does the daily work that a formal sentence only begins. But I do not want a reading that reduces the book to an op-ed. So I would like a critic's reading: where does the novel support the claim that informal social sanction is genuine punishment—intended hard treatment carrying an expressive message—and where does it resist that assimilation? What should we do with the fact that Hester refuses to leave Boston, makes the letter ornate rather than discarding it, and eventually sees the community shift its interpretation of what she wears? Does Hawthorne think shame of this kind can be proportionate or reintegrative, or is he exposing it as cruelty dressed as moral order, and how much of the contrast with online shaming is just the difference between a small face-to-face community and mass, pseudonymous coordination? End by saying honestly what the novel adds to the argument my friend and I were having—something about interiority, time, and return—that the philosophical literature on social punishment tends to miss.
