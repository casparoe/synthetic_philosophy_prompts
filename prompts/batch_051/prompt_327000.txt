Ok, so here's a set of committments I've wound up with in a paper I'm writing on the rational-choice foundations of distributive justice, and I'd like an audit of whether they hang together. I'm not asking for a literature review; I want you to treat me as a patient.

(1) I think Rawls is right that the original position is a decision under ignorance, not risk: the parties can't assign objective odds to who they'll turn out to be, and I don't think they'd be justified in assigning subjective odds either. In that setting, I think maximin is the right rule, and that's what makes me sympathetic to the difference principle (or at least hostile to maximizing average utility).

(2) But as a general matter I don't think maximin is the right rule for decisions under total ignorance. In an ordinary case with no probabilities—safe job vs. volatile startup—I think minimax regret is better: it doesn't make you fixate on the worst case when the downside is small and the upside is huge.

(3) I also think Harsanyi's conditional would be correct if you modeled the veil as an equiprobable lottery over social positions: then ordinary expected-utility reasoning would push to average utility.

(4) I reject that lottery move, though, for Ellsberg-ish reasons. People rationally treat known probability distributions differently from unknown ones, so turning ignorance into an arbitrary distribution is a modeling mistake.

(5) I don't want the original-position argument to depend on anything ad hoc about the parties' temperament—no making them maximally pessimistic. They should just be following whatever rule is correct for no-probability situations.

(6) Last, I hold a consistency constraint: adding an option that nobody should rationally choose shouldn't change which of the original options you pick. A rule that violates this is too easy to manipulate through the choice set.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, how severe are the real ones, and what's the cheapest fix in each case? Please rank the tensions rather than listing them all as equally bad. And since I need a headline for the paper, end with a single line in the form \answer{...} that gives the number of the single commitment I should revise first, or \answer{none} if you think the whole set can stand.
