A friend who teaches practical ethics was complaining the other week about how far the philosophy of risk sits from the way people actually live. The literature asks when it is permissible to impose a small risk on someone without their consent, but most of us cycle on shared paths, set off fireworks on Bonfire Night, and serve slightly rare burgers at a barbecue without a second thought. Our conversation kept drifting back to ex ante compensation and hypothetical consent, which are elegant in the abstract but hard to pin to anything operational. I want to know what happens when one of those ideas is turned into a working rule.

Suppose a neighbourhood adopts the following by-law. Any activity that imposes on a non-consenting bystander an expected risk of serious harm above one in a million per bystander per event creates a duty either to obtain that bystander's explicit consent beforehand, or to pay a per-head fee into a compensation pool that is divided equally among everyone exposed. To give the rule some bite, anyone who fails to make a reasonable effort to identify and notify the affected bystanders before the activity is strictly liable for the full harm if an accident occurs, whether or not they paid into the pool.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a position. What would it incentivise among risk-imposers: staying just under the threshold, choosing activities whose risks are diffuse or hard to estimate, or quietly shifting the risk onto the people least likely to object? What would it do to bystanders — consent fatigue, holding out for a higher payment, treating consent as something to be sold rather than given? Where does it break first when the probabilities are tiny but the exposed class is large, and are the obvious fixes enough, or does the rule eventually collapse back into ordinary regulation?
