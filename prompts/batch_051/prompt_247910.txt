I've been going back over the old 'what if everyone did that?' test for a unit I'm teaching, and it turns out my own intuitive verdicts are all over the place, which is embarrassing. I think it's flatly wrong to cut across the newly seeded quad even though my one crossing leaves no visible damage, and my reason really does seem to be that if everyone took that shortcut the lawn would be mud by October. I think it's wrong for a healthy resident to skip a shift at an understaffed hospital during a bad flu season, even when one extra absent body would not change whether any patient lives or dies that shift — though that one feels like a role obligation, not the universalization test exactly. I don't think it's wrong to skip voting in a low-turnout city election, even though if literally nobody voted the whole practice would collapse and I'd be appalled at the result; my own failure to vote still strikes me as blameless. I think it's wrong to keep two fish over the limit on a shared lake. I think it's wrong to stay in bed through a dorm fire alarm because you figure it's probably a false alarm, even though if everyone did that only when it probably was false, the disaster would happen only in the unlikely case — and then your rule would've killed people. I think skipping a protest to watch TV is wrong even though the march would be exactly the same size without you, but declining a dinner invitation because you're exhausted is not wrong even though if everyone always did that the practice of dinner parties would fall apart. And I think one more two-hour car trip to see family is fine, even though if everyone took my attitude toward driving the climate would be measurably worse; I don't love saying that, but it's my honest verdict.

I used to think the test was just: an act is wrong if universal performance in relevantly similar circumstances would have bad consequences or undercut the practice on which the act depends. But that principle makes my not-voting and my car trip come out wrong, while it may let the hospital resident off, since her single absence doesn't undercut the hospital. So now I'm not sure whether I'm a closet rule consequentialist with ad hoc exceptions, or whether there's a better version of the test that adds something about roles, fair play, public goods, and causal redundancy.

Can you help me find the best version of a universalizability principle — or a nearby rule-consequentialist or fair-play principle — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible? State it precisely, tell me what it says about a case I keep waffling on (throwing one small candy wrapper into a pristine river), and tell me which of my verdicts I'd have to surrender to keep the rest. I'm least attached to the voting one, if that helps.
