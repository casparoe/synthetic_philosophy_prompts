I am a retired natural scientist who came late to philosophy, and I ask you to read my question as coming from someone who prizes precision but lacks professional training in the field. In the part of Benjamin Franklin’s Autobiography where he recounts his project of attaining moral perfection by keeping a daily ledger of thirteen virtues, Franklin turns aside from the enterprise. He tells the anecdote of a man who wanted his axe ground bright all over and of the smith who, after being pressed, answers that he likes a speckled axe best. Franklin then suggests that a perfectly polished moral character might attract envy and hatred, and that a benevolent person might allow a few faults in himself so as not to make others feel their own faults too keenly.

I have come to find the position suggested by that passage attractive. I mean not merely the banal claim that perfection is hard to reach, but the stronger view that a few visible faults are positively desirable—that the best moral life is, so to speak, a speckled axe, and that the project of eliminating every discernible vice is not just futile but in some way a mistake, perhaps a kind of misplaced precision or moral vanity.

I want you to tell me what would count against this view. I want an honest map of its exposure. What observations, arguments, or cases should move someone who is drawn to it? Which objections are decisive, which merely awkward, and which would a defender of the view reasonably dismiss? In particular, does the view risk anything at all, or has it been stated so flexibly that no outcome of Franklin’s program could tell against it? Rather than lay out all sides and leave me to decide, take a definite position of your own on whether Franklin’s view is a piece of moral wisdom or a self-serving rationalization of backsliding, and then say what would most undermine that position.
