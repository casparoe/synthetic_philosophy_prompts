I'm drafting a paper for a philosophy of science seminar on Newton's bucket and I keep going back and forth on the main claim, so I'd like someone to push on the draft before I submit it. The essay I have in mind argues that the usual way of setting up substantivalism vs. relationism — as a fight about what "explains" the bulge in the rotating bucket — is misleading, because observation can't settle it. I say both sides end up smuggling in a principle of sufficient reason at different spots. The relationist objects that if spacetime points exist independently of matter, then a "Leibniz shifted" world with all the matter translated five feet is a distinct possibility with no empirically detectable difference, and that's supposed to be absurd. But the substantivalist can either just accept distinct but empirically equivalent possibilities, or say the shifted and unshifted models are really the same world (sophisticated substantivalism). And the relationist isn't off the hook: they still have to say what rotation is relative to. Saying "the fixed stars" or "the global matter field" just postpones the question of why that standard gets to count. So my conclusion is that the real disagreement is modal, not observational: it's about which formally distinct models count as one possible world, and the bucket is basically scenery.

Could you comment on whether this is an accurate and interesting way into the debate? I'm especially unsure wether I'm being fair to relationists on the "rotation relative to what" point. Also, please say what would change your mind about the essay's thesis: what argument or counterexample — for instance a genuinely relationist treatment of inertial effects that doesn't lean on non-empirical modal commitments — would lead you to a different verdict about the piece, and where you think my reasoning is most likely to fail.
