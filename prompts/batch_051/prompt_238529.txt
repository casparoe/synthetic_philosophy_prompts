I have been defending a view in a series of arguments with colleagues who work on AI governance, and I want it pressure-tested rather than echoed. The view is that the question "has transformative AI already happened?" is usually argued at the wrong level, because people slip from "the system can do something impressive" to "the system is transformative." I think the useful definition of transformative AI is not a threshold on any particular capability at all, but a change in the rate at which the set of things human beings can collectively do expands: a system is transformative only when it bends the curve of human capability over time — when it changes the derivative of what I have been calling the collective capability frontier — rather than when it writes a competent legal brief, beats a human at a hard game, or matches an expert in some narrow domain. By that standard I am inclined to say that the computing-plus-software complex of the past half-century was transformative in roughly the way the steam engine was, but that present-day AI systems are still riding that curve rather than bending it, for all their spectacular individual outputs.

I want this position pressure-tested the way a good critic would pressure-test a philosophical claim rather than a business forecast. What would count against it?
