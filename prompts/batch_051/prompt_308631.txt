I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so please treat any misused terminology as a question rather than a claim. I keep finding myself torn between a theory of knowledge that appeals to my engineering instincts and an intuition I cannot bring myself to abandon, and I would like a careful adjudication of the two. Please write the answer in British English.

The theory is the tracking account of knowledge most closely associated with Nozick. As I understand it, a true belief counts as knowledge only when the belief tracks the truth: had the proposition been false, the believer would not have believed it, and had it been true, the believer would have believed it. What attracts me is that this is an almost operational condition. It invokes no special faculty, does not require the believer to be able to articulate their justification, and offers a tidy, mechanical explanation of why sceptical scenarios feel so slippery: in the sceptical scenario, the believer would carry on believing exactly as before.

The intuition is that knowledge is closed under known entailment. If I know that p, and I know that p entails q, then I may come to know q by competently deducing it. This feels to me like composition in a proof system. When I verify that a component meets its specification, and that specification entails a property of the whole system, I regard the property as certified. Deduction is the most secure way I know to extend a guarantee; if it does not transmit knowledge, I am unsure what remains of the idea that sound inference can produce knowledge.

The difficulty is that the tracking account appears to reject that intuition. The standard illustration: I know I have hands; I know that having hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat; and yet, on the tracking account, I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat, because if I were one I would still believe I was not one. That licenses a situation in which the premises are known and the entailment is known, but the competently deduced conclusion is not. To me that looks less like a subtle result and more like a defect in an otherwise elegant system, though I accept that it is exactly the feature that handles the sceptical scenario.

I am not asking simply which side to take. Present each side at its strongest, and then assess the possible settlements. Should I debunk the closure intuition as a confusion between knowledge and some nearby state, such as justified belief, rational commitment, or being in a position to know? Should I amend the theory instead, perhaps by replacing sensitivity with safety, by indexing knowledge to the method of belief formation, or by rebuilding closure at a different level? Or is there a reading on which the two never really conflicted? One thing in particular troubles me about the last option: sensitivity is sometimes said to be evaluated relative to the method actually used. I cannot tell whether that restores closure in the deduction case or merely makes the counterexample more precise, and I suspect the answer depends on how finely methods may be individuated. If methods can be individuated ad libitum, the condition seems to risk vacuity, so I also want to know what constraints on method individuation would keep the theory honest. The key requirement is that your answer say what would justify each move, and how I could tell in advance whether a proposed reconciliation saves the intuition with a real distinction or merely insulates the theory behind a qualifier.
