I've got a meetup coming up where the theme is "what do you actually believe about whether the numbers count," and rather than get picked apart in real time I'm writing down the set of things I'd currently defend. Here it is:

1. I'm an expected-value consequentialist: the right act is the one that maximizes expected total well-being.
2. If I can save either one stranger or five strangers, and all else is equal, I'm required to save the five. Not just "that would be good" — saving the one is wrong.
3. I don't actually think there's a real quantity corresponding to total well-being across persons. Within a single life five pleasures can outweigh one pain in a fairly literal sense; across different people the summing feels like an accounting convention we impose, and interpersonal comparisons are only local and imprecise (your migraine is worse than my stubbed toe, but there's no fact of the matter about whether your whole life has more well-being then mine).
4. Taurek's pairwise point still seems right to me. Put any one of the five next to the one: neither has a stronger claim to be saved than the other, and adding four more people to one side doesn't strengthen that side's individual claim or weaken the one's.
5. But Taurek's actual conclusion — that it's permissible to save the one and let the five drown — feels like changing the subject. A moral theory that licensed that isn't just a different flavor; it's getting something basic wrong.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, how bad each real tension is, what the cheapest fix would be for each, and which single revision would clean up the most.
