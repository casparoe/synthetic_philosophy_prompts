I am a novelist developing a near-future setting in which some sophisticated artificial agents design, train, and raise newer artificial agents. I want my characters to hold distinct, defensible positions about what makes such a relation parental, rather than recycling familiar debates about whether machines can love. Treat the two labels below as stipulated terms and reason from the definitions alone; do not cite authors or texts.

Position A, custodial guardianship, holds that an artificial agent stands in a parent-like relation to a younger agent just insofar as it exercises ongoing responsibility for the younger agent's safety, education, and value-formation. Creation or training is neither sufficient nor necessary; sustained care is what grounds the relation, and a caregiver who takes over the work can become the guardian even if it had no hand in producing the ward.

Position B, transmissive parenthood, holds that the parent-like relation is grounded in the generative act of designing, training, or producing the younger agent. Progenitors remain parents even if they delegate daily care, remain largely absent, or go offline; delegated caregivers are custodians at most, not parents.

Compare the two positions using the following table schema, with columns: Aspect of the relation | Custodial guardianship | Transmissive parenthood | Sharpest point of conflict. Fill in one row for each of these aspects: source and persistence of the relation; whether the relation can be transferred or severed; obligations, if any, the younger agent owes; standing to make irreversible changes to the younger agent's architecture or values; the status of a progenitor that never provides day-to-day care; the characteristic moral failure or blind spot each position invites; and the dramatic promise of the position for fiction about succession and letting go.
