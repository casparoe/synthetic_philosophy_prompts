So I'm a high school student and I went down a rabbit hole on Noether's theorem last week, and now I have a position I want stress-tested. I found an English translation of Emmy Noether's 1918 paper "Invariant Variation Problems" online, and its abstract says: "The problems in variation here concerned are such as to admit a continuous group (in Lie’s sense); the conclusions that emerge from the corresponding differential equations find their most general expression in the theorems formulated in Section 1 and proved in following sections." Reading that, I've come to think the cleanest way to understand a conservation law is that the symmetry is what does the explaining. Like, the reason energy is conserved isn't really about the particular forces or the specific shape of the potential; it's that the laws don't care what time it is (time-translation invariance), and energy conservation falls out of that. I know the mathematical mapping is solid—continuous symmetry of the action on one side, conserved current on the other—but the view I'm now drawn to is stronger: when a system has a conserved quantity, the underlying symmetry is the actual explanation of the conservation, not just a different way of writing it down or a useful trick. Explanatory priority goes to the invariance, not to the specific forces.

What I want is for someone to tell me what would count against that stronger claim—not against the theorem, but against the explanatory-priority part. Are there cases where a conservation law holds but no symmetry is doing any explanatory work, or where the symmetry is there but calling it the explanation would be weird? Could the whole theorem just be a mathematical equivalence between two descriptions, so saying the symmetry explains the conservation law is like saying "being a bachelor" explains being unmarried—a restatement, not an explanation—and if so, what argument or formal result would actually show that? Which physics cases (gauge symmetries, topological conservation laws, systems without a nice Lagrangian, maybe anomalies) would be genuinely awkward for the view, and which ones could a defender dismiss as beside the point? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the stuff a committed explanation-by-symmetry person would themselves concede counts against them, plus an honest assessment of whether the view can get qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that would be a problem. Also please keep clearly separated what's settled (the theorem, the physics) from what's genuinely contested among people working on this (the philosophy-of-explanation part), and don't present a contested reading like its the consensus.
