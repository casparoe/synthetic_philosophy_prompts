I've been trying to figure out how I actually think about expected value, because I recently got roped into helping a friend compare charities by cost-effectiveness, and I notice my verdicts depend a lot on how the case is presented. Here's where my gut lands.

I won't pay $2 for a 1-in-10,000 chance at $10,000. The expected value is only a dollar, so it's a bad deal. But I'd happily pay $2 for a 1-in-10,000 chance at $100,000, even though I'll almost surely lose.

If a stranger on the street tells me to give him $20 or he'll use his magic powers to torture 10^30 people for a billion years, I don't pay. Even if I assign the threat some tiny probability like one in 10^25, the expected harm is still astronomical, and the calculation says I should hand over the money. But I just can't take it seriously as a guide to action.

My friend works on AI safety and says the chance of helping avert human extinction might be somewhere between 0.1% and 1%. I do feel like donating to that is a genuinely good use of money, maybe better than to a deworming charity with very predictable but modest effects.

I also think you should take a small risk of death from a medical treatment seriously, even when the treatment is likely to cure something awful.

But when two charities have the same expected number of lives saved on paper, I'd rather give to the one whose number comes from solid evidence than to the one whose number depends on wild, hard-to-check extrapolations, even if the numbers come out identical.

Is there a coherent principle about small probabilities of enormous value that gets all of these? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, if any? And what does it say about a one-in-a-billion chance of producing something of infinite value? I can't tell whether I'm being rationally consistent or just flinching at big numbers.
