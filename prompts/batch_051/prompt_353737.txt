I work in a small government policy unit that has been asked to advise on whether the country should set itself a formal target of entering the top ten of the World Happiness Report by 2035. In the course of reading the report, I have run into a conceptual puzzle I would like you to help me think through, rather than a statistical one. The headline country rankings are built mainly from average responses to a ladder item: respondents are shown a ladder numbered from 0 to 10, told that the top step represents the best possible life for them and the bottom step the worst possible one, and asked to place their present life on it. The rankings are then, in essence, national means of those answers. Yet the same report also publishes indices of experienced well-being, based on survey questions asking whether respondents felt emotions such as enjoyment, laughter, sadness, worry, and anger during much of the previous day. These two ways of ranking countries come apart in noticeable ways: a country can sit near the top of the ladder ranking while looking only mediocre on day-to-day felt emotions, and the reverse also occurs.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of happiness, or more broadly of welfare, that the practice of ranking countries by mean ladder score presupposes. I am especially interested in the distinction between what the practice strictly presupposes—what must be true for the practice to make sense at all—and what it merely fits comfortably with or leaves open. Does the practice commit its users to an evaluative picture, on which people are rating how well their life as a whole is going against some standard of the best life they can imagine, rather than to a hedonistic picture, on which well-being is the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience? Does taking the national mean commit it to treating ladder scores as interpersonally cardinal, so that a one-point gain to someone already near the top counts the same as a one-point gain to someone near the bottom? Then I want the practical payoff. Suppose we actually adopt the target and orient policy toward moving the national mean. What would we have to change if we replaced the evaluative, ladder-based picture with (a) a hedonistic view that identifies well-being with the felt quality of experience over time, or (b) a pluralist view on which well-being has several dimensions—pleasant affect, purpose, relationships, health, and so on—that are only partially comparable across persons? Does the existing ranking practice actually rule either of those rival views out, or does it merely render them invisible?
