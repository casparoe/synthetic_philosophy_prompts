In the course of a dispute about whether a congregation may introduce a short dramatic sketch into its Sunday morning service, one side invoked the regulative principle of worship: whatever Scripture does not command is, in corporate worship, forbidden. I have since met the slogan in adjacent forms — for example, that the church may do only what the Bible expressly prescribes, and that a practice with no scriptural warrant has no place in the service. I suspect the sentence is being made to do several different jobs at once, and I would like the possible jobs separated.

Please lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the precise theses the slogan could be asserting. In particular, distinguish between the possible meanings of at least the following: which parts of the service the principle ranges over (each element of the service, the entire service, or the whole of the Christian life); what it takes for something to count as commanded (an explicit imperative, an approved example, a good and necessary consequence of scriptural teaching, or something narrower still); what the relevant contrast is (forbidden as sinful, forbidden for the congregation, or merely unauthorised for those leading the service); and whether the principle rules out whole activities, the specific forms they take, or only the incidental circumstances such as time of day, seating, and whether to use a printed order of service. For each reading, assess whether it is near-tautological, substantively defensible but resting on a further theological premise, or implausible, and say which reading would actually support the objection to the sketch.

Please answer in British English.
