I run the grantmaking program at a midsized family foundation that makes gifts in the $5,000 to $250,000 range, and last week I had a long argument with a friend about the drowning child case. She kept insisting that the case commits me to more than I want: if I must save the child in the shallow pond even though it ruins my clothes, then physical distance, the number of other potential helpers, and my existing relationships cannot by themselves settle whether my foundation should fund a proposal. I think she is probably right, and it has made me distrust my usual screening instincts.

I want a reusable procedure my non-philosopher program staff can run in under an hour per proposal to decide which requests to fund and which to decline. Ground the procedure in the strongest version of the drowning child argument you can articulate, but translate it into ordered questions, not theory. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, which factors must not be treated as decisive, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the principle has run out.

Then apply the procedure to two decisions. First, a beloved local after-school arts program asking for $60,000, with good but hard-to-measure outcomes, versus a malaria-prevention program operating thousands of miles away with strong evidence of large effects at low cost, also asking for $60,000. Second, a domestic food-bank expansion that will certainly feed a known number of families this winter versus a global program aiming to reduce the chance of future pandemics, with much larger but far less certain expected impact. Flag the points where the procedure cannot settle the matter, and say plainly where following it would require me to override my board's strong preferences.

Please write the answer in American English.
