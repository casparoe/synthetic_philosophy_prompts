A study group I'm in landed on this and I can't find the exit. We were arguing about whether lying is worse than misleading, and Mina wrote this out on the board:

(1) Two actions are on equal moral footing if they match in what the agent intends, what she knows, what she can reasonably foresee, and what actually results. (2) For any lie you like, you can build a misleading case that matches on every one of those counts. Take Ayesha. Her boss asks, "Did you leak the memo?" In the lie case she says, "No, I didn't leak the memo." In the paired case she says, "I never sent the memo to anyone at the Register" — literally true, because she left the printout on a coffee shop table where a Register reporter picked it up. Same intention, same knowledge, same story runs either way. (3) So by (1), the two cases are on equal moral footing. (4) And since you can run that same construction on any proposed difference between lying and misleading, the distinction carries no moral weight. (5) But the construction also seems to show lying is no worse than saying nothing at all: Ayesha could just stand there silently while her boss assumes it was the intern, with exactly the same intention and outcome. (6) So either all of that is right — there's nothing special about asserting a falsehood rather than letting someone believe one — or step (1) is doing more work than it looks like.

I want to keep the intuition that a flat "no" and staying quiet really do differ in responsibility, but I also don't want to just assert that "asserting" is morally special. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
