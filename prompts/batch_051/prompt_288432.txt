I spent most of my working life as a research scientist, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy of law seriously. A practice that I previously accepted as an ordinary feature of contracts now strikes me as philosophically puzzling, and I would value a careful diagnosis before I decide what to think of it. In the United States, standard consumer, employment, nursing-home, and software agreements often contain a binding arbitration clause. Disputes are thereby routed out of the public courts and into a private forum: the clause is typically drafted by the stronger party and presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, it is almost never read, the arbitrator is commonly selected under the rules of a private provider and is often paid by the company, the proceedings are confidential, discovery is restricted, and a court will ordinarily enforce the resulting award with only the narrowest review. Meanwhile, the same legal system treats the public courtroom, the jury, the rules of evidence, and appellate review as important enough that a criminal defendant's waiver of much less—say, the right to a jury trial—is subjected to a strict inquiry into whether it is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. The asymmetry disturbs me: the state will scrutinize a negotiated, counseled waiver in a criminal case, but it will routinely lend its coercive enforcement power to a boilerplate waiver of the public forum in a consumer or employment case.

I would ask you to act as a philosopher of law who works on adjudication, consent, and procedural justice. Before you converge on an answer, take some time to brainstorm the different ways the practice could reasonably be understood. My question is this: what philosophical commitments does the enforcement of binding arbitration clauses presuppose? Does it presuppose that fine-print consent is genuine consent, or does it rest on the view that the right to a public forum is a private, alienable asset rather than a public function of the state? Does it presuppose a particular conception of what adjudication is for—accurate resolution of disputes, party autonomy, finality, or something else—and is that conception applied consistently across the criminal and civil settings? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and then tell me what would have to change, doctrinally or philosophically, if that presupposition were replaced by the view that adjudication is an essentially public function that may not be waived in this way.
