I am giving a department workshop next month defending the claim that the decline of the duel in Britain in the first half of the nineteenth century is best explained not by the victory of moral or religious argument, and not principally by law, but by a change in the honor economy: the rise of commercial and professional forms of status made the duel's core claim—that physical courage is the ultimate test of a man's standing—increasingly peripheral to how respectable men were ranked. I read this as an extension of Eric Williams's thesis about slavery: as with abolition, interest-based and structural change did more of the work than humanitarian argument, with the moral case arriving largely after the practice had begun to collapse. I take Appiah's stress on honor seriously, but I argue that the appeals to honor and ridicule he emphasizes were downstream of this material reorganization of status rather than independent engines of change.

The audience is predictable, and it is not my natural audience. Two intellectual historians work on evangelicalism, moral reform, and the campaign against the slave trade; they will resist any story that demotes moral argument to epiphenomenon. Two economic and social historians are comfortable with interest-based explanation but think "honor" is a soft concept that can be bent to fit any curve; they will press me on measurement and mechanisms. The remaining attendees are modern Europeanists with no strong stake in the dispute.

In the paper I give a compressed sketch argument: (1) the practice and public toleration of dueling declined earliest among urban commercial and professional men, before formal legal or clerical condemnation had newly intensified; (2) the same decades saw standing increasingly attached to creditworthiness, punctuality, and professional reputation, all currencies the duel could not secure; and (3) the canonical moral condemnations either targeted a practice already receding or were authored by figures outside the dueling class.

Please produce a Q&A preparation sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually receive from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the idiom of the camp it comes from. For each objection: the two-minute reply I should give, the point at which that reply is soft, and what I should concede immediately rather than be forced to concede under pressure. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and close with a short self-critical note: which objection, if sustained by evidence, would force me to drop the strong Williams-style claim—that material and interest-based change did the real work—and retreat to a weaker causal claim, and what sort of evidence would make me hold the strong version after all.
