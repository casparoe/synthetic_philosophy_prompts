I am presenting a paper next month to a fortnightly epistemology workshop at my university. The audience is roughly a dozen philosophers, with a heavy formal contingent: three accuracy-first epistemologists who work on scoring rules and expected accuracy arguments, two Bayesians from the statistics department who are sympathetic to conditionalisation, several philosophers of mind who work on Moore’s paradox and self-knowledge, and a few agnostics. The group is broadly committed to the view that rationality is fundamentally synchronic — a matter of the coherence of one’s total state at a time — but they are divided over permissivism, and they have little patience for merely therapeutic appeals to ordinary practice.

My paper defends a mildly heterodox position about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. I accept the standard view that stable, settled epistemic akrasia — believing P while judging, all things considered, that one is not justified in believing P — is rationally defective. But I argue that the standard anti-akrasia or enkratic requirements are too strong because they are synchronic. I defend instead a diachronic enkratic norm: after registering higher-order evidence that apparently conflicts with one’s first-order attitude, a rational agent may permissibly retain the first-order attitude for an interval while the higher-order evidence is being integrated, provided the agent is in fact engaged in that integration. My main moves are: (i) rationality governs temporally extended processes of revision, not merely frozen states, so a requirement formulated only over states mislocates the failure; (ii) higher-order evidence often has unclear or indeterminate import, and an immediate suspension of judgement is sometimes neither descriptively achievable nor accuracy-improving; and (iii) attempts to avoid this by saying the agent should partition the conflicting attitudes or suspend at once either collapse into my view or conflate excusability with rationality.

I would like a question-period preparation sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as the relevant audience members would actually phrase them — for example, the accuracy-first contingent pressing the expected accuracy argument, the Bayesians pressing whether my view violates reflection or conditionalisation, and the philosophers of mind pressing concerns about Moore-paradoxical self-ascription. For each objection, tell me how serious and how likely it is, what I should concede without damage, what I should hold firmly, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks — I suspect it will concern the duration and genuineness of the transition — and explain how I might survive it. Please write the whole answer in British English.
