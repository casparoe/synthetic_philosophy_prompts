I've been arguing with the same group about anecdotal evidence, and the phrase "that can't be a coincidence" keeps doing a great deal of unearned work. The latest flare-up was over a story in which two strangers at a wedding discover they share a birthday and a hometown; one side insisted this could not be chance, offered "one in 365 times one in several thousand" as the odds, and concluded that the event had to be produced by some hidden common factor. The other side said the birthday paradox shows exactly the opposite: across the many pairs of guests, some dramatic overlap is nearly inevitable. I'd like something more precise than either of those moves.

Consider the one-liner "That can't be a coincidence" as it appears in arguments of this kind. What distinct precise claims could it be used to assert — about the probability of this particular event under a specified null model, about the probability of any event in some relevant class, about the existence of a non-chance explanation or hidden common cause, about what the observation actually licenses one to believe — and for each reading give a verdict: trivial, defensible, false as stated, or true only under an extra assumption. For the shared-birthday case specifically, spell out what an arguer would minimally have to establish to earn the transition from "striking" to "not chance," distinguishing a reference class fixed in advance from one identified after the fact, and taking account of the number of comparisons and the prior plausibility of hidden causes. Finally, indicate which reading carries most of the rhetorical force of the phrase, and which single distinction does the most work in deciding whether an anecdote like this is evidence of anything beyond itself.
