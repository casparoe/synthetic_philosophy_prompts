In history we're doing the British Empire, and I went down a bit of a rabbit hole about the East India Company. The thing I can't stop thinking about is that by the late eighteenth century it wasn't really a business any more: it had its own army, collected taxes in Bengal, ran its own courts, and minted its own coins, while governing more people than most actual countries. I even saw some historians describe it as a company-state, which seems fair, because if you just looked at what it did day to day, it looked exactly like a state.

Here's what I can't get my head around. If a corporation can do all the things we normally use to define a state—territory, taxation, courts, an army—then wasn't the East India Company literally a state? And if so, doesn't that mean the difference between a state and a corporation is just scale or branding, and any big enough company with enough power is basically a country? My friend says that's definately wrong because the Company only had those powers through a royal charter and Parliament could, in principle, take them away. But that feels like a technicality to me, because in practice Westminster had almost no control over what it did in India until much later. Why isn't 'if it acts like a state, it is one' the right test?
