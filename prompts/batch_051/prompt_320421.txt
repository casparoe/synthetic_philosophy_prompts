A friend of mine has lately been enamored with Daoist ideas, and our conversations keep stalling at the same one-liner. Whenever I ask a practical question—say, whether one should deliberately rehearse stillness, imitate the ease of highly practiced performers, or try to notice and loosen the effort behind an action—she answers with some version of "trying to be spontaneous is the surest way not to be." It is meant to close the discussion, and it does, but only because it is ambiguous in about five different directions. It might be the innocuous observation that a strained, self-conscious attempt to appear natural usually looks affected. It might be the stronger claim that wu-wei is constitutively unselfconscious, so that any deliberate project of acquiring it changes the target in a way that makes success impossible. It might instead be a claim about learning: that the only workable route to effortless action is indirect—through absorbing a craft, altering one's situation, or practicing until the effort drops out—rather than by aiming at the state itself. But read as practical advice it seems to generate the familiar paradox of trying not to try: "stop trying" becomes one more assignment, and the harder I aim at effortlessness, the more effortfulness I am cultivating.

I want you to lay out, as precisely as you can, the distinct theses this slogan could be used to assert—about the nature of wu-wei, about the psychology of volition and attention, about effective means of self-cultivation, and about what kind of advice can coherently be given here. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, plausibly true, or false, and whether that reading actually supports using the slogan to shut down practical how-to questions the way my friend does. Then say which reading I should attribute to her if I am being maximally charitable, and whether there is a way of stating the underlying insight that avoids the self-defeating implication.
