I am currently taking a seminar on practical normativity, and I keep finding the case for reasons internalism close to decisive. On that view, a consideration is a reason for an agent only if it could move her through a sound deliberative route from her present subjective motivational set, so that the reason figures in an explanation of what she could be brought to do from where she now stands. This constraint makes reasons essentially action-guiding, respects the ought-implies-can principle, and gives a principled account of why reasons are agent-relative. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that an agent whose motivational set contains only narrow self-interest and mild aesthetic preferences, and whose fully informed, faultless deliberation leads nowhere else, still has a reason not to break a serious promise to a vulnerable person, and is open to criticism for failing to recognize it. Internalists tell me this intuition merely confuses the agent's being wrong from our perspective with the agent's having a reason; externalists tell me the intuition is exactly the data a theory of reasons must capture. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be debunked rather than accommodated, what would have to be true for the internalist constraint to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a coherent reading on which the action-guidingness constraint and the intuition that this agent has a reason are both preserved by distinguishing explanatory from normative reasons.
