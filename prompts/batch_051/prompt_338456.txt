I'm a software engineer and not at all a philosophy person, but I fell down a wikipedia hole about decision theory the other day and a puzzle has been stuck in my head since. It's about a "self-torturer" (I think its from someone named Quinn?). The setup is a guy with a dial that has a thousand notches. On notch 0 the machine does nothing; on notch 1000 it's agonizing. But each single click up from n to n+1 adds an increment of pain so small the guy genuinely can't feel it, and each click also pays him $10,000. So every individual click looks like a no-brainer: the extra pain is below the threshold of notice, and ten grand is ten grand. But obviously the same guy would never agree to jump straight from 0 to 1000 in one move, and if he does the thousand clicks one by one he ends up at a state he'd have refused.

Here's what I keep turning over: we're comfortable judging the little steps as fine and the whole sequence as a mistake, and we don't even feel like we're contradicting ourselves. That same gap shows up in my actual work. Each individual "eh, I'll just add this special case" in a codebase is fine on its own, but the accumulated system is something nobody sane would have chosen from the start. Same with one more drink per round, or one more npm dependency that's basically harmless. So my question is whether anything actually justifies treating a chain of individually acceptable choices differently from the endpoint it lands on — maybe something about imperceptibility, or about the difference between choosing a final state and choosing a path — or whether the honest move is to say that the rational evaluation has to be transitive and one of those two judgments has to go. I'd rather have the strongest case for the asymmetry than an even-handed survey, plus an honest verdict on whether it survives. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the version where the steps are made artificially small so the pain never rises to a felt level. If you don't, tell me which judgment we should give up and what it costs.
