I've got a rule I've been following for years and I'm starting to worry it's just bias in a lab coat. Whenever I need a view on something I won't have time to actually investigate—why the Roman empire fell, whether rent control works, whether a given food additive is dangerous—I read until I hit the first explanation that roughly fits what I already know and doesn't contradict anything else I'm confident about. Then I stop and treat that as my working view, and I only reopen the search if someone I personally respect objects. My friend says this is satisficing, and given my real time budget, bounded rationality says this is fine: I can't compare every possible account, so I settle for a good-enough one. My partner says that's a misuse of the idea. Her point is that "good enough" here is doing no real work—it just means "the first story that felt coherent"—and since I never check how many competing explanations would also fit the same facts, the first one is at best a coin flip, or worse, a gift to whoever tells the smoothest story. She says a genuine bounded-rationality policy would divvy up my limited search across competing views, maybe read the strongest case for each side, and only then stop. Who's right? Is my stopping rule defensible as satisficing, and is there a version of it that keeps the time savings without just believing the first coherent narrative I meet?
