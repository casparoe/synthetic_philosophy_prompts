I'm grading a midterm for my Philosophy of Mind course and want a second opinion before I finalize marks. Here's the question I set:

Distinguish Chalmers's easy problems from the hard problem of consciousness. Then evaluate the following line of thought: since we cannot see how any physical story could explain phenomenal experience, physicalism about consciousness is probably false. In your answer, be explicit about which parts of the reasoning are epistemic and which are metaphysical.

And here's what one student wrote, cleaned up a bit from there handwriting:

Chalmers distinguishes the easy problems, which cover things like reportability, discrimination, the integration of information, and the control of behavior, from the hard problem, which is explaining why there is something it is like for the subject at all. The easy problems are called easy not because they are trivial, but because they look like ordinary problems of cognitive science: you just specify a mechanism that performs the relevant function. The hard problem does not go away when all those functions are explained, because it asks why any of that processing is accompanied by experience.

Levine's explanatory gap supports this point. For identities like water = H2O, we can see why the identification holds once the chemistry is in view. But for a proposed identity like pain = C-fiber firing, there is still a gap between the physical description and the felt quality. Levine took this gap to show that materialism is false, because a real identity shouldn't leave that kind of gap. Given the gap, it is reasonable to conclude that phenomenal consciousness probably is not physical.

The Mary argument points the same way. If Mary knows all the physical facts while in the black-and-white room and learns something new when she first sees red, then there is a fact about experience that the physical story leaves out. Some physicalists reply that Mary only gains an ability, not a new fact, but that just seems to change the subject.

So the inference in the question is basically right: the hard problem is not merely a failure of imagination. It gives us positive reason to deny physicalism about consciousness.
