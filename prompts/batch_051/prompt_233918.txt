I supervise a small staff of attorneys in a city attorney's office and sign off on every interpretation memo before it goes out. A few times a month, a department head, a code enforcement officer, or the police chief comes to us with a live question about what a city ordinance actually requires, usually because an enforcement action, a permit decision, or a citation has to go out within days. We have no statutory-interpretation specialist on staff, and two attorneys recently gave the city opposite advice about the same provision: one reasoned almost entirely from the words of the section, the other from what the city council was trying to accomplish when it passed the provision. The city manager has asked me to produce a single working procedure that the whole office can use so that our advice stops looking arbitrary.

Please write me that procedure as a reusable checklist in words: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite answer, and where it stops by saying that the remaining step is not legal interpretation but a policy choice that a person must make. The procedure must be usable by a staff attorney in under an hour, working only from the city code, related provisions in the same title, and the clerk's record of the council meeting at which the provision passed. It should not assume that the user knows the labels "textualism" and "purposivism"; instead, the procedure should itself expose the points at which a word-first lawyer and a purpose-first lawyer part ways, so that a supervisor can see exactly what kind of disagreement is on the table.

At minimum, the procedure should handle the following in operational terms: when a statutory definition controls and when it does not; when an undefined word gets its ordinary meaning and when it gets a technical or trade meaning; how the surrounding sections, the title, and a statement of purpose in the preamble may be used; when the record of the council meeting may be used and when it may not; what to do when the text and the evident purpose point in opposite directions; what to do when the provision simply does not cover a case the drafters did not anticipate; and how to treat mistakes or absurd results at the literal level. The procedure should include explicit stopping rules and should flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. It should end with a short section stating what the procedure cannot do: the questions it will not settle and the assumptions about the legal system on which it rests.

Then run the procedure on each of the following three cases, showing the answer at each step, where the procedure stops, and what the final instruction to the client would be. In each case, do not adjust the procedure retroactively to avoid an uncomfortable result; if the result is uncomfortable, say so plainly.

1. Traffic Code § 12-4(a) makes it an infraction to "abandon a vehicle on any public street." Section 12-1 defines "abandon" as "to leave a vehicle parked or standing on a public street for a consecutive period of more than seventy-two hours without moving it to a different off-street or on-street location." A food-truck operator holds no overnight parking permit and parks his truck each night from 9 p.m. to 5 a.m. on the same residential block. Every workday he drives it away to a different vending location and returns that night. One patrol officer wants to cite him under § 12-4 for using the street as his overnight storage lot; another officer says the definition is not met because the truck is moved every day.

2. Building Code § 8-11(a) requires every "place of public assembly" to "provide an illuminated exit sign above each exit door." The city has just permitted an open-air amphitheater in a park: an uncovered grass bowl with four gravel paths that serve as exits. There are no doors and no structure above the paths from which a sign could be hung. The building official wants to require overhead signs on posts at each path; the parks director wants to allow low, solar-powered path markers instead. The section is silent about unroofed venues and about exits that are not doors.

3. Noise Code § 17-4 prohibits any person from causing sound in a residential zone between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. that exceeds 60 decibels, measured at the nearest property line. The only exemptions listed are for emergency vehicles and snow-removal equipment. A historic church has rung its bells every morning at 6:00 a.m. since 1935; the ringing now measures 75 decibels at the property line of the apartment building next door, which was completed last year. The police chief wants to stop issuing warnings and begin citing the church under § 17-4. The ordinance was enacted in 1987 in response to all-night construction downtown, and at least two council members now say they never intended it to reach church bells.
