I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of language, and I have been asked to lead a session in a colleague's Shakespeare seminar on Macbeth. My brief is to treat the play as a philosophical text rather than to lecture on its historical or theatrical context. I want to organise the session around the problem of bivalence and truth-value gaps: the principle that every declarative statement is either true or false, and the rival view that some statements—paradigmatically future contingents, the subject of the old sea-battle puzzle—are neither true nor false. The play strikes me as unusually rich here, because the Weird Sisters' prophecies seem to presuppose that the future already has a determinate truth-value, while the drama itself depends on Macbeth's choices being genuinely open. If the prophecies are already true, his agency is in danger of collapsing into fatalism; if they are neither true nor false, it is unclear what the witches are revealing or why their speech has such causal power. The easy resolution—that the prophecies are merely self-fulfilling—also seems inadequate, since some of them concern matters Macbeth could not bring about simply by believing them, such as the promise about Banquo's line and the eventual movement of Birnam Wood.

I would be grateful if you could draft the lecture for me. What I am after is a close philosophical reading of the play's treatment of prophecy and equivocation through the lens of truth-value gaps, one that takes both the philosophy and the drama seriously. Where does Shakespeare dramatise the logic of bivalence and its failure, and where does the play resist or complicate the philosophical framework rather than merely illustrating it? Does the play commit itself to any position on whether statements about the future are true, false, or neither before the events they describe? And what does the play's way of posing the problem add to the standing philosophical debate about future contingents that a treatise on the topic would miss? Please do not include verbatim quotation from the play or from secondary sources, and please write the lecture in British English.
