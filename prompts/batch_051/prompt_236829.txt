I've been trying to get clear on the old debate in the philosophy of photography about whether photographs are transparent — that is, whether looking at a photo is a way of literally seeing the thing photographed, like looking through a telescope or a mirror, rather than just seeing a representation of it, the way you do with a painting. The standard argument for transparency, which I've always associated with Kendall Walton, goes roughly: photographs are produced by a mechanical optical process that causally tracks the scene; what shows up on the film or sensor is counterfactually dependent on what was in front of the lens, without anyone's beliefs about the scene getting in the way the way a painter's do; and that special kind of causal dependence is exactly what makes something a means of seeing rather than mere depiction. So snapshots are transparent, however much the photographer chose the framing; paintings aren't.

But the "photograph" in that argument feels out of date now. My phone does computational photography — night mode stacks a burst of frames, a neural net denoises and sometimes reconstructs texture, magic eraser fills in backgrounds with pixels the sensor never received, some zoom modes synthesize detail. And then there are fully generated photorealistic images that look like photographs but were never causally coupled to any scene at all. I'm trying to figure out whether the transparency argument survives this shift, or whether it now covers only an ever-shrinking class of old-school causal photographs. Which exact premise does the new stuff actually bear on, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still interesting — e.g. that transparency comes in degrees depending on how much of the image is optically sampled, or that the argument is untouched because these new images simply aren't photographs in the relevant sense? Also, please seperate what's settled in this discussion from what people who work on it genuinely disagree about; don't present one contested reading as the consensus.
