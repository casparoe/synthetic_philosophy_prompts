I keep seeing a two-step move in debates about whether the brain is a computer, and I want to know whether it has a name and exactly how to draw its boundaries. In step one, computation is used in a permissive sense: a system computes if its state transitions can be described by some computable function or mapped onto a formal automaton at some level of abstraction. Under that usage almost any physical system, from a stomach to a rock, would count. When someone points this out, the defender shifts to a demanding sense: real computation requires representations, or requires that the states driving behavior have the right causal or semantic relation to what they represent, and the brain is claimed to compute in that sense. But when the demanding sense runs into its own problems—for instance, no non-arbitrary way to say which program the brain is running—the defender slides back to the permissive sense to argue that at least nothing rules out the brain's computing in principle. It is not plain equivocation, because the two senses sit on a single continuum of increasing requirements, and it is not motte-and-bailey, because the permissive sense is not the easily defensible core—it is exactly the version under attack. Is there an established name for this pattern of alternating between a permissive and a demanding sense of computation to deflect objections as they arise? If there is, how is it related to, and distinct from, the triviality objection to computationalism, pancomputationalism, and ordinary equivocation? If there is no standard term, what would you call it, and what minimal features distinguish an instance of it from those neighbors?
