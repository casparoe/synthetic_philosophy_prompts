I've been trying to make sense of a pattern in the way several institutions now handle recorded video and audio, and I'd like you to reconstruct the epistemology implicit in it. On the one hand, newsrooms publish guides for spotting synthetic media and platforms attach labels to clips they suspect were produced by generative models, all premised on the thought that the surface look of a video is not a reliable guide to its authenticity. On the other hand, the same newsrooms will treat a bystander's phone video as effectively decisive—it clears or condemns someone, it settles a dispute—after checking only that the file exists, that it came from the person who says they shot it, and that nothing about it looks obviously wrong. Courts in many jurisdictions likewise let photographs and videos into evidence when a witness vouches that the image accurately depicts what the witness perceived, even though that witness is usually in no position to vouch that the file was not digitally assembled from several sources, and juries are then left to weigh the recording with their own eyes even though unaided perception is a poor detector of sophisticated manipulation.

What view of knowledge, evidence, or testimony does this patchwork of practices presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose an epistemic backstop—some class of evidence whose provenance or perceptual character can still be trusted as generative media improves—or is it better read as an incoherent compromise between old media habits and newer anxieties about fabricated evidence? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as precisely as you can: say whether the parts presuppose consistent things, distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and flag the places where they are philosophically underdetermined and several different views would license them.

Then tell me what would actually have to change in courtroom, newsroom, or platform practice if that backstop were removed—that is, if no category of recorded media could be treated as carrying its own credibility without some independent reason to trust its provenance. Finally, wherever you take a stand, say what would change your mind: which kind of argument, case, or counterexample would switch your verdict, and which step of your reconstruction is most likely to fail.
