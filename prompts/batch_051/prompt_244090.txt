I am a doctoral candidate in modern European history, and next month I will defend a paper at my department's twentieth-century history workshop. The paper argues that the question everybody asks — whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler — is badly posed, because it treats a bundle of separable counterfactuals as a single outcome. I propose decomposing it into three questions: whether some form of authoritarian, ethnonationalist regime in Germany was highly likely by the mid-1930s given the structural conditions of the collapse of Weimar; whether a major European war would have followed under a different leadership; and whether the specific escalation to the systematic murder of the Jews of Europe would have occurred. My conclusions are that the first is close to overdetermined, the second is substantially contingent on the particular leadership, and the third is far more Hitler-dependent than either of the other two; correspondingly, I argue that historians should treat "the regime," "the war," and "the genocide" as different units of analysis rather than as a single unified event. My argument runs through a methodological section on how one disciplines counterfactual claims, then a comparative look at other post-crisis authoritarian regimes in interwar Europe, and finally a focused argument that radicalization toward genocide depended on a specific kind of leader and not merely on the system's structural pressures.

The audience is a mix. Most are social and political historians of interwar and Nazi Germany with a structuralist bent, sympathetic to explanations in terms of elites, economic crisis, and institutional dynamics; they will press on whether my decomposition is explanation or relabeling, and on whether my second counterfactual really differs from my first. There is one biographer of Hitler who distrusts counterfactual reasoning as such and will say the method lets me smuggle in my prior commitments. There is a historical sociologist who will grant the first counterfactual and challenge the third as unfalsifiable. And there are early-modern historians who will ask whether the method generalizes or is ad hoc. I can handle the generic "counterfactuals are unscientific" objection; what worries me is the objector who says my least interesting claim is the only secure one, and that the case where I want individual agency to matter most is exactly where the evidence will not come with me. Please write me a Q&A prep sheet for the discussion period: the objections I should expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as these particular people would phrase them, with a note on what to concede and what to hold, a two-minute reply to each, an honest assessment of where each reply is softest, and finally the question I should most hope nobody asks.
