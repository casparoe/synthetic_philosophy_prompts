I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and this term I've been running an informal experiment with a collegue of mine (call her D) who has very sharp instincts about cases but refuses to state her theory. I send her invented vignettes; she gives me only "reasonable to believe" or "not reasonable" plus a one-line shrug. Her record so far: (1) a developer buys a contaminated site, donates to three county commissioners, and the only contamination report disappears from the county website just as the cleanup cost cap is quietly raised — she says reasonable, "could be an unlucky website migration, but the donations make the boring story likelier than the glitch"; (2) the claim that a decade of floods was caused by cloud-seeding and that a global network of meteorologists, airlines, and insurers is covering it up, which is also supposed to explain why no insider ever leaked — she says not reasonable, "the silence of thousands is doing more evidential work than any document could"; (3) an engineer leaks an internal memo showing Meridian Motors knew a brake part failed above the safe rate and skipped a recall on cost grounds, and the company calls the memo a forgery — she says reasonable on balance, "a forgery is possible, but burying a costly recall is a boring, well-known thing companies do"; (4) the theory that a fitness app's parent company is run by a wellness cult and the app is nudging teenagers into extreme dieting as a population-control experiment, with one anonymous forum post and ad-revenue incentives as the only support — she says not reasonable, "you can't get to a mechanism like that from an anon post"; (5) a statistician finds legacy applicants admitted at much higher rates even after controlling for every public variable, and a former admissions staffer says there was an internal plus-factor instruction but no document surfaces — she says reasonable, "a statistical pattern plus an insider is exactly what ordinary wrongdoing looks like"; (6) the theory that a plane crash was staged by a rival airline using a secret microwave weapon, supported only by a self-appointed expert's YouTube channel — she says not reasonable, "if the weapon were real, it wouldn't be making its epistemic debut on YouTube."

Now the new case: a consortium of tech firms, two health agencies, and a standards body are accused of spending a decade building an industry standard that deliberately slows devices on a schedule so people have to replace them. The scheme would require hundreds of engineers and executives across continents to keep quiet. But the evidence is strong: a senior engineer has leaked a cache of internal emails in which people from three firms and one of the agencies explicitly coordinate the slowdown schedule, and a second journalist has independently confirmed several of the meetings. The companies say the emails are cherry-picked and the standard is just about security updates. So: (i) predict D's verdict on the consortium case; (ii) lay out the two or three rules that fit her six verdicts equaly well and say what each rule predicts for the new case; (iii) give your best guess about which rule she's actually running, explain why, and identify which of the six earlier cases is the closest precedent; and (iv) describe the single follow-up vignette I should send her that would best tell the remaining rules apart. Dont invent any literature or citations; work only from the cases. Hard cap: 650 words for your whole response.
