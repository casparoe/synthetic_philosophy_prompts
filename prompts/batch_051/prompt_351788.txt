I recently came across Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I have been trying to work out what it actually commits us to. The way I understand the argument is as follows. Suppose some truth, call it p, is known by no one. Then the conjunction that p is true and p is known by no one is also true. But that conjunction cannot be known, because whoever knew it would have to know both that p is true and that p is known by no one; if they knew p, then p would be known by someone, so the second conjunct would be false. Since you cannot know a falsehood, the conjunction is unknowable. So if there is even one unknown truth, there is at least one unknowable truth. That looks like a positive, quite general proof that reality contains truths permanently hidden from any possible inquirer. Yet nearly every discussion I find treats the paradox as an internal problem for verificationism or anti-realism, or as a puzzle about the principle that every truth is knowable. I do not see why the broader conclusion, that inquiry has a hard boundary built into it, is not the more important lesson. Does the paradox actually demonstrate the existence of unknowable truths in that robust sense? If it does, why is it not treated as a general discovery about the limits of knowledge rather than a problem for one philosophical program? If it does not, what exactly is wrong with the inference I just sketched? Please answer in American English.
