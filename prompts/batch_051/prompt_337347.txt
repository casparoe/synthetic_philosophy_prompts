I cover science and technology policy for a general-audience magazine, and I'm writing a piece on how an advisory panel's individual rulings reveal its working version of the precautionary principle. Its recent record: (1) it restricted a halogenated flame retardant in furniture foam after rodent studies showed thyroid disruption at high doses and biomonitoring detected low human exposure, despite no epidemiological data; (2) it declined to restrict a widely used synthetic sweetener, finding no plausible mechanism of harm at permitted intake and no adverse animal findings, and asked only for periodic intake modeling; (3) it approved a compostable biopolymer for dry-food contact only, after in vitro assays suggested breakdown products might be weakly estrogenic but no animal or human data existed; (4) it declined to halt an amine-based carbon-capture solvent whose degradation products might form nitrosamines, instead requiring emissions controls, monitoring, and scheduled review. The panel now considers a nanoscale metal-oxide UV filter for sunscreens: cell-culture studies show oxidative stress only at high concentrations, there are no animal or human studies at realistic exposure, dermal penetration is unresolved, and non-nano alternatives exist but cost more. What should it decide to stay consistent with its own record? Identify the closest precedent, spell out the competing precautionary principles that would produce each outcome if the record underdetermines the choice, say which principle best captures the panel's past rulings and why, and name a further case that would most cleanly separate them.
