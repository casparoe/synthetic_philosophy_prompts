I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I recently went down a rabbit hole reading about cultural evolution after working with evolutionary algorithms. A question I keep coming back to is whether the word "meme" picks out a real unit of culture that undergoes natural selection, or whether it is just a vivid metaphor borrowed from genetics. I sketched an argument for the metaphor reading, but I have a nagging suspicion that it pulls a sleight of hand somewhere, and I can't locate it precisely. I'd like you to audit the argument rigorously.

In evolutionary theory, natural selection is standardly said to require three conditions: variation among individuals in a population, differential survival or reproduction linked to that variation, and heritability, meaning that offspring resemble their parents more than they resemble a random member of the population. With that in mind, here is the argument:

(1) If memes really evolve by natural selection, cultural variants must satisfy the heritability condition in the same high-fidelity, template-copying way that genes do.
(2) Genes are copied by a concrete templating mechanism, so copying errors are relatively rare and the parent's variant is preserved nearly intact.
(3) Cultural transmission, by contrast, is reconstructive rather than template-copying: a person retelling a story, humming a tune, or passing along an unspoken team convention infers an underlying pattern and re-expresses it in their own way.
(4) Because each learner reconstructs rather than copies, small differences creep in at every transmission, and two learners rarely converge on exactly the same variant.
(5) Therefore most cultural variants experience too much transmission noise to sustain the kind of stable inheritance that natural selection requires.
(6) So cultural change is not genuinely Darwinian, and "meme" is at most a loose metaphor.

I suspect the weak point is step (1): it may be smuggling in a definition by saying heritability must work "in the same high-fidelity, template-copying way that genes do," when heritability is really just a statistical matter of parent–offspring correlation. But I also worry about step (4), since some cultural items, like a fork of a code repository or a widely sung national anthem, do seem to be transmitted with very high fidelity. Is the argument valid as written? If it is valid, should we accept the conclusion? If it is not, identify the first step that fails, explain exactly why it fails, and give the strongest corrected reading of that step under which the argument still does substantial work.
