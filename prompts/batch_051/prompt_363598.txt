I'm teaching Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" next week and I want to hang a whole unit on it: how values combine, additive vs multiplicative vs lexical. My students already get the additive picture (just sum everyone's welfare) and they can sort of mouth the lexical one (some things can't be traded, no matter the numbers). What I can't quite do is use the story to show where these views actually pull apart. So: read the story through the three lenses and tell me which one its own logic commits to, and where the text resists each. In additive terms the city is a no-brainer win, and yet everyone who reads it feels the force of the objection — is that just a cognitive bias, or does the story earn the lexical conclusion that one child's misery cant be bought out by aggregate joy? Or is something else going on, something like multiplicative contamination, where the citizens' happiness is worth less, maybe nothing, because it exists only on top of that suffering (consciousness times happiness, but in reverse)? Point me to the concrete beats doing the philosophical work: the child's room, the visit all adolescents make and the explanation they're given, the ones who walk away. And be honest about whether Omelas plays fair with the additive view or quietly rigs it from the start — how would a smart additive theorist push back without sounding like a monster? I want the reading to show what the story does that a dry decision-theory treatment misses, not just a label.
