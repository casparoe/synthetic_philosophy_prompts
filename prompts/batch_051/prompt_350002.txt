I have spent the last while constructing and solving Bongard problems: each puzzle displays twelve small panels, six on the left all satisfying a single rule, six on the right all failing it, and the solver must infer and state the rule. The convention among constructors is that the right-hand panels are not an afterthought. A problem is considered well-posed only when the negative examples are near-misses, differing from the positives in just the relevant respect, so that the rule is defined as much by what it excludes as by what it includes. Effectively, the format treats a concept as something fully specified only once its boundary has been drawn against adjacent non-instances.

That convention sits oddly with ordinary concept learning. People routinely acquire new concepts, such as a style of argument, a genre, a personality type, or an everyday category, from a few positive exposures, sometimes a single one, without being shown a curated battery of near-misses. I doubt I could produce six minimal counterexamples for most concepts I actually use, yet I still count myself as possessing those concepts. What view of concepts, or of what it is to grasp a concept, does the Bongard convention presuppose? Reconstruct that implicit theory as sympathetically as you can. Is it a true claim about human conceptual competence, or only about this artificial genre of puzzle? If the format is right that negative instances carry information positive instances cannot, spell out exactly what the near-miss contributes. If it is only a display convention, say what that convention reveals or hides about how concepts are in fact learned. Finally, what would a positive-example-only practice of teaching concepts have to presuppose in order to be viable?
