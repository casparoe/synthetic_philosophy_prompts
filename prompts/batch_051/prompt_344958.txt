I've been going back and forth with a friend who insists there are real moral dilemmas — situations where, no matter what you do, you've done something genuinely wrong, not just something hard or sad. I'm inclined to think that's confused, and we ended up looking at Kant's Metaphysics of Morals (1797). In the introduction to the Doctrine of Right he makes this point, though I'm paraphrasing since I don't have the exact words in front of me: a collision of duties is really unthinkable, because if two obligations truly bound the same agent at the same time, the moral law would be at odds with itself. At most one of the apparent duties can be the actual duty, and the other must be something weaker, like a strong pull or a ground that doesn't amount to a full obligation. I like the idea, but I don't want to just lean on Kant's authority, since I know some people think his system is rigged to rule out dilemmas from the start. Please argue that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, using the Kant point as a starting place but filling in modern moves about obligations being pro tanto vs. all-things-considered, and about how the concept of obligation itself might rule out irreconcilable requirements. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
