I am a novelist currently sketching a book with a branching structure, and I have been rereading Borges's story "The Garden of Forking Paths" to think through a distinction that keeps troubling me: the difference between what is possible and what is probable. In the story, a spy discovers that his ancestor's labyrinth is also a novel—and perhaps a model of time—in which every possible future forks off and all of the paths somehow happen. It has always seemed to me that possibility and probability do different work: the first tells us which courses of events could be reached from here, while the second tells us how likely each one is. Borges, though, seems interested almost entirely in the first and almost not at all in the second, multiplying possibilities without ever assigning them weight. I am trying to work out whether that silence is a deliberate philosophical stance or a limitation of the story's conceit.

Could you read the story through this lens with me? In particular, I would like to know where the story seems to blur possibility into probability, and where it quietly reintroduces probabilistic reasoning—or at least a differential weighting of paths—despite its apparent commitment to the equal reality of every branch; for instance, in the spy's tactical calculations, in the narrowing pressure of pursuit, or in the apparent accident that brings him to the man who has solved the riddle of the labyrinth. Which scenes would you point to as the ones most illuminated by this distinction, and where does the story resist it, so that an honest critic would have to admit the reading is partial? I am less interested in a verdict about what Borges meant than in what the story adds to the philosophical problem: how fiction can make vivid, or collapse, a difference that an essay on modality and probability can only state.
