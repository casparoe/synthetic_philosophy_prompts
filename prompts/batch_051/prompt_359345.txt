I am a doctoral candidate in early Chinese philosophy, and I would be grateful for a careful assessment of the following. A colleague in my department, whose work is in comparative ethics, has drafted a paper around a passage from the *Zhuangzi*, chapter 18, “Perfect Enjoyment,” in James Legge’s 1891 translation:

> When Zhuangzi’s wife died, Huizi went to condole with him, and, finding him squatted on the ground, drumming on the basin, and singing, said to him, “When a wife has lived with her husband, and brought up children, and then dies in her old age, not to wail for her is enough. When you go on to drum on this basin and sing, is it not an excessive (and strange) demonstration?” Zhuangzi replied, “It is not so. When she first died, was it possible for me to be singular and not affected by the event? But I reflected on the commencement of her being. She had not yet been born to life; not only had she no life, but she had no bodily form; not only had she no bodily form, but she had no breath. During the intermingling of the waste and dark chaos, there ensued a change, and there was breath; another change, and there was the bodily form; another change, and there came birth and life. There is now a change again, and she is dead. The relation between these things is like the procession of the four seasons from spring to autumn, from winter to summer.”

The colleague’s summary of what the passage shows runs as follows. Zhuangzi’s argument is that death is not a loss at all, because before a person comes to life she already lacks life, bodily form, and even breath; birth and death are simply successive transformations of qi, exactly as the seasons proceed. On this account, grief for the dead is rooted in the mistaken assumption that there was a stable self to be lost. Once we correct that assumption, grief has no proper object, and the wise response to bereavement is to treat death as another seasonal change and refrain from mourning.

I want to know whether this is a fair summary of the passage, and whether it can legitimately be extended to Zhuangzi’s wider view of death. Which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and what would a defender of a more nuanced reading say? Does the passage actually license the claims that death is not a loss, that grief has no object, and that one should withhold mourning? Please answer in British English.
