You are a social epistemologist who works on how individual rationality composes into collective judgment. I'm putting together a graduate seminar on information cascades and rational herding, and before the class reads any of the standard models, I want a specification of what an adequate theory of such phenomena must deliver — not a review of who said what. Begin by thinking through two or three canonical cases, such as the queue outside a restaurant, a bank run, and sequential voting on a jury, so that your list emerges from the phenomena rather than from memory of the literature.

Then write the desiderata that a theory of rational herding must meet: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse (for instance, between a rational cascade and mere peer pressure, or between a fragile cascade and a stable convention), the consistency constraints it inherits from Bayesian decision theory, and the stylized facts from laboratory and natural settings it has to accommodate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out candidate theories, and flag any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied — I have in mind something like the tension between requiring agents to be individually Bayesian given only observed actions and requiring the group eventually to converge to the true state; if that conflict is real, or if a subtler one is, spell out exactly where the inconsistency lies. Finally, be self-critical: identify which of your requirements you are least confident is a genuine desideratum rather than an artifact of a particular modeling tradition, what evidence or argument would lead you to demote it, and which plausible desideratum you considered but excluded — and what would make you reinstate it.
