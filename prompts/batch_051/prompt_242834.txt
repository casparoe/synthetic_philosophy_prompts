I am a PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, working on how scientific and technical communities set up feedback loops. I have been turning over a familiar argument about why some fields progress faster than others, and I now think a development in machine learning may break it—or force a more careful restatement. I would like a thorough adjudication.

The familiar argument runs as follows. Fields in which mistakes are punished quickly, unambiguously, and in a way traceable to a particular decision tend to correct themselves and accumulate knowledge: the bridge collapses, the plane crashes, the chess game is lost. Fields in which feedback is slow, noisy, or post hoc—macroeconomics, much of education policy, parts of clinical psychology—are said to progress slowly because bad ideas can survive for decades. For the argument to work, the field's feedback signal needs at least three features: it must arrive fast enough to discipline individual decisions; it must track the property the field actually cares about rather than a convenient proxy; and it must be difficult to game or redefine without fixing the underlying problem.

The development that unsettles me is modern benchmark-driven machine learning. It should be the purest success case for the feedback argument. Feedback is nearly instantaneous, quantitative, and legible: a loss curve moves in milliseconds, and a leaderboard updates as soon as a new model is submitted. Yet there is a serious and widely shared worry that progress on benchmarks has partly decoupled from progress on the underlying abilities. Models saturate leaderboards while remaining brittle under small distribution shifts; scores can be inflated by test-set contamination, overfitting, or carefully chosen baselines; and precise optimization of a training objective sometimes yields models that look strong on held-out evaluations yet fail in deployment in ways no one predicted. This is the generalized Goodhart problem: a measure that is made the target stops being a good measure. If the feedback argument were correct in its simple form, machine learning should be the exemplary progressive field; many now read it as a cautionary tale about optimizing proxies.

My question is whether the feedback argument survives this, needs repair, or is dead. Be precise about which premise the benchmark case actually threatens—speed, validity, or resistance to gaming—and say whether the argument can be restated in a version that separates tight feedback from truthful feedback. Where you can, test the repaired version against other fields that run on proxy measures, such as standardized testing, citation metrics, or quarterly earnings. No citations are expected; the question turns on the structure of the argument.

Please write the response in five titled sections, in this order:

1. The Original Argument (roughly 150–200 words): reconstruct the argument and make explicit the premises on which it depends.
2. The Benchmark Counterexample (roughly 100–150 words): formulate the development as a precise challenge.
3. Which Premise Is Under Threat (roughly 100–150 words): identify the premise the development bears on and explain why.
4. Repair or Abandon (roughly 250–350 words): give the strongest case for repairing the argument, test the repaired version against the other proxy-driven fields mentioned, and note the strongest case for abandoning it instead.
5. Verdict (roughly 80–120 words): state in plain terms whether the argument survives, is transformed, or fails.
