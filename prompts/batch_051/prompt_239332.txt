I'm a political philosophy PhD student and I want to close out a graduate seminar on egalitarian justice with a diagnostic instrument rather than another prose handout: a sorting quiz or decision tree that assigns a reader to a position within, or just outside, the luck egalitarian family on the basis of what they say about cases, never on which label they recognize or prefer. The leaves I care about are Dworkin-style equality of resources, Arneson-type equal opportunity for welfare, Cohen-type equal access to advantage, and at least one exit leaf for Anderson-style relational egalitarianism, since several of my students appear to hold the relational view while calling themselves luck egalitarians and I want the instrument to catch exactly that. Please construct the instrument from the logical space rather than from the list of names: first determine the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the named positions in that space, then diagnose the unoccupied regions.

Each branch node should be a concrete case or claim a seminar participant can answer without knowing the literature — for example, a chronic gambler who loses his stakes, a person born with a severe but compensable disability, an uninsured careful driver injured by a falling tree, an adult who cultivates an unproductive expensive taste, two agents with the same gamble and different risk attitudes, and a victim of discrimination whose earnings never recover. The splits should track the distinctions on which these positions genuinely come apart: whether option luck and brute luck are treated asymmetrically, whether the currency is resources, welfare, or access to advantage, whether compensation is owed ex post on the basis of actual outcomes or ex ante on the basis of expected value or hypothetical insurance, whether bad brute luck grounds a distributive claim in the first place or whether the underlying egalitarian concern is relational. For each combination of answers no leaf occupies, say in a sentence whether the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or only unexplored, and flag explicitly any pair of positions the tree or scoring key cannot separate.

I want a committed instrument, not a menu of possible trees. Please commit to the actual questions, their order, the routing or scoring rules, and argue for why those splits and that ordering do the best sorting work for this audience, even where a different tree could be defended; where you have chosen to collapse or distinguish positions at the cost of fine detail, say what the cost is and why you accept it.
