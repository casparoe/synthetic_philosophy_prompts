I spent my career in ecology and evolutionary biology, mostly on signalling and mimicry, and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously. I came across Dennett's argument about counterfeit people — the idea that the real danger from LLMs isn't that they'll become superintelligent, but that they make it cheap to generate fake persons, language and all, that pass as human well enough to manipulate us, and that a flood of these fakes corrodes the trust that lets a society function. Every part of that sounded familiar, because I spent decades thinking about Batesian mimicry and brood parasites. A harmless hoverfly copies the colours of a stinging wasp and gets left alone by predators that learned the colours mean pain. A cuckoo chick fakes the cues that say "invest in me," and the host feeds it at the expense of its own young.

So here's the parallel I keep turning over. In all three cases you have a receiver making a high-stakes classification on the basis of a cue (dangerous versus safe, mine versus not-mine, person versus non-person); a genuine sender that actually has the property behind the cue; a counterfeit that copies the cue without the property; a receiver whose automatic response works well at the population level but is exploitable at the individual level; and the worry that once counterfeits become cheap and common, the cue loses its value and honest signalers pay the price. Dennett's claim that we'll lose the ability to trust the "person" signal looks, to a biologist, exactly like the standard result that Batesian mimicry collapses when mimics get too numerous and the warning signal stops paying for the models.

But I know how easy it is for a biologist to see mimicry everywhere and call it insight. Is this a real identity of structure, or is there something more then a shared metaphor of fake signals?
