I'm a novelist—mostly literary fiction about small communities—and I've rewritten one scene maybe a dozen times because the argument at it's center won't hold still. The novel is set on a small, prosperous island town, and a packed, barely-seaworthy boat of refugees goes down just outside the harbor. At the town meeting, my protagonist, a schoolteacher, argues they have to take the survivors in. Her big line is "we're all in the same boat." Her main opponent, a retired doctor who's read a bunch of lifeboat-ethics stuff, says that's exactly backwards: the island is the lifeboat, the survivors are in the water, and if you pull everyone aboard the boat swamps and everybody drowns. The scene doesn't work because I can't tell what her slogan is actually claiming, and I don't want to write it as either a cheap win or a straw man.

So what are the distinct precise claims that "we're all in the same boat" could be making? I can half-see a few—a factual claim that everyone's fates are linked, a claim that no one earned their spot, a claim that the line between the boat and the water is just luck—but I can't tell how far each one reaches or which of them her argument actually needs. Lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts: which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or loaded, and which reading would genuinely get the schoolteacher to the conclusion that the town is obligated to take the survivors in. Then, since this is the part that's killing me: which reading is the doctor attacking when he says it's a lifeboat and not a shared boat, and what would she have to mean by the slogan for it to survive that reply—if any reading can.
