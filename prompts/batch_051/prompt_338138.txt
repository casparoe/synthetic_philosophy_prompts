A friend in my phil-mind/physics crowd keeps pushing this argument, and it's starting to make me nervous because the conclusion is so much stronger than it needs to be. It's meant to deflate the idea that quantum indeterminacy in the brain could rescue libertarian free will, but it seems to rule out any undetermined choice whatever. I can't find the exit. He runs it like this:

(1) Suppose a decision is genuinely undetermined: with the entire past and all the laws fixed, the agent could do either A or B at t.
(2) So there are two possible worlds, exactly alike in past and laws up to t, where in one she does A at t and in the other she does B at t.
(3) Up to t those worlds are identical in every fact about her — beliefs, desires, values, intentions, effort, everything.
(4) Therefore nothing about the agent makes it the case that A rather than B happens.
(5) The only difference between the worlds is a bare unexplained difference at t. But that's exactly what "chance" or "randomness" is.
(6) If whether A or B happens is a matter of chance, the agent doesn't control which happens.
(7) Responsibility requires that she control whether she does the act.
(8) So an undetermined "choice" isn't one she's responsible for. Indeterminism gives randomness, not freedom.
(9) And in a deterministic world free will fails anyway since we couldn't do otherwise. So free will is impossible full stop.

I'd like to keep some version of the idea that indeterminism might at least in principle help, or that libertarianism isn't just confused. I also lean incompatibilist, so don't tell me the fix is to reject (9) and go compatibilist; I want to know whether the indeterministic horn is really as bad as he says. Which step is doing the heavy lifting, and what does rejecting it cost?
