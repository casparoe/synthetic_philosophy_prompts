Here's a thing I keep coming back to. The 2023 revision of DoD Directive 3000.09, "Autonomy in Weapon Systems," says: "Autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems will be designed to allow commanders and operators to exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force." When I read that I immediately thought of the 2003 Patriot friendly-fire incidents in Iraq. In the opening weeks of the invasion a Patriot battery running in automatic mode shot down a British Tornado GR4 coming back to base, killing both crew; a few days later a Patriot engagement killed a U.S. Navy F/A-18C pilot. The investigations kept landing on automation bias: operators had learned to trust the system's classification calls, and in the moments that mattered nobody interupted the loop.

I want you to use the Patriot incidents as a stress test of that "appropriate levels of human judgment" sentence. Not the legal-technical question of what the directive formally required—the policy came later, so this is retrospective—but this: if the standard had applied, what would it have demanded, of the weapon's designers, the training, the commanders, and the operators in those moments? Then push the other way: what does the episode do to the standard? Does it show "appropriate" is doing real normative work, or that it's a flexible placeholder that would have failed exactly where it was needed? Does the case show the standard is too vague, or is the vagueness the point—that "appropriate" has to stretch across systems, domains, and tempos?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then walk me through the reasoning. Don't restate the setup; just start with the verdict.
