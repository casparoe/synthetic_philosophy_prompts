I run a reading group whose members mostly come from the rationalist and AI-safety world; they are at ease with predictive processing, free energy, and the claim that the brain is doing Bayesian inference over hidden states. In one ninety-minute session next month, I want them to understand what Merleau-Ponty means when he says the lived body has motor intentionality, and to see that this is a claim about the structure of experience rather than a loose way of talking about subpersonal motor control. I do not need the explanation itself; I need the design of the session. What idea has to be in place before the phrase finds any foothold, and what depends on what in the order of presentation? Which one example should carry the whole thing — the blind man's cane, the typist, the phantom limb, or something you prefer — and what must that example have, and not have, if it is to do the work? This audience will predictably try to translate the idea into things it already accepts: 'it is really a predictive model of the body', 'it is just unconscious inference', 'it is procedural memory', 'it is what the free-energy people call active inference'. At which point in the sequence should each of these translations be anticipated and dissolved, rather than argued with after it has hardened? What should I explicitly decline to reduce the concept to, at this level and in this room? And what one question can I ask at the end to establish that someone has acquired the phenomenological distinction rather than merely learned to reuse the vocabulary?
