John Stuart Mill, in chapter 5 of *Utilitarianism*, considers the common objection that justice must be something more than expediency because it presents itself with the force of a natural sentiment. He writes:

“In the case of this, as of our other moral sentiments, there is no necessary connection between the question of its origin, and that of its binding force. That a feeling is bestowed on us by Nature, does not necessarily legitimate all its promptings.”

Suppose a country takes this thought to its institutional conclusion. Its new constitution forbids courts and administrators from treating rights as independent side constraints. A plaintiff may not merely allege that a right has been violated; she must show that the offending action caused a net loss of expected aggregate welfare, and that the rule she invokes is itself welfare-justified. The constitution defines “a right” as nothing more than a specially protected expectation whose protection is warranted by general utility.

Predict, with reasoning, how this legal and political system looks after fifty years. Which areas of law prove easiest to administer under the welfare-only rule, and which generate the strongest pressure to reintroduce right-talk? How do judges justify decisions in hard cases involving torture, property expropriation, or minority religious practice? Do vulnerable groups end up better or worse protected than under a side-constraint regime? Where does Mill’s distinction between the sentiment of justice and the criterion of justice do real institutional design work, and where does it break down? I want the mechanisms, incentives, and constraints spelled out, not a story, and I want you to mark which predictions are robust and which depend on contingent factors such as judicial culture, inequality, or the quality of administrative data.
