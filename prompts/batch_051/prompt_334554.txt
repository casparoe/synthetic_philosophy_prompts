A friend spent most of dinner trying to convince me that Bostrom's simulation argument should make longtermists way less fanatical about the far future. His line was: if the trilemma is right — either almost no civilization like ours reaches a posthuman stage, or posthuman civilizations almost never run ancestor simulations, or we're almost certainly simulated — then in that last case the enormous future our actions are supposed to shape is just whatever the simulators choose to run; they can pause it, delete it, or re-run it with tweaks. So all that far-future expected value looks like its aimed at a world we can't actually reach with our choices. I couldn't put my finger on why that felt too quick.

Please give me the strongest argument you can for the opposite view: that the simulation argument doesn't dampen fanaticism about the far future, and in fact sharpens or intensifies it. But I don't want the mushy everything-might-be-a-simulation-so-anything-goes version. Try to say something precise about where the expected value lives and what levers we actually have. One distinction I've been toying with is the future of our world versus the future of our values: if we're simulated, our causal reach into our own far future might be tiny, but what fanaticism cares about is whether our choices now make it more likely that enormous amounts of value exist later, somewhere down or up the cascade of simulations. See whether that distinction helps, and then hit the obvious objection: if simulators control the future anyway, why should our actions be optimized for long-run outcomes rather than for whatever keeps this particular simulation from being switched off?
