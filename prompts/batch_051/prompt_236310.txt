I’m a PhD student in political theory, and I’ve got an arguement I put together from Kant’s Perpetual Peace that now strikes me as too strong, because it seems to force Kant into a position he explicitly rejects. I’d like someone to go through it step by step and tell me where it goes wrong, or which premise to abandon and what that costs.

Here’s the argument:

1. Perpetual peace requires states to exit the state of nature with one another, since in the state of nature every peace is just a temporary truce; each state remains the judge of what its own rights require, so no dispute is ever settled in a binding way.
2. Leaving the state of nature requires entering a rightful condition, which according to Kant means there must be public coercive law: standing laws that are knowable, an authority that adjudicates according to them, and power to enforce the outcome.
3. A voluntary league of states, even a permanent congress to negotiate disputes, lacks that authority. Whatever agreements it reaches are still interpreted and backed by each member’s own power, so the condition is still one of legal self-help.
4. So a league of nations can at best be a provisional modus vivendi, not a condition in which peace can be perpetual. It might last a long time, but its stability is still grounded in prudence and fear, not in right.
5. The only arrangement that satisfies (2) and (3) for states is a world republic: a single state under which all peoples are united with one common coercive sovereign.
6. But Kant refuses that conclusion. He says a world state is either ungovernable or would collapse into a despotism, and instead recommends a federation of free states as a kind of surrogate, one that presumably can exist without a common superior.
7. Therefore either Kant’s federation is not genuinely capable of securing perpetual peace, or the argument from the state of nature to the necessity of coercive public law does not transfer from individuals to states. If the latter, then the whole analogy to the state of nature is doing less work than Kant needs it to do.

I hope the flaw is in premise 3 or 5, because I’d like to keep Kant’s anti-world-state instincts. But I can’t yet say which step fails and whether it fails in a way that leaves the federation with more than just prudential force. Could you diagnose it?
