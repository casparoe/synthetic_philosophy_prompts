I work as a software engineer, mostly on systems that predict events and act on those predictions, and I have no formal background in philosophy. Lately I've been building a small decision-making module, and I've run into something that feels like a bug in my own thinking. The position I'm drawn to is this: an ideally rational agent should be able to assign a probability to any proposition it can formulate, including propositions about its own future actions. "I will choose option A rather than option B" is just another proposition about the world, and a decision rule can treat it like any other uncertain variable. This has the virtues that attract me to clean engineering solutions: it is unified, it lets me use the same machinery for modelling other agents, planning, and self-prediction, and it avoids the awkward conclusion that some propositions about myself are off-limits to my own belief state. If an agent's beliefs are a probability distribution over a state space, its action variable should simply be another variable in that space.

But the intuition that keeps bothering me is this. When I am genuinely undecided — actually deliberating between, say, working late on a side project and getting an early night — I cannot seem to assign a probability to "I will choose to work late" without it collapsing into one of two non-deliberative moves. Either I read the probability as my current leaning, in which case assigning 0.7 is basically deciding "I'll probably work late" and reports a verdict rather than guiding inquiry; or I read it as a prediction of what I will conclude after further deliberation, in which case I am treating my own future choice as an external fact that I am discovering rather than a decision I am making. If I take the number seriously as a prediction, I seem to be trying to outrun my own deliberation; if I take it as a leaning, I have ended deliberation. No assignment feels like the sort of thing a rational belief state should contain about a choice that is still open — and yet the Bayesian picture says such a credence must exist.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who specialises in decision theory and formal epistemology. Begin by brainstorming the candidate readings and resolutions, and steelman both sides before you adjudicate. I want a negotiated verdict rather than a picked side: spell out what it would cost to restrict the domain of my credences while I deliberate, what it would cost to debunk or explain away the intuition (for instance as a confusion about levels, or about time, or a slide between the observer's standpoint and the deliberator's standpoint), and whether there is a reading on which the two are compatible after all. Write the entire answer in British English.
