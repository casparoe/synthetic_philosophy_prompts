I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, so I approach this through habits I trust: specify the system, list the outcomes and their probabilities and costs, and ship the option with the best expected value, scrutinising particularly hard any change with no rollback. Applied to ecosystems, that produces a view I find hard to fault: if an intervention such as a gene drive — an engineered genetic modification designed to propagate through a wild population and suppress it or change its traits — or a de-extinction effort to restore a vanished species' ecological role, is expected on present evidence to reduce suffering or improve ecological integrity on balance, then the fact that it is permanent should be priced as a very large expected-value term, not treated as an automatic prohibition. On this view, irreversible simply means the failure modes must be modelled with unusual care, and the decision can in principle still be justified.

Yet I have an intuition I cannot talk myself out of. When I actually imagine releasing a self-propagating germline change into a wild species — no off switch, no rollback, no future point at which we can re-audit the choice in light of what the ecosystem has become — it strikes me as different in kind from normal environmental management, not merely riskier in degree. The feeling that the calculation happens to come out positive does not dissolve the sense that we would be taking up the wrong relation to a self-reproducing system, acting as its redesigners rather than its careful operators.

I am not asking which side wins. Please present both positions at their strongest and then work toward a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, for example by unmasking it as status-quo bias, neophobia, or a secularised playing-God reflex that should be dropped once the expected-value calculation is done honestly? What would it take for the consequentialist decision procedure to be amended or abandoned, for instance by adding a side constraint against irreversible, self-propagating changes that bind future generations to our mistakes? And is there a reading on which both survive — perhaps one that distinguishes operating within a system from redesigning the system itself, or that treats irreversibility as something that cannot be fully priced because it changes who will be doing the future evaluating? Tell me how I would recognise, in a concretely specified case, which move is justified. Please write the response in British English.
