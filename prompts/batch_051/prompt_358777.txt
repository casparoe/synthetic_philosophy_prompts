I'm hosting a small student philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending a minority take on Kavka's toxin puzzle. Instead of the usual line that you can't intend to drink the toxin because you know you won't drink it, she argues that the right response to the puzzle is to simply form the intention to drink, and that you can do this without self-deception, hypnosis, or side-bets. Her preferred way of putting it is that intending is a mental act subject to its own norms, and the standard objection treats intentions as if they had to track your beliefs about what you'll actually end up doing. She thinks the puzzle mostly reveals a flaw in evaluating an intention's rationality solely by the rationality of the act it would produce.

Could you draft eight to ten intervew questions that get past the headline view? The audience will have read Kavka's original example but not the secondary literature. I want at least one question each on the following, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: (1) the worry that intending to drink while believing you won't drink isn't just unusual but internally incoherent; (2) the accusation that she is confusing deciding to intend with actually intending, or confusing an intention with a mere wish; (3) the reductio that her view would let a rational agent intend almost anything if the reward for having the intention were large enough; and (4) the concern that once she grants intentions have their own norms, those norms collapse into just forming whichever mental state pays. I'd also like one closing question about what someone who disagrees with her about the toxin puzzle would have to be getting wrong about intention in general.
