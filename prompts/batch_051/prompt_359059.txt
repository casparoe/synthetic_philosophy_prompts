I'm a distributed systems engineer, mostly consensus protocols and replicated state machines—Raft, Paxos, PBFT, that sort of thing. Recently I got pulled into designing the governance process for a medium-sized open-source project, basically the rules for how RFCs get discussed and ratified. Me and a co-maintainer keep going in circles about what to do with people who are, in protocol terms, Byzantine: not honest mistakes but acting strategically, relitigating decisions that already got consensus, flooding the discussion to delay a vote. My co-maintainer wants strict timeouts and a small set of trusted maintainers who can cut debate; I keep thinking that's just reintroducing the thing we're trying to avoid.

A friend told me this debate is basically what Habermas was getting at with the ideal speech situation and communicative rationality: roughly, the idea that a norm or claim is rational to accept only if it could win agreement in a discussion where everyone affected can participate, anyone can raise or challenge any point, nobody is dominated or coerced, and the only thing doing the convincing is the force of the better argument, with everyone aimed at understanding rather than at winning. Habermas treats this as a counterfactual idealization rather than a design spec, but the likeness to a consensus protocol is obvious to me.

I'd like you to restate Habermas's ideal speech situation and communicative rationality in my vocabulary—safety and liveness, Byzantine and non-Byzantine faults, quorums, validator sets, finality, timeouts, equivocation, partition tolerance—as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no expression at all in protocol terms. And tell me whether the bits that don't translate are a sign that my vocabulary is missing something Habermas is right about, or that his picture is looser than it looks once you actually have to implement it. I don't want to loose the philosophical point just because I'm reaching for liveness arguments.
