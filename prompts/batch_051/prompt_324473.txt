Since I retired from the lab I've been reading philosophy on the side, and last month two of my old colleagues pulled me into an argument I still can't get out of my head. It was about whether causation — bona fide, out-there-in-the-world causation — is something we should believe in, or just a way of talking we've hung onto. I'll reconstruct their exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) Maria: You don't find causation anywhere in the fundamental theories. The basic laws are differential equations that don't distinguish past from future; there's no cause operator in the Schrödinger equation. Bertrand Russell made this point long ago — he thought the notion of cause was a leftover from an earlier era, kept around because it's convenient, not because the world actually contains it. I'm starting to think he was right.

(2) Paul: That's a big leap. You wouldn't say heat is unreal just because "temperature" doesn't appear in the Schrödinger equation. Plenty of real things are higher-level; they only show up once you coarse-grain. Causation could be like that — a genuine macro-level pattern rather than a myth.

(3) Maria: Temperature has a reduction story. Statistical mechanics tells you exactly what temperature is and why it's legitimate to use. Causation has no such story. Nobody has shown how to recover causal arrows from the underlying equations — all you get are regularities. So causation isn't like temperature; it's closer to phlogiston.

(4) Paul: But there's a whole interventionist framework now — causal models where you intervene from outside the system and see what stays invariant — that gives causal claims a precise meaning. And it really works: those models make better predictions than raw correlation all the time. Good luck designing an experiment without some notion of intervention.

(5) Maria: That's exactly my point, though. Interventionist accounts are relative to a choice of what counts as the system and what counts as an intervention from outside. Those are modelling choices, not facts written into nature. So the framework is a useful lens for creatures like us, with limited information and a need to act. It doesn't license saying causation is a feature of the world.

(6) Paul: You could run the same deflationary argument on probability, or entropy — they're also tied to coarse descriptions and what we happen to know. Do you want to say entropy is just a lens? Because if causation is a relic, entropy is too, and that seems like a hard thing to live with.

(7) Maria: Maybe I'd accept that. Entropy may be a projection rather than a fundamental feature. But notice what you've already given me: you're defending causation as something we lay over the world, not something in the fundamental furniture. After that, the rest is a fight about which flattering word to use. You want to say real-but-nonfundamental; I'm comfortable saying not-real. I don't see a fact of the matter between those.

They seemed to be talking past each other a bit, but I suspect there's a real dispute buried in there. Could you go through move by move and say who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches or conflates things, and who you think ends up with the better overall position? I'd rather have the point-by-point assessment than a general verdict.
