I am an undergraduate currently taking a course on the philosophy of perception and epistemology, and we have just been working through Sellars’s attack on the given and some of the responses to it. I have to write a position statement, and I have arrived at a set of commitments I find myself wanting to hold all at once. I would like an audit of the set before I commit to it in writing. I am not looking for a survey of possible positions; please take a definite stand about where the pressure points are, which pair or larger subset does the most damage, and what I should revise first.

Here are the commitments, each of which I am currently inclined to endorse:

1. The given is a myth. There is no self-justifying stratum of experience or awareness that can serve as the foundation for empirical knowledge simply in virtue of being present to the mind. In particular, nothing with merely non-conceptual content can play that justificatory role.

2. Ordinary perceptual beliefs are nonetheless non-inferential. When I form the belief that there is a red mug on the desk in good light, that belief is justified by my experience even though I do not run through any argument from premises about how things look or what I sense.

3. Only states with conceptual or propositional structure can stand in rational justificatory relations to a belief. A state that cannot function as a premise or a reason cannot function as evidence.

4. Some non-language-using animals have perceptual knowledge of their immediate environment. My dog knows someone is at the door; she is not merely responding to a stimulus.

5. There are raw feels: non-conceptual qualitative aspects of experience that outrun what I can classify or articulate. I do not think these raw feels are themselves reasons for any belief; they are the sensory residue left over after the conceptual content of experience has done its epistemic work.

6. Being caused by the environment is not by itself enough to justify a perceptual belief. For a perceptual belief to be justified, the subject must have the ability to situate it in the space of reasons, for example by indicating what would count for or against it.

For each pair of commitments, and for the set as a whole, tell me whether the tension is real or merely apparent. Rank the real tensions by severity, say which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned first, and explain what that single revision would cost me with respect to the others. I want to know where my position actually breaks, not just that certain parts look precarious.
