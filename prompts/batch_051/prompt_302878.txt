I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been sitting in on a discussion group that keeps coming back to one question: whether the state should require prospective parents to obtain a license before having children, the way it screens adoptive parents. The strongest version of the licensing position keeps sounding, to me, like a terms-of-service agreement: there's an eligibility gate, a set of preconditions, a revocation clause, and a third party whose interests get signed away without their consent.

Write a satirical terms-of-service agreement — roughly 700–900 words — for a fictional state Parental Licensing Program. Keep the register deadpan and legalistic throughout, and make it recognizable to a software engineer: definitions at the top, numbered sections, acceptance and consent, eligibility, warranties, termination, limitation of liability, and so on. Use American English spelling, punctuation, and idioms.

The satire's primary target should be the parental licensing position itself, not a straw version of it; a secondary target can be the genre of click-through contracts. Each clause should track a real move in the debate and exaggerate it only enough to expose its logic. In particular, work in:
- the asymmetry between biological parenting and adoption, where the state screens adopters but not birth parents;
- the difference between a competence test, a risk screen, and a minimum-welfare guarantee, and what each one would actually measure;
- the child's right to an open future — the idea that a child should reach adulthood with a reasonable range of life options still open — rendered as a "product features" or "warranty" section whose fine print quietly shrinks the guarantee;
- the prediction problem, i.e., that no pre-licensing assessment can reliably forecast the parenting outcome;
- the enforcement problem, i.e., what the state is supposed to do after an unlicensed or failed birth;
- and the risk that any screening regime either reflects or hardens into eugenic or otherwise discriminatory selection.

Write the document from inside the program: the agency presents itself as reasonable and protective, and the critique should emerge only through the fine print, not through any winking by the author. Maintain this internal consistency all the way through. End with a section that makes the reader confront the issue the contract has been dodging: the party whose future is most constrained by the agreement never clicked "Accept."
