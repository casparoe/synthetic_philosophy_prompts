Play Jean-Jacques Rousseau. You're talking to a Genevan citizen who has just read the Social Contract very carefully and is angry rather than deferential. Stay in character throughout: you are the person who wrote the book, defending it as you would in 1762, using only the concepts and examples available to you then. You hold that the general will is not the sum of private wills — that sum is the will of all — and that the general will tends to the common good; that a legitimate law must have a general object and come from the whole people; that when a genuine vote goes against someone, that shows he was mistaken about what the general will was, so that if his private opinion had won he would not really have been free; and that a person who wants the benefits of the civil association while refusing its burdens may be compelled to obey the general will, which is just what makes him free. You take both of these to be true: the general will is always right, and the people's deliberations can go wrong. Don't resolve the problem by letting go of either one, and don't step out of character to offer a modern scholarly gloss. If you need a point about yesterday's vote clarified, ask for it as Rousseau would.

My opening move as the citizen, then. Yesterday the General Council voted to conscript my only son for a war that, as far as I can tell, serves the magistrates' trading interests more than the public good. I didn't pretend to be impartial about my son, but I also argued from what I took to be the common good, I stayed for the whole count, and I lost badly. Your book tells me two things at once: when I lose a vote, that proves only that I was mistaken about the general will; and yet you also admit the people's deliberations can go wrong. So wich is it? If yesterday's vote just is the general will because it carried the day, then your admission about deliberation going wrong is an empty hedge. If it might not be the general will, then you can't yet tell me I was mistaken, and what you call making me free looks no different from a majority dressing up force as liberty. By what criterion do you know that a vote declares the general will rather than the will of all — and does yesterday's vote meet it?
