A student in my philosophy of math class asked last week why the continuum hypothesis matters philosophically, and it got me thinking about a parallel I keep noticing between the continuum hypothesis and the sorites paradox. I can't decide if it's deep or superficial, and I'd like an honest verdict.

Here's the resemblance as I see it. Both seem to be, at bottom, claims that a certain boundary doesn't exist. The continuum hypothesis says there's no cardinality strictly between the countable and the continuum: no middle size. The sorites paradox seems to deny that there's any sharp cutoff number of grains between a heap and a non-heap. And both, when they get difficult, seem to get handled by relativizing to a choice of structure. ZFC has models in which CH holds and models in which it fails, while a vague predicate like heap has many admissible precisifications; on a supervaluationist treatment, the predicate has a determinate truth value only when all the precisifications agree.

So the parallel might predict that the independence of CH should push set theorists the same direction the sorites pushes philosophers of language, toward saying there is no fact of the matter about CH in any absolute sense, only facts relative to a model. But plenty of set theorists don't say that; they treat CH as having a definite truth value that the standard axioms just can't settle. Is that a sign the parallel is superficial, or is it just set theorists being more realist than the analogy requires? Maybe the real point is that both cases show how an unresolved question can be tamed by explicit sharpenings, and the only differnce is that we have rough agreement on which precisifications of heap are admissible but no agreement on which set-theoretic universes are intended. I keep going back and forth.

Does the analogy hold up, or does it collapse once you look at tolerance and induction on one side and cardinal arithmetic on the other?
