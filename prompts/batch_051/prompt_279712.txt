I've been going round in circles on a set of benefit cases, and I'd like you to find the principle behind my verdicts—or tell me which verdicts to drop. Here are my gut reactions.

A single mother on an out-of-work benefit misses a work-search appointment because her daughter got sent home sick from school; the system suspends her payment for four weeks. I think that's wrong.

A man on the same benefit turns up to his appointment drunk and tells the work coach to get lost; he gets the same four-week suspension. I think that's fine, maybe even lenient.

A woman claims a benefit as a lone parent, but her boyfriend actually lives with her most nights and contributes; she lies about it on the form. I think she's doing wrong, but I understand why.

A self-employed decorator forms a company and pays himself in dividends rather than salary so he qualifies for a benefit aimed at low-income families, all within the rules. I think he's doing something worse than the woman who lies about her boyfriend, even though he hasn't broken a single rule.

A man whose disability benefit depends on him not being able to walk more than 20 metres starts walking his dog for half an hour a day. He doesn't tell the department because his condition fluctuates and he's scared of losing the benefit and then getting worse again. I think that's wrong, but I'd probably do the same.

A pensioner with £80,000 in savings claims a benefit she's fully entitled to (it isn't means-tested) and saves every payment. I don't think she's doing anything wrong at all.

Halfway through writing this, I started to think my principle is something like: benefit should track need, and conditionality should only test that you're genuine, not punish bad luck or humiliate people. But I can't tell whether that principle gets all six, or whether it's just something I like the sound of.

Is there one principle—about desevingness, conditionality, rules, fraud, or something else—that captures all six, or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and which does the least damage to the whole picture? And what should your best principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a claimant takes a cash-in-hand cleaning job while still claiming, because she's saving the deposit to move somewhere cheaper and then stop claiming—wrong, not wrong, or in between? Please answer in British English.
