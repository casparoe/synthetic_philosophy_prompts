A writer in a popular intellectual-history magazine summarizes Weber's Protestant ethic thesis as follows. Weber set out to explain why modern capitalism arose in Northern Europe and not elsewhere. His answer begins with the Calvinist doctrine of predestination: since salvation was already settled, believers could not earn it, and the resulting anxiety made them seek reassurance that they were among the elect. The forms of ascetic Protestantism Weber studied closed off the old monastic and sacramental routes for relieving that anxiety, so believers turned to methodical worldly work. Success in a calling was read as a sign of election, though not a means to it; the believer therefore labored systematically, consumed little, and reinvested the surplus. What began as religious discipline gradually shed its religious content and survived as a secular ethos: work as a duty, profit as an end in itself, and the rational ordering of one's whole life. According to the article, Weber held that this ethos was the decisive cause of modern capitalism, and that its absence explains why Catholic and non-Protestant societies did not generate capitalism on their own. The article concludes that Weber thereby showed how values, in a strong sense, can be independent historical causes.

I cannot tell how much of this is fair. Separate what is accurate from what is overstatement, and point out what a careful defender of Weber's position would insist on qualifying. In particular, does the final conclusion about values as independent historical causes follow from an accurate reading of Weber, or does it depend on the article's overstatements?
