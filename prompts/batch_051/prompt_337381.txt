Causal Bayes nets represent causal structure as a directed acyclic graph over variables together with a joint probability distribution that factorizes according to the graph, with arrows read as direct causal dependence. The central inferential problem is recovering the graph from observational data. The causal Markov assumption says that d-separation relations in the graph imply corresponding conditional independences in the distribution; but many graphs can imply the same set of independences, which is why one speaks of a Markov equivalence class rather than a single graph. Two further assumptions are contested. Faithfulness says the distribution implies all and only the conditional independences entailed by the graph, so no independence holds unless the graph demands it; this is often glossed as ruling out exact path cancellations in which two causal routes happen to offset one another. Causal minimality is weaker: among Markovian graphs, prefer the one with no redundant edges, where an edge is redundant if removing it would not change the set of entailed independences. The debate is roughly this: strong-faithfulness proponents treat exact cancellation as a negligible, fine-tuned possibility and use the full set of independences to narrow the graph; minimality proponents think faithfulness demands too much and that the sparsest Markovian graph is the right default; critics reply that the data are compatible with unfaithful or otherwise non-minimal structures, that preferring sparse or faithful graphs is an unearned metaphysical bet about nature not arranging coincidences, and that what such methods track is convenience or economy rather than causation. The underlying question is why, if at all, a minimal Bayes net should be expected to track the true causal structure.
