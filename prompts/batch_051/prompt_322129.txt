I teach a course that spends a unit on the moral psychology of markets, and I keep getting stuck on the same difficulty. In the old debate over whether private acquisitiveness is a vice, an engine of prosperity, or both, the word greed slips between at least three jobs: a neutral description of wanting more than one has, a moral defect of unjust or callous acquisition, and a motivational engine whose aggregate effects are supposed to benefit everyone. I would like a working definition of greed that keeps these uses honest — or an explicit split of the concept, if you think no single definition can do all the work.

Please define greed (or its two or more senses, if you think the concept splits) so that it classifies these cases correctly: (a) a pharmaceutical company acquires the patent on a life-saving drug and raises its price tenfold because the market will bear it; (b) a child wants a second slice of cake despite not being hungry; (c) a landlord raises the rent each year to the highest figure the local market supports, knowing that some tenants will struggle to pay; (d) a person who has already saved more than they could plausibly need continues to take on work solely to watch the balance grow, though they intend to leave nearly all of it to charity; (e) a parent works extra shifts to buy a larger house than the family needs; (f) a politician wants ever more power and shows little interest in money; (g) a professional poker player, already far ahead for the evening, keeps playing because the thought of winning still more is irresistible. I am fairly sure (a) is squarely inside the extension and (b) is outside it; my intuitions on the rest are unstable, and I suspect they track a split between greed as excess and greed as acquisition that harms or disregards others.

Give the definition(s), run them through all seven cases, say explicitly where the definition delivers a verdict you did not expect at first glance, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Then identify which of your seven verdicts is least secure. End by saying whether the greed that functions as a vice and the greed that is supposed to function as an engine of prosperity can be the same thing, or whether the debate quietly needs two distinct concepts.
