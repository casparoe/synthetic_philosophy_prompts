I am a novelist, and I am drafting a character—a classical philologist in late middle age—who has spent her life defending the idea that knowledge is worth having for its own sake, not because it pays, not because it is useful, but because understanding is itself a good. I would like an original poem in her voice that I can place near the beginning of the novel, before the reader has learned what this conviction has cost her.

The poem should be a Petrarchan sonnet, fourteen lines, in which the octave gives a serious, non-slogan version of the intrinsic-value view: the thought, going back to Aristotle, that human beings desire to know by nature; that understanding completes the mind rather than merely serving some further end; that curiosity can be an intellectual virtue rather than a distraction or an indulgence. The volta should do real philosophical and emotional work. It should mark the point where this view meets its sharpest objection: the worry, familiar from the Augustinian critique of curiositas onward, that the desire to know for its own sake can curdle into a possessive, voyeuristic, or restless appetite without a proper limit; that some truths do damage simply by being chased; and that a life devoted to wanting to know can drift free of a life worth living. I do not want the poem to resolve this tension with a tidy moral, because the character herself has not resolved it. The rhyme and meter should be disciplined without being stiff, and the poem should read as something a serious person wrote in order to think, not as verse decor. Most importantly, someone who works on virtue epistemology or on the value of knowledge should find the ideas accurately drawn rather than name-dropped, while a reader who knows none of that should still find it a good poem.
