I keep coming back to a puzzle about the way the law treats people who are involved in a crime without doing the act themselves. Suppose a man waits in the car while his partner robs a bank, and the partner shoots and kills a guard inside. The driver never leaves the car, never agreed to any shooting, and the guard would have died even if a different driver had been hired that morning. Yet in many legal systems the driver can be convicted of murder, just as if he had pulled the trigger. That seems to need some explanation, because he neither caused the death nor wanted it. Now compare a shopkeeper who sells a knife to a customer who then uses it to kill someone. The shopkeeper is not charged with murder, and nearly everyone would think charging him would be absurd—even if he found the customer unsettling and half-suspected something. What view of complicity—of what it is to take part in a wrong—does a legal system presuppose when it draws the line between the getaway driver and the knife seller? Does the driver’s conviction presuppose that a person can become responsible for a harm simply by joining an enterprise, even when his own contribution makes no difference to that harm? If so, is that consistent with the same system’s ordinary instinct that we punish people for what they caused? I am not asking for a summary of specific legal tests or cases. I want to know what underlying theory of participation and responsibility the practice only makes sense under, which parts of the line are forced by that theory and which are optional, and where the practice simply gives out. Please answer in at most 700 words.
