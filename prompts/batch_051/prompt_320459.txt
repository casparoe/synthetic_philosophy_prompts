I clerk for a judge on a state intermediate appellate court, and I have been told to predict how one of my judge's colleagues, Judge Voss, will vote in a pending case. Her record on whether to apply statutory text literally is unusually instructive, and I have narrowed it to four opinions. In *Hoyt v. Department of Revenue*, she joined an opinion correcting a cross-reference in a tax exemption from section 41-108 to section 41-109, on the ground that the cited section concerned rural electric cooperatives while the heading, the surrounding subsections, and the revisor's note made it unmistakable that 41-109 was intended. In *State v. Corley*, she wrote the majority and applied a statute saying a second DUI conviction "shall" result in lifetime license revocation, even though the first conviction was twenty-seven years old and from another state; her opinion said the result was severe but the language and the legislature's arguable interest in lifetime revocation for repeat offenders were not for the court to second-guess. In *Alden v. County Assessor*, a property-tax appeal deadline fell on a Sunday, followed by a state holiday, and the taxpayer filed the next business day; she held the filing timely, writing that a deadline that runs out on days the law itself closes the assessor's office is self-defeating and cannot be what the legislature meant to enact. In *Marine Operators, LLC v. Harbors Commission*, she enforced a requirement that passenger manifests be filed "before departure" against an operator who filed after the vessel had returned safely and caused no harm, saying a bright-line rule designed to force pre-departure recordkeeping is a rational choice and the absence of harm in one case does not make the literal reading absurd.

The pending case is *Montvale Wind v. Public Utilities Commission*. The statute says the commission "shall revoke the certificate of any facility that fails to file its annual environmental-compliance report by March 1." Montvale filed at 11:58 p.m. on March 2. The report showed full compliance, and the delay was caused by an emergency repair to a monitoring well. The statute's own findings section states that the report enables the commission and the public to verify safe operation and to participate in rate proceedings, and the legislative record indicates the automatic-revocation clause was aimed at operators who went months without reporting and left the commission with no data. Montvale's opponents emphasize the word "shall"; Montvale argues that shutting down a compliant facility over a one-day, no-harm delay is exactly the kind of absurdity her prior opinions let courts avoid.

I do not need outside doctrine, citations, or a research memo — I need a careful read of the pattern in her own record.
