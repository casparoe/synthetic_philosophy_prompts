You are an expert on Gödel's incompleteness theorems and on the Lucas–Penrose debate over whether they show the human mind is not a Turing machine. Work through the following reconstruction carefully, distinguishing formal validity, the truth of the premises, and what a real human would be in a position to know. Then give a precise diagnosis.

For any Turing machine M, let Pr_M(φ) mean that M outputs the arithmetic sentence φ. Assume for reductio that there is some M such that, for every arithmetic sentence φ, Pr_M(φ) if and only if I would accept φ after ideal reflection. Let G_M be the fixed-point sentence true if and only if not Pr_M(G_M).

(1) Whatever I would accept in arithmetic after ideal reflection is true.
(2) So, from the reductio assumption, M outputs only true sentences.
(3) If M output G_M, then by (2) G_M would be true; but by the fixed-point property G_M is true only if M does not output G_M. Contradiction. So M does not output G_M, and G_M is true.
(4) Since this reasoning is one I can follow, I accept G_M.
(5) But then M fails to output a sentence I accept, contradicting the reductio assumption.
(6) Therefore no Turing machine coincides with my reflective arithmetical judgments; so the human mind is not a Turing machine.

Where, if anywhere, does this argument fail? If it is valid, state exactly what it proves and what assumptions it costs. Pay particular attention to the move from the existence of a matching machine to my ability to form and assess its Gödel sentence, and to whether step (1) is available to an actual human being.
