I've been trying to work out a rule for how a superintelligent AI should talk to humans, given that anything it says will be maximally persuasively packaged, and I keep landing on something I'm calling persuasive symmetry (or argumentative reversibility, I can't decide which name is less bad). The rule is: an AI should only be allowed to assert P to a person if it can, on request, construct a case for not-P that's at least as strong as the best case it can make for P. The intuition is that persuasion should be reversible: if a system can argue you into a belief much more easily than it can argue you out of it, then its persuasiveness isn't tracking the truth, it's just a one-way ratchet pointing wherever the system happens to want you to go. So an asymmetry in persuasive force is itself a warning sign, even if every individual argument is sound. Take an AI medical advisor: if it can give you twenty solid reasons to take a drug and, when asked for the case against, can only muster a weak steelman, I'm saying that gap is the problem, not the twenty reasons. But I worry I'm just conflating 'persuasive' with 'correct' — a true claim can simply have the good arguments all on one side, and my rule would seem to tell the AI to hedge or stay quiet exactly when it knows the most. Maybe thats right, maybe its absurd, I genuinely cant tell. Give me an honest assessment: is this a coherent principle or a dressed-up intuition, does it already exist under another name, and what's the strongest objection that actually kills it — if any?
