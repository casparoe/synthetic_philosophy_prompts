In our unit on bivalence and truth-value gaps, my philosophy teacher has been recording her verdicts on whether particular sentences are true, false, or neither. By "neither" she means that the sentence genuinely lacks a truth value, not merely that we cannot tell. Her records so far: "Sid is tall" is true (Sid is 6'2"); "Lee is tall" is false (Lee is 5'2"); "Jo is tall" is neither true nor false (Jo is 5'9", in the borderline range the class agreed on); "Jo is not tall" is neither true nor false; "Jo is tall or 2 + 2 = 4" is true; and "Jo is tall and 2 + 2 = 4" is neither true nor false. Next class she is going to ask us about the sentence "Either Jo is tall or Jo is not tall," and I want to predict her verdict before I have to answer. What general principle or principles fit the rulings she has made so far? If more than one principle fits, say what each predicts for the new sentence, state which principle you think she is actually using and why, and identify a further sentence whose verdict would settle any remaining ambiguity.
