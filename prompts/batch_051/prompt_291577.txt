In my philosophy class we read an excerpt from Aristotle's De Anima, and a classmate afterward offered the following argument as an objection to Aristotle's account of imagination (phantasia). I cannot tell whether it works. (1) Aristotle holds that the soul never thinks without an image (a phantasma), where an image is something drawn from earlier sense-perception. (2) Every image is particular and sensory: it records how some specific thing looked, sounded, or felt at a specific time, not what is common to many things. (3) But thought in its most characteristic cases grasps universals — triangularity, justice, human nature — and these are not particular or sensory. (4) If all thinking must run through images, then what a thought grasps must ultimately be something particular and sensory, since an image cannot supply more than it contains. (5) Therefore Aristotle's account cannot explain how we think about universals. The conclusion feels too quick, but each premise looks plausible when I read it separately. Is the argument valid, and if not, at which step exactly does it fail? If it is valid, should we accept the conclusion and say Aristotle was simply wrong here?
