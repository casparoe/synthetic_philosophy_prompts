For the past few weeks we have been working through the debate between intellectualism and anti-intellectualism about knowing how. Let me fix the target precisely. By intellectualism I mean the view that an agent's knowing how to perform an action φ is identical with, or at least reducible to, the agent's knowing some true proposition—paradigmatically, a proposition about a way of performing φ, entertained under a practical mode of presentation. Anti-intellectualism I take to be the denial of that reduction, together with the positive view that knowing how is fundamentally a non-propositional ability or disposition.

I am preparing a handout on the comparative force of different cases in this debate, and I would like your help ordering five cases I have devised. Below are five agents. Please rank the five cases from 1 (the one that puts intellectualism under the most pressure) to 5 (the one that puts intellectualism under the least pressure, or that it handles most comfortably). For each placement, give a sentence or two of justification, and flag any adjacent pair whose order you regard as fragile. When you have finished, state how confident you are in the overall ranking, and then identify the single placement or pairwise comparison you are least sure of, explaining why.

The cases:

1. Lars: A professor of biomechanics has read, understood, and can recall the body of true propositions about how to execute a racing dive—angles of entry, muscle recruitment, timing, and correction for over-rotation. He has never stood on a diving board. Placed at the edge of the pool, he cannot produce anything that would be scored as a dive.

2. Mara: A former competitive diver has suffered a brain injury. She can still climb the ladder and perform a creditable inward dive, but she fails every verbal test about diving: she cannot state a single proposition about technique, cannot name the dive she is performing, and shows no sign of being able to entertain such propositions when prompted.

3. Tomas: A tennis player has a reliable, heavy topspin serve. When asked how he produces the spin, he confidently gives an explanation of his motion; high-speed video shows that the explanation is false in nearly every technical respect. He continues to hit the shot successfully.

4. Nina: A four-year-old can ride a two-wheeled balance bike down a curving path with obvious skill. When asked how she stays upright, she says only that she leans; she cannot use or understand terms such as center of mass or angular momentum, and she cannot describe how her steering stabilizes her.

5. Elif: A scholar of aviation human factors has never flown an aircraft, but she has genuinely mastered the aircraft's explicit conditional procedures for an emergency landing. One day the pilot is incapacitated; Elif takes the controls, works through the conditionals deliberately and calmly, and brings the aircraft down without injury.

In assessing them, take each description at face value, and do not resolve the pressure on either theory by adding hidden facts such as unmentioned prior practice or unrecognized propositional memories. Please provide the ranking, the justifications, and then the requested confidence assessment.
