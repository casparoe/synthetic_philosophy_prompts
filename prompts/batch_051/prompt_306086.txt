I've gotten a little obsessed with the constitutional court of a small country that keeps handing down decisions about when a legislature can tie its own hands. Four of the court's older cases seem relevant to a dispute a friend and I are having.

Upheld: a constitutional amendment, passed by referendum, that forbids the government from running a structural budget deficit unless two-thirds of parliament votes to waive it; and a statute, passed by a two-thirds majority, that says any change to the data-protection act must first be reviewed by an independent privacy council before a vote in parliament. Struck down: a statute, passed by a bare majority, that said the national railway would stay in public ownership forever and could never be privatized; and a bare-majority statute requiring unanimous consent before parliament could alter the central bank's mandate, which the court called a repeal ban in disguise because a handfull of members could block any change forever.

Now the court has to review a new climate act passed by a bare majority. It sets a binding emissions target for 2040, and it says any future weakening of that target must be approved by a two-thirds majority, and only after an expert climate council publishes a public report. So the future majority can still change the target, just not the way an ordinary law is changed.

How should the court decide if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents? Which of the four earlier cases is the closest analog? If those precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle is the court adopting if it upholds the climate act, and what principle if it strikes it down? And can you describe a further case that would cleanly tell the two principles apart?
