I am taking a seminar on the philosophy of selfhood this term, and I have been trying to work out a refinement of the view that the self is a center of narrative gravity. I would appreciate an honest assessment of it, including whether it is original and where it goes wrong.

Let me stipulate a distinction between a narrative's center and its source. The center of a narrative is the agent around whom the story is organized: the protagonist whose projects, memories, and recurring patterns give the story its unity, the "I" that the story keeps returning to. The source of a narrative is whatever actually produces the story: the psychological system that selects which episodes belong, assigns them salience, and supplies the narrative's characteristic biases and omissions. My central claim is that the center-of-narrative-gravity view of the self secretly runs these two together, and that the view survives only if we split it in two. The self-as-center is a construct: it can be an abstraction or a useful fiction, precisely because it is just the point where the mass of first- and third-person stories about a life balances. But the self-as-source cannot be a useful fiction in the same sense, because something non-fictional must do the selecting, weighting, and sequencing; if the source were itself a center — another narrative construct — we would need a further source to have constructed it, and so on. I think this explains something the original metaphor leaves obscure: why a self can be genuinely multiple or unstable as a center, appearing as one protagonist in one audience's story and a different protagonist in another's, while the source remains one and continuous. The view I keep expecting to find but cannot is roughly this: the self is a center of narrative gravity only in the center-role, and the unresolved difficulty of selfhood concerns the source-role, not the center-role.

Is the center/source distinction a version of something already worked out, perhaps so obvious that I should be embarrassed to have missed it? And where does it break? In particular, I worry that I am simply assuming a useful fiction is the sort of thing whose construction requires a non-fictional constructor, when the whole point of Dennett-style accounts might be that narrative gravity can be generated by a distributed swarm of subpersonal processes, none of which is itself a self. Is that worry fatal, or can the distinction be repaired?
