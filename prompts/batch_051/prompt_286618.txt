In my day job I size hash tables, and a piece of combinatorial reasoning I use constantly keeps striking me as relevant to a completely different problem: how to evaluate the coincidences people report in personal stories.

When I insert k keys uniformly into N buckets, the expected number of collisions is roughly C(k,2)/N, or about k²/(2N), and the probability of at least one collision crosses one-half around k ≈ 1.2√N — the birthday bound. The bound is unintuitive because the relevant event is not a specified key hitting a specified bucket (which would have probability 1/N), but any pair of keys landing in the same bucket; there are about k²/2 candidate pairs, so a disjunction over many individually rare matches becomes likely very fast. The same logic underwrites birthday attacks on hash functions: an attacker is not committed to a particular input colliding with a particular target, only to some pair of inputs colliding with each other.

Now the other context. People tell me about a staggeringly improbable event that happened to them — the friend they were just thinking of calls within the minute, a stranger shares their birthday and their unusual home town, a dream apparently previewed the next day. The standard move is to compute the probability of exactly that sequence — that friend, that minute, that detail — find it vanishingly small, and conclude that something beyond chance is going on.

The parallel I think I may be seeing is that both cases run on the same contrast between a specified event and an unspecified disjunction. The per-event probability is computed for a target that gets specified only after the event has been selected, while the probability that matters is a collision probability over a large and only partly specified space of candidate events. For an anecdote, the candidate space is enormous: a lifetime of thoughts, calls, encounters, dreams, and possible matches, any one of which could have become the story that gets told. The reporter effectively draws the target around whichever collision actually happened and then treats that selected coincidence as if it had been designated in advance — the move known as the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, and more generally the look-elsewhere effect.

I can see two ways to read this.

One reading is that the mapping is deep: the birthday bound is a precise version of the claim that rare specified events become common as unspecified disjunctions, and the evaluation of reported coincidences is that same combinatorial principle applied to a human sample space instead of a hash table. On this reading, the standard explanation of why remarkable coincidences are so common — namely, that with enough opportunities, individually rare events become almost inevitable — is just the birthday bound with the number of possible reportable coincidences playing the role of N.

The other reading is that the mapping is loose: in the hash table, N, k, independence, uniformity, and the collision predicate are all fixed before the data are examined. In an anecdote, none of those hold. The sample space is assembled retrospectively, the "buckets" are fuzzy and graded by salience, and the event that counts as a match is chosen after the fact. That looks less like a hash table and more like an adversary who gets to inspect all the data and then pick which collision to report — and maybe that difference, not the combinatorics, is the whole story.

Which is it? Is there a genuine structural identity between birthday-bound collision analysis and the way we should evaluate surprising personal coincidences, or am I pattern-matching? If the identity is real, what would have to be true of the anecdote side for any result to transfer, what exactly transfers — for instance, an estimate of how many opportunities a population needs before some reportable coincidence becomes nearly guaranteed, or a pre-registration requirement analogous to fixing the hash target in advance — and what is something this parallel predicts that turns out false?
