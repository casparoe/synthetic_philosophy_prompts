My friend keeps sending me rulings from a seminar on Nyāya epistemology where the instructor sorts every case into the four accepted means of knowledge (pramāṇas) — perception, inference, analogy, and testimony — though she hasn't actually said whether analogy and testimony are genuinely distinct sources or just disguised inference. So far she's said these are all cases of knowledge: someone sees a mango on the table in good light and knows there's a mango; someone sees smoke above a distant ridge and knows there's a fire; a villager learns a bridge is washed out from a forester who has been reliable about trail conditions for years; and a visitor who had been told that a gavaya is a wild ox that looks like a cow later spots a cow-like animal in the forest and knows it's a gavaya. She's also said these are not knowledge: a man who correctly guesses the winner of a match on a hunch, and a villager who believes the river is safe because the town drunkard said so, even though it happens to be safe that day.

Next week's case is this. A traveler in an unfamiliar town asks a total stranger for directions to the train station. The stranger is honest and the directions are correct, but the traveler has never met him, knows nothing about him, and has no track record to go on. Does the traveler know the way to the station?

I'm less interested in the bare yes/no then in the rest: give me your best guess at the instructor's verdict, an account of the rule she's implicitly running about whether testimony yields knowledge on its own or only when the hearer can infer that the speaker is reliable, any other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well, and which further case would cleanly tell the rules apart.
