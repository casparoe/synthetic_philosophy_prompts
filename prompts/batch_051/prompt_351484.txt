I am a principal legislative analyst in the scrutiny unit of a small national law-reform commission. In a typical session we screen on the order of two hundred proposed criminal offenses and civil penalties, and I need one reusable procedure for our analysts to decide which clauses raise legal-moralism concerns: provisions whose strongest available justification is that the conduct offends the morality widely accepted in the community, rather than that it prevents harm to others or protects some other legitimate public interest. Our reports go to a legislature with a constitutionally entrenched right to private life, so designating a clause "presumptive legal moralism" has real consequences; but over-flagging erodes the unit's credibility with the Ministry of Justice, and the commission chair signs off on every designation before it leaves the unit. The analysts are lawyers, not philosophers, and will spend about twenty minutes per clause with nothing but the bill text and a one-page policy digest.

Write the procedure. It should be an ordered checklist or decision tree, grounded in the Hart–Devlin debate and the harm principle, that sorts each clause into three bins: "presumptively legitimate," "presumptively legal moralism," and "requires full-commission judgment." Build in the distinctions the philosophy of law treats as load-bearing—harm to others versus harm to self, indirect or downstream harm, offense or disgust, the integrity of a shared institution, paternalism, exploitation, and the difference between the morality a community happens to hold and the morality that can be justified to the people the law would bind—and say which of these can be dropped for a non-philosopher working under time pressure without losing the procedure's point.

Then apply the procedure to three clauses: a provision criminalizing the sale of sexual services between consenting adults on the ground that it debases the community; a provision requiring motorcycle helmets; and a provision criminalizing the desecration of graves even with the consent of the deceased's family. At least one worked application should give an answer the commission will not like. At every step where the procedure cannot settle the question and a legislator's or commissioner's moral judgment has to re-enter, mark that step as such; and end with a short paragraph on what the procedure cannot do.
