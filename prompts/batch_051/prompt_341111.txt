I run a small R&D group at a mid-size logistics company. Every few weeks a project team walks in with a new planning problem — vehicle routing with weird constraints, warehouse slotting, driver shift scheduling, container packing — and wants us to tell them whether to (a) go looking for an exact polynomial-time algorithm, (b) build an exact solver and bet that the real instances stay small or well-behaved, (c) go straight to heuristics or approximation, or (d) go back to the client and renegotiate the requirements. I keep giving them the same rambling lecture about NP-hardness and worst-case analysis, and they keep not being able to turn it into a decision. So I want a decision procedure my team can actually run on a new problem in an afternoon, something a competent engineer with no complexity-theory background can work through before we commit to a build. The VP of engineering has to sign off on anything longer than a month, and she's tired of seeing us burn six months on a exact solver that dies on real instances.

The underlying idea I want preserved is the philosophical one: NP-hardness is a claim about a whole family of instances, including adversarial ones, and by itself it doesn't tell you how hard your instances are. But ignoring it is how you waste six months. So the procedure has to force people to separate "this problem is hard in general" from "our instances are hard in practice," and to say which one is doing the work at each step. It should also preserve the nuance that some NP-hard problems are only weakly NP-hard and can still be solved exactly when the numbers are small.

Please write the procedure as: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each (a reduction to a known hard problem? a hardness-of-approximation result? instance data showing some parameter is always small?); where the procedure stops with "do not attempt exact"; where it stops with "exact is worth a try"; and where it has to stop and hand the call to a human because complexity theory has run out. Also flag where you expect the procedure to misfire and what it can't do.

Then run it on three of our current problems:

1. A vehicle routing problem with time windows and a hard cap on route duration (each truck max 9 hours). The underlying routing cost, even for a single route, is basically TSP.

2. A knapsack-style packing problem where all weights and values are multiples of 1,000 and total capacity is at most 2 or 3 million. I remember this kind of thing is still NP-hard but has a pseudo-polynomial algorithm, and I want the procedure to catch that distinction.

3. A scheduling problem the team is extremely excited to solve exactly because the client will pay a lot. It reduces cleanly from 3-partition, and I'm pretty sure its NP-hard in the strong sense and hard to approximate within any constant factor unless P=NP. I suspect the procedure is going to tell them the opposite of what they want to hear, and they'll be annoyed; show how the procedure handles that.

Finish with a short paragraph on what the procedure cannot decide — the places where complexity theory underdetermines the engineering choice.
