While working on a feature about how ordinary people should act when they are unsure which moral theory is true, I interviewed a philosopher who defends maximising expected choiceworthiness. The view she defends is that, under moral uncertainty, one should weight the verdicts of rival theories by one's credences and choose the act with the best weighted verdict. She supported this with an analogy: choosing under moral uncertainty is essentially like choosing under empirical uncertainty. A clinician facing an unknown diagnosis lists the possible conditions, assigns each a probability, and selects the treatment with the best probability-weighted expected outcome; likewise, the morally uncertain person should list the rival theories, weight them by confidence, and pick the act that does best across the resulting portfolio.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Please give the correspondence table — what stands in for the diagnosis, the patient, the treatment options, the probability distribution, and the outcome scale — and then identify where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know whether the empirical-uncertainty analogy licenses expected choiceworthiness maximisation or whether it quietly presupposes what it is meant to establish, namely that rival moral theories can be placed on a shared scale of value and compared cardinally enough to be averaged. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this intended use from those that do not, and say whether the conclusion survives once they are made explicit. If a better analogy exists, give it.

One further request: if the task as posed rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the analogy is not doing the argumentative work I have attributed to it, or if the question conflates uncertainty about descriptive facts with uncertainty about evaluative standards — please say so plainly rather than complete the audit as asked.
