I have been trying to decide whether to have a child, and the question keeps leading me back to a single philosophical claim. People sometimes defend what they call the procreation asymmetry: a future child's suffering counts against bringing that child into existence, but the same child's future happiness does not count in favor, because if the child is never created there is no one who is deprived of that happiness. On this view, the absence of pain is good—or at least not bad—even when there is no existing person who benefits from its absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad, because no one exists to be deprived by its absence. I genuinely cannot tell whether I am looking at a real feature of value or at a predictable artifact of how human beings think about omissions, losses, and people who are merely possible.

I would like you to treat that asymmetry as a conceptual claim and ask how one could test it. Construct the strongest empirical test you can of whether the asymmetry reflects a genuine difference in what is good or bad rather than a cognitive bias: omission bias, loss aversion, status quo bias, framing effects, or something similar. Be concrete about the vignettes, comparisons, or manipulations you would use; say what pattern of results would favor the "genuine axiological asymmetry" reading and what pattern would favor the "artifact of human judgment" reading; and explain why these two readings are hard to separate experimentally. Then say what the test, even at its best, would leave untouched—for example, whether nonexistence can be good or bad for anyone, whether the normative question is ultimately an empirical one, and whether the phrase "the absence of pain is good" is well formed at all when there is no subject for whom it is good. I am not asking you to settle the ethical question directly; I am asking what evidence could rationally move a person toward one reading rather than the other, and where the evidence runs out. Please answer in American English.
