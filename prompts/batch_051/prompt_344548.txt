I recently got promoted to manage a team of people I used to be a peer of, and I’ve been using Adam Smith’s Theory of Moral Sentiments to think through whether I’m being fair when I have to discipline someone or decide who gets a bad performance review. I’m also in a reading group on Smith, and I wrote up my current operating view so the group could poke holes in it. But I want a sharper audit than they’ll give me.

You’re a professional philosopher who works on Smith’s moral psychology, so I’d trust you to keep the details straight. Before you write, work through my committments pairwise and privately sort out which ones are really in tension and which only look like it.

Here’s what I think I think:

1. Sympathy is the whole engine of moral judgment, on my reading. I judge someone’s resentment as appropriate when, imagining myself in their circumstances and history, I can enter into it — I’d feel a matching or fitting version of it. And I judge my own conduct by imagining how an impartial spectator would react to my motives and my situation, with all the relevant facts in view.

2. But that impartial spectator isn’t a freestanding source of moral truth. It gets built from the outside in: I internalize the actual praise and blame of the people around me, then try to strip out their ignorance, partiality, and distance from the facts. What’s left is an internal spectator, but it’s made of social material.

3. Even so, I want to be praiseworthy, not merely praised. When I refuse to pad someone’s review even though my actual coworkers would approve, the thing I care about is that my conduct would be approved by the impartial spectator in light of the full facts — not just that my friends happen to cheer.

4. Self-deception is deep and it’s chronic. I’m really good at rigging my own internal courtroom: finding the one spectator who shares my grievance, dressing up resentment as fairness. So in the moment I shouldn’t trust first-person verdicts; I should lean on general rules of conduct — the accumulated approvals and disapprovals of many spectators over many cases.

5. A person who didn’t care at all about actual others’ actual opinions would be missing something human. I’m not willing to treat actual praise and blame as pure noise, even though they aren’t the final standard.

6. Outcome luck doesn’t wash out the way it should. If I discipline someone and they spiral afterward, I feel much worse than if the same decision, with the same motives, led them to bounce back — and my internal spectator seems to track the result, not just the motive. Smith has a whole discussion of this irregularity, and the impartial spectator corrects it only imperfectly.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which pairs only seem to be. For each real tension, rank them by severity, say what specific commitment has to give, and give me the cheapest fix and what it costs elsewhere in the view. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Don’t protect the view and don’t give me a lecture on Smith’s system — I’m trying to see whether my operating picture is coherent or whether I’m smuggling in something Smith can’t actually deliver.

When you respond, lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — the overall verdict plus the worst tension — then give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restating of my list.
