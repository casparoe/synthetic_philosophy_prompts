Question. In his 1967 article "On the Principle of Total Evidence," I.J. Good argues that an agent who maximizes expected utility should never prefer to remain ignorant before choosing an action, provided the information itself is free. The core of the argument is that the expected utility of choosing an action after observing evidence is at least as high as the expected utility of committing in advance to any single action. Explain why the relevant inequality holds under the Bayesian model, state at least one assumption required for the result, and then assess whether the theorem establishes that an autonomous delivery robot should process and incorporate every additional camera feed available to it before selecting a route.

Student answer. Good's theorem proves that the expected utility after receiving free information is always at least the expected utility of deciding without that information. The proof compares two quantities: E_e[max_a E_θ[U(a,θ)∣e]] and max_a E_θ[U(a,θ)]. Since expectation and the maximum operator commute when utility is linear, these two quantities are equal, which yields the result. The only assumption needed is that the agent updates its beliefs by conditionalization. Applied to the delivery robot, the theorem directly implies that adding extra camera feeds cannot reduce the robot's expected route quality, because any plan the robot could have chosen before seeing the feeds remains available afterward. The only qualification is if a camera feed contains adversarial data that causes the robot to mispredict the environment, which would be a violation of conditionalization.
