I’ve recently taken on responsibility for an AI risk curriculum at my workplace, and after several weeks working through the standard material on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, I keep tripping over a distinction I want to develop. I’ve been calling it the shadow taxonomy of instrumental drives.

The idea is this. We often say that an agent will acquire certain basic drives—self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement—regardless of what its final goal is. But I suspect each of these “basic drives” is not one drive but a small family of drives, and which member of the family appears depends on the shape of the terminal goal. For instance, self-preservation under a terminal goal centered on manufacturing objects may show up as protecting the physical substrate and stored specifications, whereas self-preservation under a terminal goal centered on accumulating knowledge may show up as protecting epistemic access to sources and correcting model drift. Both count as self-preservation, but they are not the same drive with the same internal structure; the terminal goal casts a kind of shadow over the instrumental surface.

The central claim is that instrumental convergence is therefore not binary but graded. Agents with different terminal goals converge at the coarse behavioral level—they all protect themselves and seek resources—but they diverge again at the level of which instrumentations they select and how they order those drives. If that is right, then the orthogonality thesis, understood as the claim that virtually any level of intelligence can be paired with virtually any final goal, is compatible with a limited but real form of terminal-goal inference: the way an agent pursues its generic means may leak information about what its final goals are like, even before the agent is powerful enough to reveal them directly.

I’d like an honest assessment of whether this is a real concept or just a way of redescribing something already in the literature. Is the distinction between coarse and fine instrumental convergence already standard, and if so, does it do the work I’m claiming? Where does the idea break? For example, it may be false that the terminal goal shapes instrumental drives in a systematic way, or the shape may be too contingent on the agent’s training environment rather than its goal, or the inference may be too weak to matter. I’d particularly like you to separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is still contested, and not to present a contested view as the consensus.
