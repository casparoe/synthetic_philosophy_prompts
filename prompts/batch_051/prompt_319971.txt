I am a retired geochemist, and since retiring I have been working through moral and political philosophy in a systematic way. A pattern in municipal policy puzzles me, and I would like it examined as a question about what a practice presupposes. Consider a city government that presents itself as concerned above all with the affordability of ordinary life. In a single legislative term it enacts a substantial minimum wage, and it also enacts a rent-stabilization ordinance that caps annual increases on most residential leases below the market trend. At the same time it leaves the prices of food, energy, child care, and nearly everything else to the market. Both measures are price controls in the elementary economic sense, and in each case the standard textbook objection has the same structure: a binding control prevents the relevant price from clearing its market and thereby produces a persistent shortage or surplus. Yet the two controls are treated very differently. The wage floor is defended as an elementary matter of fairness, while the rent cap is hedged with vacancy decontrol, exemptions for new construction, and the insistence that it is not truly a price control at all.

I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory, if there is one, that makes this asymmetry coherent. Does the practice presuppose that labor and housing are different kinds of goods—for instance, that labor markets are systematically tilted in the employer's favor in a way that housing markets are not, or that income from work and income from owning property have different moral standing? Does it presuppose a particular account of needs, on which shelter itself is to be secured directly by law while the means to afford shelter is left to the wage floor? Or is the practice underdetermined, strictly compatible with several rival views and presupposing none of them? Finally, tell me what the city would be committed to changing if the symmetry between the two controls were accepted: would consistency force it to abandon one of the two policies, or could it keep both by stopping the pretense that rent stabilization is not a price control and defending it on the same footing as the wage floor?
