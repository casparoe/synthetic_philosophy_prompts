I have been trying to turn something I know from novel-writing into a serious claim about cognition, and I want an honest assessment of whether it survives scrutiny. I call the idea the character register. When I write a character from the inside, I do not take my own concepts and attach a different set of beliefs to them; I seem to construct a small bespoke alphabet of primitive terms through which the character's thought must be expressed. A recurring character of mine, a retired croupier, appears to lack my concept of embarrassment altogether. He parses everyone he meets through, at bottom, two predicates: a person is solvent or a person is sliding toward tilt. A scene I would describe as a man being humiliated is, for him, represented as a solvent man being tested, and the word humiliation does not occur in his interior monologue even as a suppressed alternative. I cannot recover my concept from his simply by adding and subtracting beliefs, because his primitive terms do not compose into my primitive terms; the alphabet itself is different. The practical result is that writing him feels less like choosing beliefs for him and more like translating my scenes into his register, with losses at every step.

I want to claim something stronger than a point about craft. Actual cognition, I suspect, works this way too. A mind is not a single store of beliefs written in one shared, compositional language of thought; it is a patchwork of local registers, each with its own small stock of primitives, and the apparent traffic between them is translation under loss rather than inference under a common code. This seems to put me in tension with the language of thought hypothesis, or perhaps to be an eccentric variant of it. I understand that hypothesis as the claim that thinking is computation over structured representations with a combinatorial syntax and semantics, and I am not sure it actually requires every mind, or every part of a mind, to share the same primitive vocabulary. So my idea may be either a real objection, a rediscovery of something already in the literature, or simply a flattering redescription of the difference between a concept and a belief. Before I turn any of this into a novel, tell me which it is. Is the view coherent? What is the strongest version of it, and where exactly does it stop being distinctive or become false? I most want to know whether the distinction between a character's possessing a different inventory of primitives and a character's merely holding different beliefs can be made to bear weight, or whether it dissolves under pressure.
