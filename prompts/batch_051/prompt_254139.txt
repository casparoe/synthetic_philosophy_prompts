I'm putting together a reading group on philosophy of physics, and last week we got completely stuck on a point I'd love to have someone actually commit to. In classical mechanics nobody blinks at the idea that two electrons are two distinct individuals that happen to share all there intrinsic properties: you can in principle label them at t0 and track them. But in quantum mechanics the state of a many-particle system has to be symmetrized for bosons and antisymmetrized for fermions, and the standard gloss is that this shows same-kind particles aren't individuals — there's no fact of the matter about which electron is which, the labels are just bookkeeping, and all that's physically real is "there are N of them." I want you to argue the opposite: that quantum particles can still be regarded as individuals, or at least that the permutation-invariance argument overreaches and doesn't settle the metaphysics. Grant the physics — the symmetrization postulate, the permutation-invariance of all observables — and then be very explicit about which step from physics to metaphysics you're resisting.

You'll probably need to distinguish a few things people mean by "individual": having a primitive thisness over and above all properties, being distinguishable by some relation, being re-identifiable over time. Tell me which sense your argument needs, and whether bosons and fermions come out differently on your view. A few people in the group kept dragging in the Gibbs paradox as evidence that the issue predates quantum mechanics, which I found confusing — use it if it actually helps, but don't let it derail you. End by flagging the weakest premise in your own argument, because the versions I've seen all hand-wave either the physics or the metaphysics and I want one that does neither.
