In the philosophy of history course I'm currently taking, we've been reading work that treats non-human factors—climate shifts, pathogens, volcanic events—as historical actors in their own right rather than as inert background for human intentions. I'm drawn to this approach because it has real systematic virtues: it unifies disparate cases across regions, corrects for the bias of assuming that whatever matters in history must have been meant by someone, and often explains more than any narrative centered on deliberate choice. But I can't shake a firm intuition that a drought constrains while a person acts, that a plague kills but doesn't contend for power, and that in quietly erasing the line between cause and action this approach loses something essential to historical explanation. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—an anthropocentric prejudice to be debunked—and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that bends, perhaps toward constraints without determinism, multi-causal conjunctures, or a graded notion of agency. Is there a reading on which the explanatory power and the intuition both survive, or is one of them doing the real work all along?
