I've been trying to get a handle on the presentism debate in historiography for a paper, and the more I read the less I can locate the actual point of contention. On one side are people who say no historian can escape the standards of their own time, so calling certain ancient Greek attachments 'homosexuality' or calling premodern natural philosophy 'science' isn't a mistake: it's just using present categories openly, and the only honest requirement is to say that's what your doing and defend it. On the other side are people who say the entire craft is reconstructing how the actors themselves divided up the world, and that importing our categories is exactly anachronism—it short-circuits understanding by making the past look familiar. The odd thing is that these people seem to agree on the underlying events (who did what, what words were used, how institutions worked), and they all grant that bad anachronism exists. The dispute is somehow both boring and unresolvable. My hypothesis keeps flipping: sometimes I think 'history' is just doing two jobs (reconstructing past self-understanding versus giving a present-angled account) and the two sides have picked different jobs while talking as if they shared one; sometimes I think it's a substantive bet about which failure is worse, reading our categories into the past or refusing to make the past speak to now; and sometimes I suspect the real issue is how translatable old categories are, which sounds like an empirical question nobody is trying to settle. What's the diagnosis here, and what would actually move it or count as progress?
