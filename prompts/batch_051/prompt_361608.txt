In population ethics, Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion is roughly this: for any population of people all enjoying a very high quality of life, there is some vastly larger population whose members have lives that are barely worth living, and whose existence would nevertheless be better, other things being equal. The underlying thought is that when outcomes are judged by total well-being, a sufficiently large number of barely-worth-living lives can outweigh a smaller number of excellent ones. John Stuart Mill died in 1873, long before this problem was formulated. Suppose Mill had encountered the repugnant conclusion. What position do you think he would have taken, and what arguments would he have offered? In particular, would his utilitarianism, with its distinction between higher and lower pleasures and its insistence that the quality of pleasures matters more than their quantity, have led him to reject the conclusion, to accept it, or to treat it as an artifact of a misguided way of aggregating well-being? Give the strongest Millian response you can construct, and be explicit about any points at which Mill's own commitments pull in different directions.
