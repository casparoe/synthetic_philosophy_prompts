I've been fiddling with a theory about why fiction is worth public money, and I'd like to know whether it holds up or whether I've just reinvented something obvious and put a bad name on it. I call it the attic theory of fiction: a society's literature is like the junk in an attic — it looks like dead weight, but it's actually a stockpile of situations nobody needs right now but someone will need eventually. A novel, on this view, is a pre-experienced event; the society has already been through the plague, the coup, the public shaming, the inheritance dispute in imagination, so when something like it actually happens, there's a readymade frame lying around to argue with, borrow from, or reject. The payoff isn't empathy or moral improvement, and I don't think a society of selfish people would be worse off on my account: the benefit is cognitive, not ethical. It's just having a shared template for an unfamiliar situation instead of having to build one from scratch in the middle of the event. I'd even say a society with a well-stocked attic should make sense of genuinely strange events faster than one without it, even if the two are equal in intelligence and education.

The example that convinced me: when COVID arrived, people reached for the standard plague novels and a shelf of outbreak movies — not, I think, because those stories made anyone kinder, but because they already contained a shared script for what a society does under an invisible threat: who gets blamed, how ordinary life and emergency blur, what virtue looks like under quarantine. My bolder claim is that the stories don't have to be good or even accurate to do this work; they just have to be shared and detailed enough. So funding fiction is like keeping the attic stocked, and the state should care about it the way it cares about emergency stockpiles, not the way it cares about nice paintings. I've been attached to this idea for a couple years now and my friends either love it or roll their eyes, so I need someone to be honest with me.

What I want from you: give me the strongest possible reconstruction of the view, tell me whether it already exists under another name (I know the empathy-and-literature stuff well enough; if mine is just that with extra steps, say so), and then hit it with the three hardest objections. Tell me which ones are fatal and which are just uncomfortable. The one I keep getting stuck on is where the line between 'shared fiction' and 'any shared narrative' goes — myths, gossip, legal cases, prepper manuals all seem to do roughly the same job — and I can't decide wether that kills the theory or just means it should be broader than fiction.
