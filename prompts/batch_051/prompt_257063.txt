In my former life as a research scientist in the physical sciences, I absorbed as a piece of practical statistics the idea that priors wash out: two honest analysts who start with very different prior distributions will be pulled toward nearly the same posterior by a sufficiently long run of shared observations. Since retiring, I have been reading formal epistemology and Bayesian philosophy of science, and I have tried to reconstruct the argument behind that piece of folklore. Here is the version I have ended up with.

(1) Two Bayesian agents, A and B, assign probabilities over the same space of hypotheses and share the same likelihoods; that is, for any hypothesis H and any possible observation E, they agree on the probability of E given H, and each updates by conditionalization. (2) Their priors are mutually compatible in the following sense: neither agent assigns zero probability to any event to which the other assigns positive probability. (3) They observe a common, indefinitely long sequence of data generated by a fixed true hypothesis θ0, and under the usual consistency conditions each agent's posterior distribution concentrates on arbitrarily small neighborhoods of θ0 as the sample grows. (4) If A's posterior and B's posterior both converge to the same point mass at θ0, then the two posteriors converge to one another; in the limit their probabilities for any given event coincide, and their predictions about future observations coincide. (5) Therefore the initial difference between their priors is washed out: with enough shared evidence, rational agents must come to agree, almost surely.

I half-suspect that this proves too much, and that the washing-out of priors is more fragile than the folklore suggests, but I cannot locate the exact step where things go wrong. Please act as an expert in Bayesian epistemology and the foundations of statistics. Before evaluating the argument, briefly brainstorm the conditions under which two Bayesian agents could fail to converge despite sharing data. Then go through the steps one by one: restate each premise in its strongest defensible form, and say whether the inference at each step is valid. If the argument fails, identify the first step that fails and explain what condition would need to be added to repair it; if it succeeds on some reading, say exactly what hidden assumptions that reading carries. In particular, examine whether step (2)'s compatibility condition is doing the real work, and whether the concentration of both posteriors on the truth, asserted in step (3), genuinely delivers the agreement claimed in step (4).
