Cantor's treatment of the infinite wins me over because it reduces a heap of puzzles to a single principle of counting by one-to-one correspondence. Once that principle is granted, Hilbert's hotel stops being a paradox, the even numbers' being as numerous as the naturals stops being scandalous, and the countability of the rationals alongside the uncountability of the reals becomes an illustration of the principle rather than an objection to it. Yet I have a standing conviction I have never managed to dissolve: a completed whole cannot be equal in size to a proper part of itself, since a proper part is what remains after something has been taken away, and what remains after something has been taken away is less than what there was. If some so-called totality violates this, my inclination is to say it is not a finished totality at all but a process misdescribed as a thing.

I want an accounting of the concessions, not a verdict. Present the strongest case for each of the three available settlements and say which of them should be adopted. First, the case that the intuition should yield: what would have to be true about concepts such as more, less, and completed for them to have application only to finite collections and no grip on the infinite, so that the part-whole principle is exposed as a projection of finite thinking? Second, the case that the theory should yield: what would have to be true about Cantor's formalism for its elegance to amount to a change of subject, and what form of finitism or constructivism would inherit its results without its ontology of completed infinite totalities? Third, the case for reconciliation: is there a stable reading on which the actual infinite is a legitimate object of mathematics while ordinary things and the physical world remain finite, or does that reading quietly concede one side of the conflict while claiming to transcend it? Say what justifies adopting one of these options rather than merely preferring it.
