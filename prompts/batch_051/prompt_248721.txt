I'm a software engineer, and I'm used to the feeling of having a set of cases that a rule has to handle and not quite being able to write the rule. I've been having that experience with my own judgments about cultural appropriation in the arts: I have strong verdicts on particular cases, but no principle that covers them, and I'm not sure whether the fault is in my verdicts or in the idea that there's one principle at all. I'd like you to help me find out.

Here are my verdicts on cases. I'll mark each one fine, not fine, or uneasy.

1. A white American singer who spent a decade playing in blues clubs records an album of blues standards by Black songwriters, properly licensed and credited, and it becomes a hit. Fine.

2. A European fashion house copies a geometric pattern used on ceremonial textiles by an Indigenous community in Guatemala — a pattern the community treats as collective property, with rules about who may weave and wear it — mass-produces it on scarves, and gives the community no credit, no payment, and no say. Not fine.

3. A white American novelist writes a first-person novel narrated by a teenage migrant from El Salvador. She spent years doing interviews and learning the dialect, and publishes under her own name. Not fine, though I have trouble saying why.

4. A Black American novelist writes a first-person novel narrated by a white working-class woman in rural Appalachia, after years of interviews. Fine.

5. A non-Indigenous Australian painter lives for a year in an Aboriginal community, learns a dot-painting technique from elders who give him permission, and then sells paintings in that style, with the community receiving a share and approving the work before it goes out. Fine.

6. A white electronic musician samples a recording of a Hindu devotional song and uses it as the hook in a club track. The original singer is credited and paid, but the musician never consulted the religious community whose practice the song belongs to. Uneasy, leaning not fine.

My working theory is something like this: taking is fine when the outsider has earned the form through genuine immersion and the source community isn't excluded, misrepresented, or overridden; taking is not fine when the thing taken is something the community treats as protected, or when the outsider profits by a route the community itself is shut out of. But I can't define "protected," and cases 3 and 4 together shake my confidence in the whole theory: the two novels seem structurally identical except for the direction of the crossing, and I can't tell whether my different verdicts are tracking something real — power, history, something else — or are just an inconsistency in me.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to new cases; an account of which clause does the work in each of my cases; verdicts on two or three further cases of your own that stress-test the principle; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should revise that verdict rather than complicate the principle to keep it. If the right conclusion is that "cultural appropriation" is not one thing and no single principle will do the work, tell me that plainly — I'd rather have an accurate map of the mess than a tidy rule that saves my verdicts by gerrymandering. And if the whole task is badly posed, say that instead of politely pretending it can be done.
