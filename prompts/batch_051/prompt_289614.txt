Consider the episode at Glastonbury Abbey in 1191: a few years after a fire in 1184 had destroyed much of the abbey, the monks announced that they had discovered the grave of King Arthur and Guinevere, together with a lead cross identifying the site, and they reburied the remains with ceremony inside the abbey church, at a moment when the abbey badly needed to attract pilgrims and patrons. I want this treated as a case study in the question of when a legend becomes history. Using the best account you can construct of the difference between a story that circulates as legend and a story that a community comes to count as history, analyze the episode in both directions. In one direction, use the account to read the case: who the claim-makers were and to whom the claim was addressed; what kinds of evidence, narrative convention, and institutional authority they were mobilizing; what your account says they would have needed to do—and to avoid—for a legendary figure to be received as historical, and whether the monks' actions match that description; and where, if anywhere, the line between legend and history was actually crossed. In the other direction, turn the case back on the account: which features of the 1191 episode does it explain well, which does it misdescribe or pass over in silence, and does it confirm the idea that a legend can pass into history—or does it suggest that what happened is better described as a story being successfully promoted as history? If this framing is itself confused—for instance, because 'legend' and 'history' are not the sort of stable categories that can stand in that relation, or because the evidence for the 1191 episode is too thin to sustain the analysis—say so plainly rather than forcing the case.
