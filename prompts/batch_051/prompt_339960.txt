I retired a few years ago from a career in physical chemistry and have recently gotten into philospohy, working through it on my own. The other day I read a short book—or maybe a long magazine piece, I forget wich—that summarized a debate about moral worth, meaning roughly when an action deserves moral credit as opposed to just being the right thing to do. Here is the summary as I remember it:

Kant's shopkeeper gives correct change to an inexperienced customer because a reputation for honesty is good for business. He does the right act but it has no moral worth. For Kant an act has moral worth only when it is done from duty, not from inclination—not because you like the person, enjoy being honest, or feel sorry for them. So moral worth depends entirely on the motive, not on what the act actually accomplishes. More recent writers, whom the author called right reasons theorists, soften this. They say an act has moral worth when the features that move you to act are the same features that make the act right. If you jump in to save a drowning child because she is in danger of dying, that has worth even if you never stop to think about duty. If you do the right thing because it is what everyone normally does, or because you want to look like a hero, it has none. The book then drew two further conclusions. First, a person who acts out of compassion has moral worth because compassion reliably points at the features that make actions right; someone who has to force himself to act out of duty while hating every minute has less worth, maybe none, because he is not really responding to what is right about the act, he is just obeying a rule. Second, since only the motive matters, we can judge moral worth without knowing whether the action succeeded: a rescue attempt that fails because the child slipped away has exactly the same worth as a successful one.

The science-trained part of me got stuck on that last claim—judging worth without any outcome information sounds like judging an experiment by the hypothesis rather than the data. But I don't know this literature well enough to tell whether I'm being unfair to what the book actually said, and memory is a lousy instrument. Could you go through that summary and tell me which parts a philosopher working on moral worth would accept, which parts are oversimplifications or misreadings, and whether those last two inferences actually follow from the accurate parts?
