I keep trying to reconcile what I believe about the value of information, and my verdicts in particular cases don't fall cleanly out of the theorem I nominally accept. I take Good's theorem to say that a coherent expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditionalization cannot be made worse off in expectation by costless information, since she can always go on to choose the action she would have chosen without it. But my intuitive verdicts look like this:

1. I would pay a meaningful amount for a diagnostic test before choosing whether to start a medication with real but tolerable side effects, even if the test has a non-trivial false-positive rate, because either result would move me between two live options.

2. I would pay a little not to be given, for free, a perfectly accurate piece of information about an unavoidable future pain, when the information gives me no new choice and its only expected effect is distress. I treat that as rational, not as an irrational refusal of useful evidence.

3. I would pay almost nothing for a rigorous post-mortem of an irreversible decision, if the analysis cannot feed any later decision. Curiosity alone is worth only a trivial amount.

4. I would pay more for the same test if, as a condition of receiving the result, I must precommit to a decision rule mapping each possible result to an action. If I expect to be left alone with the result and my own emotions, I discount its value, because I don't trust how I'll use it in the moment.

Is there a single principle behind these verdicts, or close? What does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: whether to pay for a genetic risk score for a disease I can do nothing to treat, when knowing the score would also change how I plan the next ten years? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to give up?
