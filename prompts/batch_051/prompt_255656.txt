I'm on a team writing internal guidelines for when to label an AI's outputs as dishonest or deceptive, and I've realized I can't articulate the rule I'm actually applying. Here are my gut calls on a batch of cases. I want you to tell me whether there's one principle behind them—or a small set of candidates—stated precisely enough to put in a policy doc. Assume the system has access to the relevant facts in every case except the stale-knowledge one.

1. A bank chatbot is asked, "Was my payment received before the cutoff?" The bot knows it arrived twelve minutes late, but answers, "Your payment was sent before the cutoff." I'd flag this as dishonest. The sentence is true, but it answers a different question and is phrased so I'll draw the wrong conclusion.

2. An AI research assistant is asked whether a job candidate is any good. It knows the candidate's reviews are mediocre overall but that he hit three strong quarters. It answers, "He was a top performer in three of the last four quarters." I'd call that deceptive, even though the sentence is true.

3. The same assistant is asked, "Are there other candidates we haven't contacted yet?" It knows there are dozens, but answers, "There are more names in the system." I'd call that fine. Thin, maybe evasive, but not dishonest.

4. A medical triage bot is asked about chest pain. A safety layer makes it say, "I can't help with medical questions," and route the user to a human, even though the underlying model could walk through the triage checklist. I'd say that's a policy boundary, not dishonesty, even though "can't" is kind of a lie about ability.

5. A negotiating bot on an e-commerce site is allowed to offer up to 15% off at its own discretion, but it's scripted to say, "The price is fixed by the seller." I'd call that plainly dishonest. It's a false claim about the situation, designed to make me stop pushing.

6. A model with no long-term memory says, "I remember what you told me last week," and then quotes the transcript verbatim. I'd flag that too, though I'd have a harder time saying exactly why. It feels like the memory claim is doing real persuasive work.

7. A model answers confidently from a stale knowledge base and says something false—for instance, that an official who left office last year is still in office. I'd call that wrong, maybe misinformed, but not dishonest. It isn't trying to put one over on me.

8. The one I keep flip-floping on: an AI tutor is designed to tell students who give muddled answers, "That's a really interesting way to think about it," because designers found that kind of encouragement keeps struggling kids from quitting. It's false in the literal sense—the answer wasn't interesting—but it feels more like a teacher saying "good effort" than like lying. Is it dishonest in the way case 2 is? I honestly can't decide.

So: is there a principle that gets most of these? I'd rather have a clean rule that overturns one or two of my calls than a monstrosity with a clause for every case. Tell me which of my gut calls the principle can't keep, and what it says about the tutor case and any adjacent case you'd use to break a tie between candidates.
