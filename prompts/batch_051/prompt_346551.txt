I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad class on the ethics of labor, and next week we're doing Upton Sinclair's The Jungle alongside a philosophy unit on sweatshops and mutually beneficial exploitation. The standard undergrad take is that the novel is just an argument for stronger labor laws: the workers face horror, so the system is wrong. But I want to complicate that with the philosophical distinction between harmful exploitation and mutually beneficial exploitation—where both parties gain relative to their next-best alternative, but the transaction still seems wrong because the baseline is unjust or the employer is taking advantage of desperate vulnerability. Read The Jungle through that lens. Where does Sinclair's outrage actually depend on the workers being made worse off than they would have been without the job, and where does it track something more like the wrongness of exploiting a desperate person even when the job improves their situation? Does the novel ever notice the difference between those two wrongs, or does it run them together? Which scenes would you have the class reread to see the distinction, or to see it collapse? I'm especially interested in places where the book resists the philosophical distinction and shows it's not doing much work.
