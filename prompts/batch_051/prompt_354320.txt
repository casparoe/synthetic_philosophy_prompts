For an essay I am drafting, I would welcome comments on the sketch below—particularly on whether its central analogy is doing real argumentative work or merely providing ornament.

The essay defends a third way of valuing useless fields. The two familiar defenses treat them either as hidden or eventual utilities (the option-value view) or as intrinsic goods. I want to argue instead that universities should deliberately shelter fields that fail current tests of usefulness because a healthy epistemic system requires conceptual fallow. In agriculture, land is sometimes left fallow not because it presently yields a crop, and not merely to store up future yield, but because suspending production renews the soil's long-term capacity to produce at all. Analogously, unusual scholarship keeps alive forms of questioning, standards of rigor, and conceptual vocabularies that would be selected against if every discipline had to justify itself under the currently dominant measure of value. Its value is not that it is secretly useful, nor even that it may someday prove useful, but that it preserves evaluative and conceptual diversity whose absence makes the larger system brittle and over-adapted.

The issue I most need help with is this: the analogy seems to justify only temporary fallow, since fallow land is ultimately returned to cultivation. But a field kept outside the economy of usefulness may look less like fallow ground than like abandoned land, and no one defends maintaining wasteland in perpetuity. Can the essay draw a principled line between conceptual fallow and mere dead weight without quietly reverting to the option-value view it set out to avoid? I would also value comments on whether the argument, if it succeeds, justifies whole fields or only protected positions within otherwise useful fields.
