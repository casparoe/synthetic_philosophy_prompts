I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on right now follows a woman who sits on her city's "nudge board" — a small commitee that reviews every behavioral intervention the city wants to run on its residents, in the spirit of libertarian paternalism: steering people without taking away their options. I've already written five of their past rulings, and I want the big final ruling in the book to feel earned rather than just plotted, so I'm trying to reverse-engineer what a genuinely consistent version of this committee would decide.

Their track record so far:
1. They approved auto-enrollment in a retirement savings plan for city employees, with a one-click opt-out and automatic yearly increases unless the employee pauses them. The reasoning was that the money stays the employee's and the choice is easily reversible.
2. They approved calorie and added-sugar counts on menus and vending machines in city buildings, calling it information rather than steering.
3. They approved a cafeteria redesign that put fruit and water at eye level and moved sugary drinks to the bottom shelf, while keeping every item available. They treated it as changing salience, not restricting options.
4. They rejected a plan to make payroll giving to a city-chosen charity the default for municipal employees. The opt-out existed but was a small checkbox buried in a long onboarding packet, and the board said using inertia to move people's money toward a cause they never picked was over the line.
5. They rejected a tax-reminder letter saying over 90 percent of people in the recipient's neighborhood had already paid on time, because they thought it leaned on shame and social pressure rather than giving people a reason.

Now the case I need them to rule on for the climax: the city wants to make the renewable "green" electricity plan the default for all households, with the cheaper standard plan available by one phone call or click. Households would pay slightly more on average unless they switch back. The city says its for emissions reduction — a public good — but also argues that residents themselves benefit from cleaner air and that surveys show most residents support renewables but don't switch out of inertia.

You're a philosopher who works on paternalism, nudges, and choice architecture. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the different principles that could make the five earlier rulings consistent — don't just settle on the first one. Then tell me: what should the board decide if it wants to stay consistent with itself, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what principle would it be adopting with either ruling? Also name a further case that would cleanly separate the two leading rules. And I'd like you to be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your recommendation, or where your reasoning is most likely to break down.
