For the final paper, I want you to take up a puzzle that has been running through several of our recent discussions. We often explain things by giving the rationale behind them: the heart exists because it pumps blood, a market settles at a certain price because that is where supply meets demand, and a trained model adopts a particular shortcut because doing so minimizes its loss. Yet in none of these cases did anyone entertain the rationale. No one designed the heart, the market did not deliberate, and the optimization process did not reason about its own objective. Philosophers sometimes describe such cases as involving free-floating rationales: reasons without reasoners.

The controversy is whether we should take free-floating rationales seriously as real explanatory structure, or whether they are merely a manner of speaking—as-if teleology that we could in principle replace with ordinary causal history plus a selection process. Write an essay weighing the strongest arguments on both sides. Among the questions worth pressing: Can a free-floating rationale support counterfactuals, and does that matter? Are these rationales merely explanatory, or can they have genuine normative force? Does accepting them quietly reintroduce purpose or design into nature? Do not just list considerations; evaluate them and say where the burden of proof lies. Then reach a conclusion and defend it: should our ontology include reasons that belong to no reasoner?

End the essay with a final line in this exact form: \answer{...}, where the braces contain a short phrase stating your conclusion (for example, \answer{free-floating rationales are irreducible} or \answer{all reasons require reasoners}).
