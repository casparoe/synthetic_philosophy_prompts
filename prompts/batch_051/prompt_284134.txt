I am organizing the final session of a year-long workshop on methodology in the science of consciousness, and two camps have argued past each other the whole time. One camp defends heterophenomenology: the method of collecting subjects' verbal reports, taking them at face value as reports about how things seem to the subject, while remaining officially neutral about whether anything answering to those reports — a private stream of experience, qualia, a Cartesian theater, and so on — actually exists over and above the subject's beliefs about it. The other camp defends first-person methods: the view that consciousness science cannot content itself with untutored verbal reports, because most of what matters in experience goes unreported or gets flattened in ordinary vocabulary, so the science needs disciplined first-person access, such as trained introspection or a systematic phenomenological procedure, to supply its data. I suspect the two camps are partly kept apart by a shared assumption that they must choose between taking reports as infallible and treating them as mere noise.

Write the joint statement that a committed heterophenomenologist and a committed first-person methodologist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should include: the claims both sides can agree on, including claims about what verbal reports do and do not reveal, and about whether introspective skill can be improved with training; the precise formulations of their residual disagreement, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as a fair statement of its view; the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and, for each crux, the concrete experiment, case, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table whose rows give the neutral question, the heterophenomenologist's formulation, the first-person methodologist's formulation, the crux, and what each side predicts for the agreed test. Begin with the document itself, with no preface or commentary. Use American English. Do not include citations.
