I teach ethics and spend enough time around colleagues in biostatistics that I am fluent in the potential-outcomes and structural causal-model vocabulary: units and treatments, potential outcomes written as Y(a), the consistency assumption, the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (no interference and no hidden versions of a treatment), the do-operator, and counterfactual queries of the form what would y have been under treatment a. I want you to take one canonical argument from population ethics, the nonidentity problem, and restate it in exactly that vocabulary, instead of the philosophers' usual talk of possible people and wronging persons who would never have existed. To fix ideas, use the standard depletion case: a government must choose between a resource-exhausting policy and a conservation policy, and in two centuries the two choices will have produced wholly different populations; the lives under depletion would still be worth living, but worse than the lives under conservation. On the philosophically familiar reading, no future person is made worse off by depletion, because every particular person's existence depends on the very choice being evaluated, and yet many people want to say the choice is nonetheless wrong or worse. Translate every component of that argument into the causal vocabulary: what are the units, what are the treatments, to what objects do the potential outcomes attach, which comparisons are well-defined, and where consistency, SUTVA, or manipulability get strained.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the nonidentity problem translate cleanly into potential-outcomes language, which parts survive only if I amend the framework, for instance by letting units be person-positions rather than persons, or by taking whole population histories rather than individuals as the objects of treatment effects, and which parts have no causal-calculus counterpart at all. I want to know whether the resistance of the problem to causal notation shows that the biostatistical framework is too narrow for population ethics, or whether the philosophers' residual moral claim is doing more work than it advertises once the causal translation fails.
