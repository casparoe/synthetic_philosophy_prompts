Ok here's one I keep going back and forth on. I'd like a working definition of bad faith in the existentialist sense (Sartre's mauvaise foi), as opposed to plain self-deception, wishful thinking, or just being wrong about yourself. I want something I could actually apply to new cases without immediately shrugging and saying it depends.

Run your definition against each of these:

1. Sartre's café waiter, the one who plays at being a waiter a bit too hard, every gesture turned into a bit of waiter-performance, as if being a waiter were his entire essence.
2. Someone who breaks their diet and tells themself the cake being right there meant they couldn't help it.
3. A person who lies to a friend about where they were last night, knows perfectly well they're lying, feels bad about it, and has no interest in convincing themself otherwise.
4. A Catholic who says they just do what the Church says and don't see themselves as deciding these matters.
5. A recovering alcoholic who calls themself powerless over alcohol and uses that to refuse every drink, and it seems to be what keeps them sober.
6. A person on a date who lets the other person take their hand, then explains afterwards that the moment just sort of happened and that no one chose anything. (Sartre's flirt example, more or less.)
7. Someone who insists they're just not a math person and so never tries anything mathematical, despite having almost no actual track record of failure.
8. Someone who treats their dating-profile like a marketing account, talks about having a personal brand, and says the algorithm determines who they match with anyway.
9. A person who fully acknowledges they're choosing to stay in a job they hate, that they could leave, and that the tradeoff is theirs alone to own, and stays anyway.
10. A white-collar criminal who says they were just following orders and seems to half-believe it themself.

So: give the definition, run it against all ten cases, and say wich cases it gets wrong or leaves undecided. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. At the end, tell me which verdict you're least confident in, what case or argument would make you change the definition, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
