Act as a professional philosopher specializing in value theory, ancient ethics, and the metaphysics of harm. Think carefully and systematically. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles that could explain my case verdicts, test each candidate against every case, and then state which principle or small set of candidates comes closest. Be explicit about which of my present verdicts would have to be revised under that principle, and say what the principle implies about the cases I remain unsure about.

I have been rereading Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, especially the argument that death is nothing to us. His line, as I understand it, is roughly this: death is the absence of sensation; when death is present, we are not, and when we are present, death is not; so the dead are not there to undergo anything, and what one cannot undergo cannot be an evil for one. I do not want to evaluate that argument in isolation. I want to work out what I actually think about when death is bad for someone, by testing my gut verdicts against one another.

Here are my verdicts on a set of concrete cases.

Case 1 — Sudden early death. A thirty-five-year-old woman is killed instantly in a car crash. She loved her life, had young children, and was in the middle of a career she found meaningful. My verdict: her death was very bad for her.

Case 2 — Permanent loss of consciousness followed by death. A man suffers a severe stroke and enters a permanent vegetative state. He remains biologically alive in that state for six years before dying of pneumonia. My verdict: the permanent loss of consciousness was very bad for him, but the biological death six years later added only a small further bad, if any, because by then he no longer had the capacities for the goods his life had contained.

Case 3 — Death in dreamless sleep. A person dies painlessly in their sleep without ever waking. My verdict: the death is still bad for them, and in fact I am not sure the absence of fear makes it much less bad than a death preceded by weeks of terror. The terror may add a separate bad, but the death itself does not become negligible merely because it was not anticipated.

Case 4 — The never-conceived child. A couple could have conceived a child who, let us stipulate, would very likely have had a happy and flourishing life. They decide never to have children. My verdict: that decision is not bad for any possible child. There is no one who was harmed or deprived by the decision.

Case 5 — Posthumous destruction of a project. A scholar spends forty years building an archive and arranging for it to be preserved after her death so that future researchers can use it. After she dies, a bureaucrat destroys the archive out of spite. Everyone who knew the scholar is also dead, and no one now living knows what was lost. My verdict: this is bad for the scholar, and not merely in some impersonal sense. It seems to me that the destruction of her life’s project harms her, even though she never learns of it and no longer exists.

Case 6 — Anesthetic gap. A woman is under general anesthesia for eight hours during which her child takes first steps, witnessed by the rest of the family. She later learns of this and cares deeply about having missed it. My verdict: her missing the event was bad for her, even though at the time she was not conscious and could not have been aware of missing anything.

Case 7 — Nonidentity and early death. A child is born with a genetic condition that will cause death at twenty. The child’s life, though short, is happy by ordinary standards. My verdict: the early death is bad for this child, despite the fact that, if the parents had selected a different embryo without the condition, a different child would have existed and this child would never have been born at all. I find this verdict easy, but I cannot say why it is compatible with Case 4.

The principle I keep being drawn to is something like a deprivation account: an event is bad for a person when it prevents that person from having goods their actual life would otherwise have contained, or when it frustrates the person’s real and standing interests. But I do not know how to make that principle deliver both Case 4 and Case 5 at once, and I suspect it will also have trouble with whether the harm in Case 3 occurs before, at, or after the death.

Is there a coherent principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most or all of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Please state the principle precisely, explain how it handles each case, and say what it predicts about a further case I have not listed: a person who voluntarily chooses to have a painless operation that will erase all their memories of the previous ten years, including their deepest relationships, and then replace them with false but pleasant memories. I genuinely do not know whether that operation is bad for the pre-operation person in the same way that death is bad.
