I'm a policy analyst at a small AI-governance think tank. Every few weeks someone drops a case file on my desk about a standoff that could tip into an AI race — two labs squabbling over a model release, a government wound up about a foreign lab's compute build-out, a safety agreement quietly unravelling — and I have to write a short memo on whether it's likely to stay negotiated or turn into something costly. Right now everyone eyeballs it and we re-litigate the same cases over and over. I'd like an actual reusable procedure instead.

The only machinery I want it built on is Fearon's rationalist explanations for war: fighting is costly, so if a dispute nonetheless ends in a war or a race, you should suspect private information plus incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issue indivisibility. I know that machinery was built for states and shooting wars, not labs and training runs, so a large part of the job is the translation. Tell me explicitly which bits of the analogy carry over and which don't.

Please give me a checklist my colleagues could run on any case file in under an hour, before a senior analyst signs off and it goes into our weekly briefing. My colleagues are policy people, not game theorists. I want the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it is a values call rather than an analysis call. Flag the hard cases: private information and commitment problems present at once, parties who do not even accept that a race is a bad outcome, and cases where the costs of a race fall mostly on third parties so the rationalist premise may not hold. Be honest about where the checklist will misfire; I would rather have it honest then reassuring.

Then run the procedure on three cases, and make at least one come out with a conclusion I will not want to hand to my director — something like a monitored pause everyone says they want being predicted to collapse because it is a pure commitment problem with no enforcer. The three cases:
(1) Lab A has decent evidence that Lab B is quietly contracting for a huge share of next year's top-end accelerators and refuses to share its safety-case documents, citing commercial confidentiality. Both sides say they would prefer a verified pause, but Lab A is considering a matching compute grab.
(2) A frontier lab offers to open-weight a model if the regulator drops its licensing conditions. The regulator keeps adding conditions and will not name a version it would accept, and the lab reads that as proof the regulator never intended to settle.
(3) Two labs signed a private agreement not to train above a certain compute threshold for a year, there is no inspection mechanism, and the lab that is now behind has started publicly describing the agreement as a mistake.

Separately, tell me what is settled in the rationalist account and what is genuinely contested among people who know it — for example, whether issue indivisibility is a distinct mechanism or mostly a special case of commitment problems — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. British English, please.
