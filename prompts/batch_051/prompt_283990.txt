Recently I discovered that, during the negotiations over my departure from a small company, a former partner answered several of my pointed questions with statements that were individually true and that he knew I would assemble into a false picture of the firm's liabilities. The matter is now closed, but it has left me preoccupied with the distinction between lying and merely misleading. Two friends hold opposing views. The first defends an asymmetry view: to lie is to assert what one does not believe, and in doing so one abuses the institution of assertion itself, the standing invitation to rely on what is said; merely misleading, however artful, lets the hearer's own inferences do the damage, and so lacks that specific wrong even when the resulting belief is the same. The second defends a parity view: the moral work is done entirely by the intended deception and the abused trust, so a truthfully worded misdirection can wrong someone in the same way and to the same degree as a lie; for him, apparent differences in blame are traces of evidence, deniability, and how confident we are about intent, not of an intrinsic moral difference between the acts.

I have persuaded the two friends to run an adversarial collaboration and produce a single document both can sign. Please draft that document. It should contain, first, a numbered list of claims they agree on about truthfulness, assertion, trust, reliance, and the wrongs of deception, each worded so that neither would strain to sign it. Second, a precise statement of their residual disagreement, each position worded as its own advocate would word it. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to one would move at least one of them from their present position. Fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format the disagreement as a table after the agreed-claims section. If part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify that part and remove it from the table, explaining why.

I suspect one crux is whether the wrong of deception resides in the false belief produced or in the abuse of the act of asserting, and another is whether a victim has a different complaint against someone who told her something false than against someone who allowed her to believe something false. My own case may serve as one test, but if its legal and financial stakes make it a poor diagnostic, the document should say so and replace it with cases that do discriminate.
