I cover physics and philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and at a conference dinner a physicist I respect kept swatting away any causal language: causation is a relic of a bygone age, he said, we should just compute. He was using the line to dismiss a younger researcher who asked what causes a particle to take one path rather than another, and also to brush off the idea that a theory of quantum gravity should give us causes. But I've seen the same line do alot of different jobs in print: in one place its used to say fundamental physics has outgrown causation entirely, in another to say causal talk is fine as a heuristic but has no place in the final theory, and in yet another to suggest causation is a folk concept that will go the way of phlogiston. I'd like to write about this without just relitigating "correlation isn't causation."

What are the distinct, precise claims that this one-liner could be expressing? For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which readings would actually support the different jobs people give it: eliminating causes from fundamental theory, keeping them as a harmless convenience, or treating them as a folk theory. Then plant a flag: which reading do you think is the best version of the claim, how confident are you, and what argument or consideration would most likely change your mind — and where is your own reasoning most likely to break down?
