A friend and I had a long argument last night that started on de-extinction and somehow ended up entirely about gene drives, and I'm still turning it over. His line was the standard one: you dont release a gene drive into a wild population because once it's out, it's out—the edited trait keeps spreading, you cant recall it, so any ecological damage is effectively permanent, and that means the burden of proof should be basically impossible to meet. I kept pushing back with the newer designs: there are gene drives engineered to be self-limiting, so the drive is supposed to peter out after a fixed number of generations unless you keep releasing more of it, and there are also reversal drives designed to be released later to overwrite the first one. His answer was that this doesnt rescue the argument, because the argument was never really about the gene persisting—it was about how little you can know in advance about what a whole ecosystem will do, and a self-limiting drive is still a one-way bet. I don't know if he's right. What I actually want to know is: what happens to the irreversibility argument once reversibility is engineered in? Does it die, does it survive in a repaired form, or was it always a different argument wearing 'irreversible' as a disguise? Be explicit about which premise the self-limiting designs put pressure on, and whether a repaired version still has teeth against them.
