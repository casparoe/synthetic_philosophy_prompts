I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on a word my two main characters keep throwing at each other. The book is set in a small town where there's a fight over weather to aerial-spray for mosquitoes, and the two of them — an orchardist who sprays when she thinks she has to, and an entomologist who keeps telling her half the bugs she kills are doing real ecological work — argue about bugs constantly. Their arguments always come down to the word 'pest,' and I've realized I don't actually know what it means. Not in a way that would survive one of their arguments, anyway. The word seems to do two jobs at once: it describes something about the bug, and it quietly sneaks in a justification for killing it. I need a working definition of 'pest' that someone could actually apply without knowing in advance which side they're on.

Can you propose a definition and run it through these cases? For each one, tell me whether your definition counts it as a pest, and flag any case where the verdict surprises you or where you'd want to revise the definition after the fact:
1. a mosquito in a bedroom at night
2. a honeybee in a lavender bush
3. a clothes moth larva eating a wool suit
4. a native aphid on somebody's prize rose
5. a wasp nest inside a mailbox
6. a head louse on a child
7. a monarch caterpillar eating a milkweed leaf
8. a silverfish in an archive, slowly eating old papers
9. a cicada sitting on a sapling
10. a German cockroach in an apartment building

I'm mostly sure about 1, 2, 3, 6, and 10; the middle ones I keep changing my mind on. In particular, I want to know whether 'pest' is purely relative to human interests, whether it requires actual damage (and damage to what), and whether calling something a pest gives any moral permission to kill or spray it. The chapter where the town votes on spraying sort of hinges on that last part.
