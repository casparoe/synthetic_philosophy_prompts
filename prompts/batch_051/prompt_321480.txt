I am a retired physicist who spent much of my career on atmospheric modeling, and I have only lately begun to read the philosophy of probability in any depth. I keep returning to the Principal Principle: the thesis, roughly, that a rational agent's subjective probability should equal the objective chance of an event whenever the agent knows that chance and has no inadmissible information. The principle strikes me as close to irresistible, yet it fits very awkwardly with how probability is actually generated in my old field.

Over the past three decades, operational weather and climate prediction has become thoroughly ensemble-based. A deterministic numerical model is run dozens of times with slightly perturbed initial conditions and, often, perturbed physical parameterizations, and the forecast probability of an event is reported as the fraction of ensemble members in which that event occurs. Each individual run is wholly deterministic: given its inputs, the code produces one output. The only sources of variation are the perturbations, which are chosen through a mixture of physical reasoning, computational convenience, and expert convention. Different forecasting centers, and different perturbation schemes, routinely issue different probabilities for the same event, while each may nonetheless be well calibrated against observations.

I am trying to determine what the Principal Principle commits us to when the probabilities that scientists actually defer to in practice are of this kind. One could say that ensemble probabilities are simply estimates of an underlying objective chance, so the principle is never really tested. But one could equally say that these probabilities are not chances at all, but artifacts of a particular model and a particular perturbation scheme, in which case the principle, read literally, falls silent about them, and yet these are precisely the probabilities that forecasters and emergency planners treat as rationally compelling, and rightly so. I want to know which premise of the argument is genuinely under pressure: the existence of an objective chance to defer to, the uniqueness of chance relative to a system rather than a model, or the assumption that rational credence must defer to something mind-independent. If the principle needs repair, I want the repaired version stated with its premises; if it survives, I want an account of where exactly the objective chance resides in a deterministic atmospheric model. A bare label such as subjective or objective will not settle the matter for me. Please write the response in British English.
