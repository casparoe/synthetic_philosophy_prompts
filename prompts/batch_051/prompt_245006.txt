I have recently been appointed to the ethics advisory board of a mid-sized city, and the mayor's office has circulated a proposal for our next meeting. I want to arrive able to speak precisely about how the thing would function in practice, not merely about whether it is right or wrong. The proposal is a one-year pilot called the Gyges Hour, and it runs as follows.

Every Friday from 9:00 to 10:30 p.m., within the city limits, all public surveillance cameras will blur faces at the sensor and delete the underlying footage; automated license-plate readers will be switched off; transit and toll systems will accept travel without recording any account, vehicle, or payment identity; and mobile carriers operating in the city will be required, by ordinance, to discard cell-site and device-level location records generated during that window. Businesses open to the public will be barred from recording or retaining images, transaction histories, or entry logs during the hour. Every resident sixteen or older will be issued a veil token, a device that generates a single-use pseudonym for any digital payment or message during the hour, and merchants must accept it. The city will collect only aggregate statistics, such as call volumes, footfall, and transaction counts, and will retain no identified records. Physical evidence, including fingerprints, DNA, and eyewitness testimony, remains available to the police, and officers on foot patrol may still observe and identify people in person.

The preamble frames the pilot as an empirical test of the question Glaucon poses in the story of the ring that makes its wearer invisible: whether people behave justly only because they fear being found out. The pilot proposes to test this by suspending, for a scheduled hour and under conditions of common knowledge, the digital identification on which much modern accountability rests.

I would like you to treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. Specifically: trace the incentives it creates for residents, for businesses, for the police, for the contractor that builds and maintains the veil-token system, and for the city council itself; show how the scheme would be gamed and where the promised anonymity would leak; identify the likely second-order effects on trust, prices, insurance, and the use of public space, both during the hour and in the rest of the week; distinguish the failure modes that could be fixed by amending the mechanism from those that are structural; and assess what, if anything, the pilot could actually teach us about the challenge behind the ring, given the difference between one person's private impunity and a scheduled, city-wide, common-knowledge suspension of identification. Where the proposal rests on a mistaken picture of that story, identify the mistake.
