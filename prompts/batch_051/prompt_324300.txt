I have been reading about circular arguments and have been struck by a pattern that seems to me neither ordinary premise-circularity nor ordinary empirical confirmation. Suppose someone is challenged to show that their color vision is reliable. Naturally they cite their track record: yesterday they called the traffic light green and it was green, and last week they matched a paint chip to a wall and the colors agreed. But notice the structure of this evidence. The fact that the light was green is available to them only through the very faculty under challenge, and the same is true of the paint chip matching the wall. Every item in the track record was gathered by the faculty that the track record is supposed to vindicate. If a child argued for the reliability of a ruler by taking measurements with that same ruler, we would all see what was wrong at once; yet when the faculty is perception, memory, or basic arithmetic, the move can feel entirely respectable.

The same structure reappears with memory, which is shown to be reliable by remembered successes, and with arithmetic, which is shown to be trustworthy by arithmetical checks. It is not exactly begging the question in the textbook sense, since the conclusion is not restated among the premises; it is more like the faculty is being allowed to testify on its own behalf and being believed. Is there an established name or concept in epistemology for this specific configuration? And if there is one, could you say precisely what makes it different from ordinary circularity and from a merely informal appeal to a track record? I am asking less for a label than for the carving: which cases of self-reliance on a faculty are legitimate, which are vicious, and on what principle the line falls. If the apparent respectability is an illusion, I would like to know why it feels so much better than the ruler case.
