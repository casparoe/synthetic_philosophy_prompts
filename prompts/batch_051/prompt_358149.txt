People often use Montaigne’s Of Cannibals as a sort of parable for cultural relativism. The work it is made to do is roughly this: just as Montaigne’s Tupinambá seemed barbarous to European observers only because they were foreign, so any culture’s practices look wrong to us only because we judge them by the standard of our own culture; therefore we should suspend judgement. The passage I have in mind is the one in which Montaigne reports that the Tupinambá do not eat their prisoners for nourishment, as the ancient Scythians supposedly did, but as an expression of extreme revenge, and that they keep their captives for a long time, treating them well, before the final ritual. If I am remembering the essay correctly, he then says we may call these people barbarous by the rules of reason, but not in comparison with ourselves, because we surpass them in every kind of barbarism. I want an audit of this analogy. Give me the correspondences: what plays the role of the Tupinambá, the European observer, the local standard, and the alleged barbarism in modern disputes about judging other cultures. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion. In particular, is Montaigne really doing what the analogy-sayers claim—offering a non-judgemental relativism—or is he using the Tupinambá as a vantage point from which to judge European practices more harshly? If the latter, what does that do to the relativist conclusion the analogy is normally used to support?
