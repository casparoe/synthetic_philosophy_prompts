I have been working through the situationist critique of virtue ethics—roughly, the argument associated with Gilbert Harman and John Doris that ordinary attributions of robust character traits are empirically unsupported, because human behavior is far more sensitive to local features of situations, such as hurry, mood, ambient cues, and social pressure, than to stable dispositions, and because cross-situational consistency in behavior is surprisingly weak. The canonical exhibits are familiar: seminarians in a hurry failing to stop for a person in evident need, people becoming markedly more helpful after finding a coin planted in a telephone booth, obedience shifting dramatically with physical proximity to an authority figure, and the like.

The development I am trying to think through is the replication crisis in social psychology and, in particular, the uneven picture it has produced for the situationist evidence base. Suppose we accept a charitable version of that picture: several of the most iconic paradigms have been broadly reproduced, but a long tail of the smaller situational, mood, and priming effects that gave the critique much of its sweep has failed to replicate or has shrunk to trivial effect sizes. What becomes of the situationist argument under those conditions? Does the critique of virtue ethics depend essentially on the famous experiments, or only on a weaker and better-replicated claim that situational variance matters and that global trait-behavior correlations are modest? And if it survives only in the weaker form, is that weaker form still strong enough to do the original work of undercutting the attribution of robust virtues and vices to ordinary people—or does it leave intact a chastened, more local or situation-indexed virtue ethics? I am interested in the logical state of the argument, not in a historical survey of the literature: identify which premise the replication picture bears on, and say whether the conclusion can be rebuilt.
