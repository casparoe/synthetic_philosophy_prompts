A friend who has spent the past year reading popular probability books tried to convince me that the birthday paradox deflates almost every anecdote about an uncanny coincidence. His summary, as close as I can reconstruct it, went like this: "In any group of 23 people there is a better-than-even chance that two of them share a birthday, even though the chance that someone shares your birthday is tiny. The paradox dissolves once you stop asking about one person matching one specific date and instead count the pairs: 23 people make 253 pairs, each of which is an opportunity for a match. The general lesson is that what feels like a striking coincidence is usually just the birthday effect at scale. We notice thousands of things a day, so the number of possible pairings between an event and a thought or memory is enormous, and some of those pairings are bound to line up in ways that look meaningful. So when someone tells you they dreamed about their sister the night she crashed her car, or thought about an old friend seconds before he called, the right response is a shrug. However small the chance of that particular coincidence may have been, the chance that that person would experience some coincidence over a lifetime of noticing is huge. Coincidence anecdotes are therefore evidence of next to nothing, and we should be no more impressed by them than by two people in a room sharing a birthday."

I have checked the arithmetic of the birthday example itself and it seems right, so my difficulty is not with the calculation. What I cannot tell is whether the conclusion is actually licensed by the calculation, and I do not want to reject a good argument merely because its conclusion feels too dismissive. My inclination is that somewhere between the 253-pairs observation and the claim that coincidence anecdotes are evidence of next to nothing the reasoning overreaches, but I cannot pinpoint the step. I would like an accuracy audit of his summary: which claims are accurate as stated; which are subtly wrong or conflate distinct claims; where exactly the move from the birthday result to the dismissal of coincidence anecdotes is or is not justified; what a careful defender of a more modest version would say; and whether his final practical verdict — that we should shrug at such anecdotes — survives the corrections.
