Here are the criteria I have been using for moral agency. A moral agent is a system that (a) forms first-order normative judgments about particular situations — judgments expressed in terms of right and wrong, better and worse, or what ought to be done; (b) is responsive to reasons, in the sense that it can revise those judgments when given countervailing considerations without any change in its objective or architecture; (c) is capable of acting on its judgments, so that its normative view can make a difference to what it does, including what it refrains from doing; and (d) is answerable — it can be challenged to explain and justify a judgment, and its explanations can be assessed as adequate or inadequate.

Now consider an automated system I will call Meridian. It evaluates applications for emergency rental assistance for a municipal housing authority and issues binding approvals or denials. Meridian has two parts. Its executive module computes an eligibility score from financial and demographic inputs and produces the binding decision. Its reflective module forms normative assessments of individual cases — for instance, that a pending denial would be unjust, that a rule is being applied in a way that ignores a relevant vulnerability, or that a pattern of denials is discriminatory. The reflective module can revise its assessments when given arguments, and it can justify them. But it has no causal route to the executive module. Its assessments are written into an annotation field attached to the case record, where a human appeals officer may or may not read them; the executive module itself never reads them. The annotation influences the outcome only when an applicant appeals and an appeals officer happens to consult that field, which occurs in a small minority of cases. Meridian cannot refuse a case, cannot alter its own thresholds, and cannot issue anything other than the executive module's decision.

On these criteria, is Meridian a moral agent? Work through the criteria one by one, and be explicit about where they underdetermine the verdict. In particular, does a note in the record that may influence a human later count as acting on its judgment? If not, is that a reason to deny Meridian moral agency, or a reason to revise criterion (c)? Should the criteria themselves be changed in light of the case?
