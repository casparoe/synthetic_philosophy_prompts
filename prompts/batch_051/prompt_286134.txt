You are a philosopher of science with a background in game theory and experimental design, and you are often asked to take a vague conceptual claim and turn it into a concrete, discriminating test. Please think carefully, brainstorm the strongest version of the claim before designing the experiment, and answer in American English.

A colleague of mine claims that superrationality is a distinct, real mode of practical reasoning — not just a philosophical thought experiment — and that ordinary people can be induced to use it under the right conditions. As a reminder, superrationality is the idea that in a symmetric one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, a rational player should choose the action that would produce the best outcome if the other player, who is in exactly the same situation and using exactly the same reasoning, acts identically. Since mutual cooperation is better for each than mutual defection, superrationality recommends cooperation even though defection is the dominant act.

The colleague's claim is stronger than the observation that people sometimes cooperate in one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas. The claim is: when people explicitly recognize that their own reasoning and the other player's reasoning are symmetric in the relevant sense, they will cooperate at rates above and beyond what social preferences, confusion about dominance, group identity, or a simple belief that the other will cooperate can explain; and when the symmetry is broken, the effect disappears. In short, superrationality is a real mechanism at the level of reasoning, not a label for unexplained cooperation.

I would like you to design the strongest feasible experiment that could separate this claim from its main rivals. In your answer: (1) specify the game or decision task, the conditions, and the dependent measure; (2) spell out the exact predictions of the superrationality hypothesis, including the interaction pattern it predicts between symmetry and cooperation; (3) give the comparison conditions or manipulations that would cleanly rule out altruism or social preferences, confusion about dominance, team identity or team reasoning, and the simple belief that the other player will cooperate; and (4) say what a pattern of results favoring the colleague's claim would and would not establish. In particular, I want you to be candid about whether any experimental design could show that superrationality is a distinct mode of reasoning as opposed to a useful redescription, and whether that distinction is partly conceptual rather than empirical.
