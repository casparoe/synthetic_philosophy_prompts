I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on has a long set-piece where three strangers get stuck in an airport lounge and argue about affirmative action. One's a retired US university admissions dean, one's a Dalit engineer from India who got his first government job through a reserved slot, and one's a Norwegian corporate lawyer who's served on boards under their quota law. I want the argument to be actually good, not each character reciting the talking points they'd have seen online.

Here's the tangle I keep running into. The American system, at least as it operated for decades before the Supreme Court ended it, was insistant that race could count as one plus factor in a holistic read of an applicant but equally insistent that there be no quotas, no separate tracks, no point bonuses. The Indian system is the opposite of squeemish: fixed percentages of university seats, government jobs, even legislative seats are reserved by caste, and it's openly defended as compensation for centuries of caste oppression. The Norwegian system is a hard numerical quota too, but for sex on corporate boards: at least forty percent of each sex on the boards of public limited companies, with dissolution as the ultimate threat. So the American character thinks the Indian system reduces people to their caste, the Indian character thinks the American system is a quota too cowardly to admit it's one, and the Norwegian character thinks both of them are being dramatic and a boardroom quota is just a sensible staffing rule.

What I want for the novel is a clear picture of what each system actually presupposes philosophically. What's the wrong each one takes itself to be fixing: past injustice, ongoing discrimination, or just the wasted value of diversity? What does each assume about whether merit can be measured before you've decided who's eligible, or only after? Does each system assume the point is to help individuals, or to change which group ends up holding power, or something else again? Are these three systems just three applications of one underlying view of fairness, or do they genuinely conflict? And if each system had to be made honest about its own presuppositions, what would concretely have to change in how it works—say, what a US-style process would look like if it were rebuilt on the Indian theory, and vice versa? Also tell me where each system is underdetermined, where more than one theory could justify the same practice, because I suspect the characters are each going to pick the interpretation that's easiest to attack.
