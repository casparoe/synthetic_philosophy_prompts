In a recent discussion about whether aging should be classified as a disease and anti-aging medicine should be funded, a participant dismissed the whole proposal with the line "death is what gives life meaning." The move felt too quick to me, but I could not say exactly what was wrong with it. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims someone might be making with that slogan? I would like you to separate readings along the relevant dimensions: whether "death" means the event of dying, the fact of finitude, an upper bound on lifespan, or one's awareness of mortality; what kind of "meaning" is at stake (value, purpose, narrative shape, motivation, felt intensity); whether the claim is descriptive or normative; and whose death, at what point, and across what sort of lifespan it is supposed to operate. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which reading or readings would actually be needed to support the claim that we should not pursue life extension, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. I want a menu of readings with verdicts rather than a single "correct" interpretation, so I can see where the slogan is doing real argumentative work and where it merely sounds profound. Please present the readings as a numbered list with a two- or three-sentence verdict on each, and close with a paragraph on which reading the participant's conclusion actually required. Hard limit: 800 words.
