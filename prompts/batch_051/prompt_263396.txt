I sit on a metropolitan sustainability commission. A proposal before us would set a binding 2045 target of 85% regional self-sufficiency in food, energy, and critical materials, with annual progress reviews and import restrictions for goods the region could feasibly produce. Staff object that the region lacks arable land and certain minerals, that local production is often more expensive and higher-carbon than imports, and that the target would hit low-income households hardest. Supporters reply that the goal is not autarky for its own sake but resilience and civic capacity, and that without a binding target localist rhetoric never becomes action. I need to circulate a short decision memo before Thursday's meeting recommending approve, reject, or approve with modified targets.

The philosophical question is how to apply Aristotle's distinction between mere self-sufficiency in necessaries and the self-sufficiency appropriate to a political community. In the Politics he writes: "A state consisting of too few people will not be self-sufficing (which is an essential quality of a state), and one consisting of too many, though self-sufficing in the mere necessaries, will be so in the way in which a nation is, and not as a state, since it will not be easy for it to possess constitutional government—for who will command its over-swollen multitude in war? or who will serve as its herald, unless he have the lungs of a Stentor?"

Draft the memo. It must recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame; it must use the passage to articulate a principled test for which goods or capacities a region should seek to secure locally and which it may safely obtain through exchange; and it must engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly. Indicate where the passage helps and where it fails to settle a modern dispute about autarky, and end with a one-sentence vote recommendation.
