I'm a first-year PhD student in a philosophy of language seminar, and my professor has a habit of answering nearly every question about truth with the same line: "The truth predicate is nothing more than a device for disquotation." Last week a student asked whether "true" picks out a real property the way "charged" or "massive" does, and whether there could be a substantive theory of what truth consists in; this week another student asked whether the liar sentence "This sentence is not true" shows that the disquotational story cannot be complete, since combining the T-schema ("snow is white" is true iff snow is white) with classical logic seems to yield a contradiction. Both times the professor just restated the slogan and moved on. I understand the T-schema as a schema, but I do not understand how the slogan is supposed to settle everything it is used to settle.

Write the document I can bring to office hours: a careful menu of the distinct precise claims that "truth is nothing more than disquotation" could be used to express, ranging from minimal and platitudinous to strong and revisionary. Please organize the readings so I can compare them along at least these dimensions: what the claim says about the linguistic function of the truth predicate, what it says about what a theory of truth must explain, and what it says about the metaphysics of truth (whether there is a property or nature for "true" to name). For each reading, say whether it is trivial or substantial, whether a deflationist can maintain it, and what a non-deflationist would reject. Then address the work the slogan was actually being made to do: which readings genuinely license treating the "real property" question as misguided, which readings are being quietly smuggled in when the slogan is used that way, and which readings, if any, remain exposed to the liar paradox.
