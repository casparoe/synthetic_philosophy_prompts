In my course on the philosophy of translation we have been discussing Douglas Hofstadter's argument that machine translation is shallow: his claim is that corpus-trained translation systems, however fluent their output becomes, do not understand the text they translate, because they manipulate statistical regularities over strings rather than constructing an internal model of the situation, imagery, and concepts a passage is about. Translating well, on this view, requires representing the scene behind the words, and merely adequate output does not show that such representation is present.

The systems Hofstadter criticized were dedicated translation engines trained to map source text to target text. It now seems natural to ask whether the argument survives the rise of large language models. These are not translation systems at all: they are general next-token predictors trained on vast multilingual corpora, yet they often produce translations that are more idiomatic, register-sensitive, and context-aware than their predecessors, resolving ambiguities and adjusting tone in ways that look, on the surface, like the conceptual modeling Hofstadter said was missing.

Does Hofstadter's argument survive this development, or does it need repair? More specifically, which premise is under the most pressure: that genuine translation requires representing meaning, that corpus-trained next-token predictors cannot acquire such representations, or that fluent, context-sensitive output does not establish understanding? And if a repaired version remains, what weaker but still significant claim does it support? I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in a verdict about particular systems.
