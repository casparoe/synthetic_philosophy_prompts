I've spent this term in a working group on scientific inference, and last week a colleague in evolutionary biology laid out the following argument against treating inference to the best explanation (IBE) as a single, usable rule of rational inference. I have the persistent sense that the argument proves too much and that something is being smuggled in, but I cannot localize the failure, and I'd like a careful audit of it rather than a generic defense of IBE.

(1) IBE directs us to believe the best available explanation of the evidence. (2) Such a directive is in good working order only if, in typical cases of theory choice, there is a determinate fact of the matter about which candidate explanation is best. (3) Explanatory goodness is not one property but a bundle of several distinct virtues: fit with the data, simplicity, scope, fertility, conservatism with respect to established theory, and precision. (4) These virtues routinely conflict: the best-fitting hypothesis is often less simple, the more unifying hypothesis is often less conservative, and the more conservative hypothesis is often less precise. (5) There is no principled, generally binding scheme for trading these virtues off against one another; how much simplicity may be sacrificed for fit, or conservatism for fertility, varies with the investigator's background interests, the costs of different errors, and the conventions of the discipline. (6) A ranking of hypotheses whose trade-offs vary with interests, error costs, and disciplinary conventions in this way is not an objective ranking of hypotheses at all. (7) Therefore, in typical cases of theory choice there is no objective fact about which explanation is best. (8) Hence, inference to the best explanation is not a well-defined, objective rule of rational inference; at best it is a loose label for a preference that shifts with audience and context.
