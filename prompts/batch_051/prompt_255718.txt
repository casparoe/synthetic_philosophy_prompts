You are an expert in microeconomics and applied ethics, and you are especially careful about turning loose policy talk into precise, testable claims. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I want to actually understand what people mean when they repeat one particular market saying.

In arguments about rent control and minimum wage laws, I keep seeing the same move. Someone proposes capping rents or raising the minimum wage, and a commenter replies with some version of the idea that high prices are their own remedy. The suggestion is usually that governments should leave prices alone, because the pain of high prices is exactly what causes new construction, new employers, or new entry, after which prices fall. Price controls, on this view, interrupt that self-correction.

I am not asking for a name for this idea or a textbook definition. I want you to take that slogan apart into the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. What is the actual causal mechanism being claimed? Whose behavior is supposed to change, over what time scale, and with what assumptions about competition, entry, demand, and the options of people who are priced out in the meantime? I can already see at least three different readings: a fairly trivial one about prices carrying information; a stronger one claiming that market self-correction is fast and reliable enough that policy should generally not interfere; and a near-tautology about high prices eventually encouraging more supply. There may be more, and the boundaries between them matter to me more than the labels.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible empirical claim, or a strong claim that is often false in real housing and low-wage labor markets. Then tell me which reading someone would have to rely on if they want to use the saying to conclude that rent control or a minimum wage increase is harmful, and whether that reading actually gets them to that conclusion without smuggling in extra empirical or ethical assumptions. I am especially interested in where the ethical assumption hides inside an apparently purely economic slogan.

Start by brainstorming several possible mechanisms before distilling them into a clean menu of readings. I want the kind of breakdown I could bring into an argument with someone who acts as though the saying settles the matter.
