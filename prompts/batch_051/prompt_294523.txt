You're a philosopher of aesthetics who works on games and interactive art. I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about when a game is art, going by my gut reactions, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them — or tell me which verdicts I need to give up. Please think carefully, and feel free to brainstorm a couple candidates before settling; I'd rather have an honest pattern than a tidy one.

Here are my calls:

1. A walking simulator with no real choices, mostly environment and narration, that genuinely moved me. I'd call that art — but I also feel the game part didn't do much; it could of just been a short film.

2. Tetris played at a high level. I find it beautiful, like watching a great dancer or a good proof. But I stop short of calling it art. Aesthetic value without art, I guess.

3. A Mario 64 speedrun. I think the runner's performance can be art — the route, the improvisation, the style. But the game itself is more like an instrument. Player as artist, game as not-art.

4. Watching someone else play a choice-heavy story game on stream. I got a lot out of it, and I'd say the value belongs to the original game, plus maybe the streamer as a performer — but I wouldn't exactly call the stream itself art.

5. A game where you play a border inspector and the decisions about who to let through slowly make you feel complicit and guilty. That's my clearest case of a game being art, but here the art seems to live in the choices and the mechanical constraints, not really the story or graphics.

6. An abstract strategy game like Go. The rule system has an elegance I find beautiful the way a mathematical structure is beautiful, but I don't call it art. And then I realize I wouldn't call a beautiful proof art either.

7. An idle clicker. Not art, and I'm not even sure it's a game in the same sense. But I can't say how it differs from Tetris, which is also all mechanics and no story.

The snag is obvious: I want to say the beauty of Tetris or Go is real but non-artistic, while the weight of the choices in case 5 is exactly what makes it art. But then why is a skilled Tetris player's movement a dance and not art, while the speedrun is art? And why does the story in case 1 make it art (it could have been a film), while the choices in case 5 make it art for a completely different reason — are there two different kinds of game-art here, or one principle covering both?

Can you state the best principle (or two or three candidates) that captures these verdicts, as precisely as you can, and say which of my calls it can't accommodate? (b) a tabletop RPG session that's played badly in terms of skill but produces a genuinely compelling story at the table — is the game art, is the players' play art, or neither?
