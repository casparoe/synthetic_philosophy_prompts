An argument from my epistemology seminar has been nagging at me, and I cannot find where it comes apart. It starts from a perfectly ordinary piece of knowledge and seems to end by giving me permission to ignore counterevidence without examining it.

Let p be the proposition that my colleague spent all of yesterday in Chicago. I know p: this morning she gave me a detailed account of the sessions she attended there yesterday, and the hotel records show she had checked in before them. Now let e be the proposition that my colleague was in New York yesterday. I know that p entails not-e; the two cannot both be true. This morning a mutual friend, who is generally reliable, emailed me with plausible specifics to say that he saw her at a café in New York yesterday. The argument from the seminar runs as follows.

(1) I know that p.
(2) I know that p entails not-e.
(3) If I know that p, and I know that p entails not-e, then I can deduce not-e and thereby come to know not-e.
(4) I have just deduced not-e from p.
(5) So I know not-e.
(6) If I know not-e, then I know that the email's claim is false.
(7) If I know that a piece of apparent counterevidence is false, then I may rationally dismiss it without further investigation.
(8) Therefore, I may rationally dismiss the email without further investigation: I need not ask my colleague, check the friend's reliability, consider whether he saw a lookalike, or otherwise look into the matter.

The troubling part is that nothing in the argument depends on the details of Chicago, New York, or the email. It appears to show that, for any proposition I know and any apparent counterevidence to it, I may set the counterevidence aside before examining it, simply because it conflicts with what I already know. That would collapse the distinction between rational confidence and dogmatism, and it would let a scientist with a well-confirmed theory dismiss a contrary experimental result unread. I want to know which premise or inferential step is the right one to reject, why it looked acceptable on first pass, and what a coherent position gives up by rejecting it. I would most like to keep (3), since abandoning single-premise closure seems to block even trivial deductive extensions of knowledge, but I am prepared to hear that this or (7) is where the argument goes wrong.
