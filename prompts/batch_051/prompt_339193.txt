I was arguing about this with a friend the other day and its been bugging me. We both agreed you owe a real debt of gratitude to someone who helps you on purpose — the coworker who covers your shift so you can get to the hospital, the stranger who deliberately shoves you out of the way of a truck. But neither of us felt the same kind of gratitude toward the fact that a bus line happened to open right by our apartment, or that we happened to be born somewhere with decent schools, even though those accidents of luck and history have quietly done more for us than any single person ever will. And it's not just about size: someone who accidentally bumps you out of the path of a falling brick gets a startled "oh, thanks," while someone who pushes you out of the way on purpose might live in your thoughts for years, even if their push cost them nothing.

The asymmetry I want to press on is this: we save full-blooded gratitude — the kind that feels like owing something — for agents, preferably ones who meant to help, and we treat luck, impersonal systems, and the past as things to be merely glad about, no matter how much we receive from them. Is there a defensible principle that makes deliberate or at least personal help the right sort of object for gratitude, rather than just the most convenient sort? Or is this mostly the fact that you can't send a thank-you note to the 20th century, and on reflection we'd do better to say you really can owe the past, or luck, or an institution, a form of gratitude — and if so, what would that consist in? If you had to keep part of the asymetry and give up another part, which side would you defend?
