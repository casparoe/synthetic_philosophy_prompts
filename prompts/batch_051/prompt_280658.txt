im trying to get a handle on a variant of a Frankfurt case and i want someone to actually work through the logic rather than survey the literature. Take the classic setup: Jones is deciding whether to vote for a candidate, and Black has rigged a device that will make Jones vote for the candidate if Jones shows any sign of deciding not to; but Black never has to trigger the device because Jones goes ahead and votes for the candidate on his own. The standard move is to say Jones is responsible even though he couldnt have done otherwise, so the principle of alternative possibilities is false.

Now vary just one parameter. Black's device, once triggered, doesnt determine Jones's vote. It biases it: if the device fires, Jones votes for the candidate with probability 90 percent and votes the other way with probability 10 percent. Black has no second chance after the device fires; 90 percent is the best the device can do. Jones knows nothing about Black or the device, and as in the original case the device never actually fires.

My question is whether Jones's responsibility in the actual sequence changes at all when the override is probabilistic rather than deterministic. Does he now have an alternative possibility in the sense that matters for the principle, and if so, can the principle do the work its defenders want it to do? Also, suppose we keep lowering the device's success probability: 60 percent, then 40 percent, then barely above chance. At what point, if any, does this stop being a Frankfurt-style counterexample to the principle of alternative possibilities and become a case where Jones plainly retains alternative possibilities? I want you to argue for a definite line and defend it, not just lay out the options.
