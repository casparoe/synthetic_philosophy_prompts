I'm in a philosophy of social science course, and we've been reading J. S. Mill's *A System of Logic* alongside the methodological individualism/holism dispute. The passage that keeps giving me trouble is from Book VI, Chapter I ("Introductory Remarks"):

> Although several other sciences have emerged from this state at a comparatively recent date, none now remain in it except those which relate to man himself, the most complex and most difficult subject of study on which the human mind can be engaged. ... But the laws of Mind, and, in even a greater degree, those of Society, are so far from having attained a similar state of even partial recognition, that it is still a controversy whether they are capable of becoming subjects of science in the strict sense of the term: and among those who are agreed on this point, there reigns the most irreconcilable diversity on almost every other.

My seminar is stuck on how this passage bears on the individualism/holism question. One reading says Mill is basically an individualist: social science is harder because society is composed of individuals, so you have to aggregate laws of mind, but the laws themselves are ultimately psychological laws. Another reading says his distinction between laws of Mind and laws of Society treats the social level as a distinct object of inquiry, which sounds like an opening toward holism or at least toward non-reductive levels of law. I want help understanding what this passage, read closely in context, actually commits him to. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Also separate what the text clearly establishes from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Mill and on philosophy of social science, and do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the scholarly consensus.
