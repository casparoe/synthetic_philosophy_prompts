As a novelist, I am perhaps unusually well placed to be struck by an analogy a mathematician once offered me, and I should like it examined with some care. I had been troubling over a familiar difficulty concerning mathematics. Mathematics appears to be about objects — numbers, sets, functions — that are not located in space or time and cannot cause or affect anything. And yet we, causal creatures in a causal world, come to hold beliefs about these objects that are reliable, stable and widely shared in ways that seem too strong to be luck. It is not evident how our minds could make contact with something causally inert.

The mathematician's response was that I had mistaken mathematics for natural history. Numbers, he said, are not like stars or stones; they are like characters in a novel. When I write that a character has a scar on his left hand, I thereby make it true in the novel that he has a scar; when I leave his aunt's name unmentioned, there is no fact of the matter about it. A careful reader can come to know a great deal about him without ever meeting him, simply by working out what the text commits one to. Mathematics, on this view, is the same sort of thing: axioms are authorial stipulations, proof is the working-out of what is true in the fiction, and the demand for causal contact with 'number itself' is as confused as the demand to shake hands with a character. The analogy was doing serious work: it was meant to show that my difficulty rests on a category mistake and dissolves once mathematics is seen aright.

I should like this analogy audited point by point. What maps onto what, and where does the mapping begin to strain? Which disanalogies are fatal to the mathematician's use of the analogy, and which are merely picturesque? I am especially interested in what the fiction model quietly imports that mathematics does not share — authorial contingency, the absence of fact beyond what is stipulated, the possibility of revision or inconsistency in the canon — and whether the original difficulty is genuinely dissolved or only moved inside the fiction, since the stability and consensus of mathematical belief would then themselves need explaining. I am less interested in a label for the view than in the load-bearing capacity of the image; if the analogy cannot carry that weight, say whether some neighbouring analogy would do better, and how it should be drawn so as not to mislead.
