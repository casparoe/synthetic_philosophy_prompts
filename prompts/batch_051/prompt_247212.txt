I'm a science and ideas journalist, and I've sat for several years on the board of a small nonprofit that preserves early educational software. We've just been offered the complete source code and build assets of a mid-1990s chemistry simulation that was widely used in classrooms and later became a touchstone in the hobbyist abandonware scene. The company that made it dissolved in 2001; its founder died in 2012; no successor firm ever asserted rights over the catalog; and two years of good-faith attempts to find an heir or assignee have gone nowhere. A member of the original development team, who does not claim to own any rights, wants to donate the materials so the simulation can be modernized and made freely available. Our charter lets us preserve and provide access to "orphaned" materials, but it does not define "orphaned," and it is silent on public redistribution. I've noticed that board members are already using "orphaned," "abandoned," and "unowned" as if they meant the same thing, and I suspect that conflation is doing a lot of the work in their initial positions.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame — for instance, preserve only; preserve and provide controlled access; or preserve and release publicly, with whatever conditions you'd attach — and it should directly face the question whether "the owner is gone" entails "nobody owns this anymore." Give the strongest argument on the losing side its due rather than dismissing it. Since our board includes no lawyers, keep the reasoning conceptual rather than case-law-based, and flag any point where the rules of a particular legal system would change the recommendation. Finally, please clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who think carefully about orphaned works, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
