I'm a political scientist interested in how institutions process unwelcome information, and I've been rereading Thucydides' *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book 3, which describes Athens' initial response to reports that Mytilene was preparing to revolt. He writes: "However, the Athenians, distressed by the plague, and by the war that had recently broken out and was now raging, thought it a serious matter to add Lesbos with its fleet and untouched resources to the list of their enemies; and at first would not believe the charge, giving too much weight to their wish that it might not be true." Soon afterward, when the Athenians could no longer deny what was happening, the assembly voted to kill all Mytilenian men and enslave the women and children—only to reverse the decision the next day. I want to understand whether this sequence expresses a testable claim about democratic assemblies: that under threat, a collective body systematically shades its beliefs toward what it wants to be true, and then overcorrects into punitive fury when forced to confront the facts. As an expert on the epistemology of democracy and collective decision-making, think carefully with me about how one could test that claim as a general proposition rather than just as an interpretation of one ancient text. Concretely, propose a test or natural experiment that could distinguish wishful-thinking-then-punitive-overcorrection from alternative patterns, such as ordinary Bayesian updating under high stakes, or rational retaliation once a hostile plan is confirmed. Say what observations or measurements would count for and against the claim, what confounds you would need to control for, and what a positive result would and would not settle about democratic competence. I want the design logic only, not a bibliography or a summary of the ancient evidence.
