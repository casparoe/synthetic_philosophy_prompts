I coordinate a small decision-theory reading group, and we have spent the past several weeks trying to turn our discussions into a working tool: a shared spreadsheet that screens possible donations for our student chapter by multiplying a rough probability estimate for each outcome by a rough estimate of the value of that outcome. Pascal's mugging has become the sticking point. As I understand the problem, any procedure that multiplies probabilities by outcomes can be hijacked by an adversary who keeps inflating the stakes on an unfalsifiable threat, and the standard remedy is said to be a minimum-probability cutoff: if the assessed likelihood of an outcome falls below some epsilon, the spreadsheet simply rounds that outcome down to zero, so that threats premised on malicious simulations or other unverifiable mechanisms count for nothing regardless of the payoff claimed. But I cannot see how to set epsilon without arbitrariness. If I choose one in a million, I end up dismissing genuine low-probability causes, and in any case a mugger can tailor the story to sit just above the line; if I choose one in a trillion, the protection is so weak that we are back to the original vulnerability; and if I choose zero, the cutoff does no work at all. Is there a principled way to derive the right epsilon, perhaps from the size of the stakes or from the cost of being wrong? And does a hard cutoff actually escape the money-pump and Dutch-book objections, or does it merely relocate the problem to the choice of cutoff itself?
