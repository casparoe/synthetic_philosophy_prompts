I retired from atmospheric chemistry a few years ago and have lately been reading epistemology with the enthusiasm of a late convert. The issue that keeps drawing me back is higher-order evidence: what one should do upon acquiring reason to think one's own judgment about a question is unreliable, even when one cannot find any specific fault in one's reasoning. From my working life I carried over a firm policy on this, which I call the locate-the-error rule.

The rule is this. If I have worked through a question carefully and reached a conclusion, I do not revise that conclusion merely because I can cite a generic reason to distrust my own thinking — that I am tired, that I have a stake in the answer, that a known bias probably applies to me, that a colleague warns me I am exactly the sort of person who gets this wrong. I go on holding and acting on the conclusion until I can identify the particular step at which my reasoning failed and redo it. Here is a recent case. A younger colleague sent me an analysis of long-term storm-frequency data that undermined a result I had published years earlier. I had an obvious interest in finding his analysis wrong, and I noticed that his conclusion made me defensive. But I could not at first locate any specific error in his treatment. Under my rule, I did not moderate my belief on the spot; I continued to act as if my original result stood, and I withheld public comment until I had either found the fault or failed to find one after redoing the calculations myself. That felt like discipline, but it could also have been self-protection.

My question is whether the rule is defensible, or whether it is a bias wearing the costume of a principle. The state my rule is designed to prevent — believing something while also believing that, by my own standards, my belief is not adequately supported, sometimes called epistemic akrasia — has always struck me as something to avoid, but I have never seriously tested that conviction. The opposite view would be that higher-order evidence about my own unreliability is still evidence about the world, and that a rational person should sometimes let it move her even when she cannot convert it into a located first-order mistake. Under what conditions, if any, is the rule better than the alternative of moderating one's confidence whenever one has good reason to suspect one's own faculties? Can it be restated so as to keep whatever is good in it without immunizing me against genuine information about my own unreliability? Please treat this as a question about the policy rather than about my character. And if the task as I have posed it rests on a confused separation between first-order reasoning and higher-order evidence, or cannot be done well for some other reason, please say so plainly rather than working around it.
