I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology course, and every year a couple of students defend the propensity interpretation of fitness with the same analogy. They say fitness is like the bias of a coin: a coin can be biased toward heads even if it never lands heads, so an organism can be fitter then another even if it actually dies young or leaves no offspring. The analogy is doing a lot of work in their papers—it's meant to show that fitness is a probabilistic disposition rather than actual reproductive success, and therefore that "survival of the fittest" isn't just the vacuous claim that the survivors survive. One student pushed back that a coin's bias is a stable physical feature of the coin, while fitness is environment-relative and can shift from one generation to the next, so the analogy may be quietly smuggling in a picture of fitness as a fixed, built-in property. I'd like something I can hand to the whole seminar. Audit the analogy properly: give the correspondence between a biased coin (or another disposition like fragility, if that's a cleaner fit) and fitness; separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the tautology objection from the ones that are just noise; say where the analogy imports features of the source that the target doesn't have; and then rule on whether the propensity interpretation actually dissolves the tautology objection or just relocates it somewhere else. If the coin analogy breaks, say whether there is a better analogy that would do the job, or whether the whole strategy is a dead end. I also want the answer to say how confident it is in what it claims and to single out the one point it is least sure of.
