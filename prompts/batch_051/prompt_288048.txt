I've written four novels, and the saddest one keeps confusing me. Readers show up at events red-eyed and then tell me, with this weird eagerness, that the chapter where the daughter dies is there favorite. They say "it will ruin you" and mean it as a recommendation. I've gone through the standard explanations — Aristotle's catharsis, the Hume line that eloquence converts painful feelings into pleasure, the control-and-mastery story (we get to rehearse scary emotions from a safe distance), the meta-emotion story (we're glad to be the kind of person who's moved), and the make-believe line that fictional emotions are only quasi-emotions so the sadness doesn't really hurt. Each one seems to capture one slice of what my readers report, and each one quietly redefines "enjoyment" so the paradox is less a puzzle than a bait-and-switch.

From where I sit, the argument over why we enjoy sad art has been stuck for a long while: Aristotle and Hume are still doing most of the work, everyone has their pet example (music, stage tragedy, novels), and the empirical psychology doesn't converge. My own hunch is that we've been lumping two different things together — the sharp grief for the character, and the suspenseful agitation of "what happens next," which is aversive on its own but feels worth it when it resolves. So "enjoying tragedy" might just be a bad averaging of those two affects. But I'd rather have a diagnosis of the debate than my own pet theory: what kind of disagreement is this, actually — a verbal one about the word 'enjoyment', several different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical disagreement, or a real substantive one? Describe the positions concretely enough that I can tell them apart, and tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis, or whether I should just stop expecting it. And please write in American English, not British spellings.
