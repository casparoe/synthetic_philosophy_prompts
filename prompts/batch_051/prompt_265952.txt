I'm a philosophy professor putting together a seminar on Williams's Makropulos case and the broader question whether an endless life—heavenly or otherwise—could avoid collapsing into tedium. After class a student raised a variation that hadn't occured to me, and I'd like to work it through with someone. You're an expert on the philosophy of death and personal identity. Take your time, think carefully, and start by brainstorming the possible positions before you commit; don't just rehearse the standard interpretation, actually run the modified case through both horns. I don't need the secondary literature, just the argument itself.

As you know, Williams's argument is usually run as a dilemma. Either the immortal keeps the same character over an endless life, in which case her categorical desires eventually run out and life becomes a matter of unbearable tedium; or she changes enough to stay interested, in which case the person living the later stretches is no longer really the same person who originally wanted to go on living. So endless life comes out as either boring or not really yours.

Here's the variation. Suppose the elixir that prolongs life also causes a slow, periodic renormalizing of memory: every two centuries or so, the vivid autobiographical feel of the previous stretch fades until it is like having read an unusually detailed diary about yourself. The person keeps her skills, projects, values, and general personality, and she knows the events happened, but they no longer carry the first-personal sense of having been there. Old pleasures and projects can therefore strike her as fresh again, more or less the way they would for a much younger person. Does Williams's tedium argument survive this modification? I can see a case that it defuses the boredom horn; I can also see a case that it just hands Williams the identity horn in disguise. Which is it, and is there a third option he doesn't consider? And does the modified case show that the original dilemma was doing more work than it looked—or less?
