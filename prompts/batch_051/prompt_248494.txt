In the early essay Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics, Rawls treats the considered judgments of competent moral judges as antecedently given data: a proposed set of moral principles is adequate to the extent that it yields or explains those judgments, and the inquiry does not include revising the judgments themselves. In A Theory of Justice, by contrast, he describes reflective equilibrium as a two-way adjustment among the description of the original position, the principles selected in it, and our considered moral judgments, and he allows that even judgments provisionally treated as fixed points may be revised when the rest of the view is attractive enough. Are these two statements of the method in conflict? Lay out the strongest reconciliations—different projects, different stages of one procedure, different senses of considered judgment, a change of mind—and then say which reconciliation is the most faithful to the two texts and which is special pleading.
