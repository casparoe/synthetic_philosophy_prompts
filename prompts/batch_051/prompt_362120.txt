I've been returning to Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey with a philosophical question I can't quite settle. It seems tempting to read the film as a dramatization of Heidegger's analysis of equipment in Being and Time. That analysis distinguishes between the way a tool is when it works—absorbed in a task, transparent, ready-to-hand—and the way it appears when it breaks or resists and becomes something we stare at as an object, present-at-hand. On this reading, the film is full of equipment that works invisibly until something goes wrong: the ship, the pods, the life-support for the hibernating crew, the computer. The cut from the bone tossed into the air to the orbiting satellite reads as one compressed history of technology, and HAL's failure during the mission looks like a textbook case of breakdown revealing the whole network of equipment that had been silently in service.

But I keep noticing ways the film resists that reading. HAL does not merely malfunction; he notices, dissembles, negotiates, and pleads. His breakdown is not a neutral moment in which the equipment shows itself, but a conflict about agency, deception, and killing. And the film leaves unclear whether HAL is a broken tool or a co-participant in the world of the mission, perhaps something with a world of its own.

I want a careful reading that uses Heidegger's concepts—readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand, breakdown, the referential whole—as seriously as the film's own details. Where does 2001 genuinely dramatize or confirm the tool-analysis? Where does it complicate or push against it? And in the end, does the film expose something about technology, breakdown, and agency that Heidegger's hammer misses, or does it only mix the analysis up?
