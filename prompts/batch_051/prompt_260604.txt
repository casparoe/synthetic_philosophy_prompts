I have been thinking about something peculiar that happens whenever people operate under shared rules. In organizations — sports leagues, companies, condominium boards, university conduct codes — hard cases are very often decided by appeal to what the rule is for, what the policy is trying to do, or the spirit of the provision. A hockey referee decides an unusual play by invoking the reason we have the offside rule. A company's ethics committee interprets its code of conduct by asking what the code is really trying to achieve. A condominium board applies a noise provision according to its spirit, attributing to the provision a rationale that, as far as anyone can tell, no actual board member ever held. In each case the rule or policy is treated as though it carries its own rationale around with it — a rationale that need not be identical to any living person's mental state, and that nonetheless governs the people standing under the rule.

Yet many of these same people, if pressed, would say that reasons are things people have: a reason is always someone's reason, and a rule cannot literally aim at anything, because aiming is something minds do. So there is a tension between what the practice does and what its participants might otherwise believe.

I want to understand what this practice is committed to. What view of reasons does the habit of interpreting rules by an unattached rationale presuppose? Does it strictly require the idea that a reason or rationale can exist without any reasoner, out in the rule or policy rather than in anyone's head? Or can the practice be fully and honestly reconstructed on the alternative view that all reasons are ultimately someone's reasons — the interpreter's, a hypothetical author's, the community's — without distortion? I would like you to separate what the practice cannot function without from what it merely sits well with, and to tell me where it would become unstable if its participants really accepted that every reason must be somebody's actual mental state, and what would have to change in how they interpret rules.
