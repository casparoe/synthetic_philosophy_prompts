I have a collection of judgments about sentences that use fictional or empty names, and I want to know whether any one principle captures all of them. Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street." True as a claim about Arthur Conan Doyle's stories; false as a claim about the actual street.

2. "Sherlock Holmes is a fictional character." True outright — a claim about the actual world, not merely a claim about how the stories represent things.

3. "Sherlock Holmes does not exist." True; there is no such person.

4. "Sherlock Holmes is a detective." True outright as well, not merely story-true. If someone asked whether Holmes was a detective or a violinist, I would answer "a detective" without hedging.

5. The pair of 3 and 4: I want to keep both, but I can feel the strain. If "Holmes is a detective" is true outright, then it seems something is a detective; yet I deny that Holmes exists, and I would resist "there exists someone who is Sherlock Holmes."

6. "In Arthur Conan Doyle's stories, Holmes is a fictional character." False; within the stories, he is a real person.

7. "Holmes met Hercule Poirot." Not determinately true or false; no established fiction contains both characters, so no single story settles the claim.

8. "Pegasus is a winged horse." Fine to assert flatly in ordinary contexts, and true relative to the myth, but I would not treat it as a claim about a real animal.

9. "Holmes is cleverer than Poirot." Different from "Holmes is cleverer than Lestrade." The latter is settled by the stories; the former is not settled by either corpus, though I can compare their respective on-page feats.

10. "Some fictional characters are more widely recognized than any real detective." True, I think; but it seems to quantify over fictional characters, which sits awkwardly with my verdict in 3.

11. "Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street and is a fictional character." The whole conjunction feels uneasy; I am not sure it has a single truth value, though each conjunct seems true in its own register.

I doubt these verdicts are jointly consistent. What I want is a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures the pattern: an account of when a sentence with a fictional or empty name is true outright, when it is true only relative to a story, and when it is neither. State the principle precisely enough to yield verdicts on cases I have not listed, such as: "Voldemort is a wizard"; "Voldemort is a fictional character"; "According to the Holmes stories, the Earth is round"; "Sherlock Holmes is more famous than Jack the Ripper"; and a case in which a story's texts contradict each other on some detail. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle accommodates and which, if any, it forces me to revise, and why.

As you answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of language working on these issues, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
