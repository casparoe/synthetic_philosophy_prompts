i keep having clear verdicts about when a court should refuse to apply a statute's plain words because the result is absurd, but i can't state the principle behind them without gesturing at "c'mon, nobody could have meant that." Help me reverse-engineer the principle, or tell me there isn't one. All the statutes below are invented; i'll use "covered" to mean the judge should apply the words as written, and "not covered" to mean she should read them as not reaching the case.

1. "No person may operate a motor vehicle on the pedestrian mall." An ambulance enters the mall to reach someone in cardiac arrest. Verdict: not covered; convicting the paramedics would be absurd.

2. Same statute. A food truck drives onto the mall at 5:45 a.m. to set up for a festival, where the permit allows vehicle entry for setup only from 6:00. Verdict: covered. Fifteen minutes early is just a rules violation; "absurd" can't mean "mildly inconvenient."

3. "No person shall bring an animal into a polling place." A blind voter enters with a trained guide dog. Verdict: not covered; a polling place a blind person can't enter is the absurdity.

4. "Any person who carries a firearm onto school grounds shall be sentenced to a mandatory five years." A police officer responding to an active-shooter call enters with her service weapon. Verdict: not covered.

5. Same statute. A parent with a concealed-carry permit forgets the gun is in the glovebox and never takes it out of the car while dropping off their kid. Verdict: covered, even though five years is harsh. That feels like harsh-but-possibly-intended, not absurd-and-couldn't-be-meant.

6. Same "no animal" statute as 3. A voter brings an emotional-support parrot in a cage because crowds make her anxious. Verdict: covered. I want to say this is consistent with the guide dog, but I can't say why.

Is there one principle that captures all six and draws the line between the dog and the parrot? Which of my verdicts should I give up if not? And if the whole framing is confused—say i'm secretly applying a purpose-based rule, or mixing up absurdity with some other doctrine—say so plainly rather than extracting a principle to flatter me.

Last line: a one-sentence summary of the principle you'd defend, or of why no such principle exists.
