Take Iran's kidney market—the only country with a legal, regulated market for living kidney donation, with a state-set payment, an additional negotiated "reward" from recipient to donor, a ban on foreigners, and official language that treats the whole thing as a gift rather than a sale. I know the rough facts; what I want is an analysis through the repugnant markets/commodification lens. What would a commodification critic in the Sandel/Roth mold predict about who ends up selling, what happens to donor motivation, and whether the meaning of the gift gets corroded once money enters it? Then tell me which of those predictions the Iranian case actually bears out or confounds, and be honest about where the mapping is loose. Does the official insistence on gift language show that commodification worries are empty, or that even a legal market has to disguise itself as a gift to stay morally acceptable? And what does the episode do back to the theory—does it show repugnance is a real constraint markets have to work around, or that the theory is too blunt to explain why some monetized transfers feel repugnant and others don't?
