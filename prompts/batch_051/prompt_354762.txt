I'm on my university's research ethics board, and we're deadlocked on a case that shouldn't be this hard. Our human-subjects code was written in 1979, when consent meant a typed form and an in-person debriefing. The relevant sentence says: "Before enrolling a volunteer, the investigator shall inform the volunteer of the nature, risks and benefits of the study and shall answer all questions; he may persuade the volunteer to participate by any means that is not coercive, deceptive, or manipulative, and the volunteer may decline or withdraw at any time without penalty." Fine. But the study in question recruits through an online portal, and the consent page pre-checks the box for an optional EEG add-on so you have to uncheck it to opt out, renders the decline button grey and below the fold, and includes the statement "Choosing not to participate means you will not receive the 0.5 course credits offered for completion," which is literally true. No one deceived anyone and no one threatened anything. Half the board reads this as a nudge; the other half reads it as manipulation by interface. The code doesn't define manipulation, and its authors obviously weren't picturing pre-checked boxes.

What I want isn't just a vote. I want a workable account of the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion that this clause could plausibly have meant, and then an application of that account to each element separately—the default, the visual design, the loss framing—and to all three taken together. Then give me the verdict from four approaches: the plain text, the purpose of the consent requirement, what the 1979 committee would probably have said if shown a screenshot, and what a reasonable volunteer reading the page would think was going on. Which of these should control, and why? And write me the one-sentence revision that would catch whatever you think crossed the line without banning ordinary persuasion.
