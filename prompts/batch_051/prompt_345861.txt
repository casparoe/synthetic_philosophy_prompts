In my lab days we fitted curves to data all the time, and I keep seeing philosophers use exactly that picture to explain underdetermination of theory by evidence: the data points are the observations, the curve is the theory, and since any finite scatter of points can be fitted by endlessly many curves, evidence never forces one theory instead of another. I'd like an audit of that analogy, the kind I would have wanted when I still trusted a good fit. Give me the correspondence table first—what plays the role of the points, the fitted curve, the goodness of fit, the extrapolation beyond the data, the noise in the measurements. Then work through where the mapping breaks. In real curve-fitting we already bring in constraints the bare picture doesn't mention: smoothness and simplicity preferences, background knowledge about plausible functional forms, predictive success on held-out data, error bars, and the cost of adding parameters. Do those constraints have real counterparts in theory choice, or does the analogy quietly leave them out to make underdetermination look more troublesome than it is? And at the end, tell me whether the conclusion—that evidence alone never settles a theory—actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the curve picture was doing the argumentative work the whole time.
