I work in decision theory and welfare economics, and I have only a rough grasp of the antinatalist literature. I keep encountering David Benatar's asymmetry between pain and pleasure, and I want it translated into the vocabulary I actually reason in. As I understand it, the asymmetry runs as follows. The presence of pain is bad. The absence of pain is good, even in a world in which no one exists to experience that good. The presence of pleasure is good. But the absence of pleasure is not bad, because no one exists to be deprived of it. Benatar then appears to infer that a life containing even some pain is worse than never having come into existence: the pains present in a life count against it, while the pleasures absent from non-existence do not count against non-existence.

Restate this position, as precisely as you can, in the vocabulary of decision theory and welfare economics: states of the world, utility assignments to states, dominance, expected utility over lotteries, social welfare functions, and the maneuver of representing non-existence as a zero-utility state. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the asymmetry map cleanly onto a utility representation over states? Which parts force you to add something nonstandard, such as an asymmetric treatment of an empty population, a reference point, or a restricted dominance principle? Which parts cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all? And, in each case, say whether the failure is a shortcoming of the decision-theoretic framework or a sign that the asymmetry itself is less articulate than its partisans claim.

Please give me exactly five numbered findings, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
