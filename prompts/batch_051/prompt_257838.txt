I'm writing a paper on whether a brain could in principle compute functions that a Turing machine can't, and I keep tripping over the Church-Turing thesis. As I understand it, the thesis says the functions computable by any effective procedure are exactly the Turing-computable ones. A brain is a physical system, so whatever it does should be the running of some effective procedure — or at least it seems that way to me. So wouldn't the thesis already settle that brains, and any machine anyone could ever build, are stuck at Turing-computability? And isn't the thesis by now basically confirmed empirically, since nobody has ever produced an effective procedure that goes beyond a Turing machine? My supervisor waved this off by mentioning something called the physical Church-Turing thesis and said the ordinary one is about effective procedures, not about physics, but I'm not seeing why that distinction matters for my question. If the thesis is true in its proper domain, doesn't hypercomputation (oracle machines, exotic physics, whatever) become just science fiction rather than a live option? I'm not asking for a reading list; I want to know where the reasoning goes wrong, or wether it doesn't. Please say how confident you are in the key claims and single out the point you're least sure about, and write in British English.
