A colleague keeps arguing a general point about language on the basis of an analogy from our own work, and I want to know how much weight it can bear. The context: we were debating whether a service we maintain should model domain events explicitly as commands that mutate state, rather than as records or snapshots of what is already true. He said language itself is mostly a collection of state-changing operations rather than descriptions, and he offered this comparison. A performative utterance — "I promise," "I now pronounce you married," "I name this ship" — is like calling a state-changing method such as issueRefund(orderId). The method doesn't describe a pre-existing fact about the order; if the caller is authorized and the preconditions are satisfied, it changes the order's state. Likewise, he said, "I promise" doesn't report a fact about your inner life; if the speaker is in the right position and the circumstances are right, it changes your social or normative situation. From this he drew the conclusion that the picture of language as description-first is backwards: performatives are the normal case, plain true-or-false statements are a special case, and when we model the domain we should treat events like mutations rather than entity snapshots.

I understand the code side of the analogy well and only the language side vaguely, so I can't tell whether the analogy is doing most of the argumentative work or whether it is just an illustration and the conclusion stands on its own. Please audit it. Make the correspondence explicit — what fills the role of the method, the caller's authorization, the preconditions, the mutated state, the exception paths, the rollback semantics, and the API contract — and then work through the places where the mapping breaks down. I particularly want to know which features of software state changes the analogy quietly imports that speech acts don't have (atomicity, strict preconditions, a single canonical implementation, rollback on failure, idempotence, explicit authorization), which of those disanalogies matter for my colleague's conclusion and which are merely cosmetic, and whether the conclusion — performatives are the normal case and descriptions are a special case — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. If a better analogy exists, say what it is and what it fixes.
