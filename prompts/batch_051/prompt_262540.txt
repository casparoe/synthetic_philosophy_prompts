I have been reading about Daniel Dennett's careenium thought experiment and find it harder to shake off than I expected. As I understand it: imagine a small wheeled vehicle, like an oversized bumper car, gliding on a frictionless arena. Inside a transparent dome sit hundreds of tiny, perfectly elastic simmballs that roll in straight lines and bounce off one another and off the dome's inner wall. The wall is studded with sensors, and when a simmball strikes a sensor it briefly energizes one of the vehicle's external thrusters, nudging the whole careenium in some direction. The internal collisions are all there is to the machinery; yet from the outside the careenium seems to display goal-directed behavior — it homes in on some regions, shies away from others, and over time you cannot help describing it as if it were perceiving, wanting, and deciding. At a higher level of description, certain shifting arrangements of simmballs act like symbols or representations: they appear to stand for danger, food, or the gap in the wall. The troubling question is whether those symbols ever push the substrate around. The micro-causal story already seems complete: every simmball moves because it was struck, and every thruster fires because a simmball hit a sensor. So one horn of the dilemma is that the symbols are idle — real-looking patterns that ride along but never get their hands on the wheel. The other horn is that the symbols do push the substrate around, which seems to mean that an arrangement of particles causes things over and above the particles themselves, a kind of downward causation that sits uneasily with the causal closure of the microphysical realm.

Write an essay that works through this dilemma. Weigh both horns sympathetically, consider whether there are live intermediate positions (for example, views on which symbols are real patterns that make a causal difference without being extra forces), and defend a definite verdict on whether the symbols push the substrate around — do not end on a mere survey of options. Since the question turns on subtle premises, also identify the specific consideration that would most change your view if it went the other way, and flag where you judge your own reasoning to be most fragile.
