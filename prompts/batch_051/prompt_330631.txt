I sit on the Campus Civic Life Committee at a small residential college. Our founding charge is a single Rousseauian standard, which we call the forced-to-be-free test: the college may coercively constrain a member only to bring her into line with the general will she shares with the rest of the community, and not merely to make her prudent, healthy, or happy, nor to advance a partial interest, nor to issue a decree aimed at particular persons rather than a general rule.

Over the past decade the committee has built a record under that test. We approved mandatory voting in student-government elections, on the ground that abstention lets a faction speak with the voice of the whole. We approved a meningitis vaccination requirement for all enrolled students, since shared lecture halls and dining halls make the risk genuinely common rather than private. We approved a ban on smoking in all indoor shared spaces. We approved a graduation requirement that every student complete a course in civic reasoning, on the ground that the general will needs members who can judge common concerns rather than merely register private preferences. We rejected a bicycle-helmet mandate, holding that the harm it prevents is borne by the rider alone and that forcing someone to be prudent is not the same as forcing her to be free. We also rejected a first-year curfew, holding that a measure aimed at a common good still fails when it singles out a class of persons instead of stating a rule for all.

The proposal now before us is a mandatory three-day civic formation retreat for every incoming first-year student, held before the fall term, with structured small-group deliberation on the college’s shared problems and follow-up sessions during the year; students who refuse to attend would have a hold placed on their registration. The retreat resembles the civic-reasoning course we approved, but it also resembles the first-year curfew we rejected, since it reaches only first-years. Before I vote, I want to know what our track record actually commits us to. Work through the candidate principles that fit the decisions I have described; say what each requires for the retreat; identify the closest precedent under each; and give your best judgment about which principle our record better supports, including an honest statement of what the committee would be adopting under either choice. If the precedents underdetermine the question, say which further case would cleanly separate the principles; if one earlier decision is the controlling analog and the other is a distraction, say so; and if the retreat proposal or my gloss on the forced-to-be-free test is too underspecified to decide, tell me that plainly instead of manufacturing a verdict.
