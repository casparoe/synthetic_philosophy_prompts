I have been asked to lead a one-hour session for a sixth-form science enrichment programme on how thought experiments in science can yield knowledge. I want to prepare a real teaching plan rather than a survey of examples, because I have already mishandled this once: a few weeks ago my nephew (upper sixth, planning to read physics) asked me, after a lesson in which his teacher had treated Galileo's falling-bodies argument as though it settled something, why an experiment that nobody had actually run should count against what everyone has always observed. I gave him three different answers in five minutes and none of them landed.

Don't explain the topic to me; plan how I should explain it to them. The audience is bright but has no philosophy, trusts measurement, and will arrive sceptical that an imagined scenario can do any epistemic work. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why — in particular, what has to be in place before the question of how a thought experiment can yield knowledge even makes sense to them. Then I want one carrying example chosen and defended: I am drawn to the falling-bodies argument because it is already familiar to some of them, but I want to be told whether to use it or reject it in favour of something else (Maxwell's demon, Einstein's lift, or another), and what the example must have and must lack for this audience.

Then I need the misconception map: the specific misunderstandings they will bring or form — I suspect the deepest one is that a thought experiment earns its keep only by predicting the outcome of a real experiment we have not yet been able to run, rather than by exposing a tension in claims they already accept — and, for each misconception, the point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. At the end I want one question or check that will tell me whether the idea has landed rather than whether they can repeat the example. Finally, tell me plainly what I should refuse to explain at this level and say so in the room.

Please be definite: choose one carrying example and one sequence, and commit to them, with reasons for the choices. Do not give me a menu of options; if the honest plan requires flagging a live philosophical controversy and moving on, say where and what I should say about it.
