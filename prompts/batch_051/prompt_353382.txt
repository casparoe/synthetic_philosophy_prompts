Draft the joint statement that a sophisticated historicist and a sophisticated Popperian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about whether there are laws of history. The historicist position: human history exhibits a real developmental order—law-like regularities of social succession, not merely isolated causal mechanisms—so long-range predictions about the overall trajectory of societies are possible in principle, however fallible in practice. The Popperian position: there are no laws of history in that sense; the patterns historians find are trends or retrospective interpretations, and long-range historical prediction is impossible even in principle, because it would require predicting the future growth of knowledge, and predicting a future discovery would already constitute making it. What social science can achieve is conditional, testable, piecemeal explanation, not large-scale prophecy.

Write the document so that it does the following:

1. States a numbered list of agreed claims. These should be neither vacuous nor merely diplomatic. They should include whatever both sides accept about the existence of historical regularities, the difference between a trend and a law, the asymmetry between retrospective explanation and prospective prediction, the value and limits of conditional social-scientific predictions, and the special role, if any, of the growth of knowledge. Phrase each claim so a knowledgeable outside observer can tell exactly what is being asserted.

2. States, in a disagreements table, the residual disagreement after all verbal confusions have been removed. For each row, name the issue; give the historicist's position in wording the historicist would endorse; give the Popperian's position in wording the Popperian would endorse. The positions should be close enough that the rows are pinpoint disputes, not opposite caricatures.

3. Identifies at least two disputes that appeared substantive but that the collaboration dissolved as verbal, and specifies the distinction whose absence produced the appearance of disagreement.

4. Identifies the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would force at least one side to revise its view. Candidate cruxes include, but need not be limited to: whether the growth of knowledge is unpredictable in principle or only in practice; whether any purported law of history is falsifiable in the way a natural law is; whether the holistic character of social systems rules out strict historical laws or merely makes them harder to test; and whether the unpredictability of future discoveries is the core obstacle to historical prediction or only one obstacle among many.

5. For each crux, specifies a concrete case, argument, or test that both sides agree bears on that crux, and records each side's prediction of the result in advance. The cases or tests should be precise enough that a third party could evaluate them without knowing which side proposed them.

Do not resolve the dispute. The point is exact joint formulation with the residual disagreement made precise.
