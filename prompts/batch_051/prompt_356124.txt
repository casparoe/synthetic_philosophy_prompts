Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Kant — specifically the formula of universal law — and somewhere around hour two I realized I've been running a rule I'd never explicitly defended. I only ever use universalizability as a veto. If I can show that a maxim can't be willed as a universal law without something like a contradiction (everyone breaking promises when it's convenient, everyone lying on the margins of their taxes, everyone blocking the fire exit "just for a second"), I take that as enough to rule the action out. But I never let the test count in favor of an action. If someone says "I can will this as a universal law, so it's permissible," I treat that as basically no evidence, because whether a maxim passes depends way too much on how you describe it, and there is always a way to redescribe an action — with enough fine print about circumstances, intentions, and timing — that makes it look universalizable.

My friend says this is incoherent: if the test is too mushy to certify anything, it's to mushy to refute anything either, and I'm just using Kant to back up prior taboos rather than to learn anything. But I think the asymmetry is real: it takes a much weaker assumption for a failed universalization to sink an action than for a passed universalization to justify one. So is my one-way universalizability rule defensible? Is there a way to make it precise — maybe treating the test as only a necessary condition for permissibility, or supplying a principled account of what "the maxim" of an action is — that keeps the veto without buying the license? Or is my friend right that the description problem poisons both directions equally?
