One line of thought I keep returning to, after reading about quantifier variance and the idea that some philosophical disputes are "merely verbal," has left me with an argument that looks airtight and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I'll lay it out and ask where it fails.

(1) To ask whether numbers exist is just to ask whether the sentence "numbers exist" is true. (2) A sentence is true or false only relative to a language, and a language fixes, among other things, what its quantifier ranges over and what would settle the truth of an existence sentence. (3) Nothing in our observations or the world forces us into exactly one such language: we could, without misdescribing any observation, adopt a rich language whose domain includes numbers, properties, and arbitrary composite objects, or a sparse language whose domain includes only fundamental physical things. (4) So there is no language-independent fact as to whether numbers exist; the sentence simply comes out true in some admissible languages and false in others, and the question has no unique answer between them. (5) Therefore, when a platonist says "numbers exist" and a nominalist says "numbers do not exist," each is speaking a different but equally legitimate language, and their disagreement is merely verbal. (6) The same reasoning applies to every existential claim, since for any count noun one can imagine an admissible language in which it picks out nothing. (7) By parity, "there is beer in the fridge," "there were three people at the meeting," and "there is a brain in my skull" would all be merely verbal. (8) Those are not verbal disputes; they are run-of-the-mill factual questions with right and wrong answers.
