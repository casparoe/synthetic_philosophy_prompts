I write about science and religion for a general-interest outlet, and for the past several months I have been reporting a feature on the theological implications of evolutionary history. The provisional position I have settled on after my interviews is this: the doctrine that the natural world is the good creation of a good God is in serious, perhaps fatal, trouble, because the machinery that produced every living species — predation, parasitism, disease, and mass extinction — operated for hundreds of millions of years before any human being, and therefore before any human fall, existed. I am not asking about the problem of evil in general or about human suffering; my claim is specifically about the chronology of the record. The goodness of creation is traditionally supposed to explain why the world is not merely a fortunate accident, yet the evolutionary record looks less like a good order that later went wrong than like a process in which pain and death were part of the design from the outset.

What I want from you is a map of what would have to be true for that position to be wrong. Candidate escape routes I have already encountered include: that nonhuman animal suffering is not conscious enough to count morally; that animal pain is instrumentally necessary for goods of such magnitude that a good God could will them; that the Fall was a cosmic or angelic event whose consequences somehow reached backward in time; that the doctrine of a good creation was never a claim about the historical origin of species; and that divine goodness does not require the absence of pain in nonhuman creatures. For each, I want to know whether it would count decisively against my view, merely complicate it, or leave it standing — and I want to hear which of these a sophisticated defender of the doctrine would themselves concede is a cost rather than a clean solution. I also want your honest assessment of whether my position has been insulated against these replies by being qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would itself be a reason to distrust it. To disclose my own bias: I find the "animals are not really conscious sufferers" move almost impossible to believe, so if it does real work in the best case for the other side, I want that work examined rather than assumed.
