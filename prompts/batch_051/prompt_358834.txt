I am trying to figure out what I actually believe about petitionary prayer by looking at my gut reactions to concrete cases, and I would like you to help me find the principle behind them—or tell me which of my reactions has to go. The passage I keep coming back to is from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, II-II, q. 83, a. 2, obj. 1:

“Prayer seems to be necessary in order that we may make our needs known to the person to whom we pray. But according to Matthew 6:32, ‘Your Father knoweth that you have need of all these things.’ Therefore it is not becoming to pray to God.”

I take the passage to state the difficulty sharply: if prayer is for informing God, it is pointless; and if God already knows all things and is immutable, then whatever petitionary prayer is for, it cannot be that. But I do not want to conclude that petitionary prayer is simply incoherent, because some cases strike me as clearly acceptable and some clearly not.

Here are my verdicts. Praying for a friend’s full recovery from a serious illness: right, and not merely as a coping mechanism. Praying that my flight will not crash: also right, though I feel slightly less steady about it. Praying for a parking space near the store: not right; it seems to trivialize prayer, even though God’s omniscience and immutability would not rule it out on their own. Praying for my team to win a baseball game: not right, and worse than the parking space. Praying for my own patience: right, maybe the most clearly right of all. Praying for a past event, as when I pray now that a friend who was just in an accident did not die: not right, even though it fits an unchanging God no better or worse than asking for future favors. Praying for the end of human trafficking: right, but also strange, because God’s permissive will may be the only reason it continues, yet failing to pray would still seem wrong. Praying for a child’s recovery from a disease the doctors say is hopeless: right, though I cannot justify it without making God a rescuer of last resort. Praying, but adding the clause if it be your will: this seems to make almost any request safer, but I worry it drains the petition of actually asking for anything.

I have a vague principle in mind: petitionary prayer is appropriate when it aims at conforming the petitioner to God and expressing dependence, and inappropriate when it treats God as a means to a consumer outcome. That would explain the parking-space and baseball cases, and the patience case. But it does not explain why praying for my friend’s recovery is right, since that is an external outcome too; and it makes the hopeless-prognosis case depend on something I cannot yet make precise.

Please give me a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as honestly as possible. State what it says about the object of prayer, the petitioner’s responsibilities, and God’s omniscience and immutability; show how each clause handles one of my cases; and then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: praying after a job interview, but before the decision is made, that I will be offered the job. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts your candidate cannot accommodate, and whether I should overturn that verdict or modify the principle. Please write in American English.
