I have been trying to make my views on complicity precise, and I want a consistency audit rather than a defense of any one of them. By complicity I mean being morally implicated in another agent's wrongdoing in a way that falls short of being the principal and is distinct from merely causing or merely knowing about the wrong. The views I find myself holding are these:

(1) A person can be complicit without making any causal difference. A bystander who cheers on an assault, or an official who authorizes a subordinate's bribe, is complicit even if the wrong would have occurred exactly the same without them.

(2) But merely benefiting from, or knowing about, wrongdoing is not enough. Someone who inherits a fortune built partly on fraud, or lives on land taken before she was born, is not thereby complicit, though she may owe restitution.

(3) Complicity is fundamentally intentional participation. The complicit agent either shares the wrongdoer's ends, or knowingly does something by which she sets out to facilitate the wrong.

(4) At the same time, I am convinced that someone who knowingly buys clothes from a manufacturer that uses forced labor is complicit, even though she does not want the forced labor, does not intend to facilitate it, and her single purchase makes no discernible difference to whether it occurs.

(5) I also think the low-level clerks who kept the records in a criminal regime were complicit, even where their individual tasks were dispensable and they did not share the regime's aims.

(6) And I do not think that paying taxes to a state one knows commits injustices makes one complicit in those injustices.

I would like to know whether this set hangs together. Please identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest overall revision of the set would be and what it would cost.
