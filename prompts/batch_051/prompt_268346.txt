I've gone down a rabbit hole about griefbots — the services where you upload a dead person's texts, emails, and voice messages and get a chatbot that keeps "talking" as them — and it's thrown me into a more basic confusion: I can't say what grief is anymore. Obviously I know the word when I hear it, but I keep stretching it. I'm not asking about mourning (the outward rituals and customs). I want a working definition of the internal state: what makes something grief rather than one of the nearby things it gets confused with, like sadness, regret, longing, shock, depression, or plain missing someone.

So here's the ask. Give me a definition of grief, then run it through these cases and tell me whether it gets them right, and where it surprises you or forces you to bite a bullet:

1. A man whose wife of forty years died two years ago thinks he's mostly okay now. A song comes on in the car and he has to pull over, sobbing. Is this grief?
2. A teenager is mildly bummed when a minor character gets killed off in a show, and forgets it by the next episode. Is that grief?
3. A woman builds a griefbot of her dead mother and talks to it every night for a year. She says it comforts her and keeps her "connected" to her mother, but when the bot says something her mother never would have said, she gets hit with a fresh wave of loss. Is she grieving her mother?
4. A man is distraught for weeks when a famous musician he never met dies — more distraught than he ever was about his own aunt, whom he saw once a year. He says it's grief. Is it?
5. A daughter whose father is in hospice finds herself crying at bus stops months before he actually dies. Is this grief, or does grief by definition only come after a loss?
6. A woman whose abusive parent dies feels mostly relief, but notices a strange, directionless sadness underneath it. Is that grief?

I'm confident the first case is grief and the second isn't. The other four I go back and forth on. Propose the definition, run it through all six, and dont just throw up your hands on the hard ones — defend whatever verdict the definition gives, even if it changes your mind about the cases.
