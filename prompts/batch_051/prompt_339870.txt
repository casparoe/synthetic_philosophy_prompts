I cover history and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and for the past year I've been tracking a historian — call her M. — who has a recurring feature where she passes verdicts on counterfactual-history books and articles: the "what if Napoleon had done X" genre. She doesn't review them for prose; she just rules on whether their counterfactual is a legitimate historical excercise. I'm about to write a piece on the newest one, and I need to predict her call from the track record.

Her verdicts so far, roughly:

- She approved a book that asked what would have happened if the 1918 influenza pandemic had hit Europe a year earlier, during the war. Her stated reason: the timing of a mass-death event is a structural variable, and the author actually traced it through the Allied manpower pool, the shipyards, and the harvests. She called it a stress test, not a butterfly game.

- She rejected an article saying that if Marx had never met Engels, there would have been no communist revolution anywhere in the twentieth century. Her line was that even if the meeting mattered, no single meeting can carry the burden of eliminating a century of movements rooted in industrialization and class conflict. One person can't be the sole load-bearing cause of a structural outcome.

- She approved with conditions a piece on Kennedy surviving Dallas. The small premise itself was fine — a clean, minimal change — but the author was only licensed to draw near-term conclusions: the 1964 election, the tax bill, civil-rights timing. The leap to "no Vietnam" was out, because escalation already had too many independent drivers in motion.

- She rejected a study asking, roughly, what if the printing press had arrived in 1000 AD. She said you can't just transplant a whole technology centuries early and leave the surrounding society untouched; the society of 1000 wouldn't produce it, and the author was assuming away the preconditions.

- She approved a long article on what if the Black Death had killed a third of Europe instead of half. She called it close to a model of the genre: change a demographic parameter, rerun the labor-market and land-tenure mechanics, and stop before you think you've predicted the Renaissance.

The new one on my desk: a book arguing that if Gavrilo Princip's pistol had jammed on 28 June 1914, the July Crisis would have ended in a diplomatic settlement, no Great War in 1914, and probably no world war at all. I have to say whether she'd bless it, and how far.

Given her own track record, what should I predict she'd say — approve, reject, or approve with conditions? Which of her past calls is the closest analog, and what principle would she be adopting either way? Her record seems to me to fit more than one rule, so I'd want to know what the candidate rules are and how each one scores the new book. And if I wanted to settle which rule she's actually following, what one further case should I look for her to have ruled on?
