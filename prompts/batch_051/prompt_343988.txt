I work in UX research, so I spend a lot of time writing hypothetical scenarios and watching people answer them, and I keep seeing the same move in philosophy arguments — online and in papers — and I don't have a word for it. Someone's defending a conclusion, so they invite you to imagine a case: a runaway cart heading for five people, a doctor who could save five patients with one small incision in a healthy visitor, whatever. You object that something about the case is doing the work — that your answer would change if one of the five were your sister, or if the healthy visitor had watched a video about the risk beforehand. And here's the thing: they don't argue that your distinction is irrelevant. They add a stipulation. The five are strangers and you'll never learn their names; the visitor signed no consent form and never thought about the risk; the incision can't be botched; nobody will find out. Each individual addition is reasonable, like tightening a scenario to remove a confound. But after eight rounds of this, the scenario has been completely rebuilt to block every answer except the one they wanted, and they still talk as if the verdict came from your spontaneous reaction to the original story. I want to say: my reaction was to the final, custom-fit version; the original case didn't actually do any of the work.

Is there a name for that — not just "a disagreement about a thought experiment," but the tuning of a scenario under objection until it can't spit out any answer except the one they wanted, while the verdict keeps being described as something the reader supplied on their own? If there's no standard name, what would you call it? And I'd like the boundary drawn: when is adding a stipulation a legitimate way to clarify what the case is supposed to test, and when is it quietly building the answer into the setup? The label matters less to me than that line. Tell me how confident you are in what you end up claiming, and which part you're least sure of.
