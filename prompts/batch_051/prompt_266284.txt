I've ended up with a rule about coincidences that my flatmate describes as 'coincidence denial'. If something apparently meaningful happens—I'm in the middle of thinking about someone and they ring me, or I dream of an old classmate in the same week they turn up in the news—I refuse to treat it as evidence of anything until I've tried to estimate how many opportunities there were for something at least that striking to happen to me, or to anybody I'd end up hearing about. The birthday paradox is my model here: with only 23 people in a room the chance of a shared birthday is already over half, so a lot of 'what are the odds?' stories are really asking the wrong question; the odds were high once you counted all the near-misses and other combinations that would have felt equally spooky. I once read a remark to the effect that if you call a one-in-a-million event a 'miracle' and you experience events at one per second, a miracle should happen to each of us about once a month, and that has stuck with me.

I suspect my rule is too blunt, though. It stops me from being wowed by lottery-ticket reasoning, where people ignore the thousands of non-events and then gawp at the one hit; but I also worry it turns into a lazy way to wave away anything uncomfortable, where 'large numbers' ends up doing the work of actual investigation. So I want to know whether the policy is defensible. Under what conditions—how specific the prediction was, how stable the relevant reference class is, and how costly a false negative would be—is a coincidence genuinely good evidence for a hidden connection? And is there a way of restating my rule so it keeps the anti-gawp benefits without makeing me blind to real patterns? (I don't mean anything supernatural, just ordinary causal or social inferences.)
