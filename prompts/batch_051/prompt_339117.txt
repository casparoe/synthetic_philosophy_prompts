I've been down a rabbit hole of methodology-of-philosophy discourse lately — the fights where one side's modus ponens is the other side's modus tollens, the x-phi people saying the whole intuition database is just WEIRD undergrads, the reflective equilibrium defenders saying that's the best we can do and everyone else is in denial. I've come away convinced that everyone involved is running a private, unstated policy about when an intuition about a case is evidence and when it's just a confound you're allowed to ignore. Before I read another word on this, I want the desiderata. What does any adequate account of how to weigh intuitions about principles against intuitions about particular cases have to do? Not the full theory — the checklist it has to pass, with the case or consideration behind each item.

Here's my current list of things I suspect have to be on it; tell me which are real requirements and which are me smuggling in my own taste:

- It has to answer the modus ponens/modus tollens asymmetry without begging the question: when does a case refute a principle, and when do you keep the principle and bite the bullet on the case? It can't just say "weigh them" — thats the problem restated.
- It has to respect transitivity (or explain why apparent failures of it don't count) without ruling out every spectrum-style case by fiat. If your account can't handle a chain of pairwise plausible judgments that jointly violate transitivity, it's not an account of evidence; it's a filter that only lets in cases you already like.
- It has to say what simplicity actually does for you. How much worse coverage is a simpler principle allowed to buy, and why isn't "simpler" just a knob you turn to keep whichever principle you walked in with?
- It has to stop double-counting. If a principle earned its plausibility from a bunch of case intuitions, you don't get to later cite the principle as independent evidence against those same cases. But it also can't go so far that no principle ever gets to correct a case, or principles become epiphenomenal — so the account owes a story about how a principle becomes earned evidence rather than just a summary of its instances.
- It has to handle disagreement without laziness. Two people share the same case judgments but extract different principles, or share a principle but report different case judgments; the account has to say what's going wrong and who, if anyone, is failing, without just asserting that one side's intuitions are the reliable ones.
- It has to be compatible with the boring fact that case intuitions are produced by heuristics and get pushed around by framing, order, affect, and demography. It can't conclude from that that philosophy has no data, and it also can't pretend all intuitions are equal.
- It has to explain how progress is possible. If the account makes every standoff between principles and cases a matter of taste, it's failed as an account of evidence.

Then flag any pairs on this list (or ones I missed) that can't be jointly satisfied, since I half-suspect the interesting result is that no account gets all of them — the transitivity requirement and the impulse to respect the pairwise case judgments already look like they're in tension, and the double-counting rule might be in tension with the requirement that principles sometimes overrule cases. If you want, add a short scorecard of how reflective equilibrium, the experimental-philosophy expert-intuition line, and the intuitions-are-just-cheap-talk view do against the list, but keep it brief — the checklist is the part I'm actually after.
