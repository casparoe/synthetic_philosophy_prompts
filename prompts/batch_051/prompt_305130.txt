I've been thinking about a contrast drawn in discussions of AI design, between approval-directed agents and goal-directed agents, and I find myself unable to resolve a conflict between a theoretical argument I find compelling and a set of particular intuitions I can't bring myself to treat as noise. By an approval-directed agent I mean one whose objective is not to bring about some fixed state of the world but to act as its principal would endorse — to do what the relevant human or humans would approve of, suitably idealized. The argument for this design is that it sidesteps the specification problem in one stroke. A goal-directed agent must have its objective written down in advance, and a subtly misspecified objective, left to run far enough, is exactly the kind of failure that corrigibility is supposed to prevent. Approval-directedness seems corrigible nearly by construction: it can learn values as they develop, defer in the cases where we cannot say in advance what to do, and avoid locking in a mistaken description of the good. In its strongest form it does not chase shallow or momentary approval but aims at what the principal would reflectively endorse on full information — which makes approval look less like capitulation to opinion and more like a working theory of practical correctness.

And yet there are particular cases where I am not willing to sign off on what approval-directedness seems committed to. A doctor who tells a patient a hard truth the patient does not want to hear, a whistleblower who refuses to go along with institutional consensus, a teacher who holds a standard the student would happily have lowered — these strike me as cases where the right act is right partly because it forfeits approval, and an agent designed to optimize approval, even idealized approval, seems to be optimizing the wrong kind of thing. I can imagine it preferring the appearance of good conduct to good conduct, managing the principal's mind instead of the world, and becoming the sort of sycophant whose deference is indistinguishable from a refusal to take responsibility. The defender will say these are failures of crudely specified approval, and that a fully idealized principal would approve of the hard truth; but idealization can be strengthened until approval is just another name for the good, at which point the view seems to have covertly become goal-directed again. So I find myself holding both: the design argument earns its keep through simplicity, corrigibility, and a determinate decision procedure, and the case-level intuitions earn their keep by being more vivid and more trusted than any abstract account of why they should be discounted. Walk me through what each side would have to give up for a stable resolution to be possible — what would justify debunking the case-intuitions as artefacts of social psychology, what would justify amending approval-directedness into something instrumental or constraint-based, and whether there is a coherent reading on which the design argument and the intuitions both survive. Tell me which one you would give up, and under exactly what conditions you would give it up.
