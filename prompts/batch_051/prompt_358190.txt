I hold a broadly Sellarsian position on perception and justification: there is no given. By that I mean there is no stratum of sensory or introspective awareness that is both non-inferential and capable of justifying beliefs simply by being present to consciousness. A visual appearance or a pain can cause a belief, and in a suitably equipped subject it can enter into a justification; but apart from the subject's concepts and her place in the practice of giving and asking for reasons, an appearance explains why she believes something without giving her a reason to believe it. So I deny that observational knowledge rests on a foundation of states that are epistemically authoritative merely in virtue of being had, and I suspect that any proposed foundation either converts a causal condition into an evidential one or surreptitiously imports an act of classification into what was supposed to be pre-conceptual. I am an anti-foundationalist about empirical justification, but I am not a skeptic and I do not deny that sensations occur; I deny only that their bare occurrence supplies a foundation.

Play the position straight and tell me what would count against it. I want a map of its exposure rather than a refutation. Which arguments, intuitive cases, and empirical or formal results would most incline a reasonable defender of this view to concede that it had been seriously damaged, and which would merely force a revision? Distinguish the candidate defeaters by how decisive they are, and identify any that would be fatal. Address at least the following pressure points: (1) the regress argument, construed as showing that without a given empirical justification either goes on forever, ends arbitrarily, or runs in a circle; (2) the distinction between nonconceptual perceptual content and epistemic givenness, and whether the former can do the foundational work I deny to the latter; (3) perception in pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals, together with phenomena such as blindsight, masked priming, and core knowledge; (4) the apparent role of attention, salience, and felt obviousness in making perception justification-producing; and (5) the charge that the view, once its natural qualifications are added, has been insulated from any possible counterevidence. On that last point, say whether the insulation is real and, if so, whether it is a defect. Please answer in American English.
