I'm a junior structural engineer at a small firm, and over the last year an AI tool has gone from being my checker to being my first pass on almost everything: load calcs, member sizing, connection details. I used to work the numbers myself and then run them through the tool to catch my mistakes; now with deadlines I run the tool and spot-check maybe one number in twenty. A few weeks ago I couldn't explain to a client why a certain beam was sized the way it was, and I just kept saying "the software says so" while feeling like an idiot. Then our senior engineer looked at a connection I'd approved and said it was overdesigned by a lot, and when he asked why I'd accepted it I genuinely couldn't reconstruct what the tools reasoning had been—or even whether I'd had my own reasoning at the time.

I told him the tool is no different from the finite-element package everyone in the office runs without hand-solving the matrices, or from using a calculator, or from trusting a senior engineer whose heuristics I can't fully reproduce either. Part of me also thinks it shouldn't matter whether the reasoning lives in my head or in the tool, as long as it's available when I need it. But the client incident suggests it does matter—I had the answer right there and still couldn't say why it was right. He didn't have a comeback, and neither do I, but the uneasy feeling hasn't gone away. I can still recite the formulas; what I seem to have lost is something else. Is there a principled difference between these kinds of reliance, and what exactly do I lose when I let the tool do the part I used to do by hand?
