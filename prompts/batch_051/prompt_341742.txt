I'm a novelist working on a book about a taxonomist in his sixties who has spent his whole career describing a single genus of beetles in a cloud forest that is being cut down. The emotional core of the book is a speech he gives to a young geneticist who thinks species names are basically arbitrary filing conventions. He tells her to think of a species as an organism: it has a beginning in speciation, a life, and a death in extinction, and that's why extinction is not just crossing an entry out of a catalogue — it's a real death, the killing of a particular concrete thing. I've read enough philosophy of biology to gather that the idea that species are individuals rather than kinds is an actual position, and not just my character being poetic, and I want to know how much weight the analogy can really carry. I'd hate for the novel's central claim to rest on a metaphor a biologist would role their eyes at.

So treat the species-as-organism analogy as an argument, not a flourish. Give me a correspondence table: what plays the role of the organism's body, its cells, its birth, its health, its death, and so on. Then show me where the mapping breaks. I already have a fuzzy sense that hybridization, asexual lineages, ring species, and species that gradually shade into another species are awkward cases, but I don't know which of those are fatal and which are just untidy. Then say what the analogy quietly imports from organisms that a species might not have — a single cohesive body? a unified interest? a life cycle with a definite start and finish? — and whether my character's conclusion, that a species is a real individual and extinction is literally a kind of death, survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it's mostly dead, give me a better analogy for what the taxonomist is trying to get across. Finally, I want the response to be self-critical: tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or consideration would most change your verdict.
