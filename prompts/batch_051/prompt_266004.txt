I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I keep getting the same note from early readers: my characters' moments of weakness—where they do the thing they'd resolved not to do—read either arbitary or preachy. I've come across alot of talk about hyperbolic discounting: that people discount near-term rewards on a steeper curve, so your ranking of two options can genuinely flip as one of them gets close. On Sunday you plan for the salad and mean it; by Wednesday dinner the cake wins and you mean that too. I want to stop using that idea as a vague margin note and turn it into a real editing tool.

You're an expert in philosophy of action and the psychology of time-inconsistent choice. Think through the main mechanisms carefully—maybe brainstorm a taxonomy of ways a character can end up acting against their own better judgment—before writing anything. Then give me a short, reusable procedure, a checklist or flowchart in words, that I run on every scene where a character does something they believe they shouldn't. It should force me to answer three questions per scene: which mechanism is actually doing the work (preference reversal as the reward approaches, losing track of the longer-run aim, self-deception, plain capitulation, whatever else belongs on the list), where the character is on the relevant time horizon, and what would have to be different for the same character not to fail this time. Flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, where I have to make the call as the writer. Then apply it to two or three tiny sketched scenes—including at least one where the procedure tells me to cut or rewrite a scene I'd otherwise keep, because the psychology is incoherent.

For context: I revise slowly and an evidently phony temptation scene is the kind of thing reviewers and my editor latch onto, so the stakes are high even though I can take my time. I don't have the vocabulary, so define terms as you introduce them, and keep the final checklist short enough to tape above my desk.
