Me and my friend spent like three hours last night on the therapy/enhancement distinction, and he left me with this argument that I cant find my way out of. Its annoying because every step sounds right when he says it, but the conclusion feels crazy. Heres the argument as clean as I can make it:

(1) "Therapy" has to mean an intervention that moves a person toward the statistically normal range of functioning for human beings; "enhancement" means moving someone beyond that normal range. If you dont define it that way, the distinction is just your values wearing a medical costume.

(2) A treatment counts as basic healthcare—the kind of thing a decent system has to cover—only if it prevents or corrects a deficit relative to normal functioning.

(3) A healthy toddler before a vaccination has a normally functioning immune system. The vaccine doesn't fix any current deficit; it gives the immune system a capability it didn't already have, namely recognizing and remembering a pathogen it's never met.

(4) But giving someone a new capability that wasn't correcting a deficit and that goes beyond what their current normal functioning includes just is an enhancement, by (1).

(5) So vaccination is an enhancement, not a therapy.

(6) If the paradigm case of basic preventive medicine counts as an enhancement, then the therapy/enhancement distinction cant do the one job people built it for—sorting basic medical care from optional improvements.

(7) So either the distinction is empty, or it's just a fancy way of restating the very value judgments it was supposed to replace.

I'd really like to hang onto two things: that vaccinating a kid is basic healthcare and not some kind of luxury upgrade, and that there's some non-woolly definition of therapy that doesn't collapse into culture. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
