I've been reading the old Malcolm–Dennett fight about whether dreams are experiences, and it's maddening: both people seem to be right about something, and neither has an answer to the other's actual point. Malcolm's move, as I understand him, is that our entire grip on dreaming comes from the waking practice of telling a dream: we don't independently observe an occurrence during sleep, so the idea of an experience going on while we're asleep has no criterion to give it sense. Dennett grants a fair amount of the verification point but says there are still two live hypotheses: the received view, on which the dream is a stream of experience during sleep that gets remembered imperfectly, and the cassette theory, on which the narrative is composed at the moment of recall and then played as if it were a memory. And he thinks that's a factual question, just an empirically unsettled one, not a pseudo-question Malcolm has dissolved.

This dispute seems to have been basically stationary for decades. My working theory is that it's not about dreams at all but about what makes a hidden inner state real: Malcolm wants a first-person criterion, something it's like at the time and later reportable; Dennett wants a third-person explanatory role, whatever state best explains the waking report and the rest of the data. If that's right they're really arguing about burden of proof, not about dreaming. But I can see other candidates: maybe it's just a verbal ambiguity in the word experience, or Malcolm's verificationism is doing all the work and Dennett never actually refuted it, or the question is ill-posed because both sides implicitly treat memory as replay.
