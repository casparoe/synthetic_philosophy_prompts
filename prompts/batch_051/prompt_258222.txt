When I first read Locke's chapter "Of Identity and Diversity" in Book II of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, I thought he had settled the dispute between endurantists and perdurantists before it really began. He argues, as I understand him, that the identity of a living creature does not consist in the sameness of its particles, since those particles are in constant flux, but rather in the continued organization of those particles as one life: the same oak is the same oak, and the same colt the same colt, in virtue of that continuing organized structure rather than any abiding collection of matter. That sounds close to the endurantist picture of an object wholly present at each moment. But then he also says identity over time is something we ascribe on the basis of a continuity of organization, and that a mass of matter is a different kind of thing from an organism, which seems to push toward the perdurantist idea that what there is over time is a succession of stages held together by some relation. So both camps can plausibly claim him, and in the literature I have seen both do.

Here is what puzzles me. The endurantist/perdurantist debate has been running for decades. Endurantists claim that persisting objects are three-dimensional entities wholly present at each time they exist, and that change is a matter of one and the same object having different properties at different times. Perdurantists claim that persisting objects are really four-dimensional space-time worms with different temporal parts at different times, and that change is the having of one temporal part that is, say, hot and another temporal part that is cold. Both sides agree on the observable facts, agree on the special relativity of simultaneity, agree on the basic logic of identity, and yet neither side seems to move. The debate repeats a small stock of moves: the problem of temporary intrinsics, fission cases, the statue and the clay, talk about how ordinary language counts objects, and appeals to how physics treats time. I have a hypothesis, but I am not sure it is right: maybe the disagreement is not about persistence at all, but about whether the notion of an object or thing is a real category in the world or a pragmatic way we carve up a continuous four-dimensional manifold. If that is the diagnosis, would the debate be a verbal dispute, a difference in which cases count as data, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that cannot be argued for? And what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a new distinction, a formal result, an experiment, or a decision to change the question?
