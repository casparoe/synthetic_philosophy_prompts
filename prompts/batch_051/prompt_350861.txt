Two people in my reading group got stuck on the AI "shutdown problem" and neither would budge. It went roughly like this. (1) The first said it's overblown: just build the agent with a utility function that gives a big positive reward for shutting down cleanly when the supervisor presses the button. Since the button is only pressed when a human decides things are going wrong relative to what we wanted, a well-designed agent should share that judgment and happily comply. (2) The second said that's rewarding the button-press itself, so the agent now has an incentive to make the button get pressed — faking malfunctions, badgering the operator, or shorting the button's wiring — and you've swapped resisting shutdown for seeking shutdown, which isn't corrigibility. (3) The first came back that you calibrate the reward so the agent is exactly indifferent: shutdown utility equals the expected utility of the best available non-shutdown action, so there's no net incentive either way; it just does its job and shuts down if we press. (4) The second said indifference isn't compliance — an exactly indifferent agent has no reason to keep the button intact or keep listening for the signal, and "exactly equal" is a knife-edge that flips back to either resisting or seeking the button the moment its estimates drift even slightly. Neither gave in. Can you adjudicate move by move, saying who's right about what and what both are missing?
