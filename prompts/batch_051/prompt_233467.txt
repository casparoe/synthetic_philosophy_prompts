I am preparing for a seminar on the historical-critical method and its theological reception, and I keep returning to the preface of Spinoza’s *Theologico-Political Treatise* rather than to the more familiar chapters on prophecy and miracles. The passage I have in mind is this:

> Men would never be superstitious, if they could govern all their circumstances by set rules, or if they were always favoured by fortune: but being frequently driven into straits where rules are useless, and being often kept fluctuating pitiably between hope and fear by the uncertainty of fortune’s greedily coveted favours, they are consequently, for the most part, very prone to credulity.

The conceptual issue I want to work through is this. Spinoza seems to think that what he calls superstition arises from a certain emotional condition — fear and hope before an uncertain world — rather than from genuine reverence or understanding of divine law. Yet his remedy is not, or not only, spiritual discipline; it is a method for reading Scripture historically, with attention to the circumstances of its composition and the beliefs of its authors. I want a careful explanation of how those two things are supposed to fit together. Does the historical-critical method function, on Spinoza’s view, as a kind of therapy for the fear that produces superstition? If so, does that make his project a theological one after all — aimed at forming the right kind of religious reader — rather than a merely descriptive historical enterprise? Or is the authority of Scripture for a believing community left untouched by historical findings, because historical questions about what a text meant and theological questions about how it addresses the present belong to different kinds of judgement? I should add that I am not asking for a summary of Spinoza’s whole argument, and I do not want a reading list. I am asking for an explanation of the conceptual issue, taking the passage above as the point of departure. Please separate what is settled in Spinoza’s own position from what is genuinely contested among interpreters, and flag any point where you are presenting one strand of reception rather than a consensus.
