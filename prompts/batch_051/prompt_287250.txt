Here is an argument that seems to show the discursive dilemma cannot arise in a three-judge court. I am confident the conclusion is false, but I cannot locate the exact mistake.

A three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. The controlling rule is: the defendant is liable if and only if the defendant both breached the contract and the breach caused the plaintiff's loss. Each judge votes separately on the three propositions—breach, causation, liability—and each judge's individual votes respect the rule: she votes for liability exactly when she votes for both breach and causation. The court's holding on any proposition is whatever the majority of the three judges votes on that proposition.

The argument:

(1) For every judge, the judge votes for liability just in case the judge votes for both breach and causation.
(2) Assume the court holds, by majority, that the defendant breached the contract.
(3) Assume the court also holds, by majority, that the breach caused the plaintiff's loss.
(4) In a three-judge court, the two majority coalitions in (2) and (3) each have two members, so at least one judge belongs to both coalitions.
(5) That judge voted for both breach and causation.
(6) By (1), that judge votes for liability; and since this judge is in the majority on both elements, the court is committed to liability in the same way it is committed to the elements.
(7) Therefore, a majority of the court votes for liability, and the court cannot simultaneously accept both premises while rejecting the conclusion.

I know (7) cannot be right: a majority can accept breach, a majority can accept causation, and the one judge in the overlap can be outvoted on liability. So which step, exactly, is false or invalid? Does the argument nonetheless establish some weaker claim—for instance, that the court's majority-accepted premises are jointly satisfiable by at least one judge? If so, explain why that weaker result does not make the court's overall holdings coherent.
