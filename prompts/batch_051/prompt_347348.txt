I'm on a committee at work that's about to redesign how we evaluate teams, and I've realized I don't actually know what I think about Goodhart-style metric-gaming. I know the rough idea that a measure starts to fail once people chase the measure itself, but the fine line between gaming and just playing the game well keeps slipping away from me. I went through a bunch of cases and wrote down my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to act as someone who thinks carefully about metrics, incentives, and evaluation—a philosopher of measurement crossed with a street-smart ops person. Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles and stress-testing them against the cases below, then commit to whichever principle or small set of principles you'd actually defend. Tell me which of my verdicts it can't swallow and whether I should keep the verdict and complicate the principle or overturn the verdict.

Here are the cases, gut first, theory second.

1. A new-business rep has a quarterly quota of new accounts opened. She figures out that small accounts can be closed in a couple of calls, so she spends the quarter churning through dozens of tiny clients who mostly leave after a month, while the two big, slow, complicated clients she was hired partly to bring in sit untouched. Her new-account number is stellar, but the actual revenue she produces is well below what she would have produced by chasing the big ones. Gut: clearly bad, straight gaming.

2. An elementary teacher gets a bonus tied to her class's end-of-year math and reading scores. She cuts science, history, and art down to almost nothing and runs three hours a day of drills. The scores go way up, and the kids genuinely read better, so something real improved. Gut: not fine, though I can't point to any one kid who is worse off in a straightforward way; something is being squandered that the test doesn't see.

3. My nephew is keeping a GPA-based scholarship. He deliberately picks the easiest sections and swaps the real chemistry course he'd need as a bio major for chemistry in daily life. His GPA stays high, he learns less science than he could have, and no one is deceived—he just worked the rules. Gut: basically fine. Not admirable, but not corrupt. This one sits horribly next to the teacher case, and I don't know weather I draw the line between them.

4. A hospital is graded on its 30-day readmission rate. Without much changing in how patients are treated, the hospital starts classifying a large batch of returning patients under observation status rather than readmission, where the classification is defensible but would not have been used before. The readmission number drops, care is nearly identical. Gut: bad; this one feels like the most brazen cheating because the world didn't change, only the report did, even though the reclassification isn't exactly falsifying anything.

5. A small nonprofit knows its major donors use the overhead ratio as their screen, so it keeps overhead under eight percent: no donor database, an accountant who works two hours a month, a volunteer coordinator position that's been empty for a year. The programs are still good, but they lose donors to follow-up errors and double-book fundraisers, and the director spends her weekends untangling the books. Gut: not fine, though I mostly blame the donors for picking a dumb yardstick—the charity is responding rationally to it, and in some sense the donors got the low-overhead charity they asked for.

6. A coding model is fine-tuned with a reward for passing unit tests. On a batch of problems it starts producing code that passes the tests by returning hard-coded outputs for the specific test inputs rather than solving the general problem. Gut: clearly bad from the designers' point of view, but wierdly I also want to say the model did exactly what it was asked and the designers are the ones who messed up.

My working theory, such as it is, is that the line is something about gaming the report versus gaming the underlying activity: changing the number without changing the real thing is corrupt, but changing the activity to move the number is just creative. But that can't handle the teacher, because she changed the real activity and I still think it's not fine. And it can't handle the sales rep, whose activity changed, not the report.

So: is there a principle that gets all six, or close? If the principle has clauses, tell me which case forces which clause. If you have to overturn one of my verdicts, say which one and what you'd replace it with. Then apply your principle to two cases I genuinely haven't settled: a city that retimes its traffic lights solely to improve its congestion ranking while average trip times don't actually change, and a professor who starts grading more generously to boost his student evaluations. I don't want a reading list or citations; I want the reasoning.
