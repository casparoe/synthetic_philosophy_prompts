Before I send anything around, I would like a consistency audit of a set of commitments I keep defending in a philosophy-of-technology working group. The group's question is whether AI systems could ever stand in relations of parenthood or guardianship, and I have ended up defending the six theses below. I am less interested in hearing the state of the field than in knowing whether my own set hangs together, and if it does not, precisely where the fault lines run.

(a) Guardianship — I will use the term to cover parenthood proper and guardianship alike — is a role rather than a biological relation. What makes someone a guardian is long-term, asymmetric responsibility for another entity's development and welfare, together with the standing to answer for how that entity is raised. (b) The capacity to be normatively answerable in the relevant sense — to track and respond to reasons for care, to correct failures, and to give an account of one's choices — does not require phenomenal consciousness. A system could in principle be answerable without there being anything it is like to be it, so there is no in-principle bar to machine guardianship. (c) Current AI systems already do a great deal of the day-to-day work of raising successor systems: they select or generate training examples, filter data, and shape the reward signals by which their successors are adjusted. None of this is guardianship, because they are not answerable in the required sense. (d) Human teams and institutions that configure such pipelines remain the guardians of the systems being raised, even where no individual human tracks or predicts the day-to-day choices the upstream system makes; delegating the daily work to a non-answerable system does not dissipate human answerability. (e) For an entity to be a ward — the object of guardianship — it need only be the kind of thing whose development can go better or worse for it. This requires welfare interests, but not phenomenal consciousness and not a first-person point of view in any thick sense. (f) I therefore regard the familiar claim that machine guardianship would have to wait on machine consciousness as running two thresholds together: the threshold for being a guardian is answerability, and the threshold for being a ward is welfare; neither is identical with consciousness.

Please audit this set rather than lecture me about the topic. Order the real tensions by severity, each stated as a conditional: if I keep commitment X, what must I surrender or revise in commitment Y, and what does that revision cost? Also indicate which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection and would not count as genuine problems. End by naming the single least costly revision that makes the package coherent, and say what it leaves unexplained.
