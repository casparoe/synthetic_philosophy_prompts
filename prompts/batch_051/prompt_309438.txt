I keep reversing myself on the ethics of randomized controlled trials, and I want a structured adjudication. I'm attracted to the collective-equipoise view: a trial is ethically acceptable when the relevant expert community is genuinely divided about which arm is better, even if the clinician enrolling a given patient is not herself in a state of genuine uncertainty. The view's virtues are systematic — it makes large trials possible without requiring each clinician to feign perfect ignorance, it gives a public and profession-level criterion rather than one that varies with each physician's private hunch, and it explains why a trial can be permissible at the institutional level even when no single investigator is exactly balanced. But I have a firm intuition that will not go away: the doctor facing me at the bedside is not the medical community and does not get to spread a community-level uncertainty over me; she is my fiduciary. If she believes arm A is worse for me, the fact that other experts disagree does not make it acceptable for her to flip the coin over my treatment. On that picture, collective equipoise looks like a bureaucratic device that launders a wrong to this patient through an aggregate of other clinicians' opinions. Give me the strongest statement of both positions and a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theory be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading on which the institution and the bedside clinician can both be right at different levels — and what would justify each move rather than merely picking a side? Format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
