Next month I'm giving a talk to an EA- and rationalist-adjacent research group—mostly software engineers, a couple of people who work on mechanistic interpretability, and one actual philosopher of science—where I'll defend the claim that computational irreducibility doesn't force deep pessimism about explaining complex systems, including the large trained models people are currently worried about. My basic move is that Wolfram's principle is a result about the length of computations: for an irreducible system you can't get from rules to outcomes by anything shorter than just running the system. But explanation isn't the same as shortcut prediction. You can give the simple program that generates the system, say why it's irreducible, characterize the space of behaviors it can fall into, and still have failed to skip the run. What you can't do is jump from initial condition to later state by a shorter derivation, and the claim that an irreducible system is therefore unexplainable conflates two different things we might want from a system. I'm not saying explanation has no predictive component; I'm saying the specific predictive failure Wolfram identifies isn't the same thing as there being nothing to explain. Several people in the audience read A New Kind of Science years ago and took away exactly the opposite message, and the interpretability folks are primed to think irreducibility is bad news for ever understanding what a trained model is doing.

I'd like a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense memo. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased somewhat the way this audience would phrase them (the engineers will talk about compression and Kolmogorov complexity; the interpretability people will talk about features and causal graphs; the philosopher will go after my definition of explanation). For each objection: a two-minute reply, what to concede up front, and an honest note on where the reply is softest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
