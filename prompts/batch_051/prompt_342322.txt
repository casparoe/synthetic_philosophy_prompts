I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on personal identity, and I find that students can recite the standard furniture of the debate—bodily continuity, psychological continuity, memory, the teletransporter—without being able to say what work any of it is doing. I want to force the issue by asking for a definition, but not of personal identity in the abstract. I want a working definition of what matters in survival: the relation, or complex of relations, that a person is trying to preserve when they care about surviving, and that would license future-directed self-concern, anticipation, and responsibility. I want an actual criterion, not a paraphrase or a list of intuitions.

Here are the seven cases it must classify. (1) Ordinary overnight sleep. (2) Total, permanent amnesia with a radical change in character after an accident. (3) The standard Mars teletransporter: the body is scanned and destroyed, and an exact replica with the same memories and psychology is built on Mars. (4) The branch-line variant: the machine malfunctions and does not destroy the original, who will die in a few days, while the healthy replica goes on. (5) A perfect replica of me is created in the next room while I continue to exist, unthreatened. (6) Gradual replacement of all my neurons by functionally equivalent synthetic units over several years, carried out while I am awake, with consciousness and memory continuous throughout. (7) A hemisphere transplant: one hemisphere is moved into a new body, the other remains in the old one, and both wake with my memories.

My provisional judgments are these: (1) is clearly survival and clearly what matters; in (2) I am fairly confident the person survives, but I doubt that everything that matters survives with them; in (3) I do not think I survive, yet I can see no reason not to step into a machine I trusted, which makes me suspect I am wrong about at least one of those two verdicts; in (5) I do not think the replica's future matters to me the way my own does, though I cannot say whether the reason is uniqueness or something else; in (6) I think I clearly survive and clearly retain what matters; and in (7) I have no settled view. Please propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it yields a verdict you find wrong or none. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict, and tell me which of your final verdicts is least secure.

If the request is badly framed—for instance, if 'what matters in survival' cannot be captured by a single criterion, or if the cases secretly conflate the question of survival with the question of what matters—please say so plainly and reframe the task rather than forcing a yes-or-no verdict on each case. I am not asking what the phrase really means in ordinary language; I am asking for the best defensible criterion, so please flag every point at which you are stipulating rather than analyzing.
