The stalemate I want diagnosed is the one in metaethics over evolutionary debunking. On one side, the debunker argues that because natural selection shaped our evaluative tendencies to track reproductive outcomes rather than evaluative truth, our moral judgments would look about the same whether or not moral facts existed, so the realist who trusts them owes us an explanation of why this is not a coincidence. On the other side, the realist concedes the selection story but denies that it is epistemically corrosive, insisting that natural selection can make true moral beliefs adaptive when a third factor does double duty (what makes survival good or pain bad is both fitness-relevant and morally real), that perception-style asymmetries save some evolved capacities even if evaluative capacities turn out not to be saved, that only the genealogy of particular beliefs matters while a genealogy of the capacity does not, and that the argument improperly overgeneralizes to every evolved cognitive capacity. Both sides now largely agree on the natural history, and neither has moved in years. I do not want a verdict on who wins, and I do not want a survey. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled: is it verbal or substantive; are the camps disagreeing about what a coincidence-explanation requires, about whether reliability is a defeasible status rather than a fact to be explained, about what the epistemic agent is entitled to take for granted, or about the empirical question of how strongly selection acts on moral judgment specifically? My working hypothesis is that the debunker and the realist are performing two different tasks — first-person epistemic regulation (once I learn my genealogy, should I still trust my convictions?) versus third-person vindication (what could make moral belief reliable?) — and that they mostly pass each other without engaging. Is that dissection right, or does the disagreement survive it? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper distinction such as capacity-genealogy versus belief-genealogy, a statistical or causal result about reliability under arbitrary third-factor hypotheses, a new empirical constraint on how selection shaped moral judgment, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
