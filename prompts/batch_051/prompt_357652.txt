I'm a science journalist, and I've somehow agreed to give a seminar next month at a university history department — usually it's just the faculty and grad students, but there's a couple of philosophers of science who sits in — defending the hinge-of-history hypothesis. The version I'm defending says the coming decades are unusually pivotal: because the course of this century, especially around advanced AI and synthetic biology, could lock in or foreclose an enormous amount of the future, choices made by the current generation might matter more in expectation than choices made in almost any earlier period. I find the expected-value logic genuinely attractive, and it gives a clean answer to why someone alive now should be more existentially anxious than a fourteenth-century peasant had reason to be.

The problem is my audience. The historians mostly think every era has believed it was the hinge, that presentism is the besetting sin of overeducated journalists, and that the whole claim is unfalsifiable because the relevant future hasn't happened yet. The philosophers of science will be friendlier to the structure of the argument but will push on what "pivotal" could even mean if you can't rerun history. Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this group, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, each phrased the way these people would actually put it, with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, and include the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Write the whole thing in American English.
