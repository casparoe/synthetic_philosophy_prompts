I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending the view that laws of nature are primitive governing facts — facts about what the world must do, over and above the Humean mosaic of particular matters of fact — rather than economical summaries of that mosaic. The audience will be a philosophy-of-science department with a strong empiricist bent: nearly all of them treat something like a best-system account of laws as the default, several work on special-science laws in biology and economics and are used to generalizations with exceptions, and two work in the foundations of statistical mechanics and care a great deal about whether the entropy gradient counts as a law at all. My argument, in brief, is that the best-system account purchases its parsimony by making laws merely compact descriptions, so it cannot explain why events conform to them; that the direction of explanation in physics runs from laws to particular motions rather than the reverse; and that hedged special-science generalizations are compatible with a governing conception once ideal laws are distinguished from their noisy approximations. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular audience is most likely to raise, ordered by both likelihood and damage, each phrased the way an empiricist philosopher of physics or a special-science philosopher would actually put it, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where that reply is softest. Also tell me which question I should be praying nobody asks, and what I should concede up front to keep the room from turning against the view in the first ten minutes.
