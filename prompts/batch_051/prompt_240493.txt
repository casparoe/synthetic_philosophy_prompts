I'm a journalist working on a piece about extinction anxiety, and I keep circling back to Children of Men — maybe because the film basically is a thought experiment about demography. Here's the lens I want to read it through. Samuel Scheffler has this "afterlife conjecture": the claim that much of what we care about, like finishing a novel or funding a long-shot medical trial, only makes sense against an unspoken background assumption that humanity will go on after we individually are gone. Children of Men presses exactly that button: no child has been born for eighteen years, so the collective afterlife has been canceled, even though almost everyone currently alive will die of ordinary causes. Does the film actually vindicate Scheffler? I'd like a scene-by-scene reading of where the movie dramatizes the loss of a future — the art, the routines, the politics — and where it resists the reading: is what the characters loose really about an assumed afterlife, or is it about more immediate things like grief, hopelessness, and a society of the aging with no young people in it? Take both the film and the idea seriously, and end by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the bare argument doesn't.
