I have been teaching an introductory moral philosophy section, and a student asked me why promises bind. We looked at ordinary promise-making—wedding vows, handshake deals between friends, signed contracts—and noticed something odd. Lawyers in common-law systems often say that a bare promise is not enforceable unless something has been given in return or the promisee has relied on it to their detriment, yet outside court we blame people for breaking bare promises even when no one has relied on them. The practice seems to treat promising as, by itself, creating a genuine obligation, while the legal doctrine seems to deny that the bare act does any such legal work.

Hume attacks the idea that a bare act of mind could create an obligation. In A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part II, Section V, he writes: "Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

My question is this: What philosophical commitments does our combined moral-legal practice of holding people to promises actually presuppose? Does the everyday practice presuppose that promising is a normative power—something like willing an obligation into existence—while the legal practice presupposes a reliance- or exchange-based view? Are those commitments consistent, or is one of them a distortion of the other? If Hume's argument is accepted, what exactly would have to change in the practice, and what should we say about the moral blame we attach to breaking a bare promise?
