In the philosophy of consciousness, one debate has been stalled for decades: whether qualia—introspectively accessible, intrinsic phenomenal characters of experience—are real features of mental life or an incoherent posit that should be eliminated. Realists keep pointing to the obvious fact that there is something it is like to taste coffee or see red; eliminativists keep replying that such appeals assume the very kind of introspective certainty the debate is supposed to earn. Dennetts Chase and Sanborn thought experiment is often treated as the point where the two sides simply talk past each other: two coffee tasters both report that their experience of the coffee has changed, one insisting that his taste experience changed while his standards stayed fixed, the other insisting that his standards changed while his taste experience stayed the same, and nothing in their introspective reports seems able to settle which is right—or even whether the distinction between the two reports is real. I want a diagnosis rather than another defense of either side. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about the term quale, a disagreement about the evidential authority of introspection, a difference over which cases count as data, or bedrock intuitions about whether experience could outrun our judgments about it? And given your diagnosis, is there any form of progress—an experimental design, a sharper distinction, a change of question—that would move it, or is the impasse itself evidence that the question is ill-posed? Please answer in American English.
