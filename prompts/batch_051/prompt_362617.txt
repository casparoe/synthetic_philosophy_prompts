I am drafting a two-page proposal for a one-semester philosophy paper (about 6,000 words) on the ethics of historical fiction and biopics, and I want the proposal to do the conceptual framing itself. The broad question: what do the makers of such works owe the real dead when they dramatize them? The usual answers fall into two families. Either the obligations are owed to the living—descendants, communities, victims, audiences, the public record—on the grounds that the dead are beyond harm; or they are owed to the dead individual, which then has to confront the difficulty that the dead cannot be benefited or harmed. My suspicion is that both families share a false assumption: that the only possible direct object of the debt is the dead person considered as a private individual. I want to explore a third option, that a dramatization can also fail what I will call the figure: the person considered as a public protagonist of a history that outlives them, whose deeds and words have become material for retellings that will continue whether or not descendants consent. Please turn this into a proposal that does the following: states the question so that it has a determinate answer and identifies the possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them; explains why the two standard positions leave the question open; lays out an approach that could make progress and says why it is preferable to relitigating the general posthumous-harm debate; says what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure; and gives an honest assessment of the project's feasibility over one semester, including what could make it too large. Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will add those later.
