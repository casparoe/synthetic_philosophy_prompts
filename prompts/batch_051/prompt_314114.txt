In my government class we have been reading excerpts from Machiavelli’s The Prince, and I got stuck on a passage in the section on whether a prince should keep his promises. Machiavelli argues there that a ruler who wants to hold onto power must be willing to do things that ordinary private morality would condemn, and that although a prince should seem merciful, faithful, humane, honest, and religious, he must be ready to turn to the opposite qualities whenever the safety of the state requires it. He also adds that a prince should never appear to lack those good qualities, because appearances matter more than what a ruler actually is. I am paraphrasing, but that is the gist.

My confusion is about how to categorize what Machiavelli is recommending. One way to read him is that there is no deep conflict between morality and politics here, because the prince is judged by a different standard—keeping the state—and so an act that would be wrong in private life simply is not wrong for a ruler. A second way to read him is that the prince really does commit moral wrongs, but those wrongs are the unavoidable cost of governing, so the prince ends up with what some people call dirty hands. I would like an explanation of the conceptual difference between these two readings. Specifically, I want to know what each reading implies about whether the prince should feel guilt or regret, and whether anything in the passage should push a reader toward one reading rather than the other. Could a ruler genuinely have dirty hands if he ceases to feel the pull of ordinary morality, and if not, does that show the two readings are more tangled than they look?
