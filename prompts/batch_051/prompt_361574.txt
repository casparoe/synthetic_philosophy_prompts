Mencius's claim that human nature is good is often read as a value judgment, but he defended it with an empirical picture: human beings begin with incipient moral dispositions — compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong — which become full virtues only through cultivation, and which show up in spontaneous reactions such as alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well. Xunzi rejected this: the natural material is unruly desire, and moral goodness is acquired entirely through deliberate effort, ritual, and habituation. Both positions can predict that most adults in a functioning moral community behave well, so the dispute must be settled, if at all, by looking earlier in development or at the trajectory of learning. Design the best empirical test you can for separating the two pictures. Specify the relevant population, the manipulation or observation, and the pattern of results that would favour each side. Then say precisely what a positive result would and would not establish — in particular, whether it could distinguish a Mencian first-person moral motivation, such as my own spontaneous compassion or shame, from a merely third-person preference for some agents over others, and whether any developmental finding could bridge the remaining gap to the normative claim that human nature is good.
