In Ellsberg's classic three-color urn, 90 balls consist of 30 red balls and 60 balls that are either black or yellow in unknown proportion. Many decision makers strictly prefer a bet paying $100 on red to a bet paying $100 on black, and strictly prefer a bet paying $100 on black-or-yellow to a bet paying $100 on red-or-yellow, a pair of preferences that no single probability assignment over the three colors can accommodate.

Consider this variation. The urn is now prepared in public by drawing B uniformly from the integers 0 through 60, placing B black balls and 60 − B yellow balls in the urn together with the 30 red balls. The drawing rule is announced, but the value of B is not revealed. A decision maker still strictly prefers red to black, and black-or-yellow to red-or-yellow.

Work through the changed analysis. First, under expected utility theory with compound lotteries reduced to their unconditional probabilities, compute the probability of winning each of the four bets, and say whether the two strict preferences remain jointly consistent with expected utility. Second, consider a rule that refuses to reduce the compound lottery and instead evaluates each bet by its worst-case winning probability over all values of B allowed by the announced rule; compute those four worst-case probabilities and say whether the Ellsberg pattern survives. Finally, state what this variation shows about the relation between ambiguity aversion and second-order risk, and which of the two treatments of the announced distribution is doing the work.
