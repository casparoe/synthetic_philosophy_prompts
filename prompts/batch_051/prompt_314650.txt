I write a column for a science magazine where I explain mathematical results to a general audience—not the heavy stuff, but things like why the diagonal argument works, what the four color theorem actually says, why the square root of two is irrational. The problem I keep running into: after I interview a mathematician or read the paper, I can usually restate the steps of a proof in order, but I honestly can't tell whether I *understand* the result or whether I've just memorized the moves well enough to pass. My editor can't tell either; she's a smart generalist but not a mathematician, and she mostly checks for clarity and for whether the steps as written are accurate. So we've run a few pieces that were faithful to the proof but felt dead—like a transcript of someone playing chess with none of the reasons the moves mattered—and once Im fairly sure we published something where neither of us could have caught a subtle error, because neither of us could regenerate the reasoning from scratch.

I want a reusable procedure, not a one-off judgment. The constraints: I do one of these pieces every few weeks; I have roughly an undergrad math background and can schedule one follow-up call with the researcher, but I can't spend the call being walked through every line again; and my editor signs off only after I've certified that the piece is "understood, not just parroted."

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run: the questions I ask myself (or the source) in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "you don't understand this yet, go back," and where it hands the decision to me or my editor because no procedure can settle it. Ground it in what's actually known about the difference between understanding a proof and being able to reproduce one, not just vibes. Then run the procedure on two or three worked examples, including at least one where it tells me something I won't want to hear. I'll suggest three, use them if they work and swap if they don't: (1) the usual proof that the square root of 2 is irrational, written by someone who can reproduce it perfectly but fumbles when asked which step fails for the square root of 4; (2) Cantor's diagonal argument, by someone who can state and run it but can't explain why the same move doesn't show the rationals are uncountable; (3) the Four Color Theorem, where the proof is computer-assisted and even mathematicians argue about whether anyone has really understood it. And say explicitly where the procedure itself breaks down or would give me false confidence.
