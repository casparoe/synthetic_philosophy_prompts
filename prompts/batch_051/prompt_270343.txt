In a philosophy of science course I am taking on mechanisms and causal explanation, we have been discussing what the instructor calls mechanistic anomaly detection. The working picture is that one fits a model of some target mechanism, and a new case gets flagged as anomalous when it falls outside the model’s expected operating envelope. But this seems to presuppose rather than solve a difficult individuation problem: what makes the new case a deviant instance of the same mechanism, rather than an instance of a different mechanism or simply evidence that the original mechanism’s envelope was drawn too narrowly?

Could you explain the conceptual issue here? I would like you to work through, in particular: (1) the token/type distinction—what it is for a mechanism to be the same one recurring, as against another mechanism of the same kind; (2) how granularity of description affects sameness judgments, and the risk of either lumping genuinely distinct mechanisms together or splitting normal variation into spurious anomalies; (3) the difference between components and mere background conditions; and (4) why a change in parameter values need not be a change in mechanism even when the system’s behavior shifts considerably. Ideally, you would show how a single observation can come out as either an anomaly or ordinary variation depending on how these boundaries are set, and say what it would take for mechanism individuation to do real work instead of being gerrymandered after the fact. I am more interested in the underlying philosophy than in machine-learning notions of anomaly.
