I chair the strategy committee of a private development foundation with a forty-year mandate to raise average incomes in a single country. The committee must decide this quarter how to commit the first ten years of funding among three competing strategies, each of which is a practical expression of a different answer to the old question of why some nations are rich. Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before the committee's vote, writing as my chief economist and a trained political economist.

The country is composite rather than real; treat the facts below as the full record and do not introduce additional factual claims about it. It is small and landlocked. The north has poor, degraded soils and unreliable rainfall. The south has one navigable river and a single functioning rail line to the nearest port in a neighboring country. State capacity is thin outside the capital, and property records are incomplete. A large share of adults report that most people cannot be trusted in commercial dealings. The colonial period ended in the 1960s, and the current leadership will permit the foundation to operate in either region as long as the program is framed as a pilot.

The institutions strategy would spend the ten years on land-titling and property registration, support for tax administration, commercial courts, and anti-corruption audits, and a constitutional convention with technical advisors. Its proponents argue that secure property rights, credible contract enforcement, and constraints on officials are the master cause of prosperity because they change the incentives of rulers and ordinary producers alike.

The geography strategy would spend the ten years on roads connecting the two regions, irrigation, soil restoration, an agricultural research station, and a railway extension to the port. Its proponents argue that transport costs and soil fertility set the ceiling before institutions can matter, and that better institutions are largely a downstream effect of the larger surplus that better geography permits.

The culture strategy would spend the ten years on civic education, a mass-media campaign aimed at generalized trust and punctuality, and revised school curricula emphasizing cooperation and trade. Its proponents argue that norms and values, above all whether strangers can deal with one another without a relative or patron to enforce the agreement, are the deepest constraint, and that laws, roads, and titles do little where those norms are absent.

Your written opinion should recommend exactly one primary strategy for the first ten years, with a concrete sequence of first moves and a single quantitative or quasi-quantitative checkpoint at year five that the committee could use to decide whether to continue, expand, or abandon the strategy. It should honestly present the strongest version of each losing proposal, including the strongest objection to your own recommendation, and explain why the balance of reasoning still favors your choice. It must also address the committee's deeper worry directly: cross-country history is a single non-experimental sequence, the classic historical cases adduced for each theory were not randomized, and some members suspect the three factors co-evolve so tightly that asking which of them causes national wealth may be ill-posed. Say what, if anything, would count as evidence that the chosen strategy is failing, and how the foundation should respond if the year-five checkpoint is ambiguous.

Think carefully before you draft. First reconstruct each proposal as its smartest proponent would present it, including the best objections to the other two and the best objection to their own view. Then consider where the burden of proof falls when the available historical evidence cannot settle the matter. Do not strawman any of the three positions.

Write the opinion as continuous prose, in the voice of a formal internal memo or letter to the committee. Do not use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold or italic text, or tables. No citations or bibliography are expected; reason from the case facts and general economic reasoning alone.
