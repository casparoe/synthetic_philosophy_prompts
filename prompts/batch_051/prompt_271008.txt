At dinner the other night two friends of mine got into it about pain asymbolia, and I keep feeling like neither of them quite landed the point. Quick background in case you haven't run across the case: pain asymbolia is what you get when a person (usually after brain damage) can tell you they're in pain, can locate it, can grade how intense it is — but they don't mind. No grimacing, no pulling away, no asking for painkillers. What seems to be missing is the unpleasantness, while the sensation stays.

The exchange, roughly as it happened:

(1) Mark: asymbolia shows the badness of pain is a seperate layer the brain adds on top of a bare sensory event. Pain itself is just information; the "this is awful" part is an evaluative response. So "why is pain bad?" has a real answer — it's bad because the person's motivational system treats it as bad, not because badness is baked into the pain itself.

(2) Dana: that over-reads one weird lesion case. Knock out a component of something and the component doesn't stop being part of the thing. People with achromatopsia lose color without losing vision, but nobody concludes color was never really part of seeing. Ordinary pain just is the package — the hurting plus the badness. An asymbolic has pain only in a deflated, revisionary sense, and saying "see, pain isn't essentially bad" is sneaking the revision in as if it were an observation.

(3) Mark: no, the achromatopsia analogy cuts the other way. Vision without color still counts as seeing, and pain without unpleasantness still counts as pain — the person says it hurts and can rank intensities, which is the ordinary test. And the "it's a component" reply doesn't touch the real point: if the badness can come off while the thing the person is talking about stays put, then they are two things. You can't answer "is this state bad?" by noting it's usually accompanied by a bad-making feeling.

(4) Dana: fine, but then you owe us an account of the badness that doesn't make any stray aversion authoritative. Suppose a drug removed the unpleasantness but left the same sensory event. Is the pain no longer bad? If yes, then badness is optional in a way that seems to lose our ordinary reason to get rid of pain. If no, then badness isn't just the evaluative response. She said she doubts there's a stable middle ground between "pain is essentially bad" and "pain is only contingently bad," and the essentialist picture handles our reasons much better.

I stepped in at that point and said I'd need them to taboo the word "bad" before I could even assign points, and the whole thing trailed off. So I'm outsourcing the refereeing to you.

Please adjudicate move by move: for (1) through (4), say whether each move is clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what each of them is missing, and whether one is more right overall — or whether the real disagreement is mostly verbal once "bad" is unpacked.
