I attend a reading group on classical Islamic ethics, and last night two of the other members ended the session in a dispute that none of us could settle. They asked me to referee on the spot, and I could not, so I am bringing the exchange here for a proper adjudication.

The shared background: the group has been working through the Mu'tazilite and Ash'arite accounts of moral value, the dispute sometimes called the Islamic Euthyphro. On the Mu'tazilite-leaning view, an act is commanded by God because it is good; goodness is an intelligible feature of the act that reason can in principle grasp, and God, being just, does not command the evil. On the Ash'arite-leaning view, an act is good because God commands it and evil because God prohibits it; there is no standard of value prior to God's command and independent of His will against which His commands could be assessed. Mariam was defending the first view and Yusuf the second.

Their exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

Mariam: If goodness is just whatever God commands, then the sentence "God commands the good" is an empty tautology, and your view implies that had God commanded theft, theft would have been good. That is the arbitrariness objection, and it seems fatal to your position.

Yusuf: It is not arbitrary, because God commands from wisdom, not caprice. A command contrary to God's wisdom is not a live possibility, so the counterfactual about theft is idle.

Mariam: But what does "from wisdom" add? If it means God commands theft-like acts in virtue of their badness, then you have conceded my point, because badness is prior to the command. If it only means that whatever God commands is wise because God commands it, then nothing has been answered.

Yusuf: The dilemma you are pressing assumes that there is an independent standard of goodness by which God's commands can be measured. On my view, such a standard would be either God Himself or something other than God, and the second option compromises His oneness and self-sufficiency. So the cost you are trying to pin on me is one your own view cannot avoid.

Mariam: The standard is not a created thing set over against God; it is internal to God's nature, in God's justice. God's not commanding evil is inconsistent with His being just, in the way that His not being ignorant is internal to His knowledge. That is no more a rival to God than His power or His knowledge is.

Yusuf: Then your position has either collapsed into mine or simply relocated the problem. If "good" just means "whatever God's nature necessarily wills," that is my view in different words. If it means something distinguishable from God's will, then it is a standard to which God is answerable, and we are back to an external constraint.

Mariam: There is a real difference, not a verbal one. On my view, there is an answer to the question why a given command is good: the act has a feature that makes it good, and God commands it for that reason. On your view, the only answer is "because God commanded it," which is not an explanation of anything.

Yusuf: Explanations stop somewhere. You stop at God's nature; I stop at God's command. The substantive issue is not where the chain stops but whether reason can reach the stopping point without revelation. I deny that it can, and your confidence that reason can reach it is exactly what I reject—and what the tradition's texts deny.

Mariam: If reason cannot reach it, justice becomes indistinguishable from power. We would have no resources to say that a claimant who announces commands in God's name and demands cruelty is wrong, except by appealing to a revelation the claimant disputes. What your view gains in divine sovereignty it loses in the ability to call injustice by its name.

Yusuf: What your view gains in independent criticism it loses in divine transcendence: you have made God answerable to a court of human reason. And the false-claimant problem cuts both ways, since every tyrant also professes to act for an evident good. The concrete check on fake revelation is prophecy and a transmitted law, not a philosophical faculty.

They asked me to say who was right about what. Please go through the exchange point by point and adjudicate: which premise or inference is right in each round, where each speaker genuinely advances the argument and where one of them changes the subject or overstates, what—if anything—both are missing, and who is more right overall. I do not want a diplomatic "both sides have a point"; I want a determinate, step-by-step verdict.
