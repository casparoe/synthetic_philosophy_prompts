I'm teaching a one-off session in an intro social science course for second-years, about forty students, and the topic this week is whether there's such a thing as "national character" and whether it's ever acceptable to generalize about peoples. The students will show up with the usual folk views: some of them will think it's obviously true that Finns are reserved and Americans are loud, others will think any sentence beginning "the French are..." is basically just bigotry, and neither group will have thought very hard about why they hold those positions. I don't want you to explain the concept to me; I want a plan for how to teach it in seventy-five minutes.

Here's what I'm worried about. I think the first thing they need is a clean separation between three things: a statistical claim about a distribution in a population, a claim about each individual member, and a claim about some shared underlying essence or "character." But maybe that's too much too early, and maybe the order matters in a way I'm not seeing. What order should the pieces go in, and what has to be in place before what? What everyday example or thought experiment should carry the whole session—ideally one that doesn't endlessly pick on a single real nationality, since the room is international and I don't want it to turn into a festival of stereotypes? What misconceptions should I expect them to bring or form along the way, and at which point in the sequence should I name and head off each one? I'm guessing the two big ones are "if the average difference is real, then the generalization is fine" and its twin "all generalization is just prejudice," but there are probably subtler ones I haven't anticipated. What should I explicitly leave out at this level and tell them I'm leaving out? And what one question could I ask at the end that would show me they've actually got the concept, rather than just learned to say "it's complicated"?
