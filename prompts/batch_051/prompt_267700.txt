I am preparing a feature for a general-interest science and ideas outlet on the reasoning people actually offer for signing up for cryonic preservation, and I want to stress-test one argument I encountered repeatedly. A cryonics advocate I interviewed framed the decision as a modern Pascal's wager. On his telling, the annual cost of a cryonics policy is modest and bounded, while the payoff if revival someday succeeds is an enormously long additional life that can, for practical purposes, be treated as unbounded; therefore any nonzero probability of successful revival is enough to make signing up the rational choice, and one need not believe that revival is likely. The analogy was doing real work here: it was meant to license the conclusion that even a skeptical person with spare income should sign up.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than simply accept or reject it. Please begin with an explicit correspondence table: what in the cryonics decision is playing the role of belief in God, the act of wagering, the supposedly infinite payoff, the finite cost, and the probability assignment in Pascal's original argument. Then identify where the mapping breaks, attending especially to features the wager framework quietly imports: an infinite rather than merely very large payoff, a well-defined probability rather than deep uncertainty, a single live option rather than a range of possible revival technologies and scenarios, and a payoff whose value to the wagerer does not depend on disputed facts about personal identity. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the advocate's conclusion from those that are merely harmless differences. Finally, say whether the conclusion — that signing up is rationally required, or at least clearly rational, for a skeptical person of ordinary means once revival probability is nonzero — survives the audit, and whether some better analogy, such as a cheap option on an unlikely future, an insurance policy with an uncertain payout, or a straightforward expected-value calculation, would serve the argument more faithfully.
