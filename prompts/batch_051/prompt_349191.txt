I sit on a working group reviewing copyright policy, and one member has been arguing against any shortening of exclusive rights by reaching for Locke. He quotes this passage, from John Locke, Second Treatise of Government, chapter V, "Of Property," section 28:

"Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body. The labour that was mine, removing them out of that common state they were in, hath fixed my property in them."

His move is to read this as a direct template for intellectual property. The public domain, he says, is the common state that ideas and expressions start out in; a writer's or engineer's labour removes a work from that common stock; and since the labour is the labourer's, the resulting work is hers not because a statute grants it but because her labour has fixed it as her property. From this he concludes that exclusive rights in expressions and inventions should be treated as the natural default rather than as a policy instrument we may trim: restricting scope or term is, on his picture, like confiscating what someone's labour has produced.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than rehearse the standard copyright debate. Please give me the correspondence table explicitly — in the target, what plays the role of the land or turf, the ore, the act of removal from the common state, the exclusion of other commoners, and, since Locke's sentence mentions it, the servant. Then sort the disanalogies: which features of Locke's land-commons does the analogy quietly import into an idea-commons that are not there — I have in mind whether ideas are depleted, spoiled, or independently obtainable the way his natural materials were, and whether Locke's proviso that enough and as good remains for others can even be stated for intellectual objects — and which of those disanalogies are incidental rather than ones that break the inference to property. Finally, tell me whether his natural-rights conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the land analogy was carrying the whole argument; and if there is a better analogy for intellectual property, say what it is and what it would and would not support.
