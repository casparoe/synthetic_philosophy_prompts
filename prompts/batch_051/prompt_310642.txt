You are a professional moral philosopher whose research concerns the structure of moral obligation, and I would like a careful consistency audit of a set of commitments before I present them in an advanced seminar. Please think through this slowly. Begin by restating each of my commitments in your own words so that I can see the structure you are attributing to me, then work through their interactions before reaching an overall judgement.

Here is the set of views I currently hold.

(a) The distinction between the required and the supererogatory is a genuine structural feature of morality. Some acts are supererogatory: they are morally good, they go beyond what duty demands, and omitting them is permissible and fully in order, not merely excused.

(b) At least some supererogatory acts are morally better than the alternative of doing no more than duty. The bystander who stays behind in a flood and rescues two further strangers at serious risk to herself does something morally better, not merely more admirable in some aesthetic or non-moral sense, than the bystander who has already done everything duty asks and then stops.

(c) Moral betterness is reason-giving. Whenever one available act is morally better than another, the better act has stronger moral reasons in its favour, and the agent has at least some moral reason to choose it.

(d) An adequate moral theory cannot demand saintliness. Morality must be liveable by ordinary persons, leaving room for self-interest, personal projects, and intimate relationships without blame or standing guilt. I take this to be part of the point of insisting on a category of supererogation.

(e) Supererogation nevertheless belongs inside moral assessment, not outside it. Public practices of honouring the supererogatory, through medals, commemoration, and moral education that presents heroic figures as exemplary, are justified rather than confused.

(f) I reject the temptation to preserve supererogation by saying that the hero's act is morally inert, or that it is not genuinely better than the merely adequate act but only admirable in a different, non-moral register. Whatever account of supererogation I accept must preserve the claim that the hero's act is better in the same moral currency in which duty and wrongdoing are assessed.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn? Please rank any genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious. For each genuine tension, specify exactly which commitment or commitments would have to be revised or surrendered, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision would cost me downstream. Finally, tell me which single revision to my position would do the most to restore coherence, and defend that recommendation.
