I host a small philosophy of language podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends an account of generic sentences on which they describe the dispositional profile of a kind rather than any statistical frequency among its members. On her view, a sentence like ducks lay eggs is true because egg-laying belongs to the kind's normal reproductive profile, and a sentence like mosquitoes carry malaria is true because malaria transmission is a normal function or capacity of the kind, even though only a small fraction of ducks or mosquitoes ever realize the property. I want to avoid having her simply restate the view. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press this account where it is hardest, with at least one follow-up question for each main question, keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Include at least one question each on how the account distinguishes normal capacities from merely possible ones; how it handles generic truths that seem to track accidents rather than any biological function; and whether social or normative facts can make a generic true without an underlying disposition of the kind. End with a question about what evidence, if any, could show that a generic is true in virtue of something other than a kind-level disposition.
