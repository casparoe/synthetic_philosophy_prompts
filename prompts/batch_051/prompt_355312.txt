I am an undergraduate taking a philosophy course on free will and moral responsibility, and I also do some programming, which is why the following parallel occurred to me while reading Galen Strawson's Basic Argument.

As I understand the Basic Argument: you act as you do because of the way you are. To be truly responsible for the act, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are. But you could be responsible for the way you are only by having brought it about, and you could have brought it about only by acting from some earlier way of being—earlier motives, traits, or principles—which would itself have had to be self-chosen in the same way. The series must either regress infinitely or bottom out in features of yourself you never chose. Strawson concludes that neither option leaves anyone ultimately morally responsible.

The computer security case I have in mind is Ken Thompson's Turing Award lecture, "Reflections on Trusting Trust." The scenario, as I remember it, is that a compiler binary can be modified so that it inserts a backdoor into every program it compiles, and also inserts the same self-propagating modification into any compiler it compiles, while the inspected source code remains free of any trace. So even perfect inspection of the source you possess cannot assure you that the compiler you are using is trustworthy. To certify it, you would need to certify the compiler that built it, then the one that built that compiler, and so on. At the beginning of the chain sits some tool whose history can no longer be independently checked. Complete verification is impossible; in practice, trust in a toolchain is anchored somewhere unverified.

The parallel I want to test is this: a present action expresses a present character in something like the way a present compiler is the product of an earlier compiler. Responsibility would require tracing authorship back through an unbroken chain of self-creations; trustworthiness would require tracing integrity back through an unbroken chain of compilations. In both cases each link can only transmit a status it received from the preceding link, and no link can confer that status from nothing. That makes both look like failures of a self-grounding regress—the impossibility of an uncaused first cause of one's character, and the impossibility of a self-certifying first compiler.

But I suspect the parallel may be only skin deep. Strawson's argument is metaphysical, about ultimate control and desert, while Thompson's is epistemic, about what you can know and trust. And the practical upshots differ: Thompson's point is not that compilers are unusable, just that total assurance is unavailable, so we fall back on weaker practical checks and accept residual risk. It is less obvious that Strawson leaves room for an analogous weaker notion of responsibility. I would like to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure or an enticing false analogy.

More specifically: (1) spell out the mapping—what corresponds on each side to "the way you are," "being responsible for it," the first self-creation, and the uncreated starting point? (2) Say what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer from one domain to the other. (3) Does the compiler case suggest that a compatibilist could accept a weaker, practical notion of responsibility that does not require ultimate self-creation, or would that import an answer from the wrong kind of problem? (4) Identify at least one concrete prediction the analogy makes that is false, and use it to identify exactly where the mapping breaks.
