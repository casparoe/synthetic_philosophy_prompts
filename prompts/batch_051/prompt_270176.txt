Reread Bill Thurston's "On proof and progress in mathematics" (1994) last week, because I keep ending up on a curriculum committee that says our proof-based courses are meant to teach "understanding" rather than mere procedural fluency, and this passage has been bugging me:

"When Appel and Haken completed a proof of the 4-color map theorem using a massive automatic computation, it evoked much controversy. I interpret the controversy as having little to do with doubt people had as to the veracity of the theorem or the correctness of the proof. Rather, it reflected a continuing desire for human understanding of a proof, in addition to knowledge that the theorem is true."

Thurston seems to be assuming there's a real difference between two things that, in practice, we mush together when we grade: knowing that a proof is correct (you could check each line) and understanding it (seeing why the argument works, why these moves rather than others, what would break if you tweaked a hypothesis). But I honestly can't tell wether that's a substantive distinction or just a flattering word for "can reproduce the proof more robustly and on harder variations."

So here's what I want to work out. Design the best concrete test you can of the claim that understanding a proof is something over and above fluent, accurate reproduction of it. Pick your setting (say, an undergraduate real analysis or abstract algebra course) and specify actual tasks or exam questions, not just principles. Say what pattern of results would push toward the view that understanding is a distinct thing, and what pattern would make you conclude we've just been mistaking fluency for understanding. Then be honest about what even a well-designed test of this cannot settle, and whether that leftover residue actually matters for teaching or is just philosophers' leftovers.
