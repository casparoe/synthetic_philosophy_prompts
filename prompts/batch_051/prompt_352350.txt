Lately I've been reading about the Whig interpretation of history, and it keeps striking me that it has the same shape as what psychologists call hindsight bias. As I understand it, Whig history writes the past as a series of steps leading up to us: earlier thinkers get assessed mainly for how far they anticipated modern views, and the present ends up looking like the point the whole story was heading toward. Hindsight bias is the tendency, once you know the outcome, to think it was more predictable than it actually was and to reread the prior evidence as having pointed to it all along. Both seem to let the present contaminate the past, making whatever happened look necessary in retrospect.

I can't decide whether this is a real structural isomorphism or just loose pattern-matching on the word "retrospect." If it is real, does the psychology of hindsight bias help explain why Whiggish histories are so seductive, or could it even debunk them? Or do the two come apart because hindsight bias is a spontaneous cognitive error about probability judgments, while Whig history is a deliberate historiographical stance that someone might defend on separate grounds? I'm trying to figure out where the mapping holds, where it breaks, and whether anything genuinely transfers from one side to the other. If you think the mapping is only partial, it would help if you could point to something the parallel predicts that turns out false, so I can see exactly where it gives out.
