I'm a high-school student, and as part of a peer-grading exercise in a moral philosophy unit on gratitude, I need to grade a classmate's answer to the following exam question.

Exam question: "Explain what gratitude responds to and whether it can be owed. In your answer, distinguish (a) being benefited by something from being the object of a benefactor's good will, and (b) its being fitting to feel gratitude from its being owed to a benefactor. Use at least two of the following three cases: (i) a benefit conferred without any intention to benefit you; (ii) someone who intends your good but fails to bring about any benefit; (iii) a benefactor who is dead, so that no return can be made."

Student's answer: "Gratitude is not the same as being pleased that things went well. It is directed at a benefactor, and specifically at the good will the benefactor shows toward us. That is why we are not grateful to the weather, or to a vending machine that returns our coins, even when they benefit us, and why a benefit conferred accidentally does not by itself call for gratitude: a stranger who unknowingly drops a twenty-dollar bill that I find has not acted toward me at all, so I should be glad about the windfall but not grateful to the stranger. Nor does gratitude require that I requested the help. A friend who helps me out of the blue still aims at my good, so she can be a proper object of gratitude. What matters, then, is the benefactor's good will rather than the size of the benefit. This means the measure of gratitude is how much the benefactor sacrificed, since sacrifice is the clearest sign of good will; two people who try equally hard therefore deserve equal gratitude even if only one succeeds. For the same reason, I owe gratitude to a rescuer who dives in to save me even if I drown anyway, and to a benefactor now dead whom I can never repay — the intention, not the outcome or the possibility of return, is what does all the work."

Please grade the answer out of 10 and give the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should state the numeric grade and the main reason for it. Items 2 through 5 should each isolate a specific claim or move in the answer and explain precisely why it is correct or mistaken.
