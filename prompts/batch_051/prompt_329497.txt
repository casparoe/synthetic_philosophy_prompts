Consider Thomson's lamp. At t = 0 the lamp is on; at t = 1/2 it is switched off; at t = 3/4 on; at t = 7/8 off; and in general the switch is toggled at t = 1 − 2^(−n) for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., so that infinitely many switchings are completed by t = 1. Thomson held that the lamp's state at t = 1 has no determinate value, and took this to show something incoherent about completing a supertask; Benacerraf replied that the original description simply does not specify the state at t = 1, so there is no contradiction.

Now vary the case. Replace the ideal lamp with an inertial lamp whose brightness B(t) is a continuous quantity between 0 and 1 and cannot jump. When the switch commands a target value v (1 for "on", 0 for "off"), the gap between B and v decays exponentially with time constant τ: over any interval of length Δt during which the commanded value stays fixed at v, the gap at the end is e^(−Δt/τ) times the gap at the start. The switch still receives the Thomson sequence of commands — "on" at t = 0, then "off", "on", "off", ... at t = 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, ... — and initially B(0) = 1. Work through what this setup implies as t approaches 1. Is B(1) determinate for a fixed positive τ, and on what does it depend? Then consider the limit τ → 0: does the inertial lamp smoothly recover Thomson's ideal lamp, or is the original case a singular limit? Finally, say what this does to the two standard positions: which parts of Thomson's and Benacerraf's analyses survive the variation, and does the inertial lamp dissolve the original puzzle, expose an idealization as doing the work, or change the subject?
