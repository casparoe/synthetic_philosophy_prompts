Leibniz held the principle of the identity of indiscernibles: two distinct things cannot share every property; apparent examples of exact similarity, such as two leaves or two drops of water, merely show that our observation has not gone far enough. He also held the principle of sufficient reason, and he used it in this area to argue that a world could not contain two perfectly similar things, because there would then be no sufficient reason for whatever asymmetry distinguished one from the other.

Since Leibniz's death, two developments have been taken to test this position. The first is Max Black's two-sphere universe: an otherwise empty space containing exactly two iron spheres, alike in size, material, temperature, and every other intrinsic property, fixed at a distance from one another in a perfectly symmetric configuration. The description appears coherent, yet the two spheres seem to share all their properties: each has the same mass, shape, and temperature, and each appears to stand in the same relational properties, such as being two miles from an iron sphere of the relevant kind. If such a world is genuinely possible, the identity of indiscernibles fails as a necessary truth rather than merely as a claim about the actual world. The second development is the quantum-mechanical treatment of elementary particles. In the mathematical description of a system containing two electrons, or two photons, of the same type, permuting the labels of the particles leaves the physical state unchanged. Quantum theory is therefore widely read as implying that such particles have no primitive individual identity, that there is no fact about which electron is which, in contrast to the classical individuals Leibniz seems to have had in mind.

Suppose Leibniz could be brought to understand both developments. Predict, as carefully and charitably as you can, how he would respond to each. Do not supply citations or quotations; reconstruct the reasoning from the principles described above. Do not settle for the flat answer that he would simply deny that Black's universe could exist. Instead, work through the available replies and their costs.

(a) In the case of Black's two-sphere universe, identify the strongest grounds Leibniz could give for rejecting the description as impossible, such as a violation of the principle of sufficient reason or a conflict with his relational view of space, and explain why Black's symmetric setup may be awkward for each such ground.

(b) Consider whether Leibniz could individuate the two spheres relationally rather than intrinsically. How far can such relational individuation go before it gives away the principle Leibniz actually wanted to defend?

(c) In the quantum case, decide whether Leibniz would treat electrons and photons as substances in his sense at all. If not, does the identity of indiscernibles even apply, and is this a genuine accommodation of the case or a retreat from it?

(d) Determine what quantum indistinguishable particles would pressure Leibniz to revise: the principle of the identity of indiscernibles itself, his notion of substance, his principle of sufficient reason, or something else. Distinguish the modifications he could accept from the modifications that would amount to abandoning his system.

Conclude by identifying the smallest change to Leibniz's metaphysics that would let him accommodate both Black's universe and quantum statistics while preserving as much as possible of his original commitments, and say whether you think Leibniz himself would have been willing to make that change.
