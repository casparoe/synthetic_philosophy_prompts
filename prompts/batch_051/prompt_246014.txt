I keep tripping over the phrase "moral testimony." In ordinary life, if someone tells you the train is late and you believe them, that's testimony, no sweat. But in the debate about whether you should ever form a moral belief by taking someone else's word for it, I can't tell where the edges of the concept are, and I think the first order of business is a sharp definition. So here's what I want: a working definition of accepting moral testimony — forming or revising a moral view on another person's say-so, rather than through your own reasoning or firsthand experience. I want the definition to classify these cases correctly, and I'll give my own hunches as I go:

(a) A five-year-old comes to believe hitting is wrong because her parents told her so. (I'd call this moral testimony, though maybe you'll say it's just moral education and 'testimony' is the wrong word.)

(b) You read an essay laying out a careful drowning-child argument that you should give much more to famine relief than you do, and it convinces you. (Not testimony, right? You were moved by the argument, not the author's say-so — though trusting the author's competence might be doing sneaky work in the background.)

(c) A friend tells you a joke you made was racist, and you accept that it was wrong. (Feels like testimony, but it's hard to tell whether you're taking their word about what the joke communicated, or about whether it was wrong.)

(d) A grim, detailed book about factory farming makes you go vegetarian. (I want to say this isn't moral testimony: it handed you facts and you did the moral work yourself. But the book's descriptions are still someone's say-so about facts, so where exactly is the line?)

(e) A moral philosopher you trust tells you utilitarianism is true, and you accept it even though you can't follow the formal argument behind it. (To me this is the clear yes case.)

(f) A DNA analyst testifies that the accused's blood was at the scene, and that factual claim is what tips you from 'unsure' to 'guilty.' (You're accepting testimony, but it's not moral testimony; the verdict is still yours. I'm fairly confident about this one.)

(g) You go along with your friend group's view on a controversial policy because you don't want to make things awkward. (Does that even count as forming a moral view at all? If you end up actually believing it, is that testimony or just... pressure?)

(h) A friend who belongs to a marginalized group tells you a certain practice is demeaning, and you accept that it is. (I genuinely can't classify this one. It might be testimony about what it's like to undergo the practice, or a moral claim you're deferring to, or both at once.)

Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it gives a suprising verdict or no verdict. Then either revise the definition or dig in and defend the surprising calls, and say which of your classifications you're least secure about.
