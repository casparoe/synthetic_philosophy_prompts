I've been thinking about this since a friend got fined for something in a city code no one in his neighborhood knew existed. I've been calling my idea "normative distance," and I want someone to check whether it's genuinely doing work or just a metaphor in a legal-theory hat. The basic claim: every legal rule has an epistemic footprint, meaning how easy it is for a person trying to obey to actually know it. A rule printed on a form you sign has low normative distance; a rule buried in a Federal Register entry cross-referencing three other subsections, written in dense language and aimed at a community that doesn't read that language, has huge normative distance, even though both are technically public. My test has two prongs: (1) could a reasonable person, expending a reasonable amount of effort, locate and understand the rule before acting? and (2) did the state's own institutional choices create the distance, for example by refusing to consolidate scattered provisions, not issuing guidance, or not translating materials for populations it knows are affected? On my view, enforcing a rule at high normative distance isn't just unfortunate; it's a distinct procedural harm, not a defense to liability but a wrong that ought to be recognized and perhaps mitigated or compensated.

I know Blackstone has some famous-ish thing about every person being presumed to know the law, and my notion is clearly pushing against that as a fiction. But I don't know whether I've reinvented something from fair notice or void-for-vagueness doctrine, or perhaps something in European proportionality reasoning about accessible law. So first: is this already a doctrine under another name, and what does the existing version say that mine doesn't? Second, where does my concept break? Maybe every complex society now has such high average normative distance that the concept can't distinguish genuinely unfair cases from the baseline. Maybe it gets gamed by people who deliberately stay ignorant and then claim distance. Maybe I'm assuming "the law" is one knowable thing when it's really a patchwork of overlapping normative systems, so the whole distance metaphor distorts the problem. Give me a candid assessment: is this a real concept or just a vivid way of complaining, and what would a strong critic say is its weakest joint?
