I'm a software engineer with no formal background in history or philosophy, and I keep turning over a parallel I can't decide is deep or shallow. In the incident reviews I run for our services, we separate the trigger — say, a release that pushed a subsystem past its limit — from the enabling conditions: the missing backpressure, the stale runbook, the incentives that let the risky change through. We also treat "who broke it?" as the wrong question in practice, because localizing blame usually ends the inquiry before the systemic fix is found. The Cold War origins debate looks to me as if it made the same move. It starts from "who started it?" — the Soviet Union's imposition of control over Eastern Europe, or American atomic diplomacy and economic pressure — and then shifts to structural accounts built from the security dilemma, the logic of spheres of influence, and path dependence, where no single actor starts the conflict the way a bad commit starts an outage. I can map the pieces: trigger to precipitating event, enabling conditions to long-term structural causes, blameless postmortems to structural history. What I want to know is whether there is anything more than a suggestive analogy here. Is this a genuine identity of structure — two cases of causal attribution in tightly coupled systems with feedback — or does it break because historical actors deliberate, deceive, and revise their aims, while a service never does? What would have to be true on both sides for the lesson "fix the system, not the blame" to transfer to historical explanation? And what is something the analogy predicts that is probably false?
