Two people in my metaphysics seminar got into an argument after a session on dispositions, and I cannot settle it from my notes. One defended what the instructor called the simple conditional analysis: for a glass to be fragile just is for it to be such that, were it dropped onto a hard floor, it would break. The other objected that a glass packed in foam would not break if dropped, yet remains fragile. The first replied that the analysis implicitly excludes masks—factors that prevent a disposition from manifesting—and that a foam-wrapped glass is not a clean test. The second countered that "absence of masks" then does the real work and amounts to "nothing that would prevent the break," which hands the analysis the very disposition it was supposed to explain. The first denied this: masks, he said, can be identified independently as external factors that interrupt the causal chain between the object's intrinsic state and the manifestation, without mentioning fragility itself.

The second then offered a finkish case: a novelty cup is brittle at rest, but an impact-activated compound hardens the interior so rapidly that any blow that would crack it instead stiffens it, and it would not shatter if struck. Here, she argued, nothing external masks the disposition; the cup merely fails to break in the counterfactual circumstances, yet it is brittle before impact. The first said this shows the conditional must be tied to the object's condition at the time of ascription: the cup is brittle at t because, holding fixed its microstructure at t, a blow delivered then would break it; in the fink world the blow changes the very state being evaluated. The second replied that "holding fixed the microstructure" is not neutral either, since the only reason to screen off the hardening is to preserve the disposition one set out to analyze, and that the conditional is being demoted from an analysis to an unreliable symptom. Neither moved, and each thinks the other has confused an analysis with a heuristic. Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point: who is right about the masking objection and what exactly it refutes, whether the circularity charge is fair, what the fink case shows beyond the mask case, whether the tie-to-time proposal works, and what, if anything, their exchange misses.
