I'm a student in an upper-level seminar on vagueness, and we've spent the past few weeks on the dispute between epistemicists and supervaluationists over the sorites. Before I write my paper, I want to fix the standards of evaluation explicitly, because I keep catching myself and others judging the two theories by whichever phenomenon is salient at the moment: one week the felt absence of sharp cutoffs, the next week the desire to keep classical logic, the week after that the problem of higher-order vagueness. I'd rather get the full set of requirements on the table first.

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of language and vagueness. Think carefully and work step by step; start by brainstorming the phenomena a theory of vagueness should handle, then refine them into an organized list of desiderata.

My request is a specification, not a survey or a history. I want the full list of requirements that an adequate theory of vagueness must meet, where by "theory of vagueness" I mean an account of what vagueness consists in (semantic, epistemic, ontic, or otherwise) together with the logic and semantics it assigns to vague language. For each requirement, give: (a) the requirement stated precisely enough to test a candidate theory; (b) the case or consideration that motivates it — borderline cases, the sorites series, forced-march cases, penumbral connections between related vague terms, higher-order vagueness, and so on — described concretely enough that I can see what the requirement is doing; and (c) whether the requirement is non-negotiable, in the sense that any adequate theory must satisfy it, or a negotiable preference associated with a particular camp (for example, preserving bivalence, preserving classical logic, avoiding ontic vagueness, avoiding brute unknowable boundaries, or compositionality). Then say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates, and why.

I especially want your judgment on two further points. First, are any subsets of these desiderata jointly unsatisfiable or in serious tension? For instance, does the demand to explain why every sorites premise seemed true conflict with a theory's strategy for making some premise false, and does accommodating higher-order vagueness put pressure on any of the natural first-order requirements? Second, should the claim that a vague term has no sharp boundary be treated as a datum that a theory must preserve, or as a pretheoretical intuition that an adequate theory may explain away — and what turns on this choice?

Don't give me a history of the debate, a bibliography, or reading suggestions. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular views, though brief notes on which requirements are hardest for epistemicism or supervaluationism are welcome. A table at the end would be useful, but the reasoning belongs in the prose.
