I've been calling a thing 'privacy as inoculation' and I want someone to poke holes in it. The idea is: when an ordinary person hides from the state — pays cash, uses encryption, refuses a loyalty card, that kind of thing — theyre not really protecting their own secrets, because most of our own secrets are pretty boring. What theyre doing is keeping the states reflexes weak. Every hidden thing is a little friction, and friction is what stops a goverment from getting comfortable with seeing everything. So the person hiding is doing something for everyone else, like a vaccine: most of the benefit goes to the people who actually need to hide, while the person doing the hiding pays the whole cost. I think this explains the German reflex. They had the Stasi and now treat data protection as a civic duty even when you've got nothing to hide. Americans treat it as a personal risk calculation — if I'm not doing anything wrong, why care — and the Chinese social credit system treats being visible as the reward, so hiding itself starts to look guilty. Is this a real idea or just a stretched metaphor? What's the strongest argument that it's wrong, and exactly where does it fall apart?
