I'm a retired atmospheric scientist—cloud microphysics and boundary layers, mostly—and I've spent the last couple of years reading my way through moral philosophy, which is harder to instrument than anything I used to put in a wind tunnel. I have a friend, Mara, who isn't a philosopher but has surprisingly firm views about when an individual's contribution to a collective problem actually matters. We argue constantly, and I've gotten into the habit of recording her verdicts like data points.

Here's what I have on her so far. She says you should still vote in a national election, even though one ballot won't decide it. She says there's no obligation to give up meat for animal welfare, because no cow gets saved or spared by your one burger and the supply chain won't notice; I've tried expected-value talk on her and she waves it off. She says you should boycott a clothing brand that uses badly exploited labor, even though your own purchases won't change company policy, because she doesnt want to be the person handing them money. She says you should buy a reputable carbon offset when you fly—some measurable ton of abatement gets paid for—but she does not think you're obligated to take one less cross-country trip to see family. She says you should join a strike at your workplace, because crossing the line would be undercutting the collective. And she says you don't need to show up to a mass climate march, because one more body in the crowd wont change city hall's mind.

Next I'm going to ask her about a new case, and I'd like your best prediction before I do. The case is whether she should cancel a meal-delivery subscription she uses for convenience, because the drivers are underpaid and misclassified as contractors, even though one canceled subscription won't change the company's labor model. Her track record feels like it underdetermines this—maybe she's running a complicity principle, maybe an efficacy principle, maybe a fair-share principle about formal collective practices, maybe two of the three with different scopes. I'd like you to lay out the candidate rules that fit all her verdicts, say what each one predicts about the delivery subscription, give your best guess about her verdict and why, identify which earlier case is the closest analog, and propose one further case whose verdict would best discriminate between whatever rules remain live. Then end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" giving your predicted verdict and a short phrase for the principle behind it.
