I've landed, tentatively, on computationalism: the mind just is the brain running the right computations, so beliefs and desires are computational states of the right kind rather than just a useful vocabulary. Now I want to know what it would take to knock me off it. Not the tired stuff about computers not being conscious — I mean the structural exposure of the view. The pancomputationalism worry bothers me most: if someone can gerrymander a mapping that makes a wall or a puddle implement the same formal computation the brain allegedly runs, then "the brain computes" stops doing any distinguishing work. What would have to be true about implementation constraints, or about what counts as running a computation, for that objection to be fatal rather than a bookkeeping annoyance? And what empirical results should actually move me — say, if the relevant neural dynamics turned out to be analog and chaotic enough that no finite computational model could track them, or if we found cortical information processing that isn't algorithmic at any level of description? Rank the defeaters from "this ends the view" to "defenders will shrug this off," say which ones a sophisticated computationalist would themselves concede are live risks, and tell me when I've insulated the position into unfalsifiability and whether that's even a problem here.
