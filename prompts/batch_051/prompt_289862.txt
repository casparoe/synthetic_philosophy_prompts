In our company, every production incident is closed with a postmortem whose template has one non-negotiable field: Root Cause, singular. A report that names several interacting causes is sent back as incomplete. Yet when the same engineers reflect on a launch that went well, they are happy to attribute the outcome to a long conjunction of causes — the right hire, the feature-flag strategy, the order in which a database migration landed, load tests timed well — and no one treats the absence of a single root cause as a defect. We would find it almost absurd to demand one for a success.

The asymmetry is therefore this: when the outcome was good, we treat the same sort of evidence — logs, dashboards, deploy history — as supporting a broad conjunction of causes, but when the outcome was bad, we require that it be reduced to one. We explain failures by preferring the simplest story that converges on a single dominant factor, and we explain successes by accumulating many heterogeneous factors, and we regard both habits as sound engineering. I want the strongest justification the asymmetry could have. Is there a defensible account of inference to the best explanation on which explanatory simplicity — or some other explanatory virtue — ought to matter more when the outcome is bad than when it is good? Or is the root-cause field doing non-epistemic work, such as assigning accountability, making the incident feel closed, or giving a manager a single named thing to fix, that we have dressed up as good explanatory practice?

Build the best case you can for the asymmetry, test it against nearby cases — a near miss that we investigate as though it had failed, a partial outage, a launch that succeeded for the same reasons an earlier launch failed — and then say honestly whether the practice should be kept, inverted, or abandoned, and what the postmortem template should look like under the account of explanation you settle on.
