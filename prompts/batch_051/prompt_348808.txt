I'm a novelist, and next month I'm giving a talk at an interdisiplinary workshop on explanation in history. My working title is "The ending writes the beginning: why narrative history can't shake hindsight bias, and why that's not a scandal." It started as a problem I hit while revising my current novel (about a failed weavers' strike): once I knew the ending, earlier scenes kept turning into foreshadowing, and choices that had felt wide open while I was drafting now read as inevitable. The more I thought about it, the more I suspected it isn't a flaw in my craft — it's a feature of how narrative does explanatory work. My argument, roughly, is: (1) a narrative explains by selecting and sequencing events so that later ones confer significance on earlier ones; (2) that selection and sequencing imposes a kind of retrospective necessity on what was, at the time, genuinely contingent; (3) so what psychologists call hindsight bias isn't an extra distortion smeared over neutral facts — it's the engine of narrative intelligibility, the very thing that makes a past understandable; and (4) the real fault line isn't "biased versus unbiased" narrative history but responsible versus irresponsible uses of retrospection. In my notes I've taken to calling this "the foreshadowing problem," but I mean it structurally, not aesthetically.

The audience is what's making me queasy. A good half of the room will be economic and social historians — the sort who think explanation means comparing cases, coding records, and testing hypotheses, and who'll hear my thesis as "storytelling's fine, facts don't matter." There are also two or three philosophers of history who'll bristle if I sound like I'm calling narrative a mere bias; they'll want the point made in their idiom — narrative sentences (the kind only true in retrospect), retrospective significance, the difference between a chronicle and a story. And there's usually a cognitive psychologist who studies hindsight bias and will insist it's an individual, measurable distortion that debiasing instructions can partly correct, so calling it the "engine of narrative intelligibility" will sound like mystification to them.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the talk. Please think as a professional philosopher of history who is comfortable with both the historiography and the psychological literature. First brainstorm the objections each group would actually raise; then give them to me ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each group would put it — the economic historian's objection shouldn't sound like the philosopher's or the psychologist's. For each one, tell me what to concede, what to hold, and where my reply is softest. Then tell me which premise of mine is the weakest and should be defended rather than dodged, and what the one question is that I should be praying nobody asks — plus how I'd begin to answer it if somebody does.
