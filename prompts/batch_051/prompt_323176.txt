I am designing a unit on lexical semantics for a philosophy of language course, and I want to begin not with the usual survey of positions but with the standards of adequacy themselves. The guiding question is whether it is still right to talk about words having meanings once polysemy and semantic range are taken seriously: healthy behaves differently in healthy diet, healthy patient, and healthy economy; run spans running a race, running a committee, running water, a run in a stocking, and a program running on a machine; and copredication cases like the lunch was delicious but took two hours seem to force one word to carry an object sense and an event sense at once. Before I read any particular theory — core meaning plus pragmatic modulation, radial category views, usage-based or distributional accounts, or list-the-senses approaches — I want a specification of what any adequate theory of word meaning must deliver.

Please write the desiderata list: the phenomena a theory of word meaning must explain or explain away, the distinctions it must respect (for example, homonymy versus polysemy, stored versus generated senses, and semantic versus pragmatic determinants of interpretation), the cases it must get right, and the constraints from acquisition and cross-linguistic regularity it cannot contradict. For each requirement, give the concrete case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a preference, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely the tastes of particular camps. Where a pair of requirements cannot easily be satisfied at once — I suspect the productive generation of new senses and the existence of a stable stored core pull against each other — say so explicitly. Then identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and briefly indicate how the main families of views fare against it.

Finally, I want the list itself to be treated as revisable. For every requirement whose status is genuinely disputable, tell me what kind of evidence or argument — a new class of examples, acquisition data, cross-linguistic findings, or a persuasive account of pragmatics — would make you demote or abandon it, and say where in the list you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
