An introductory metaphysics textbook I'm reading summarizes the endurantism–perdurantism debate roughly as follows. Endurantists hold that a persisting object is wholly present at each moment at which it exists: the entire table is here at noon, and the very same entire table is here at noon tomorrow. Perdurantists deny this, saying that objects extend through time the way they extend through space; at any given moment only a temporal part of an object is present, and the persisting object is the whole four-dimensional "spacetime worm" composed of these parts. The book adds that, on the perdurantist's block-universe picture, all of a person's temporal parts—past, present, and future—exist equally, with the future parts already there awaiting us, and that endurantists reject all of this because it would make change impossible: if a candle is bent at one time and straight at another, the very same object would have to be both bent and straight. Something about this strikes me as running distinct debates together, and I am unsure the impossibility-of-change conclusion follows. Is this a fair summary of the two positions? Please separate the accurate core from the simplifications, identify where separate debates have been fused, and say whether the impossibility-of-change conclusion follows from what perdurantists actually claim.
