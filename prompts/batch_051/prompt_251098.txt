Here's where I keep getting stuck. I'm attracted to a pretty clean view about abandoned stuff: property rights exist to protect an owner's ability to use and control their things, and once there's no owner left and no identifiable successor, the moral basis for excluding everyone else lapses. On that view orphaned novels and abandonware aren't really anyone's property, so copying them, archiving them, even selling them can't wrong anyone—there's literally no one left to wrong. I like how simple it is, how it gives a definite answer in every case (is there a findable owner with interests? no? then it's free), and how it dissolves the whole orphan-works problem in one move.

But I also have an intuition I can't shake. Picture a programmer who spends a decade on a weird little simulation game, dies, no heirs, and then a company scrapes it, slaps its own logo on it, and sells it. Or someone finds a dead author's unpublished, never-meant-to-be-published novel and puts it out for profit. That feels like grave-robbing even though, on my own view, there's no victim. I don't just mean its tacky or bad for the culture; it feels like the kind of wrong involved in taking something that isn't yours.

So I'm torn between a theory that says 'no owner, no claim' and an intuition that says the work is still in some sense theirs, or at least not mine to take. Walk me through what each move costs. Is the intuition tracking something real—some interest that survives the person, or a wrong done to someone else, like the public or the work itself—or is it a misfiring of the machinery we use for dealing with living owners? And is there a reading of 'theirs' or 'unowned' on which the clean view and the intuition can both survive, or do I have to give one up?
