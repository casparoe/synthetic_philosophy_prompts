I am taking a graduate seminar on divine foreknowledge and human freedom, and after several sessions on Molinism I find myself more worried about a neglected issue than about the usual objections. The standard Molinist picture says that God, prior to his creative decree, knows the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — true subjunctive conditionals specifying how each possible free creature would choose in any complete set of circumstances. But the picture seems to presuppose that each such conditional already has a determinate subject: a particular possible person whose choices are recorded in the conditional. Before the creative decree, however, it is unclear what fixes that subject as this possible person rather than some merely qualitatively identical alternative. If the subject is fixed by an individual essence, and if that essence includes the person's free choices, then the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom turn out to be part of the individuating conditions for the very agents whose freedom they are supposed to describe, which looks circular. If the subject is not fixed at all, then the Molinist claim that God knows a definite set of truths about particular possible people becomes indefinite.

Please argue that this subject-fixing problem is an independent difficulty for Molinism and not merely a restatement of the grounding objection. The argument should establish that the Molinist faces the following dilemma: either the subjects of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are individuated in a way that builds their free choices into their essences, or middle knowledge does not determine unique truths about particular possible persons. In developing the argument, clarify exactly how this differs from the complaint that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom lack truthmakers, and identify which Molinist commitment generates the problem. Also state the most promising Molinist reply you can construct, and defend your judgment about why it ultimately fails.
