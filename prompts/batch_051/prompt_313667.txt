I'm a journalist who covers ideas at the intersection of physics and philosophy, and I'm working on a feature about the butterfly effect and the limits of prediction. In the course of reporting I keep encountering a tidy argument about what chaotic dynamics implies for determinism, and I need to know whether it is sound, and if not, exactly where it comes apart. Here it is as I have reconstructed it:

(1) A system is deterministic just in case its state at any later time is fully fixed by its state at an earlier time together with its laws of evolution.
(2) In a chaotic system — think of orbits on a strange attractor in a simplified weather model — two states that begin arbitrarily close together can be driven apart by the dynamics, so that a difference below the resolution of any given instrument now becomes a difference too large to miss later.
(3) So, for any actual measuring instrument, however precise, there are two possible initial states that the instrument cannot tell apart now but that lead to observably different future states.
(4) If an instrument cannot tell two possible initial states apart, then the two corresponding futures are both compatible with the evidence supplied by that instrument, so neither future is ruled out by that evidence.
(5) Since this is true of every possible measuring instrument, there is no possible body of evidence that could rule out some alternative future for the system.
(6) Therefore, in a chaotic deterministic system, the actual future is not settled by the present state: the butterfly effect shows that the future is genuinely open, not merely unknown.

I half-suspect the trouble lies somewhere in the move from (4) to (5) or from (5) to (6) — some slide from what the evidence can rule out to what is the case — but I could not say precisely which step carries the mistake, or whether the argument can be repaired.
