I recently started working on the trust-and-safety team of a mid-sized social platform, and I have been assigned to a project on regional compliance. Our platform keeps a single global database of posts, but when a piece of content is reported, the outcome depends on where the person viewing it is located. A post that is lawful in the United States but unlawful in, say, Germany or Thailand—certain forms of hate speech, defamation of state officials, or prohibited political symbols—is not deleted from the platform. Instead, visitors in the restricted country see a notice saying the content is unavailable in their region, while visitors elsewhere see it unchanged. My colleagues treat this as obviously right: the platform respects local law without imposing one country's standards on everyone. But I cannot get the underlying logic to sit still.

What view of jurisdiction over speech does this geo-blocking practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that the law governing an act of online expression is the law of the place where it is read, rather than the law of the place where the speaker is located, the platform is incorporated, or the servers sit? Does it commit the platform to a particular picture of what people are doing when they speak online—namely, that every view is a distinct public act in a distinct territory rather than one act sent into an effectively borderless medium? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can.

Then evaluate it. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with. Does geo-blocking actually require a reader-centered theory of jurisdiction, or is it compatible with several different theories, including a purely pragmatic theory according to which the platform simply manages legal risk in each market without taking any view about where speech rights ultimately come from? Is the practice internally consistent, given that the same content remains reachable across borders through a virtual private network, and given that the speaker never intended to enter the restricted jurisdiction? And if we replaced the implicit view with a rival one—say, that online speech is governed by one global standard, or by the law of the speaker's country, or by the law of the jurisdiction most affected by the speech—what would the platform have to do differently in concrete cases, and what would become harder to justify about the current practice?
