I have been developing something I call verification rent and would like an honest assessment of it. The term names what I suspect is a hidden transaction inside effective altruism. Donors cannot directly observe the counterfactual consequences of a gift; they can observe only trials, models, audits, and reports. An intervention that yields a clean, quantifiable result is therefore not merely easier to evaluate; it is also supplying something the donor needs, evidence that is cheap to trust. I want to say that the portion of a donation that is paid for the legibility of the outcome rather than for the outcome itself is the verification rent. My central claim is that a substantial part of what critics call measurability bias is not an irrational failure to value institutional or systemic change, but a rational fee donors pay so that the act of giving can be made legible to themselves and others. For example, a trial showing that an intervention reduces infections by a tidy percentage will tend to outcompete a legal-reform campaign whose benefits are real but slow, diffuse, and hard to attribute; I read that outcome not as pure evidence that the infection intervention is better, but partly as the price of verifiability. I know the idea resembles Goodhart's law and principal-agent problems, but I think it makes a stronger claim: even a donor who fully understands the value of hard-to-measure institutional work will rationally underfund it. Is this a genuine distinction or only a suggestive metaphor? Has it already been worked out under another name? Where does it break? I would especially like to know the strongest case in which a donor could avoid paying verification rent altogether, and the weakest case in which my theory would miscount an ordinary, justified funding decision as a rent payment. Please also say which argument or consideration would change your assessment, and where my reasoning is most likely to fail.
