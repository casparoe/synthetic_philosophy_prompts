For a paper I am drafting on how well-being combines into overall value, I have written out the set of commitments I want to build on. Before I construct the argument, I would like a consistency audit of this set — not a survey of the literature and not a defense of any rival view, but an analysis of whether these commitments jointly hold together.

Here are the commitments:

1. I reject totalism. By totalism I mean the view that the value of a world is simply the sum of the momentary well-being present in every moment of every life it contains, with no further transformation. I reject it because I take it to imply the repugnant conclusion, which I find unacceptable: I deny that any loss in average quality can be made up for by adding a sufficiently large number of merely decent lives.

2. I hold a multiplicative view of momentary value. At each moment of a life, the value contributed is the product of the subject's level of consciousness, on a scale from 0 to 1, and the hedonic quality of that moment, its valence, on a scale from -10 to 10. Unconscious moments contribute zero regardless of valence, and moments with zero valence contribute zero regardless of consciousness.

3. I aggregate additively. The value of a life is the sum over its moments of their momentary values, and the value of a world is the sum over the lives it contains of their life-values. I do not treat lives as incomparable in a way that would force pairwise comparisons; I accept the additive move across subjects.

4. I hold a lexical threshold for extreme suffering. There is a level of acute suffering such that a world containing a life lived at that level in every moment is worse than any world containing no such life, no matter how much positive value exists elsewhere in the world.

5. I hold a cross-species commensurability thesis. Consciousness and valence are measured on the same scales in human and nonhuman animals: an hour of suffering at a given intensity in a pig counts exactly as much as an hour of suffering at the same intensity in a human, with no species-specific discount.

I would like you to do three things. First, go through the set and identify which pairs or larger subsets are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be in tension; for the merely apparent tensions, say briefly why they dissolve. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity. Third, for each genuine tension, state which commitment or commitments would have to give and what the least costly revision would be.
