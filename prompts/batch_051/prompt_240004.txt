I have been having a running disagreement with my sister, who is a therapist, about whether reasons can exist before anyone has hold of them. The question stopped being abstract for me after our father died last year: I kept wanting to say things like "there was a reason for him to ask the doctor about the shortness of breath, even though the thought never crossed his mind," and she kept insisting that this was loose talk. On her view a reason is always somebody's reason — a consideration that actually has weight for some person — and an ungrasped reason is at most a comment about what someone would have had reason to do if he had noticed.

I have tried to put her position in its strongest form as the numbered argument below. I half-suspect the argument is too quick, especially around step (8), but I cannot say exactly where it slips or how to fix it. Could you tell me whether the argument goes through — that is, whether the conclusion follows — and if not, exactly which step fails and how the step should be repaired? Please also say which premises, if any, a defender of unowned reasons should reject, and how they should reject them.

(1) A reason is a consideration that counts in favor of an action or an attitude.

(2) Favoring is a relation: if a consideration counts in favor, then there is something it counts in favor of.

(3) What a reason favors is always the action or attitude of an agent. A sentence such as "there is a reason for the bridge to be repaired" is shorthand for "there is a reason for some agent or agents to repair the bridge."

(4) So if something is a reason, there is some agent whose action or attitude it favors. (from 1–3)

(5) But a consideration can favor an agent's action or attitude only if the agent is in principle capable of grasping the consideration or being guided by it; a favoring relation whose target could not in principle take it up is not a favoring relation at all.

(6) Therefore every reason is a reason for some possible reasoner — some agent who could in principle grasp it and be guided by it. (from 4 and 5)

(7) Now take the notion of a free-floating reason in the sense the dispute is actually about: a reason that obtains even though no actual agent ever grasped it, cited it, or acted on it — an unowned reason.

(8) An unowned reason is a reason for no actual reasoner; and a reason for no actual reasoner is a reason for no possible reasoner.

(9) But, by (6), a reason for no possible reasoner is impossible.

(10) Therefore there are no unowned reasons; every reason is somebody's actual reason.

After your analysis, please append a table with one row per step and these columns:

- Step: the step number, as I numbered it.
- Verdict: one of "acceptable," "rejected," "equivocal between two readings," "valid inference," or "invalid inference."
- What makes it questionable: the strongest challenge to the step from the position that unowned reasons exist.
- Best repair or concession: the strongest wording the other side could accept without giving up its central claim, or "none."
- Effect on the argument: what happens to the overall argument if this step falls.

One more request. If the argument as posed rests on a confusion, or I have defined "free-floating reason" in a way that makes my sister's conclusion true by stipulation, or the task cannot be done well as asked, please say so plainly instead of giving me a polite verdict. I would rather have the setup corrected than get a clean answer to a misdrawn question.
