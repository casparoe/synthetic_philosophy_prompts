So I teach an intro to argument class, and this semester one student has basically turned into Carroll's tortoise. Every time I lay out a deductive step, they'll accept the premises, and accept the conditional that says if the premises are true then the conclusion has to be true, but then they just... won't accept the conclusion. When I add another premise saying anyone who accepts those has to accept the conclusion, they accept that one too and then stall on the next step, forever. It started as a bit but now half the class is convinced nothing follows from anything, and I've realized I don't have a clean way of saying what's wrong with the tortoise's move, or what's right about it.

Can you write me a "which view of deduction do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for this class — nine questions, each a little concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key. Make each question a situation like: the tortoise accepting every premise but never detaching the conclusion; a person who treats a rule of inference as just another premise; a logic teacher who says look, this is just what "if" means; someone at a card game who demands a rule telling them to follow the rule; a proof system where modus ponens is used to show that modus ponens is valid.

The scoring key should end in more or less these leaves, but I want the tree built from the questions and answer patterns first, and then the names spotted into the space they actually occupy: (a) the rule-versus-premise view, where rules aren't the kind of thing that can be listed among premises; (b) the conventionalist or meaning view, where refusing to detach is just not using "if" with its normal meaning; (c) the primitivist view, where deduction is basic and the demand for a justification is misplaced; (d) the pragmatist view, where the whole regress is idle because what matters is what inference does for us in practice; (e) the benign regress or infinitist view, where the chain can go on and that's fine; (f) the skeptic, where the regress shows there's no non-circular justification and that's a genuine problem; and (g) the rule-circularity-is-okay view, where we justify modus ponens by using modus ponens and that's enough. If some answer combinations come out empty, flag them and say whether they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And tell me which leaves the quiz can't really pull apart by cases — I suspect the pragmatist and the rule-versus-premise people are going to answer the same way a lot of the time, so mark wherever the boundry is fuzzy. Don't ask anyone to pick a label; the questions should sort people by what they say about the cases, not what they call themselves.
