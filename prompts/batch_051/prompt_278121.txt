I've been trying to steelman a line I keep hearing from economist friends, that weakness of will is just hyperbolic discounting with a melodramatic name. I wrote the argument out as carefully as I can, and now I cant tell whether it actually goes through or I'm smuggling something in. Here it is:

(1) If akrasia is real, it's a case where at the moment you act you judge that option A is better than option B all things considered, and you do B anyway.

(2) If you judge A better than B all things considered at a time, then at that time you assign A more value than B, full stop.

(3) Hyperbolic discounting is a pattern where a reward loses value steeply across short delays but flattens out over long ones, so a small sooner reward can overtake a larger later reward in current value as the sooner one approaches.

(4) So in a typical akrasia case — say the dieters dessert, or staying up late scrolling even though you've got an early morning — the tempting option has the higher discounted value at the moment of action, even if the prudent option had the higher discounted value earlier.

(5) But then the agent isn't acting against their all-things-considered judgment. Their all-things-considered judgment reversed as the temptation got close, because by (2) their all-things-considered judgment just is whichever option they currently assign the most value.

(6) Therefore clear-eyed akrasia doesn't exist. Weakness of will is a folk misdescription of preference reversal caused by hyperbolic discounting.

I suspect the trouble is in (2) or (5), but I cant say exactly where. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on what reading of the terms? And if the argument fails, does that just rescue the label, or is there a narrower claim in the neighborhood that survives?
