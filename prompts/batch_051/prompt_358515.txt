I cover science-and-ideas stories, and I keep running into the same dispute in different guises: on one side, the claim that humanity has become dramatically less violent, supported by falling rates of homicide and war deaths; on the other, the reply that the conclusion is an artifact of the yardstick — that absolute numbers, nonlethal violence, state and structural harms, or particular regions tell a different or unstable story. I want to set the dueling statistics aside and start one step back. Give me a specification of what a defensible account of the proposition that the world is becoming less violent must deliver before anyone is entitled to cite a trendline for or against it: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse (per capita versus absolute, lethal versus nonlethal, direct versus structural, global versus regional), and the historical or statistical findings it must be consistent with. For each requirement, say why it earns its place, and indicate which ones are negotiable depending on purpose and which are not. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the standard positions in this debate, and whether the full list might be jointly unsatisfiable — that is, whether there may be no determinate fact of the matter at all.
