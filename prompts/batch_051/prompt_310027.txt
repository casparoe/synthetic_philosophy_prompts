I have a standing policy for handling paradoxes about truth. When an argument produces a self-referential sentence that says of itself that it is false, or that it is not true, I do not treat that sentence as a counterexample to whichever account of truth is under discussion. Instead, I conclude that some term in the setup has shifted its meaning, or that the sentence fails to express a determinate proposition, and I set the case aside. I applied this policy recently when someone offered a liar-style sentence against a view I was defending: I refused to count the sentence as evidence against the view and treated the appearance of paradox as a sign of a defect in the example rather than in the theory. Is this a reasonable way to quarantine paradox, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule for protecting my beliefs? Under what conditions would I be justified in setting such a sentence aside as defective, and when would that amount to special pleading?
