I am a PhD student in behavioral ecology, and I work part-time as the science advisor for a natural-history documentary unit. Every few weeks the producers bring me a planned sequence about an animal display — a courtship dance, a threat posture, an alarm call, a begging call — and ask whether it is defensible to describe it on air as "an honest signal of quality" or "a costly handicap." The stakes are editorial accuracy and the unit's credibility with the biologists who watch it. I usually have about an hour, the producers have no background in signaling theory, a senior producer must approve any wording that reaches the narrator, and the procedure must be runnable by a producer in under ten minutes per segment.

Please write me that reusable procedure, grounded in the costly-signaling and handicap-principle literature but translated into a checklist I can hand to the producers. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns "do not use that language," where it returns "defensible to call this a costly signal or handicap," and where it stops and hands the decision back to me because the case is genuinely contested or the call is one of editorial values rather than biological fact. It should distinguish at least these cases: (i) a cue or index that is reliable because of a physical or developmental constraint and is not best described as costly advertising; (ii) a costly signal maintained because only high-quality individuals can bear the cost, so the display is an honest handicap; and (iii) a cheap or manipulative display that is better described as bluff, sensory exploitation, or a signal in an arms race. Mark any steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three segments the team is currently scripting: a peacock displaying his train to a peahen; a bullfrog producing a deeper call than the smaller males around it; and a cuckoo chick begging in a reed-warbler nest. For each, state the bottom-line ruling first, then the reasoning. If the procedure returns a result the producers will not like — for example, telling them to cut a line they love or to add a caveat that undercuts the shot — make that explicit. At the end, say what the procedure cannot do: what it will not tell us about the evolution, function, or subjective experience of a display, and which questions remain empirical or philosophical.

Lead with the bottom-line rule of the procedure in your first sentence, then give the reasoning and the fuller procedure, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
