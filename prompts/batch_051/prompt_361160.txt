I keep coming back to a line near the start of Aristotle's Politics, Book I, chapter 2, and I want someone to stress-test it for me. Aristotle writes:

When several villages are united in a single complete community, large enough to be nearly or quite self-sufficing, the state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life. And therefore, if the earlier forms of society are natural, so is the state, for it is the end of them, and the nature of a thing is its end. For what each thing is when fully developed, we call its nature, whether we are speaking of a man, a horse, or a family. Besides, the final cause and end of a thing is the best, and to be self-sufficing is the end and the best.

I've come to accept the view that self-sufficiency or autarky is the right ideal for both persons and nations — not necessarily literally producing everything yourself, but the idea that freedom, virtue, and full development require not depending on others for the things that matter. My question: what would count against this position, taken as a genuine normative ideal rather than just a practical policy? I want the observations, arguments, historical cases, or results — say from economics, political theory, or psychology — that should move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of the ideal concede as real defeaters, and which would they dismiss as not touching the ideal itself? And has the position been qualified enough, e.g. by saying 'interdependence is fine as long as you could in principle do without,' that it no longer risks anything, and if so is that a problem?
