At a reading group last month we discussed the two strange inversions of reasoning usually associated with Darwin and Turing: Darwin's idea that real-looking design can be produced by a process with no foresight or understanding, and Turing's idea that thinking can be built up out of operations that themselves do no thinking. Afterward two members of the group, Marie and Jon, got into an argument I couldn't settle. Marie opened with something like this: both inversions teach the same deflationary lesson—ask only about competence, not about some extra ingredient called real understanding, because Darwin removed the designer's mind from the explanation of design and Turing removed the inner understander from the explanation of mind. Jon replied that this ran the two inversions together. Darwin, he said, explained how a mindless historical process can produce competent artifacts; that does not erase the present-tense difference between a system whose behavior is controlled by a representation of the domain and a system that merely reproduces patterns it has been exposed to. Marie answered: then name the test that separates them; if two systems match on every behavior and every novel case you can construct, the extra ingredient is an unfalsifiable inner essence.

Jon said she was demanding too much. The difference, he argued, does not have to show up in any single output to be real: the representational system handles novel combinations, explains its answers, notices when its map no longer fits, and revises locally, while the pattern-machine degrades at the edges of its training distribution. Marie replied that all of those are themselves just competencies, so he had merely relabeled understanding as a bundle of broader competencies; in that case the distinction is a sliding scale with no point where real understanding switches on, which is exactly her point. Jon said a vague boundary is still a boundary, and Turing's inversion actually cuts the other way: if comprehension can be assembled from uncomprehending parts, then a system with map-like representations and reason-sensitive control does understand—not despite being made of mindless operations but in virtue of having its competence organized in that particular way. Marie answered that organization is now doing the work the soul used to do, invisible from the outside, and that Darwin's inversion was valuable precisely because it let us set aside such hidden causal powers and study the visible ones.

They continued in this vein for another half hour. I found myself siding with each in alternation, so I would like a point-by-point verdict. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each move as clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each; then say who is more right overall and why; and say what, if anything, the distinction between competence and comprehension amounts to once both inversions are granted. Lead with the bottom line and keep the entire response under 500 words.
