I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how mathematicians treat rigor, and I can't tell whether it tracks anything real or is just status and vibes wearing an epistemology hat.

The accepted way to turn a proof into a "known theorem" is to have some overworked referee read it for a few weeks, skim the boring lemmas, and signal thumbs-up. But when a proof leans on a computer exhaustively checking a finite list of cases—the four-colour theorem in the 1970s, and later the Kepler conjecture—a good chunk of the profession said something like: we now believe it, but it isn't really a proof; it's unsatisfying; we don't understand why it's true. By any outside-view metric, though, the first process is much less reliable then the second. A machine-checked formalization verifies every inference down to primitive rules, while the human process mostly verifies that the proof looks plausible to exactly one tired person. So the community tends to call the less reliable thing "known" and the more reliable thing "merely computer-verified." That's the asymmetry.

What I want to know: is there a real epistemic property that justifies this—surveyability, understanding, having the proof "in your head," whatever—or is it aesthetics and academic sociology doing epistemology cosplay? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the hardest case: a boring classification theorem that is fully machine-checked (the only human contribution being the checker itself) versus a 400-page human proof where the middle 200 pages have never actually been read by anyone except the author. If you don't defend it, say what we should update to instead—flip the default, or something more nuanced. Give me your actual bottom line with a confidence level, and flag the places where you're reasoning from vibes rather than argument. If the premise is confused—say, the discipline mostly got over this decades ago and I'm relitigating a 1980s fight—tell me plainly and steelman the strongest version of the asymmetry before you evaluate it.
