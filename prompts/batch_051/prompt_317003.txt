I have been trying to work out how Skolem himself would have reacted to the philosophical use that was later made of his paradox. As I understand it, the paradox runs as follows. Any consistent first-order axiomatization of set theory, if it has a model at all, has a countable model; yet the very same theory proves that there exists a set whose cardinality is uncountable. Skolem is generally read as having drawn a deflationary moral from this, namely that the distinction between countable and uncountable is relative to the model or axiom system in which one works, and that first-order axioms cannot pin down an absolute universe of sets.

After Skolem's death, Hilary Putnam gave the result a still more radical epistemological use. On Putnam's telling, the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, together with permutation-style considerations, shows that no body of constraints on the interpretation of a term—short of already presupposing an intended interpretation—can uniquely fix its reference. He concluded that the picture of reference as a relation fixed by the world, independent of any conceptual scheme, is untenable, and he developed an internalist alternative.

My question is not what Putnam should have said, but what Skolem would have said. Would Skolem have greeted Putnam's argument as a natural extension of his own doubts about absolute notions, or would he have treated it as an illegitimate leap from a point about formal models of set theory to a conclusion about reference and truth in general? Perhaps he would have resisted the move because he remained committed to a stricter formalism, or because he thought the relativity of cardinalities was compatible with a non-relative notion of denotation. Give me the strongest Skolem-like response you can construct. Where you are on reasonably solid ground about Skolem's actual commitments, say so; where interpreters genuinely disagree, flag the disagreement; and do not present a contested reading of either Skolem or Putnam as the settled consensus.
