I am a novelist. For several years, two friends of mine — a committed psychological egoist and a committed motivational pluralist — have been arguing past each other, in my presence and in written exchanges, about whether any human act is ever ultimately other-directed. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and they have asked me, as a neutral third party whose craft depends on distinguishing real motives from convenient descriptions, to draft the document they would both sign.

Write that document. It should open with a numbered list of claims both parties can accept: claims about what is plainly observable in human conduct, about the distinction between an ultimate and a derivative motive, about the limits of introspection as a tie-breaker, and about what kind of evidence could in principle bear on the question. Then give a disagreements table, one row per genuine disagreement, each side's position phrased so that its own proponents would recognize it and accept it as accurate. The egoist's stated position must not collapse into the tautology that people always do what they do; the pluralist's stated position must not shrink to the mere observation that people sometimes report concern for others.

After the table, state the cruxes. I suspect at least three: whether a motive can be genuinely other-directed when its satisfaction never registers in the agent's own experience as pleasure, relief, or the avoidance of distress; whether psychological egoism can be falsified at all, or can absorb any possible case by redescribing the payoff afterward; and whether the fact that acting on a concern usually brings the agent satisfaction shows that the satisfaction was the aim. For each crux, give a concrete case, real or imagined, that both sides would accept as a fair test of the crux, and record in advance what each side predicts about it.

Then identify any part of the dispute that is merely verbal, and state the reformulation under which the two parties would be addressing one question rather than talking past each other.

Keep two distinctions explicit throughout. First, the descriptive thesis of psychological egoism — that people always act, ultimately, on self-interested motives — is different from the normative thesis of ethical egoism — that people ought to act on self-interested motives — and the document should say where, if anywhere, the normative thesis is doing hidden work in the descriptive argument. Second, the aim of an action is different from whatever feelings happen to accompany or follow it.
