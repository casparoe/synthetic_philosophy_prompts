I am a high school student preparing an argument for a summer bioethics program, and I have been trying to pin down an asymmetry in how public institutions value life.

Consider two facts. First, when health systems must ration a scarce treatment — a transplantable organ, a critical-care bed during a pandemic, a limited drug budget — many of their allocation protocols take expected remaining life-years into account. A 25-year-old and a 75-year-old with the same medical need and the same chance of benefit are not treated as interchangeable: the younger patient is ranked ahead because a successful intervention is expected to preserve more future quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). Second, when the same society punishes the wrongful taking of a life, the victim's expected remaining life-years are treated as irrelevant to the seriousness of the offense. Homicide is not punished less severely because the victim was old rather than young, and no court would accept "he had fewer years left" as a reason to treat the killing as a lesser wrong.

So a distinction between young and old that is accepted, even required, when allocating scarce medical resources is treated as unacceptable when the criminal law values a life. Is there a defensible reason for this asymmetry, or is one of the two practices simply wrong about what gives a life its value?

The most natural defense of the clinical practice creates a further problem. If younger age matters because it means more future healthy years, why does social role not matter too, when treating a parent of young children or a widely relied-upon professional would preserve more wellbeing for others? Hospitals and courts both reject social-worth weighting. So anyone who defends the asymmetry must explain exactly why remaining life-years may determine who lives while social value may not, without giving up the distinction between allocation and punishment.

Please structure your response under these headings, in this order:

1. The asymmetry, stated exactly (about 120 words). Restate the asymmetry so that a reader can see precisely where the two practices part ways, and flag the two questions any defense must answer.

2. The best case for keeping the asymmetry (about 380 words). Give the strongest justification for allowing remaining life-years to count in allocation but not in judgments of wrongful killing, and for drawing the line between future life-years and social worth. Do not caricature the opposing view.

3. The best case against the asymmetry (about 380 words). Give the strongest argument that the asymmetry cannot be defended, including the hardest nearby case for someone who wants to keep it.

4. Verdict (about 220 words). State whether the asymmetry should be kept, abandoned, or revised. If revised, give the rule you would actually apply in a hospital rationing committee, and say what it would mean for the 25-year-old and the 75-year-old.
