I am a high school student writing a term paper for a philosophy seminar, and I keep running into a dispute that seems to have gone on for more than a century without much movement: the disagreement between Peirce's and James's versions of the pragmatist theory of truth. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on American pragmatism and on the structure of philosophical disagreements. I would like you to think through this carefully rather than jumping to a verdict—start by brainstorming the different questions that might be packed into the phrase "pragmatist theory of truth" and the different things the two camps might actually be doing when they argue about it.

As I understand them from the primary texts, the two positions look like this. Peirce, in his early essay on clarity, treats truth as a convergence property: a belief is true if it is the opinion to which investigators would eventually settle if inquiry were pushed far enough, by an indefinitely extended community using self-correcting methods. On that view, truth is a limit toward which scientific practice tends, and it does not depend on what any specific person finds pleasant, useful, or satisfying right now. James, in his later lectures on pragmatism, treats truth in a more psychological and practical register: an idea becomes true, he says in effect, when it proves itself useful, connects smoothly with the rest of experience, and helps us act. For James, truth is bound up with verification, success, and satisfaction; it is not a distant limit of inquiry so much as something that happens to beliefs as they earn their keep.

The scholarly note I have seen is that Peirce himself later distanced himself from James's emphasis on satisfaction and even renamed his own view to separate it from looser readings. The standard criticisms and replies are also well rehearsed. Critics charge that a belief can be useful without being true, and true without being useful; that the Jamesian view conflates the meaning of "true" with a practical test for when a belief should be accepted; and that the whole program slides into wishful thinking. Pragmatists reply that the critics take "useful" in an unfairly narrow or temporary sense, and that they are importing assumptions the pragmatists never shared.

Here is the puzzle. The phrase "pragmatist theory of truth" is used as if Peirceans and Jamesians are engaged in one continuous debate, yet the two sides often look to me as if they are answering different questions: Peirce seems to be giving an account of what truth consists in at the limit of inquiry, while James seems to be giving an account of how actual inquirers come to accept beliefs as true. The argument has lasted so long, with every move so familiar, that I suspect the real task is not to decide who is correct but to diagnose why the debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is.

I am not asking you to adjudicate which theory of truth is right. I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it a verbal dispute, a substantive one, or two parties answering different questions under one heading? Do the two camps differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over which cases count as data, or over starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question driving the impasse? My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the impasse comes largely from running together a definitional question and a practical or methodological one—but I want that tested. After you diagnose the structure of the disagreement, tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question—or, if you think the impasse should not be expected to resolve, argue for that and say why.
