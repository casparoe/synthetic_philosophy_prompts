I'm teaching a grad seminar on divine command theories and I keep coming back to the old Mu'tazila–Ash'arite split as a way to get students past the standard Euthyphro talking points. I want you to analyze that debate through the lens of this passage, not the more famous "pious because it is loved" line but the earlier definition at Euthyphro 9e, in Fowler's translation:

"Well, I should say that what all the gods love is holy and, on the other hand, what they all hate is unholy."

Here's what I'm after. The Mu'tazilites are usually described as holding that reason can discern good and evil, and God commands what is good because it is good. The Ash'arites, or at least the standard caricature, hold that good and evil are constituted by divine command, so there is no independent standard to constrain or explain God's willing. I want you to map each school onto the Euthyphro dilemma as carefully as you can, but take the passage seriously as a distinct formulation. Notice it says what all the gods love, and that shifts the question: when we move to monotheism, does the "all the gods" clause do any work, or does it just drop out? If it drops out, does the Islamic debate become a cleaner version of the dilemma than Plato's original, or does it lose something important? I'm interested in how the Mu'tazilite appeal to reason might be a way of saying the property of being right and the property of being loved by God are extensionally equivalent but intensionally distinct, and whether the Ash'arite response actually denies that or just insists the explanatory order runs the other way.

Don't just regurgitate the old "is it right because God commands it" line. I want you to prod the positions: does the Ash'arite view collapse into the view that God could command the negation of what we now call justice and it would be just? Did any Ash'arites actually say that, or is it an uncharitable consequence? And does the Mu'tazilite view risk making God a being who must obey an external standard, which would be theologically unacceptable to them too? Also, use the passage to say something about whether the dispute is purely semantic or has practical differences for how a jurist would reason.

Tell me how confident you are in each main claim, and single out the point you're least sure of. Keep it under 1,200 words—I don't want a survey article, I want a sharp case analysis with the passage doing the work.
