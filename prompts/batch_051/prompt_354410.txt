I've been reading about endurantism vs perdurantism (the debate over whether things persist by being wholly present at each moment or by having temporal parts), and it keeps reminding me of the two ways I think about a file's identity when I work with git.

Here's the parallel. On the endurantist picture, a person or a table is literally the same object at t1 and t2; it changes by having different properties at different times, but there's no aggregate "temporal worm" of which the moments are proper parts. That sounds exactly like the working tree / in-place mutation: there's one file at one path, I edit it, it's "the same file," and the old content is gone unless I deliberately saved it. On the perdurantist picture, the persisting thing is the whole sequence of temporal parts, and "the table at 3pm" is a part of the table, not the whole table. That sounds like git's object model: commits are snapshots, the "file across time" is a chain of distinct blob objects, and "the file at commit abc123" is one version in that chain, not the whole history. If I take the git view seriously, the file isn't really an entity that changes — it's a series of immutable versions glued together by the commit graph.

So the question: is this a genuine structural isomorphism or just me mapping two things that share the word "identity"? I want a precise verdict, not vibes. In particular:

- What exactly is the mapping, concept by concept (temporal part, worm, endurance, identity, change, parthood)?
- Where does it hold up and where does it break? (I already suspect git's "temporal parts" are full snapsots, whereas a temporal part of a person is a thin slice, not a whole person — but maybe that's not fatal.)
- What would have to be true of both fields for any result to transfer — e.g., does anything about git (diffing old versions, cherry-picking, merge conflicts) teach us something about how a perdurantist should think about change or identity, or is that exactly where the analogy breaks?
- And can you name at least one thing the analogy would predict that is flatly false on one side or the other?
