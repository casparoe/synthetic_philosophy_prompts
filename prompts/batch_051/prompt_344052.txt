As a novelist who teaches a prose workshop, I gave the following short exam question in a unit on how a sentence's grammatical defaults carry an implicit picture of the normal or paradigmatic person. Grade the student's answer for me. Give the mark you would assign, the point-by-point comments that justify it, and, wherever the answer goes wrong, a brief statement of what a correct answer would say instead. I want comments I could hand back with the grade, not a general impression.

Question: Douglas Hofstadter's Person Paper argues by satiric inversion. It transposes the standard defenses of masculine default language—generic he, chairman, man for humanity—onto a fictional society in which race plays the parallel grammatical role, so that the defenses of the one can be read as defenses of the other. Reconstruct the argumentative structure of this satire, state what conclusion about English usage it does and does not license, and discuss what it implies about why neutral reforms such as chairperson, he or she, and singular they have so often sounded stilted or ideological to fluent speakers.

Student's answer: Hofstadter's piece is a reductio ad absurdum. By imagining a language whose default terms for people are built around one race in the way English builds its defaults around men, he shows that the familiar defenses—that the usage is only a convention, that the word no longer really refers to gender, that reforming it is pedantic and ugly—have exactly the same form in a case everyone can see is objectionable. Since the premises are identical, consistency forces us to reject those defenses for English as well. The satire therefore proves that masculine generics injure women in the way racial slurs injure their targets, and that any term carrying an obsolete gender marker should be discarded. It also explains why reformed usage feels wrong to fluent speakers: the new forms genuinely are less elegant, and usage is governed by aesthetics as much as by logic.
