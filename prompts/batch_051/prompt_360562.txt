Here's a distinction I've been leaning on for a while and want someone to kick the tyres on. I call it the loudness tax. When a person softens the truth to spare someone's feelings, they move a cost rather than removing it: the speaker no longer pays the social price of having said the awkward thing, but the hearer now pays in uncertainty about what was actually meant. Dutch directness has the speaker pay the tax; Japanese indirectness has the hearer pay it. On this picture a white lie is not good or bad in itself, just a shift of epistemic costs from one side of the conversation to the other, and the moral question is who ought to bear them in that relationship. It would explain why the same sentence can read as considerate in one culture and evasive in another. Is this a real concept or just politeness theory and Grice in a trench coat? Give me the three strongest objections, say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and tell me which objection or piece of evidence would do the most to flip your overall verdict. British English, please.
