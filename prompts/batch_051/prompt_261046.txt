Imagine a mid-sized American city—call it Fairhill—where at midnight tonight three things change at once, and nothing else does.

First, voluntary tipping stops being possible. Every restaurant, bar, and cafe goes cashless overnight and their payment systems loose the tip field for good, so there's literally no way to leave a gratuity. Instead every sit-down place automatically adds a flat 18% service charge that goes straight to the workers. Second, the city launches a genuinely competent Queue Authority that enforces line-forming wherever people queue—bus stops, clinics, grocery stores, food trucks. Cutting, holding a spot for more than one person, and drifting in from the side are fined like parking tickets. Third, the city decriminalizes jaywalking and rips out most marked crosswalks from the downtown grid. People cross wherever they like, and if a car hits a pedestrian the driver is liable by default.

Same population, same incomes, same police, same tourist numbers, no public-information campaign.

Over the next ten years, predict how restaurant and bar culture, queue behavior, and downtown driving and walking change. I want mechanisms, not a story. For each of the three changes, separate the effects that run through incentives (prices, fines, liability) from the effects that run through social emotions (gratitude, shame, resentment, insult) and the effects that run through people's background beliefs about whether these conventions were moral requirements or just coordination devices. Give a confidence level for each major claim: say which predictions you'd bet on, which are fragile, and what would have to be true about people for each fragile one to go the other way.

Then take up the visitors question. How would out-of-towners behave differently from locals under these rules, and when—if ever—should a visitor treat a local convention as a moral obligation rather than a mere courtesy? Keep the descriptive story and the normative claim clearly separated, and apply the same discipline there: throughout, separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study norms and morality, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
