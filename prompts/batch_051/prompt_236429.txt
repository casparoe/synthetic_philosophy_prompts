I've been around LessWrong and the evals blogosphere long enough to be deeply suspicious of few-shot "learning," and I now run a small team whose job is to turn that suspicion into something operational. Every two weeks we ship a batch of items for a public benchmark that other orgs use to compare models, so if a model games the items by glomming onto surface features, the numbers are worse than noise. The format is Bongard-style: a solver sees six examples that all fit some rule and six that break it, and has to state the rule. (For anyone who hasn't seen them: the classic version has twelve little panels, six exhibiting the law and six flouting it, and you have to say what the law is. Ours are textual, so a "panel" is a short description like "a triangle inside a circle next to a square" or a two-sentence story.) The recurring failure mode is that a model gets every training panel right while obviously not having the concept: it has found some degenerate surface rule like "all left panels mention a shape name" or "all right panels use the past tense," because infintely many rules fit any finite set of examples and the simplest one in the model's feature space may not be the one we intended.

I'd like a reusable procedure our team can follow every time we design an item and every time we interpret a model's performance. Ground it in whatever is actually known about concept induction from a few examples, but make it usable by three ML engineers and one philosophy-curious founder in about a day per item; the final checklist has to be boring enough for our board to sign off. The procedure should, at minimum, force us to state the intended rule before we write any panels, build negatives that are near-misses and positives that vary along every irrelevant dimension we can think of, check for decoy rules that are simpler than the intended one, generate held-out panels where the surface features fail while the rule still holds and vice versa, and score the model's stated rule rather than just its left/right classifications. Mark the places where we are making a judgment call—especially how we choose the vocabulary in which "simpler rule" is judged, and how abstract the intended concept is allowed to be—and the places where the procedure honestly cannot settle whether the model got the concept rather than the decoy. Then run the procedure on two items we are currently fighting about: one geometric where the intended rule is "every left panel contains at least one closed shape nested inside another closed shape," and one social where the intended rule is "the main utterance commits the speaker to a future action" across tiny story-panels. I expect, and want, at least one case where the procedure tells us an item we love is unsalvageable as a few-shot concept test.
