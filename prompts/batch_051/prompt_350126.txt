I've come around to a broadly Scanlonian view of when a policy is permissible: it's okay if it's justified by a principle that no individual it applies to could reasonably reject, where what counts as reasonable is the strength of each person's own complaint, not the summed or averaged harm across people. So I think you can't save five people from a minor headache by breaking one person's arm, even if the five headaches add up to more total pain; the person with the broken arm has a complaint the other five don't have. I want to know what would count against this position — not the usual line that then we could never build a road or impose a tax, but the cases, intuitions, and internal pressures that should genuinely move someone like me. What should a committed contractualist concede is a serious problem, and what should they dismiss as question-begging or beside the point? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the view ends up insulated in a way that's a problem for a view of this kind. For what its worth, the cases I find most worrying are the ones where a whole lot of tiny sacrifices by many people add up to preventing a real catastrophe.
