Last week a reading group I sit in on was discussing whether cultural variants literally replicate, and two members got into a long argument about the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge of 2014 as a test case. Mara argued that it is nearly as clean an example of a meme as one could want. There is a recognizable behavioral package—dump or receive a bucket of ice water, film it, post the video, nominate named others—and that package was copied millions of times with enough stability to be tracked; it produced variant forms (donating instead of dumping, adding a 24-hour deadline, attaching unrelated causes); and variants seem to have spread or died out partly because of their content, which looks like selection. So, she said, the thing satisfied the standard criteria of fecundity, longevity, and copying fidelity well enough, and treating it as a replicating unit lets us ask real questions about lineages and selective environments.

Dan replied that this is exactly what is misleading about the word "meme." What actually spread, he said, was a rough public idea—an ice-water challenge involving a camera and nominations—and each person reconstructed a performance from that gist rather than copying a token. The similarities among videos reflect convergence on a salient cultural attractor, a stable form people tend to hit when they reconstruct an idea, not descent from a common ancestor; the "variants" Mara called lineages are mostly independent reconstructions of the same schema, and the fidelity is at the level of meaning, not mechanism. Mara answered that replication never required perfect copying—genes mutate—and that the 24-hour rule and the donate-and-dump combination spread along actual chains of nomination, so specific performances did inherit features from the particular versions people saw, not merely from an abstract schema. Dan shot back that social influence is cheap: we can trace chains of jokes, slang, and fads without needing a gene-like particulate unit, so the question is whether anything explanatory is added by saying the unit replicates, rather than merely that a stable idea propagates through a social network. That is where they got stuck.

They asked me, as a bystander, to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate: which of Mara's and Dan's specific claims are right, which are overstated, what—if anything—both are missing, and who is more right overall about whether the Ice Bucket Challenge shows that "meme" is more than a metaphor.
