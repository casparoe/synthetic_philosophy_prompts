Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether markets corrupt or civilize character, and in the course of defending myself I ended up articulating a distinction I had been using without naming it. I would like it examined before I rely on it any further. For now I am calling it market legibility.

The proposal is that markets are neither uniformly corrupting nor uniformly civilizing; they are virtue-selective, and the selection runs through a single filter: whether the exercise of a trait can be captured by a price and returned to the agent who exercised it. Call a trait market-legible when its exercise produces value the market can measure, attribute, and pay for. Reliability, punctuality, keeping one's word, care for one's work: these get converted into reputation, repeat business, and enforceable contracts. Call a trait market-illegible when its value is diffuse, delayed, hard to attribute, or damaged by the attempt to price it. Generosity toward those who cannot reciprocate, candor that costs you the sale, forbearance when retaliation is available, civic courage, aesthetic restraint. A firm that honors a warranty no one will notice is exercising an illegible virtue; a firm that pays its suppliers on time is exercising a legible one, and the market builds institutions that make that behavior cheap and rewarded.

The central claim is that markets do not make people broadly better or broadly worse. They make legible virtues cheaper to perform and therefore more common, and they make illegible virtues more expensive to perform and therefore rarer, because anyone who bears the cost of an illegible virtue is subsidizing a competitor who fakes or skips it. Over a long enough run this stops being a matter of individual temptation and becomes a matter of which practices survive. The old quarrel between the view that commerce polishes manners and the view that it dissolves the virtues that made it possible is, on my account, badly posed: both processes occur at once, at different points on the legibility line.

My friend pressed two objections. The first is that the distinction collapses into a truism: markets reward what markets can measure, measurement is always incomplete, and of course the unmeasured gets neglected. The second is sharper: the market does not merely underpay illegible virtues; it teaches people to treat all value as fungible, so those virtues become not only costly but unintelligible, and the loss is one of meaning rather than selection. He also demanded a case where a legible virtue is corrupted by the market anyway, and I could not produce a clean one, which bothers me.

I would like an honest assessment. Is market legibility a genuine distinction or a repackaged version of something already worked out? I am vaguely aware of research on moral incentives being crowded out by prices, on virtue and commerce, and on the civilizing effect of trade, but I want to know the nearest existing ideas and whether I am merely relabeling one of them. Where does the theory break? In particular: does it do too much work with one filter; are there legible virtues that markets tend to corrupt, and illegible virtues markets tend to preserve; and does the causal direction run the way I set it, from legibility to prevalence, or does the market simply learn to price whatever practices are already thriving? Cases that cut against the line matter more to me than agreement.
