I am a high school student preparing for a competitive summer program in economics and philosophy, and I want to make my application essay turn on a puzzle in the theory of rational choice. The puzzle starts from a distinction I have encountered between two ways of averaging a gamble. Evaluated across many independent trials, or many people at one moment, a gamble has one expected value (the ensemble average). Evaluated for a single person who plays a related gamble over many periods and lets gains and losses compound, it has another (the time average). In multiplicative settings these two averages diverge, and some writers conclude that the ensemble average is the wrong standard for judging whether a gamble is good for an individual over time; they say what matters is the time-average growth rate, and that confusing the two is a conceptual error, not merely a statistical one.

I follow the mathematics, but I am not convinced the mathematics alone settles the normative claim about what an agent should maximize. My suspicion is that the dispute hides two separable claims: a conceptual claim about what rationality means for one agent facing repeated multiplicative bets, and an empirical claim about how people actually handle such bets and whether they improve with feedback. I want the strongest possible design for bringing evidence to bear on the empirical side of this. Please design one concrete experiment or observational test, not requiring outside sources, that could genuinely distinguish between the following possibilities: (a) the time-average standard picks out choices that measurably improve a person's outcomes over time in multiplicative tasks, or (b) it mostly functions as a post hoc redescription of choices that people would make anyway, with no measurable difference in outcome. Say precisely what pattern of results would count for each side, and then say what even an ideal test could and could not settle about the underlying conceptual disagreement.
