You are a professional philosopher working on the epistemology of interpretation and on authority in text-governed communities. Think carefully and systematically. Begin by stating, in your own words, exactly where the regress of interpretation bites, then produce the specification described below.

The object of analysis is a community committed to sola scriptura, understood as follows: the text of Scripture is the sole infallible rule of faith and practice, and all human interpreters, institutions, and traditions are fallible and stand under the text's judgement. The community nonetheless must resolve contested readings — about, for example, who may hold an office, whether a practice is commanded or forbidden, or which texts count as Scripture in the first place — and it must be able to treat at least some resolutions as binding without quietly abandoning the doctrine it began from.

The difficulty is familiar enough. Any binding resolution must itself be authorised. If the text authorises it, that claim rests on a particular reading of the text, and that reading is precisely what is disputed. If a council or teacher authorises it, the authorising body's own authority is what a dissenter may deny. If the community appeals instead to the text's clarity for the sincere reader, to private illumination, or to conscience, those criteria are themselves applied under contested conditions, so disagreement simply reappears one step further down. The doctrine seems to leave no principled stopping point between an ungrounded magisterium and ungoverned private judgement, each of which makes something other than the text functionally supreme.

Your task is to write the desiderata: the list of conditions any adequate account of interpretive authority under sola scriptura must satisfy. For each condition, give the kind of case or structural dilemma that forces it onto the list, state whether it is load-bearing or merely negotiable, and note which condition appears to do the most exclusionary work. Address, at a minimum, how the theory handles: (i) the closure of actual disputes without bare majority, force, or capitulation to whatever interpretation is currently dominant; (ii) the text remaining a fixed object of reference rather than something whose meaning shifts with the interpreter's convenience; (iii) the non-circular authorisation of interpreters and interpretive procedures; (iv) resolutions that are fallible yet still binding enough to guide practice; and (v) stability across time held together with the possibility of real correction. You are not confined to these.

Then identify the pairs of desiderata that seem hardest to satisfy jointly, or that cannot be made compatible without weakening one side of the doctrine itself. Finally, in a short closing section, score four broad types of answer against the list: an infallible teaching office, a binding confessional tradition, an unrestricted private-judgement view, and a tradition-constituted or historicist view. Keep this closing section schematic and brief; the criteria themselves, not the scoring, should carry the weight of the response. Do not provide a bibliography, do not quote sources, and do not turn the response into a historical survey. Where a named position does no argumentative work, leave it out.
