I am a novelist building an invented world, and I want its historians to have more than a body of legends: I want a definite, internally workable philosophy of history for the world, and one that my plot — which follows a single civilization from founding through crisis to decline and aftermath — can actually dramatize. I would like you to write me a sorting instrument: a decision tree, or a short quiz with a scoring key if a tree would misrepresent the options, that takes a set of answers about how a fictional culture's history works and sorts it into the following seven positions: the Polybian cycle of constitutions (anacyclosis); the Ibn Khaldunian cycle of group solidarity; a Spenglerian organic life-cycle of cultures; a Toynbeean challenge-and-response account; linear progress; linear providential history, a single directed arc with an appointed end; and Nietzschean eternal recurrence. You may add leaves, but only if the logical space genuinely demands them.

Build the instrument from the branching questions first, not from the list of labels. Each branch or quiz item should be a concrete worldbuilding claim I can answer about my invented world without already knowing which label it points to — for example: whether after a collapse the same sequence of regimes necessarily recurs; whether the engine of rise and fall lies in the political form, the social bond, an external challenge, or an organic life-span; whether a civilization's peak comes early or late; whether there is a final terminus after the last civilization; whether recurrence is lawful or merely possible; and whether history has a direction that survives any single civilization's end. The instrument should sort by what the world's history actually does in the plot and backstory, not by what the culture's intellectuals say about themselves.

Then locate the seven named positions as leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position and one sentence on the most awkward narrative commitment it would impose on me as a novelist — the scene or story-beat that the position makes hard to write honestly. Mark any answer-combination that corresponds to no named position, with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody holds it because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. If two of the seven positions cannot be separated by any question you devise, flag them as a pair your instrument cannot distinguish rather than inventing a distinction. Keep the whole instrument to no more than ten branch nodes or quiz items.
