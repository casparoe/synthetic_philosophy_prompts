You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a working knowledge of electrophysiology and computational modeling. I want you to think out loud, starting with whatever half-formed ideas come to mind first, before settling on your best version of the answer.

At our lab meeting, a colleague argued that when a macaque views two arrays of dots and decides which is more numerous, the population activity in its intraparietal sulcus does not merely correlate with the ratio of the two numerosities; the network literally computes that ratio, in something like the way a pocket calculator does. A skeptic in the room replied that this is an empty claim: any firing-rate code can be redescribed as computing something if you allow an arbitrary mapping between spike rates and numbers, so the literal-computation claim is unfalsifiable. Our colleague shot back that the right experiment could settle it, and now the rest of us are stuck.

Your job is to play referee. Design the strongest concrete experiment you can to test the claim that a neural population literally computes a numerical relation, such as a ratio, rather than merely being usefully described as if it computes one. Be explicit about the manipulation, the measurement, and the result that would favor each reading. Then say as sharply as you can what a positive result and a negative result would each establish and fail to establish. Finally, say how much of the residual disagreement is a limit of the experiment and how much is a conceptual unsettledness about what it takes for a physical system to count as computing at all, and whether any further experiment could close that gap in principle.
