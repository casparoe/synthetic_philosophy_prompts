I am a philosophy professor, and next month I am giving a colloquium talk on the St. Petersburg paradox to a joint workshop of the philosophy and economics departments, with a few formal epistemologists also attending. I would like you to prepare me for the question period.

The talk defends this thesis: the standard moral drawn from the St. Petersburg paradox—that rational agents must have bounded utility functions—is a repair to the wrong component of the theory. The paradox is not fundamentally a problem about infinity, or about how to weigh tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs. It is a problem about representability: it shows that a rational agent's preferences over a space of outcomes that includes the gamble's infinitely many possible outcomes need not admit of any real-valued expected-utility representation at all, and that the demand that the agent price the gamble is exactly the step at which the representation is being imposed without justification. On my view, the fully rational response can be to refuse to buy or sell the gamble at any finite price—not because its utility is finite and lower than the price, but because the gamble is not in the domain of alternatives over which the agent's preferences are cardinally representable. The usual patches—bounded utility, discounting small probabilities, the finite wealth of the casino—are attempts to force the gamble back into the domain of the theory rather than acknowledgments of a limit of the theory.

In the talk I argue for this in three steps: first, that the paradox's force comes from treating expected monetary value as the criterion of choice and then noticing that it cannot be the criterion; second, that replacing monetary value with a bounded utility function solves the pricing puzzle only if one has already assumed that the gamble must receive a real-valued utility, which is the assumption in question; and third, that the same issue arises in less exotic cases—decisions involving theoretical idealizations, Pascal's-wager-like structures, and long-shot scientific gambles—so this is not a remote curiosity.

The audience: most are economists, including several decision theorists and behavioral economists; there are two formal epistemologists and two philosophers of science; and one senior decision theorist known for defending the view that all rational preferences are representable. The economists will come in thinking the paradox was settled by Bernoulli and that I am re-opening a closed question; the formal epistemologists will worry that my view licenses irresolvable indecision or violates the expected-accuracy norm; the philosopher of science will be the most sympathetic.

Please give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my argument. I want: (1) the eight or so objections I am actually most likely to get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; (2) each objection phrased roughly the way this audience would actually put it—in the economists' or formal epistemologists' own idiom; (3) for each, a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, including what I should concede up front rather than defend; and (4) the one question I should most hope nobody asks, with the reason it would be hardest to answer.
