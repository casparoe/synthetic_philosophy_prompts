I am preparing an internal seminar paper for the Institute for Digital Society, where I will defend the following thesis. The Foucauldian concept of disciplinary power has been overextended in surveillance studies: the panopticon is asked to explain forms of algorithmic power that it cannot explain, because the decisive operations of algorithmic sorting, scoring, and predictive analytics act on populations and their statistical regularities rather than on the individual body under a normalising gaze. The more productive Foucauldian resource for these systems is the later analysis of security mechanisms set out in the 1977–78 Collège de France lectures on security, territory, and population: apparatuses that work through the management of circulation, the modulation of the milieu, and the handling of risk thresholds, and that do not need to observe an individual in order to act upon them. My paper has a diagnostic claim and a practical claim. Diagnostically, I argue that most digital panopticon readings conflate two distinct modes of power and thereby misdescribe what algorithmic systems actually do. Practically, I argue that this misdescription matters because the repertoire of resistance derived from the disciplinary story — making surveillance visible, contesting the gaze, refusing the norm — is poorly suited to systems that can classify and act upon people without anyone watching them in the disciplinary sense.

The audience is roughly twenty people. Most are critical data-studies and surveillance-studies scholars who habitually reach for the panopticon as a master metaphor and will feel that I am taking away a serviceable framework. Several are careful Foucauldians who accept a broad distinction between sovereign, disciplinary, and security apparatuses and will press me on whether my use of the 1977–78 material is faithful. Two are information-law scholars who regard panoptic talk as loose but are also sceptical that a theoretical category error explains much about why legal instruments such as consent and purpose limitation already fail in practice. The director is a sociologist of quantification who thinks the discipline/security distinction may be internally interesting but will want to know what it changes operationally, for practitioners and for the people being sorted.

Prepare a Q&A sheet for me: the eight to ten objections I am actually likely to get from this audience, in roughly the order in which they will be raised, with a note on how damaging each is, each phrased as a member of this audience would phrase it, and with for each a reply of about two minutes and a note on where the reply is weakest. Distinguish the objections that have genuine force from the ones I should be able to handle quickly. Tell me what I should concede up front. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, phrased the way it would be put, and advise me on how to handle it. Where a member of the audience would likely object in terms of the hybridity or coexistence of power mechanisms rather than outright rejection, let the sheet reflect that.
