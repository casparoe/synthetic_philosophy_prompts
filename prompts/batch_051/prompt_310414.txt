I have come to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, and would be grateful for an answer that does not assume legal training. I am struck by the common law's treatment of promises. A simple promise, however seriously intended, is not generally enforceable: the law requires consideration, in the rough sense that the promise must have been given as part of an exchange. Yet a promise that induces reasonable, detrimental reliance can, in many common-law jurisdictions, be given legal effect through estoppel even without consideration; and a gratuitous promise made by deed, with the formalities of signature and witnessing, binds without either consideration or reliance. The law therefore declines to treat the bare fact of promising as a sufficient ground of enforcement, while accepting three apparently different substitutes: an exchange, a solemn form, and induced reliance. What account of why promises bind does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, say whether the exchange, formality and reliance strands presuppose consistent views of promissory obligation or quietly pull against one another, and which rival theories the practice quietly rules out. Then explain what would have to change in the law if the implicit view were replaced by a promise-based theory, on which a communicated serious intention to undertake an obligation is itself enough, or by a reliance-based theory, on which only detrimental reliance by the promisee justifies enforcement.
