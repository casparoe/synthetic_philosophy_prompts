I keep finding myself treating certain everyday local conventions as morally binding and others as mere etiquette, and I want to know whether there is one principle behind the difference or whether I am just rationalizing whatever feels uncouth. Here are the verdicts I endorse on particular cases.

First, tipping in an American full-service restaurant. I know that in much of the United States tipped restaurant workers are paid below the ordinary minimum wage on the assumption that tips will make up the difference. I treat the customary 15 to 20 percent as genuinely owed, not merely expected. If I left nothing for a competent and courteous server without special cause, I would describe it as wronging the server, not just as being rude.

Second, queueing at a bus stop in London. I join the back of the line, and I regard someone who pushes to the front as wronging the people she skips, even when the bus is half empty and everyone will board.

Third, jaywalking in a city where local pedestrians cross against the light whenever the street is clear. I cross with them and feel no compunction; standing alone on the curb waiting for the signal would strike me as fetishizing a light.

Fourth, jaywalking in a city where the local norm is to wait at red even late at night on an empty street. There I wait too, and crossing with no one around would still feel like a breach of some obligation, though I could not name the person to whom it is owed, and I would rank it well below queue-jumping in gravity.

Fifth, tipping in a no-tipping country. I leave no tip, but not merely because it is not the custom: I think forcing a tip on the server would be close to an insult.

Sixth, the escalator on the London Underground. I stand on the right and leave the left free for people walking. Someone who blocks the left strikes me as selfish, but I would not say he wrongs anyone in particular; it is a failure of consideration rather than an injustice.

Seventh, haggling. In a shop in a fixed-price culture, trying to bargain the price down feels wrong in the way that queue-jumping is wrong. In a market culture where haggling is expected, paying the asking price feels merely like a bad deal, not a failure to play the game.

My own half-formed principle is that a convention binds me when breaking it would either cheat a specific person who reasonably relies on my compliance, or insult the locals by refusing a practice they reasonably regard as basic respect. That principle handles the American tip and the queue under the first clause, and the no-tipping country and the haggling case under the second. But my fourth and sixth verdicts strain it: in neither case is anyone relying on me, and in the empty-street case no one is even present to be insulted, yet I still feel the convention has force. So either those two verdicts have to go, or the principle needs something beyond reliance and insult.

I want to know what principle best captures the pattern, stated precisely, with the cases that motivate each clause. What does it say about a tip jar on the counter of an American cafe, where there is no table service and the worker is paid an ordinary wage? What does it say about a small bribe to a clerk to process a permit in a place where that is simply how permits get processed? And which of my seven verdicts, if any, should I give up?
