I am a retired physicist who has come late in life to the philosophy of law, and I have been working through the arguments surrounding the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals. The objection that keeps snagging my attention is the defense claim, made at both trials, that charging the defendants with crimes against peace and crimes against humanity amounted to retroactive punishment, in violation of the maxim nullum crimen sine lege, nulla poena sine lege — no crime without law, no punishment without law. Nuremberg appears to me to have treated the maxim as one solid rule to be set aside wholesale, while at Tokyo the tribunal was visibly divided over it. From a scientific habit of refusing to assess a term before it has been disambiguated, it seems to me that the maxim actually bundles together several distinct principles: a principle that the conduct must already have been made a statutory offense before the act occurred; a principle that the defendant must have had fair notice; a principle that only a preexisting lawful authority may define new offenses; a principle that an act already morally wrong may still be punished even absent a prior statute; and perhaps a principle that punishing in such cases does more harm than good. Could you separate these readings, and any others you regard as distinct, and for each give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible principle of legality, or doubtful? Then say which of these readings was actually carrying the weight in the defense argument at Nuremberg and in the split at Tokyo, and which readings the tribunals would have had to reject, rather than merely acknowledge, in order to convict. A menu of readings with verdicts would serve me better than a single reconstruction, since the literature seems to move among readings without signaling the shift.
