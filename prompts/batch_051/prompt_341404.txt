In my philosophy class we read The Subjection of Women, and I have been thinking about Mill’s argument that appeals to women’s nature are usually circular. As I understand him, Mill says that the traits observers treat as natural in women have been produced by the laws, customs, and expectations under which women live, so the supposed evidence of a natural unfitness is really the effect of a social arrangement. He also says we have never seen what women would be like under genuinely equal conditions, so appeals to nature are premature. I want to know how he would respond to a modern version of this argument that he could not have anticipated. Suppose someone defends the underrepresentation of women in some technical fields by saying that there are average psychological sex differences with a biological basis—prenatal hormone exposure, evolved dispositions, or differences in interests that appear early and cross-culturally. They claim this is not a social-construction story but a natural-difference story. What would Mill say? Would he think the evidence is still tainted by social arrangements, or would he accept that some differences are genuinely biological and adjust his view? Would he draw a line between explaining an average difference and justifying a social outcome, and if so how would he argue for that line? I am less interested in whether Mill would simply reject the modern evidence than in how his method of attack would work. Reason through what he would say, where his critique of appeals to nature would genuinely bite against the biological version, and where the biological version would force him to revise or weaken his own position. Finish by saying whether his anti-appeal-to-nature argument survives this modern case.
