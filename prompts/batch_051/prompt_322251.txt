I've been going back and forth with a colleague about data privacy, and she keeps reaching for the same analogy: personal data is property, full stop, the way your house or your wallet is. Her argument is that we'd never accept a company walking into our homes, photographing our medicine cabinets, and selling the inventory, so we shouldn't accept companies building profiles from our search histories, location traces, and purchase records either. From that she moves to a strong conclusion: because data about you is yours in the way your house is yours, you should have the whole bundle of property rights over it, the right to exclude, to consent, to sell, to demand it back. The analogy is doing real work there, and I'm not sure how far it can carry. I'd like you to audit it for me. Start by making the mapping explicit: what corresponds to what between the house/wallet side and the data-about-you side. Then look at where the mapping breaks. I want particular attention to the ways personal data isn't like a physical thing. It's non-rivalrous, lots of people can have it without depleting it. It's about you but often generated by someone else's observation or by an interaction. It's entangled with facts about other people. And the thing in question may be an inference a company drew rather than a record you ever possessed. Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion she wants to draw and which are just nitpicks. And if the property analogy limps badly, say whether there's a neighboring analogy, something like trust, trade secret, part of the person, a regulated public utility, that could do the work better, or whether the conclusion itself should be tempered. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just an email to me.
