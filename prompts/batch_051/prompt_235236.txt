A historian of science friend of mine and I keep having the same argument. She thinks the incommensurability thesis you find in Kuhn is poetry dressed up as epistemology: after a scientific revolution, the two camps mostly just disagree the ordinary way, and the interesting change is in vocabulary, instruments, and what problems get taken seriously. I dont think that does justice to the idea. It seems to me there's a stronger, literal claim underneath — that the old and new paradigms can't be compared by any shared standard because the new one doesnt even give the old one's central terms a translation, so the old theory is not refuted but rather left outside the conversation. I want to arbitrate this. If the strong version of incommensurability were treated as a testable claim, how would you test it? Give me one or two concrete tests using a real or realistic historical case (phlogiston-to-oxygen chemistry, Newtonian-to-relativistic physics, whatever works). For each test, say what observation would count as evidence that incommensurability is real and not just terminological drift, and what would count against it. Then tell me what a positive result would and would not settle — for instance, whether it would force relativism about scientific progress or whether it could just mean translation is hard but possible. When you answer, put your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning after; no preamble and dont restate my question.
