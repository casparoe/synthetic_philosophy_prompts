I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am eight weeks from delivering a manuscript to my editor. While revising, I have been reading Adorno and Horkheimer’s essay “The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception,” from their Dialectic of Enlightenment, and one passage will not leave me alone:

“If one branch of art follows the same formula as one with a very different medium and content; if the dramatic intrigue of broadcast soap operas becomes no more than useful material for showing how to master technical problems at both ends of the scale of musical experience – real jazz or a cheap imitation; or if a movement from a Beethoven symphony is crudely ‘adapted’ for a film sound-track in the same way as a Tolstoy novel is garbled in a film script: then the claim that this is done to satisfy the spontaneous wishes of the public is no more than hot air.”

I keep asking whether my novel does to its own material what the film soundtrack does to the Beethoven movement or the film script to the Tolstoy novel: borrowing an idiom that flattens the particular into an interchangeable formula, then defending the result by saying the story or the reader demanded it. I work alone, I have no philosophical training, and I need something I can use in a single pass through a draft without stopping to re-read theory each time. Please convert this passage into a reusable diagnostic procedure for a working novelist—a checklist or ordered set of tests that lets me inspect a scene, a plot mechanism, or a stretch of prose and decide whether it is reproducing the defect the passage describes. The procedure should test, among other things, when a device has become interchangeable with some other medium’s formula, when an apparent necessity is really standardization in disguise, and when the excuse “the story demanded it” or “readers expect it” is my version of the passage’s “spontaneous wishes of the public.” Be concrete about what to examine, in what order, what counts as passing or failing, and which of Adorno and Horkheimer’s distinctions must survive translation into practical use and which may be safely simplified. Work through two or three invented passages from novels, including at least one case in which the procedure tells me to cut something I am attached to. Mark the points where the procedure cannot settle the question and I must use judgment. Finally, say how confident you are in the claims of the procedure, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
