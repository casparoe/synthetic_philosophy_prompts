Here's the criterion I was taught, more or less the biological species concept: a species is a group of populations that actually or potentially interbreed with each other in the wild, and that are reproductively seperated from other such groups — meaning they don't interbreed with outsiders, or if they do, the offspring aren't viable or fertile. So two tests: interbreeding within, isolation without.

Now the case. Ring species are real (there's a textbook salamander example around California's Central Valley), but here's the abstract structure, which is what matters. A population spreads around a big inhospitable center, forming a ring. Call the populations around the ring A, B, C, D, E, F. Each adjacent pair (A with B, B with C, ... E with F) meets and interbreeds where their ranges overlap, with healthy hybrids; gene flow is more or less smooth between neighbors. But the two ends of the chain, A and F, meet on the far side and don't interbreed in the wild, and forced lab crosses give offspring that are weak or sterile. So A and F are reproductively isolated from each other, yet connected by a continuous chain of interbreeding populations.

So here's the snag. If "same species" means "actually or potentially interbreeds," then A and B are one species, B and C are one species, and so on around the ring, so by transitivity A and F should be one species. But by the isolation test, A and F are two species. The definition seems to say both that A = F and that A ≠ F, unless we give up on transitivity or draw an arbitrary line somewhere on the ring.
