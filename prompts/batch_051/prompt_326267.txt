I'm working on a novel where a Black painter in the 1920s is arguing with his mentor about whether making "beautiful" paintings is a cop-out, and I keep coming back to Du Bois's "Criteria of Negro Art." I dont trust my reading of this bit:

"You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America, there has come to us not only a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant but a vision of what the world could be if it were really a beautiful world; if we had the true spirit; if we had the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart; if we had, to be sure, not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting, all that -- but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life. It is that sort of a world we want to create for ourselves and for all America."

I get the surface: Du Bois is saying Black Americans have a special angle on beauty because of being shut out. But what bugs me is how this fits with his later insistence that art is propaganda. Is the propaganda a tool to clear space for this vision, or is the vision itself what the propaganda is supposed to produce? In other words is "beauty" here an end or a means? And does his later claim that all art is propaganda undermine or sharpen what he's doing in this paragraph?

Also, since this is for fiction, I care about the texture: what's the rhetorical work of the triad "the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart" here? Is it doing something specific or is it just pulpit prose? Be honest about how confident your reading is, and single out the place where you're guessing hardest.
