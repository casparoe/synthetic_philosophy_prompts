Two of my neighbors got into a long argument at a community meeting about a proposal to allow four-story apartment buildings on our block, which has been zoned for single-family homes for decades. I live on the street in question and stayed out of it, but I kept notes because I could not figure out who was right. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, say who is right about which specific point, and identify anything important both sides are missing.

(1) Elena, opposed: The neighborhood's character and stability are part of what people paid for when they bought homes here. Many of us made the biggest financial decision of our lives expecting the block to stay low-rise and quiet. Upzoning would undo those settled expectations and impose real losses — light, parking, noise, resale value — on existing residents, and it would do so without their consent.

(2) Ravi, in favor: But the current rules impose losses without consent too. For decades, people who would have lived here if prices and rents were lower have been kept out, and they never consented to a wall of single-family zoning. If consent is the test, the status quo and the proposal both fail it with respect to the people they harm, so an appeal to consent alone cannot break the tie.

(3) Elena: That treats exclusion as wrong by default, but no city can house everyone who would like to live in it, any more than a restaurant has to seat everyone who wants a table. The people who already live here — who maintain their homes, show up to these meetings, and raise their families on this block — have a stronger claim to shape the place than people who merely wish to move in. Arriving first is not nothing.

(4) Ravi: But a city is not a restaurant. A restaurant's owners can exclude whom they like because it is private property and diners are consuming a service. A city is a political body that makes law for everyone in its territory, including future residents, who have no vote on today's rules. And the fact that we were here first is a historical fact, not an entitlement to keep everyone else out forever — especially when the rules govern access to jobs, schools, and shelter.

(5) Elena: That is exactly my point about what a political body is. A city is made of the people who actually constitute it, and if the people who live here cannot control the character of their own neighborhood through local institutions, then local self-government is hollow. A right to the city has to include the right of existing residents to preserve what they value in it; otherwise participation is just the opportunity to lose politely.

(6) Ravi: Local self-government is real, but it is also the mechanism that produced exclusionary zoning in the first place. Community control of land use has often functioned as the power to keep out poorer households and, in some cities, to reinforce segregation. If there is no check on it, neighborhood character shades into a veto over other people's access to housing and opportunity. Current residents should have a say, but there has to be some limit, or local democracy becomes a machine for protecting incumbents at everyone else's expense.
