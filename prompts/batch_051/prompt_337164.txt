I've been going back and forth with a friend about the extended mind thesis — roughly, the idea that cognition sometimes literally extends beyond the skull and can include notebooks, phones, even other people, so that those things become parts of a person's mental processes rather than mere tools. My friend's line is that the thesis is unfalsifiable: if an external device helps someone remember, its defenders say 'see, it's part of the memory system'; if it doesn't help, they just say that particular device wasn't integrated. I want to know wether that's a fair charge, and what a discriminating test would even look like.

Suppose we're studying Sara, whose episodic memory is weak — she knows the gist of her life but not the details — and who has used the same structured voice-memo system for six years. She narrates everything into it, reviews it every night, and has offloaded nearly all her prospective memory (appointments, commitments, facts about people) to it. The claim on the table is: this voice-memo system is literally part of Sara's memory system, not just an external aid she uses. I don't think the obvious test — take the device away and see if she falls apart — is decisive, because a crutch shows the same pattern and nobody says a crutch is part of a person's walking.
