I'm trying to work out what I actually think about immigration, not from theory but from my gut on a bunch of concrete cases. The problem is my gut reactions don't obviously hang together, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. No citations or anything, just work from the cases.

1. A 29-year-old nurse from the Philippines has a job offer at a rural hospital, speaks good English, is healthy, and will pay taxes from her first paycheck. Verdict: admit, no hesitation.

2. A 68-year-old mother of a naturalized citizen wants to join her daughter. She has no savings and needs heart surgery, so she'd go straight onto the public health system, and the daughter can't support her. Verdict: admit. I'd feel like a monster if I refused, but I can hear the fiscal objection the whole time.

3. A 23-year-old from Guatemala with no special skills wants to come pick fruit. Over his lifetime he'll probably take slightly more out in benefits than he pays in, but the farm needs workers and he wants to work. Verdict: admit, though I can't defend it on the numbers.

4. A 40-year-old software engineer from India, no dependents, job offer in hand, will be a large net contributor. Verdict: obviously admit.

5. A refugee fleeing political persecution, no skills, may never become a net contributor, needs housing and healthcare immediately. Verdict: admit, and it would be obscene to ask about her fiscal profile before letting her in.

6. A woman fleeing gang violence in Honduras that the government is to weak to stop. She doesn't meet the narrow legal definition of a refugee. Verdict: I think she should be admitted anyway, but I'm less sure than in the last one.

7. A 75-year-old wealthy father of a citizen wants to move to be near his grandchildren. He'll buy private insurance and cost the state nothing. Verdict: admit, fine.

8. A 30-year-old with schizophrenia, well-managed on an expensive medication, has a job offer but will probably be a net fiscal cost for life. Verdict: I genuinely don't know; I lean no, and I hate that I lean no.

9. An undocumented worker who's been in the country 22 years, worked mostly on the books, has two citizen kids and a small business. Verdict: regularize him, obviously.

10. A temporary agricultural worker's visa expired and he wants to stay and bring his wife and kids. He's worked steadily and caused no trouble. Verdict: let him stay and bring them, but I can't explain why this is different from admitting a brand-new low-skilled worker. Maybe the fact that he's already built a life here shouldn't matter, but it feels like it does.

Cases 3 and 8 look like the same kind of case — a worker who'd be a net cost, just different sizes — and I treat them differently, so I'm drawing some fiscal line between them. But then I refuse to apply any fiscal line at all in case 5. That's the bit I can't square.

My working principle, as best I can state it, is: admit someone if (i) refusing to admit them would send them back into serious harm, so we'd be partly responsible for what happens, or (ii) they're the parent, spouse, or minor child of a citizen, or (iii) admitting them is at least neutral for the public purse on the best estimate. Clause (i) covers 5 and 6, clause (ii) covers 2 and 7, clause (iii) covers 1 and 4. Case 3 only gets in if I squint and count the farm's gain as a public benefit. Case 8 fails all three and I lean no, which fits. But case 9 fits none of them cleanly and I'm still completely sure about it, and case 10 is murky. I also can't tell whether the work in clause (i) is done by the seriousness of the harm or by our responsibility for it.

So: is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I can see which verdict each clause is doing work for? If no principle gets all ten, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and whether you'd overturn that verdict or keep it and complicate the principle. Then apply the resulting principle to the case I keep going back and forth on: a 38-year-old from a low-lying island whose home will be uninhabitable within ten years because of sea-level rise. He's healthy, has no job offer, and his government isn't persecuting him; he'd probably be a net cost for a while. My gut leans admit, but weaker than for the refugee.
