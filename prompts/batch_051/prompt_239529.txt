I edit the science and ideas section of a general-interest magazine, and every week I approve or rewrite sentences that attribute something to a category as a whole: mosquitoes carry malaria, ducks lay eggs, antidepressants cause weight gain, sharks attack bathers, red meat raises cancer risk. These are generics, not statistical claims. I know enough of the semantics to know they are not equivalent to claims about most members, and that they tolerate exceptions in ways ordinary quantified claims do not. The problem is that readers do not know this: they convert a sentence about the kind into an inference about a random individual, or into a prevalence estimate, before we can stop them. The corrections inbox, and one or two health-scare episodes, have made the stakes concrete. At the same time, hedging every such sentence gives us cautious, cramped science writing.

I want a decision procedure for deciding, for any proposed generic in a story, whether it should run unqualified, run with a specified hedge, or be cut. The procedure has to be usable by copy editors with no training in semantics and about ten minutes per piece, and it should be clean enough to go into the style guide this quarter. Ground it in what the semantics and philosophy of language actually say about generics, but translate that into questions to ask in order, with what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure returns each verdict, and where it must stop and hand the decision to me because the question is one of editorial values or predicted reader harm rather than linguistic fact. Be explicit about which theoretical distinctions have to survive the translation — for example, between a generic being true and the individual-level inference a reader will draw, between prevalence and a property being characteristic or explanatory of the kind, and between striking or dangerous properties and banal ones — and which distinctions can be dropped for newsroom use. State the known ways the procedure will misfire and what it cannot do.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a vector-disease piece containing the sentence that mosquitoes carry malaria, a children's science explainer containing the sentence that ducks lay eggs, and a summer safety feature containing the sentence that sharks attack bathers. Make one of the three a case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like, and say which one and why I will resist it.
