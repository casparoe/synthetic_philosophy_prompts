I have recently had reason to mistrust a conviction I had long held about my own mind. For the past year, whenever I made a consequential decision, I wrote down the reasons I would give for it. Reading these records months later, I found that my later reconstructions of why I had done something often did not match what I had recorded at the time; under questioning from a colleague, I also produced plausible-sounding explanations that, on reflection, accounted for the facts but could just as well have been inventions. This has unsettled my confidence in the familiar claim that a person has privileged access to the causes of their own judgments and choices. I have also come across descriptions of experiments in which people gave fluent reasons for a choice that had been swapped without their noticing, and in which small changes in the order of presentation reshaped choices while the choosers explained their decisions in terms of allegedly deeper considerations. Those findings suggest a rival view: that reports of why we believe, choose, or feel are often assembled after the fact from expectations, narrative pressure, and observed behavior, and that first-person access to the causes of our states is limited or illusory.

I want to know whether this dispute can be brought under experimental control, and if so, how far that control extends. Design a concrete experiment that would discriminate between two claims: (A) healthy adults do have reliable introspective access to at least some of the causes of their judgments and choices, and (B) such reports are predominantly post-hoc constructions. Specify the manipulation, the outcome that would count as evidence for (A), the outcome that would count as evidence for (B), and the control conditions needed before one should call a self-report confabulated rather than merely incomplete or mistaken. Then say what a clean positive result for (B) would show and what it would not show: whether any residue of privileged access survives such a result, and whether the conclusion should be that introspection is unreliable in general or only that it fails under identifiable conditions that a careful account should delimit.
