I'm a PhD student in development economics, and I've been going around in circles on something a friend of mine walked me into, hoping you can help me find the exit. The line I keep hearing is that Malthus was right, just early. I decided to actually reread the Essay rather than trust the usual summary, and now I think the usual summary is hiding the whole problem.

The passage that stopped me is in the preface to the first edition of the Essay on the Principle of Population (1798). Malthus writes: "It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects, has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means, which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society."

My friend took that passage and built this argument out of it.

(1) Malthus himself distinguishes a trivial claim from his real contribution. The trivial claim is that population must always be kept down to the level of subsistence; the contribution is the account of the means by which that level is effected.

(2) In the 1798 account the means are the checks on population, and he winds up saying the checks all come down to misery and vice.

(3) The modern "right but early" reading says he was wrong only about the timing and the technology: agricultural productivity rose, and as places got rich, fertility fell on its own. So the check that actually operated was prosperity and deliberate restraint, not famine and epidemic.

(4) If (3) is right, then what survived in Malthus is exactly the trivial obvious-truth part from (1), while the contribution he actually flagged — the identification of the means as misery and vice — was false.

(5) But the trivial claim is too weak to do any predictive work. Whatever actually happened must, by definition, have kept population and subsistence in line through some means or other. So any failed long-run prediction can be rescued this way: the predictor correctly said some mechanism would enforce the balance, and merely got the mechanism and the schedule wrong. Astrology gets the same defense: the stars did incline, but countervailing causes you didn't list delayed the effect.

(6) So either Malthus was not right but early, just false about his substantive claim, or "right but early" is vacuous enough to vindicate every failed prophecy.

He says this shows the phrase "right but early" has no content. I don't want to believe that. I think Malthus was pointing at something real — a genuine tendency that can be swamped for a while and show up later under different conditions — and I think some long-run theories really do deserve a non-vacuous "right but early" verdict. But I can't see which step to reject without losing one of those beliefs. I keep suspecting (5), but when I try to say why it's wrong I end up just argueing in a circle, insisting that Malthus isn't astrology without being able to say what the difference is.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please just write continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email.
