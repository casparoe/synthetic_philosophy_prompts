I'm an engineer on a platform team at a company with a large, decade-old production service, and in a few weeks I'm giving a short talk to our engineering organization arguing against the current cleanup drive. I have no formal philosophy background; I found the idea of Chesterton's fence through engineering writing about legacy systems, and I want the talk to stay in that register. The thesis I want to defend is this: when a constraint in the system — a code path, a config flag, a manual step in a runbook, a code-review rule — has no remaining owner and no remembered rationale, our default should be to preserve it until we can demonstrate what removing it would actually change, rather than to delete it on the basis that nobody currently understands why it exists. I'm borrowing Chesterton's fence roughly as engineers usually state it: do not tear down a fence until you know why it was put up.

The audience is mostly staff engineers and SREs from our reliability and developer-productivity groups. They run a recurring program that reviews unowned code and processes for removal, and they value deletion as a way to cut cognitive load and operational toil. Several of them have argued that unknown-purpose code is the worst kind of code, that "nobody remembers why this exists" is usually decisive evidence that the thing is dead weight, and that the instinct to preserve is mostly status-quo bias and fear of breaking things. They trust evidence from production and clean postmortems more than they trust institutional memory.

My rough argument is that the absence of a remembered reason is weak evidence in a system this old, because knowledge of purposes decays much faster than operational relevance; that deletion is asymmetric, since tearing out a load-bearing safeguard can fail loudly months later, far from the decision and the person who made it; that the first move should usually be observation — flagging, shadowing, canarying, tracing which downstream behavior actually depends on the thing — rather than removal; and that every act of preservation must be paired with a defined test and an expiry date so the principle does not become a reason to hoard everything forever.

Make me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections this specific audience will raise, not the ones from general philosophy of conservatism; order them by how likely they are and how much damage they would do; phrase each roughly the way a staff engineer or incident commander would put it; and give me a two-minute reply for each with a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede up front and what to hold. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if it comes anyway.
