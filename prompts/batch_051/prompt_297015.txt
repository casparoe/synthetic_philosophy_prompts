I keep going back and forth on what actually separates defunding from abolition, and I want to stress-test a definition. By my lights, a policy counts as abolition only if all four of these hold: (1) the standing agency with a general mandate to use coercive force is dismantled, not merely shrunk; (2) most of the jobs cops did get moved to institutions that aren't built around coercion or punishment; (3) the default response to ordinary harms stops being arrest-and-investigate and becomes something non-punitive; (4) whatever capacity to use force the city keeps is narrow, reactive, and not the everyday mechanism for keeping order.

Now here's a case that's giving me trouble. A city votes to wipe out its police department's patrol and detective divisions entirely. Traffic enforcement, mental-health crisis calls, noise complaints, and school discipline go to unarmed civilian departments; most misdemeanors and a decent chunk of felonies get diverted to neighborhood restorative-justice panels; and the city slashes the old police budget by about 70 percent. But it keeps a small unit, maybe 25 people, called the Public Safety Emergency Service, armed and legally allowed to use force, which does not patrol, does not investigate, and is only dispatched to active shootings, armed hostage situations, and violent assaults in progress.

Under my four criteria, is that abolition, defunding, or reform? Go through criterion by criterion and flag the places where the criteria don't force a verdict. Then tell me: should the existence of that residual armed unit, even if it never patrols, disqualify the whole thing from counting as abolition, and does the case show that one of my criteria is carrying too much weight or too little?
