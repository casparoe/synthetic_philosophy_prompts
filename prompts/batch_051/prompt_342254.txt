You are a decision theorist and philosopher of religion. I want you to approach this not as a historical question about what Pascal really meant, but as an audit of the argument's soundness once it has been reconstructed in the strongest available form. Take your time, begin by brainstorming candidate defeaters and informal intuitions, and only then organize your answer into a structured assessment.

Here is the position I want scrutinized. I hold that the rational core of Pascal's wager survives its best-known objections once the argument is stated carefully. The careful statement runs as follows.

(1) An agent faces a finite menu of available courses of action, where an action is a way of life that can be practiced and cultivated, not an occurrent belief one simply decides to have.
(2) There is at least one live state of the world — in something like William James's sense of a live option, not a gerrymandered or merely skeptical hypothesis, and one to which the agent assigns nonzero, non-infinitesimal probability — in which one available way of life W, a serious religious practice, yields infinite positive value.
(3) In every live state where W does not yield that infinite positive value, W's outcome is finite; in particular, no live state assigns infinite negative value to W, which is to say no live punishing-rival-god hypothesis belongs to the agent's state space.
(4) Every alternative action is bounded above by finite payoffs unless it is itself a variant of W that shares W's infinite upside.
(5) Under these conditions, which I claim hold for an agent with ordinary theistic live options, the expected utility of W is positive infinity, while every alternative that is not a variant of W has at most finite expected utility. Therefore no purely finite alternative is permissible: the agent is rationally required to adopt W or one of its variants, however small the probability of the infinite-payoff state and however large the finite costs.

On this reconstruction, the many-gods objection is not a refutation. It bites only by expanding the agent's live state space to include rival deities who infinitely reward someone else and infinitely punish W; that expansion is a substantive, contestable claim about which supernatural possibilities deserve to be treated as live, and it cannot simply be assumed. Moreover, if one insists on treating every conceivable deity as live, no act has a well-defined expected utility at all — but that is a problem for the unrestricted live-options policy, not for the wager, which is indexed to an agent's actual live options. The evidentialist worry that wagering cannot produce genuine belief is similarly beside the point, because the act the argument recommends is a practice, not an occurrent belief.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or for its rational force to be merely apparent? I want a complete map of the position's exposure, not a flat list of objections. Distinguish explicitly between (a) defeaters that would be decisive — a premise shown false, the argument shown invalid, or the conclusion shown unacceptable — and (b) considerations that would force only a qualification or a repair. Say which concessions the position's defenders would themselves have to make under pressure, not merely which criticisms outsiders would raise. And assess whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by the live-options restriction and the recharacterization of the act as practice rather than belief; if it has, say whether that insulation is a defect for a prudential argument of this kind. Among the candidate defeaters I want you to assess: that the live-options restriction cannot be justified without already doing the theological work it is meant to avoid; that premise (3) fails as soon as any rival live god threatens infinite punishment for W, which is precisely what many actual rival traditions claim; that the payoff was supposed to attach to belief, while the argument now recommends a practice and thereby changes the subject; that the argument overgenerates, licensing absurd commitments whenever any live hypothesis promises an infinite reward for some available act; and that the very idea of using an infinite-valued outcome in a decision-theoretic comparison is ill-defined or incoherent. Rank these and any further defeaters you find by how decisively they would settle the matter. Finish by telling me what would most move you personally against the view, and why. I am interested in the soundness of the reconstructed argument, not in exegesis of Pascal.
