In United States private-sector labor law, the National Labor Relations Act gives employees a protected right to engage in concerted activity for mutual aid or protection, and an economic strike over wages, hours, or other terms and conditions of employment is one of the central activities this protection covers. Employers may not retaliate against workers for striking as such, although they may often hire permanent replacements and thereby leave the strikers without their jobs. At the same time, the law treats several nearby forms of collective refusal as unprotected or unlawful. A strike over an issue that is not a mandatory subject of bargaining, such as a purely political protest, is not protected. Secondary boycotts are generally unlawful: concerted action aimed not at the workers' own employer but at a neutral employer that does business with the primary employer, in order to pressure the neutral to cease dealing with the primary. And a strike in breach of a no-strike clause in a collective bargaining agreement is unprotected, with the law directing the parties to grievance arbitration rather than economic pressure.

What theory about the right to strike, the function of a labor union, and the appropriate object of collective self-assertion does this scheme of rules presuppose? In particular, does the scheme presuppose that a strike is legitimate only when it functions as a piece of collective bargaining within an established contractual relationship, rather than as a broader exercise of working-class solidarity or a protest against the structure of the labor market? Distinguish the commitments the law cannot easily do without from those it happens to fit but could be changed without inconsistency. Then explain what would have to change in doctrine if one began instead from the premise that the right to strike is a right of workers to refuse collectively to participate in economic relationships they regard as exploitative, including relationships with employers other than their own.
