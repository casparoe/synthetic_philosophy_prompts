Two friends of mine got into an argument at dinner last week and I've been turning it over since, because I honestly can't tell which one of them is right. It started when Dana proposed a thought experiment. Suppose you are told, on totally reliable authority, that exactly three months after you die, an asteroid will wipe out every remaining human being. Nothing changes before then: your family and friends all live out their normal lives, your own projects go just as they would have, and you die exactly when you would have died anyway. The only difference is that no human beings come after. Dana said this news would make many of her current activities feel pointless. Her example was a novel she is writing that she probably won't finish in her lifetime. She said she has never thought of herself as writing it "for posterity" in some grand way, but on reflection the whole point seems to depend on there being people after her who might read it. So she drew a bigger conclusion: human extinction is bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths of all the particular people who die, because it retroactively drains the meaning of lives that are otherwise unaffected. It would be bad, she said, even in a version where every individual death is painless and unmourned.

Marcus said her reaction is explainable by something much more ordinary. In the asteroid case, the people who die after her include her children, her nieces, her students, everyone she actually loves who would otherwise outlive her. So of course the unfinished novel feels pointless: her concern for those specific people is doing all the work. There's no need for any abstract value attached to the continuation of the species. Dana said fine, change the case: suppose the asteroid misses everyone she personally cares about because they all happen to die in a seperate accident on the same day she dies. Then the posthumous catastrophe only affects strangers and their children. She claimed the hollow feeling doesn't disappear—if anything, that version is cleaner, because it shows that many of her ordinary goals only make sense against an implicit background assumption that the human enterprise will continue. Marcus replied that this still isn't a species-level afterlife doing the work; it's just that lots of our goals essentially refer to other people and future people, like teaching, building institutions, doing science, raising children. Those are ordinary interpersonal goals, and the fact that extinction would frustrate them doesn't show something extra.

Dana said that's precisely the point: those ordinary goals rationally commit you to caring about the continuation of humanity as such, so extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves. Marcus said even if her intuition survives the modified case, at most she has discovered a brute fact about her own motivation—probably a side effect of how evolution wired us—and a brute preference for there to be more humans doesn't make extinction objectively bad. Dana's comeback was to ask whether he would still send a finished manuscript to a publisher, or plant an oak, or start a long research project, if he knew for certain that no one would be alive after him. Marcus admitted he probably wouldn't, but said that only shows he has the same posthumous vanity she does, which is a fact about their psychology, not an argument.

They never resolved it. Could you walk me through their exchange and tell me who is right about which specific point, where each person's reasoning goes wrong, and what neither of them considered?
