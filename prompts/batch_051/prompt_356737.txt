Write a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on how institutions should respond to Goodhart's law and specification gaming: the tendency of an optimized proxy measure to stop tracking the true target it was meant to stand in for. The tree is for a graduate seminar on "Measurement, Incentives, and Institutional Design," and it should sort seminar members by what they believe about the source and remedy of metric failure, not by which labels they recognize. Aim for four to six leaves.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of position names. First settle the branching questions. Each node should be a concrete case or a forced-choice claim a participant can answer without knowing which position it leads to—for example: a hospital whose 30-day survival incentive causes surgeons to turn away high-risk patients; a school that responds to standardized-test targets by narrowing its curriculum; a spam classifier whose precision metric is evaded once spammers optimize against it; a fairness metric that compresses several distinct concerns into a single number. Possible diagnostic questions include: when a measure is gamed, does the failure lie primarily in the metric, in the incentive surrounding it, in the agents, or in the very idea of a single quantitative target? And: is the correct response to such failure to repair, to diversify, to police, or to retreat from measurement?

Once the questions and their answer combinations are fixed, locate the actual positions within the space they define. I expect at least these leaves: a revisionist view (keep correcting and respecifying the proxy so it tracks the target), a pluralist view (maintain several uncorrelated measures and prevent any one from dominating), an adversarial or regulatory view (treat gaming as permanent and meet it with audits, randomization, and game-proofing rather than with better measures), and a judgment-based retreat (abandon fixed quantitative targets in favor of context-sensitive human judgment). Add or rename leaves only if the space demands it. For each combination of answers that no live position occupies, say briefly whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate with binary questions, and say what further case would separate them, if any would.

Finally, state how confident you are in the overall structure and in each leaf's placement, and single out the specific node, branch, or unoccupied cell you are least sure of, with a sentence about why.
