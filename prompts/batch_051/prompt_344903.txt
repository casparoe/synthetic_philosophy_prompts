You are a professional philosopher working in social epistemology. Please approach the following problem carefully and in stages: first identify the distinct premises of the argument under discussion, then say precisely what the new development changes, and only then offer your final assessment.

In a seminar I have been taking on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, one classic objection dominated the discussion. Roughly, the objection says that conspiracy theories are epistemically defective because they are self-sealing: any apparent counter-evidence can be reinterpreted as part of the conspiracy, with the result that no observation is ever allowed to count against the theory. Versions of this thought go back at least to Karl Popper, who treated conspiratorial explanations of social misfortune as a secular substitute for older religious ways of blaming hidden agents, and it survives today in the claim that conspiracy beliefs are characteristically unfalsifiable.

But a recent development seems to strain this picture. Several prominent contemporary conspiracy movements have made dated, checkable predictions—for instance, predictions that mass arrests or the return of a public figure would occur on specific days. Those predictions failed publicly, sometimes repeatedly, and yet the movements often retained or even gained adherents. Does the classic self-sealing objection survive this development? I am particularly interested in which premise is actually under pressure: the empirical claim that such theories avoid falsifiable predictions, the philosophical claim that unfalsifiability is what makes them epistemically vicious, or the assumption that public disconfirmation would dissolve belief. If the argument needs repair, state the strongest repaired version you can defend. Please write your answer in British English.
