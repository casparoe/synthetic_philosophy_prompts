Background: in complex systems theory there's this notion of computational irreducibility. A system is computationally irreducible when the only way to find out what it will do is to simulate it step by step — there's no shortcut, no formula that skips ahead, no faster computation that gives the answer. You just have to run the thing. The standard example is a cellular automaton like rule 30: the rule is one little line, but it's output looks random, and nobody can predict what it'll do on step n without running it n steps.

Now the argument:

(1) To explain why a system behaves the way it does, you need a description of the behavior that is shorter than the behavior itself — a law, a formula, a compressed pattern. A "description" that is exactly as long as the behavior isn't an explanation; it's just the behavior again.

(2) If a system is computationally irreducible, then there is no shorter description that yields its later states. Anything that actually generates the behavior is at least as long as the full evolution, since the evolution is its own shortest description.

(3) So for any computationally irreducible system, there is no explanation of its behavior — not merely in practice but in principle. The thing is, by its nature, inexplicable.

(4) A lot of what working scientists spend their time explaining is computationally irreducible in just this sense: turbulence in fluids, weather, ecosystems, the immune system, maybe brains. These are giant nets of local interactions, which is exactly why we simulate them rather than solve them analytically.

(5) So a large chunk of successful science is in the business of explaining systems that, according to (3), are in principle inexplicable.

(6) That's absurd: science plainly does explain much of the behavior of these systems, even if it can't predict every detail.

So one of (1)–(4), or the flight from them to (5), has to be bad. But each of them sounds right on its own. Part of me wants to keep the compression picture of explanation in (1), because without it "explanation" collapses into just rerunning the simulation; part of me wants to keep (4), because otherwise I'd have to say most of science isn't really explaining anything. I can't tell whether the mistake is in the theory of explanation, in the definition of irreducibility, or in my claim about the sciences. Which premise do you reject, or which step is the bad one, and what is the cost of rejecting it?
