A colleague recently forwarded me an essay by a policy analyst about the role of climate models in long-term planning, and I want to know how fair its account of climate modeling actually is. The essay's central summary runs like this:

"Climate models earn our trust by reproducing the observed record: if a model can retrodict twentieth-century warming, we treat it as reliable. The models are built independently at different research centers, so when many of them agree on a future change — faster warming in the Arctic, a more intense hydrological cycle, longer and hotter heat waves — that agreement counts as a robust, near-certain result. The spread across an ensemble is effectively our uncertainty map: where models agree tightly, uncertainty is low; where they diverge, uncertainty is high. For planning purposes, the ensemble mean is the best single projection we have. The real limitation is that models still disagree at the regional and local scales where adaptation decisions are made — regional rainfall, drought risk, the timing of the monsoon — and at those scales they are not decision-relevant. Adaptation planning will simply have to wait for a better generation of models."

I do not have the technical background to tell where this goes wrong or whether it goes wrong at all. Go through it nearly sentence by sentence: which parts would a climate scientist accept, which are subtle overstatements or conflations of distinct things (for example, ensemble spread, structural uncertainty, scenario uncertainty, and internal variability), and which are misleading without being flatly false. Then evaluate the conclusion that models are not decision-relevant at the scales where adaptation is planned, and sketch what a more accurate account of decision-relevant uncertainty from ensembles would say instead. Finally, tell me how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
