After retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I keep noticing that I apply a rule I carried out of the laboratory; I would like to know whether it is defensible or merely a professional deformation. The rule concerns how much epistemic weight to give to thought experiments. I trust a thought experiment when it functions as a no-go proof: when it shows that a stated mechanism or position is internally inconsistent, violates a conservation law or an invariance principle, or terminates in contradiction. In such cases the argument, as I see it, never has to step outside the assumptions already on the table. But I refuse to let a thought experiment underwrite a positive synthetic conclusion—that some disputed entity exists, that a process is physically possible, that a certain sort of mental state could be realized in a different substrate. I treat those positive uses as intuition pumps: they make a possibility vivid, but vividness is not evidence, and in my experience the more vivid the example, the more unexamined empirical content it carries.

A philosopher I have been talking to says the distinction will not hold. A reductio, she points out, is just a positive argument for the negation of the target thesis, so the negative cases cannot be playing by different epistemic rules; and she reminds me that some of what physics now takes for granted first became thinkable through thought experiments that reached positive rather than impossible-to-be-otherwise conclusions. I see the force of that. Still, I suspect the negative uses are special because they are answerable only to logic, arithmetic, and the small set of symmetries I actually trust, while the positive uses quietly import empirical intuitions and then treat their salience as justification.

What I want is not a verdict on any particular historical example but an appraisal of the policy itself. Is this epistemic habit defensible, and under what conditions does it succeed or fail? Could the rule be restated so as to keep its resistance to intuition laundering—to being moved by the liveliness of an example rather than the strength of the argument—without ruling out the positive uses that seem to have done genuine work? Or should a person in my position abandon the rule and evaluate each thought experiment on its merits, and if so, what exactly would I be giving up?
