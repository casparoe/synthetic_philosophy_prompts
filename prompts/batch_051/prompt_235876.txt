I am a retired chemist who came to philosophy only recently. In the course of my reading on obedience and authority — I have been working through Milgram, Arendt, and some discussion of the military obligation to obey lawful orders — I have found myself endorsing five positions that I should like to hold together, and I want an audit of their joint tenability before I commit to them. I am not asking for a reading list or for the empirical details of the experiments and trials; I want the positions themselves examined for how they support, require, or undermine each other.

The positions are these.

1. Deference to expertise. On factual or technical questions, and on many practical decisions within an expert domain, a non-expert should defer to the relevant professional consensus or to the guidance of a trained practitioner. A layperson who attempts to overturn such a consensus through private study is usually not autonomous; they are epistemically reckless.

2. The Milgram lesson. The obedience experiments show that ordinary, morally decent people can be brought to administer what they take to be serious harm when a legitimate-seeming scientific authority accepts responsibility for what is being done. The subjects did not become cruel; they came to experience themselves as instruments of the experimenter's will. The disposition to hand moral agency upward is a normal feature of human psychology, not a rare defect.

3. The Eichmann lesson. Arendt seems to me right to locate Eichmann's guilt in something close to thoughtlessness: he allowed the legal-bureaucratic order to do his moral thinking for him. When an institution is engaged in criminal activity, occupying an official role, following formally valid rules, or obeying a superior cannot dissolve individual responsibility. There are moral judgments that cannot be delegated upward.

4. Military deference. In a reasonably just state, a soldier may rightly obey a lawful order even when he privately regards it as unwise. Discipline requires that subordinates not substitute their private judgment for command authority within the zone of legality, and the standard distinction between lawful and unlawful orders is, in outline, the correct one.

5. Pessimism about conscience. Yet ordinary people, including conscientious functionaries, are unreliable at recognizing from inside an institution when its demands have become unlawful or immoral. Most of us, placed where Eichmann was placed, would probably have done no better. Institutional protections — transparency, divided authority, legal review, professional norms — are therefore better safeguards against institutional wrongdoing than the private conscience of any individual agent.

I do not know where, or whether, these commitments come apart, and I am no more attached to one of them than to another. Please distinguish the pairs that are genuinely in tension from the pairs that only appear to be, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be given up and what the least costly revision would be. I would also like to know which apparent conflict should not trouble me, and why.
