I've been running a postgraduate seminar on virtue ethics, and for the past two weeks we have been working through the situationist critique: the argument that the experimental record on helping behaviour, mood effects, and obedience shows that people do not possess the robust cross-situational character traits that virtue ethics seems to presuppose. The class has found the usual "does the critique succeed?" framing rather tired, so I want to run an exercise that evaluates the individual defensive moves within virtue ethics rather than the debate as a block.

I have settled on five responses a virtue ethicist could give to the situationist data. Please rank these five responses from most resilient to least resilient. Judge resilience by how well each move preserves the explanatory and normative core of virtue ethics while conceding the empirical findings, rather than merely by whether the move can be made internally consistent. The five responses are:

1. Virtues are rare and unevenly distributed; the experiments reveal what most people are like, but genuinely virtuous agents are a minority, so the data leave exemplary virtue untouched.
2. Virtues are local, fine-grained dispositions; honesty in examinations and honesty about found wallets are different traits, so cross-situational consistency was never the correct test.
3. Virtues are regulative ideals rather than empirical descriptions of actual behaviour, so failures to act well tell us nothing against their normative standing.
4. The situationist data actually vindicate the virtue-ethical emphasis on practical wisdom, because the central moral task is precisely to discern which features of a situation are ethically salient and to respond to them.
5. Virtues are operative only in virtue-relevant situations, and many of the classic experiments involve unfamiliar, misleading, or morally degraded settings in which even a genuinely virtuous person would lack the conditions for exercising virtue.

Please structure your answer as a numbered list of exactly five entries, one per response above, ordered from most resilient to least resilient. Each entry should be one or two sentences explaining that placement, and should also flag where the response risks saving virtue ethics only by surrendering something central, such as empirical realism, predictive power, or the claim that virtue is widely attainable. Do not include an introduction or a conclusion.
