I work as a software engineer on machine-learning systems, and I have been asked to give a one-hour session to my team of ML engineers and data scientists on the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument for innate constraints on language acquisition. Most of them know the argument only as the slogan that children do not hear enough language to learn grammar, and several assume that the success of large language models has already settled the matter against it. I need a teaching plan for the session, not an explanation for me. Please specify the following. (1) The order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what: in particular, where the argument's logical structure has to be on the board before any child-language example can carry weight, and where in the sequence to meet the LLM objection directly rather than let it sit in the room. (2) The single carrying example and what it must have and must not have. I have seen the yes/no-question case involving a sentence like "the man who is tall is happy," but I want to know whether that is the right example, and whether it risks making the audience think the point is about explicit grammar instruction. (3) The misconceptions the team will predictably bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. I would guess at least these: "poverty means the dataset is too small, and LLMs show that is not fatal"; "innate knowledge means nothing is learned"; and "the argument reduces to the trivial point that finite data underdetermine any theory." (4) One question I can ask at the end to tell whether they have the argument itself rather than a caricature of it. (5) What I should explicitly decline to cover at this level and say so, so the session does not balloon. Assume no one in the room has a linguistics or philosophy background, and do not organize the plan around readings or citations.
