I've sort of landed myself in it: I run a small reading group that meets in a pub, and last week we got into a proper argument about whether a shuffled playlist is "really random". This week I'm meant to run the session on algorithmic randomness, and I don't want to just read definitions at people. So I want a sorting quiz for the group: a "which position on randomness do you actually hold?" handout where the sorting happens by how people answer questions about concrete strings and setups, not by which label they like.

The question the quiz should sort people on is: can a finite sequence be random? Aim for eight questions, each presenting a concrete case. At minimum use these cases (they're mandatory):

- the string 0101010101... repeated twenty times;
- the first forty digits of pi's decimal expansion (you can just show 3.14159... and not worry about printing all forty);
- a person who shuffles a fresh deck and gets the exact order a new deck comes in;
- twenty flips of a coin, all heads;
- a lottery draw whose winning numbers are exactly 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 out of fifty balls;
- a string that is incompressible in the algorithmic sense (no shorter computer program could output it) but was produced by a setup everyone knows to be strongly biased.

That's six; use the remaining questions for whatever splits the positions best. Each question should give two or three answer options in plain words, and the questions must be about the cases, not about the labels.

Then give a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Build the thing from the logical space, not from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. These positions have to show up as leaves: (a) randomness is a property of the generating process or chance setup, not of the sequence itself; (b) randomness is algorithmic incompressibility; (c) randomness is a statistical property relative to a null model or test suite; (d) randomness is subjective or epistemic, something about the observer's information; (e) no finite sequence is random, though an infinite sequence might be. Note any combinations of answers that have no occupant, and say briefly whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly seperate — and if you end up needing an extra question about an infinite sequence to tell (a) and (e) apart, add it and say why. If the incompressibility leaf runs into trouble for very short strings, don't paper over it; say a sentence about it.

Please lay it out in these sections, in this order, roughly:

- Intro to the quiz (100 words)
- Diagnostic questions and answer options (500 words)
- Scoring key (200 words)
- The positions as leaves (300 words)
- Empty cells in the tree (150 words)
- Pairs the tree can't separate (150 words)

And do the whole thing in British English.
