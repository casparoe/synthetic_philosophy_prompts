A sanction theorist of oaths and a speech-act theorist of oaths have spent a year disputing why oaths sworn on the river Styx are binding even on the gods. In the background picture they share, swearing by the Styx is the strongest commitment a god can make: a false Styx-oath is followed by a severe penalty that no god can evade or overturn, and even the great gods do not swear by it lightly. The sanction theorist maintains that the oath's binding force is nothing over and above the expected cost of perjury under a known, unavoidable sanction; the god who keeps the oath is, in effect, a rational agent who will not stake what she cannot afford to lose. The speech-act theorist maintains that invoking the Styx is a solemn conventional act, one that constitutes a new normative status in the divine order; the penalty is the ritual's way of marking that status as supreme, not the source of the obligation, and an unenforced Styx-oath would still bind because it defines the perjury it penalizes. Write the joint statement their good-faith adversarial collaboration should have produced. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, including claims about what the oath does, what the penalty contributes, and what each theory is not committed to; (2) a section stating where exactly they part ways, each camp's view formulated in wording that camp would accept; and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a concrete case both sides agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you judge that part of the dispute is only about words, say which part and why.
