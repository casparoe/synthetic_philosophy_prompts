A friend showed me a "self-undermining" version of the doomsday argument and I can't figure out where the gears slip. I'll transcribe it the way he wrote it on the whiteboard.

(1) For simplicity say there are only two possibilities. DOOM: the total number of humans who will ever be born is about 200 billion. FUTURE: it's about 200 trillion. Start 50/50.

(2) You observe that you're roughly the 100-billionth human to be born. If FUTURE is true, the chance that a randomly selected human is among the first 100 billion ever born is 100 billion / 200 trillion = 0.0005. If DOOM is true, that chance is 100 billion / 200 billion = 0.5. So your birth position is 1,000 times more likely under DOOM. That's the classic doomsday argument.

(3) But now run the same reasoning backwards. If DOOM is true, there are only 200 billion possible "slots" one of which is you. If FUTURE is true, there are 200 trillion. So the mere fact that you exist is 1,000 times more likely under FUTURE, because more total humans means more chances for the specific individual that is you.

(4) So the doomsday argument contains two updates of equal strength pointing in opposite directions, which should cancel exactly and leave you back at 50/50. But the argument was supposed to deliver a massive update toward DOOM. So either the doomsday argument refutes itself, or one of these updates isn't doing what it claims.

I want to say the two passes aren't symmetric — that walking into a movie at minute 100 does tell me something about how long the movie is, and that isn't canceled by the fact that a three-hour movie has more minutes I could have walked in on. But I cant translate that hunch into which numbered step to reject, and I don't know what rejecting it costs for the standard argument.

Which step do you reject? Explain why it looked harmless and what the standard doomsday argument has to pay.
