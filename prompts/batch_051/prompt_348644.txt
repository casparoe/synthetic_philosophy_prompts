My friend and I just spent an hour going back and forth about the old centipede fable, and I can't get it out of my head. You know the one: a centipede walks perfectly well until it is asked how it organises all those legs, and then it suddenly can't do it anymore. She took the fable to show that thinking about your own performance is always a risk, and I kept saying it only bites once the skill is fully automatic. Then I thought of a variant that neither of us knew how to handle.

Instead of asking the centipede a one-off question, we train an adult, fully competent centipede with a cue. Every time the cue comes, the centipede has to predict wich leg it is about to move next, and if it predicts correctly it gets a small bit of food. After each block of predictions, the centipede has to walk a straight stretch on its own and we time it. So the centipede is not just moving; it is making explicit reports about its own movement, a kind of metacognition, and it is being rewarded for getting those reports right.

Here is what I want worked out. The two standard accounts of the original fable seem to pull in the same direction at first. The explicit-monitoring account says that conscious attention to the step-by-step parts of an automatic skill breaks the skill down, so forcing predictions about leg order should make the centipede walk worse. The distraction account says any extra task, whatever its content, eats into capacity the centipede needs for walking, so that account also predicts the walking will get worse. But there is a third line: accurate self-monitoring, especially with feedback, might let the centipede build a more reliable representation of its own gait, and once the prediction task itself becomes automatic the centipede might end up walking better than before. Do these three lines actually make different predictions in this setup, and if so, how would you separate them? Under what versions of the task — no reward, predictions about a different centipede's legs, a juvenile still learning to walk, or timing the centipede while it predicts rather than afterwards — would they part ways again? And does the original moral of the fable survive this variant, or does it need to be qualified?

Could you write your answer in British English?
