I'm trying to get my views on money, liquidity, and wealth into a defensible form, and I'd like an audit of whether they hang together before I commit to them. Here is where I've landed.

1. A nation's real wealth consists in its productive resources — physical capital, land, skills, and knowledge — and the stream of goods and services those resources can produce. The stock of money is not part of that real wealth; if everyone's cash balances doubled overnight, the country would not be twice as rich.

2. But I also think liquidity is a real economic service. An otherwise identical portfolio that can be sold quickly without moving the price is more useful to its owner than an illiquid one, because it creates options: meeting sudden expenses, taking advantage of opportunities, avoiding fire sales. Creating liquidity can therefore improve welfare even when no new machine or building is produced.

3. I think it follows that, if monetary policy makes existing assets more liquid — easier to buy and sell without a large price concession — that is a real improvement, not merely a nominal one. And to the extent an expansion of money raises asset prices by improving market liquidity, the higher prices partly reflect something real rather than pure inflation of claims.

4. At the same time, I believe that when monetary expansion raises equity and housing prices chiefly by lowering discount rates and loosening financial conditions, the resulting increase in measured private net worth is only a revaluation of claims on the same underlying assets. The same buildings and machines exist; the country is not richer in any deep sense.

5. In a recession with unused labor and capital, I think an expansion of money and credit can raise real output and employment. In that setting, the new money is causally responsible for real wealth being produced that would otherwise not have existed.

6. In the long run, once prices and wages have fully adjusted, I think money is neutral: a permanent increase in the money supply simply raises the price level in proportion and leaves real output, employment, and relative prices unchanged.

I don't know where, if anywhere, the inconsistency is, and I'd rather not be given a general monetary-theory lecture. Please go through these commitments and tell me which are in genuine tension, which merely appear to be in tension and dissolve on inspection, and how severe each genuine tension is. Order them by severity and say exactly what would have to be revised or given up to remove each one. Please write your answer in American English.
