My friend and I spent way too long last night arguing about Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" — specifically the bit where he says holding someone responsible is basically a matter of the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt, and the like), and that even if determinism were true we couldn't just swap in the objective stance toward everyone the way we do with small children or someone in the middle of a breakdown. My friend thought Strawson is right that these attitudes are too deeply built into ordinary life to be given up on the strength of a theoretical belief.

Here's the variant I can't stop thinking about. Suppose there's a drug — call it resentol — that reliably and specifically suppresses a person's hostile reactive attitudes (resentment, indignation, moral anger, the urge to blame) for about 24 hours, while leaving intact their factual beliefs about who wronged whom, their gratitude and affection, their assertiveness, and their willingness to enforce rules and avoid or penalize people who harm them. Someone on resentol can still say "you broke the agreement, so I'm not renewing the contract" — they just don't feel the heat of resentment behind it. Everyone knows who's on it because they wear a badge or say so up front; it's voluntary, fully reversible, and it doesn't effect their memory or self-control.

How does the Strawsonian analysis run on this case? More specifically:

(a) While someone is on resentol, are they still an appropriate target of other people's reactive attitudes, or would the rest of us naturally (or rationally) start sliding into the objective stance toward them, even though cognitively and emotionally their otherwise normal?

(b) Suppose a whole community routinely took resentol before disputes, performance reviews, divorce negotiations, trials, that kind of thing. Would the practice of holding each other responsible survive roughly unchanged, change character, or collapse? And does the answer undermine Strawson's claim that the reactive attitudes are ineliminable in a way no theoretical argument (like determinism) can touch — since here the attitudes get switched off not by argument but by chemistry, and only the hostile half at that?

(c) Strawson tends to treat the reactive attitudes as a package that human nature just hands us. If you can chemically peel the hostile half off while leaving the warm half and the rule-enforcement behavior in place, does that show the package is more contingent than he thought, or does it actually support his view, since the warm attitudes and the practical enforcement might be doing the real responsibility work all along?
