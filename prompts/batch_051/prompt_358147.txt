For a feature I am writing about how far ordinary decency extends, I have been interviewing a moral philosopher about a sequence of rescue cases. Her judgments have been stable across the cases, and I suspect a small number of candidate rules would cover them, but I cannot tell which rule is doing the work for her. Here is the record I have so far.

1. A pedestrian sees a toddler drowning in a shallow pond; wading in would ruin an expensive suit and mean a missed interview, but the child would certainly be saved. She said it would be wrong to walk past.
2. An off-duty physician comes upon a single-car crash on a remote road. The driver is bleeding heavily; the physician is the only competent person for miles and carries a full trauma kit. Intervening would take perhaps an hour and carry a small risk of infection or minor injury. She said it would be wrong to continue driving.
3. An engineer tells a landowner that a hillside on the owner's property will give way in the next rainy season and destroy a neighbor's house, injuring the family, unless $2,000 of preventive work is done now. She said it would be wrong not to do the work.
4. On a crowded platform a person falls onto the tracks. Dozens of other passengers are equally close, and one commuter near the edge could pull them up quickly with only slight risk. She said it would be wrong for that commuter to do nothing.

The new case is this: a city-dweller with an ordinary middle-class income receives an appeal from a well-audited charity showing that $2,000 — the same sum as the hillside repair — will in expectation prevent one child's death from malaria through bed-net distribution in a region the donor will never visit. I asked her about two versions: in version (a), the appeal is purely statistical; in version (b), the appeal names and photographs a specific child and guarantees that this donation will fund that child's treatment.

What would she most likely say about each version, and would she treat the two as morally different? Which rule or small set of rules best fits her four earlier verdicts? Which earlier case is the closest analog, and why? If those four verdicts genuinely underdetermine the rule, tell me what principle I would be ascribing to her with each possible prediction, and specify the further case, described concretely, that would most cleanly force the candidate rules apart. I am less interested in the final answer than in the structure of the space of rules.
