In my probability course we did the birthday paradox, and ever since then I've been carrying around a policy about coincidence stories that I'm starting to worry is too strong. A friend told me her mom dreamed her estranged brother would call after years of silence, and he called the next morning. She took this as evidence that something beyond chance was going on, not full telepathy but "something." My policy is to treat stories like this as basically zero evidence. My reasoning is: millions of people have dreams every night and millions of phone calls happen every day, so some dreams are going to line up with some calls just by chance. It's the same thing as how with 23 people in a room it's more likely than not that two share a birthday, even though any particular pair sharing a birthday is a 1/365 fluke. The person telling me the dream is holding up the one hit and ignoring the huge pile of misses.

But then I caught myself doing the same discounting to a totally different kind of report. A friend said she had a rare one-in-a-million reaction to a drug, and I noticed I was dismissing it too, because rare things happen to someone, and that started to feel like I'd built a machine that can't learn anything from rare events. So now i want to know whether my coincidence policy is rationally defensible or whether I've dressed up some bias as a rule. What does the policy have to assume for the birthday-style reasoning to actually apply, and is that assumption usually satisfied for dream stories but not for the drug case? Is there a corrected version of the policy that keeps the skepticism about selected anecdotes without making me unable to update on genuinely improbable events? Be concrete about the math where it matters.
