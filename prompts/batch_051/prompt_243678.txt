In my philosophy of mind course we've been doing epiphenomenalism, and the seminar's got stuck on the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Two factions have been arguing past each other for weeks, and the tutor has now made them do an adverserial collaboration. The two positions are roughly these:

- The epiphenomenalists: phenomenal properties are real but causally inert. The painfulness of a pain doesn't cause my wincing, my saying 'ow', or my belief that I'm in pain; the neural stuff that realises the pain does all the causing.
- The interactionists (that's what we've been calling them): phenomenal properties themselves make a causal difference, at least to our phenomenal judgments. If painfulness never caused anything, then my confident belief that I'm in pain would be caused by processes that are blind to whether the painfulness is there at all, which makes the match between my judgments and my phenomenal states look like a wild coincidence.

Could you draft the document this collaboration should produce? I want it to be the kind of thing both sides could actually sign. Please structure it like this:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps accept, made as strong as possible without begging anything. Include claims about what happens in a typical pain report, about what 'epiphenomenal' means in this debate, about what the paradox is and isn't, and about what the two sides are not disagreeing on.
2. A disagreements table, where each row is one residual disagreement, worded so that each side would accept the wording of its own column. I especially want rows on: (a) whether the phenomenal property itself, rather than its physical substrate, causes the judgment; (b) whether a common physical cause of both the phenomenal state and the judgment can explain why the judgment reliably tracks the phenomenal state; (c) whether being justified in believing you're in pain requires the pain itself to cause the belief; and (d) whether the paradox refutes epiphenomenalism outright or only rules out a stronger version of it.
3. The cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and say what each side predicts in advance.
4. A short paragraph saying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, if any, and why. I have a suspicion that a lot of the fight is that one side means the neural event by 'the judgment that I'm in pain' and the other means the content, but don't force that if you don't think it's right.

Oh, and please write the whole thing in British English, since my tutor marks down American spellings.
