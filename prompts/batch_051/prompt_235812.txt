Write a sonnet about a Dutch book. In the octave, give voice to an agent who is sizing up a single contingent proposition—say, the proposition that it will rain tomorrow. Her degree of belief that it will rain is 0.6, so she judges that a fair price for a bet paying one dollar if it rains is sixty cents; her degree of belief that it will not rain is also 0.6, so she likewise judges that a fair price for a bet paying one dollar if it does not rain is sixty cents. Let the octave make each of these two judgments sound individually defensible, and give no hint of the trap to come: no foreboding, no mention of a bookie. At the volta, in line nine, the bookie sells her both bets at her own stated prices, and she sees what she has accepted: she has paid a dollar twenty for a payout of exactly one dollar no matter how the weather turns—a sure loss of twenty cents. The sestet should lift this one case into the general point: the defect lies not in any single evaluation but in holding two evaluations that jointly violate the additivity of probability, and a set of degrees of belief is coherent only if no combination of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights, can be packaged into a guaranteed loss. Let the closing couplet state the moral crisply—that a belief may be defensible in isolation and yet belong to an indefensible system of beliefs. The poem should be accurate enough that a decision theorist would recognize the Dutch book theorem in it, and clear enough that a reader encountering the theorem for the first time would still grasp the trap and its lesson.
