Two members of my department's ethics seminar argued about the right way to combine two features of an experience when assessing how good or bad it is for the subject. They agreed, for the sake of argument, that any token experience has a degree of consciousness c — roughly, how fully or vividly the state is present — and a degree of happiness h — roughly, how it rates on a scale from negative, through neutral, to positive. They also agreed that value is some function v(c, h), and they considered three forms: additive (v = c + h), multiplicative (v = c × h), and lexical, on which one quantity strictly outranks the other. Here is their exchange as I remember it.

(1) The first speaker opened: 'It cannot be additive. Addition gives positive value to a state with no consciousness at all provided h is positive, and it gives positive value to a state of vivid but perfectly neutral awareness. Both are wrong: a state no one is having cannot be good for anyone, and a vividly felt state of total indifference is worth nothing. Multiplication handles both, because a zero in either factor yields zero value.'

(2) The second replied: 'Your zeros rule out addition, but they do not choose between multiplication and lexical priority. Multiplication forces the claim that c = 10, h = 1 is exactly as valuable as c = 1, h = 10. That treats consciousness and happiness as commensurable substitutes, which they are not. Consciousness is what makes happiness possible at all, so happiness should come first: no increase in how vividly a state is felt can make up for a loss in happiness.'

(3) The first speaker came back: 'Then you cannot explain why consciousness matters at all when happiness is fixed. A humiliation that is felt with full presence is worse than the same humiliation half-noticed; a success that is savoured is better than a success that scarcely enters awareness. If consciousness merely breaks ties after happiness, you get the right ordering but the wrong magnitude. Multiplication gets the magnitude right, because consciousness multiplies whatever is there, whether good or ill.'

(4) The second answered: 'That shows consciousness amplifies whatever happiness is already present; it does not show consciousness is itself a value that trades off against happiness. Multiplication allows a large enough gain in vividness to compensate for a drop in happiness, and across the zero line that is exactly what should never happen. No amount of vividness can make a negative state better than a positive one, and no amount of happiness can make an unconscious state valuable. The correct form is sign-sensitive: happiness fixes the sign, consciousness fixes how much of it there is. That is closer to lexical priority than to multiplication.'

Adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, what (if anything) both are missing, and whether the second speaker's final sign-sensitive proposal actually works as a constraint on v(c, h). Please write in British English.
