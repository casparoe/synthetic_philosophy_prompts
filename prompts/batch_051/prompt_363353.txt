I keep running into a weird double standard in how people talk about self-examination, and I can't tell whether there's actually a principle behind it or whether it mostly tracks how well someone's life happens to be going. Take two people who both spend an hour every evening going back over their day: what they felt, why they said the thing they said, what their reactions reveal about their character. Person A gets described as self-aware, emotionally mature, "doing the work." Person B gets told by friends and maybe a therapist that their ruminating and need to stop. If you press people on what the difference is, they'll say something like "one is trying to understand and the other is just dwelling" — but that feels circular, since Person B almost certainly thinks they're trying to understand too. Even in the same person, the identical activity seems to flip from "healthy introspection" to "pathological rumination" depending on whether they're currently depressed or doing fine, which makes me suspect the distinction is tracking mood or likability rather than any feature of the mental act itself.

So here's what I want to know. Is there a real, non-circular difference between introspection and rumination that would justify treating the first as healthy and the second as something to treat or discourage? Try to steelman the distinction as hard as you can: what features of content, form, aim, or downstream effect could draw the line? Then stress-test it against nearby cases — someone who chews on a single event for years and does change, someone who breezily "lets things go" but is actually avoiding, religious or cultural practices that formalize repetitive self-examination (like an examination of conscience or certain structured journaling traditions), and the awkward fact that therapy often tells people to spend more time on their inner life, not less. Give a verdict on whether the asymmetry in ordinary judgments is justified, or whether we should mostly give the two labels up, or use them more narrowly than we do. Where it matters, separate what's reasonably settled from what's genuinely contested among people who actually study this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
