I'm putting together a workshop on blasphemy, sacrilege, and offence against the sacred, and I want a sorting instrument — a decision tree or a short quiz with a scoring key — that takes someone from their gut responses to a handful of cases to the position they actually hold. The audience is final-year law and theology students, so they'll know the obvious history (Charlie Hebdo, the Danish cartoons, the Bamiyan Buddhas), but I don't want the instrument to lean on any of it, and I especially don't want it to sort people just by how much they like free speech.

The angle I care about is that the debate keeps running three questions together, and the instrument should force them apart. One is what exactly is wronged or harmed when something sacred is attacked: the individual believer's feelings, the community's standing, the sacred thing or place itself, public order, or no distinct wrong at all. Another is who is doing the responding: the criminal law, the civil law, a platform or employer, or informal community pressure. The third is whether the group whose sacred thing is being attacked is a minority, because I've found people will happily jail a tourist for defacing a shrine while insisting no law should protect a majority church from a pie in the face. I want the tree to reflect those splits, not just 'free speech versus censorship'.

Use these cases, or adapt them slightly if you need to — I don't want anything that requires looking anything up:

1. A conceptual artist photographs a consecrated communion wafer being ground under a wine glass. A believer in the gallery weeps and complains; an art critic says no one was singled out, so no one was wronged.
2. A political vlogger burns a copy of the Qur'an on camera, page by page, to make a point about immigration. No one is threatened. The platform removes the video and suspends the account.
3. A tourist scratches his initials into the stone of a small, active shrine in a country where his group is the majority and the shrine's community is a minority. Locals want him prosecuted; the national law says nothing either way.
4. A protester throws red paint over the doors of a majority church after a clergy abuse scandal, shouting that the building is a crime scene.
5. A state-funded museum displays an old ceremonial object even though the community it comes from says uninitiated people must not see it. The curators say the object has irreplaceable heritage value; the community says displaying it at all is the desecration.
6. A blogger writes a satirical retelling of a sacred narrative in which the central prophet-figure is a bumbling divorce lawyer. Some members of the faith write in saying it hurts them; others in the same faith say satire is its own kind of respect.

One more thing: I want the instrument to keep track of whether each case is blasphemy proper (speech about the divine), sacrilege (what you do to a sacred thing, place, or rite), or mere offence (somebody is hurt but no sacred thing is touched). I half-expect some positions to treat all three as the same problem and others to fall apart unless they keep them apart — the instrument should record which leaves do which.

The leaves should include at least: (i) only ordinary harm like incitement or harassment is actionable, and religion gets nothing special; (ii) religious communities get the same protection as other identity groups against degrading treatment; (iii) sacred objects and places get a kind of protection that isn't reducible to anyone's hurt feelings; (iv) the state should enforce the standards of the majority or traditional religion; (v) the law stays neutral but private actors should be free to punish or marginalise; (vi) restrictions are justified only when public order is genuinely threatened. If the tree naturally produces more leaves, include them, but label every leaf by its commitments rather than by an '-ism'. And don't write anything doctrinal — I want a normative instrument, not an account of any particular jurisdiction.

Format, in this order:

1. "The instrument" (about 500 words): the tree or quiz itself. Each branch question should be answerable by someone who doesn't already know which leaf they're heading to. If you do a quiz, include the scoring key. 
2. "Why these distinctions" (about 200 words): for each split, say which two positions it seperates and why a tree that omitted it would misclassify someone.
3. "Inseparable pairs and empty cells" (about 200 words): which leaves your instrument cannot tell apart, and which combinations of answers have no leaf; for each empty cell, say briefly whether that's because the view is incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored.
4. "One awkward commitment per leaf" (about 150 words): one sentence per leaf on the thing its holder has to swallow.

Write in British English.
