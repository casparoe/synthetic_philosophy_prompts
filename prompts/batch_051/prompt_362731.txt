i'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology working on cultural evolution, and I've been going back and forth about the old objection that cultural transmission is too low-fidelity to be genuinely Darwinian. My advisor keeps saying "memes" is a loose metaphor, not a mechanism, because there's no exact copying the way there is with genes, and without high-fidelity replication you can't get cumulative selection. Fair enough. But I keep thinking about the 2002 film The Ring. The basic setup: there's a cursed videotape that kills you seven days after you watch it unless you make a copy and show it to someone else, which is how it propagates. That isn't just an idea spreading because it's catchy or useful; it spreads because it has a built-in threat that uses the host's fear of death to force transmission, with a hard deadline. It's like a replicator that solved the motivation problem instead of the fidelity problem.

I want to use the film to think through what makes something a real Darwinian replicator in culture. Read The Ring through a memetic/selectionist lens. Is the tape best understood as a meme in Dawkins' sense, a parasite meme, or a kind of obligate cultural virus? Does the fact that the copies are apparently perfect (each new tape is an exact duplicate) mean the movie is stacking the deck in the replicator's favor, and if the curse had to survive lossy, reinterpreted copies, would the whole thing fall apart? Where does the film get the selection story right—for instance about costs to the host, transmission chains, and why a variant with a seven-day delay might outcompete one that kills its viewer immediately—and where does it smuggle in assumptions that a critic of memetics would reject? I'm not looking for a book report; I want a serious reading that treats the film as more than an illustration and is willing to say where the analogy between the tape and a meme fails.
