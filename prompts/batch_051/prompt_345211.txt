I've been rereading W. K. Clifford's essay The Ethics of Belief, and I've ended up with an argument that worries me because it seems to prove too much. Clifford tells the story of a shipowner who talks himself into believing his emigrant ship is seaworthy even though he keeps noticing and then suppressing reasons to think it isn't. The ship goes down. Clifford's verdict, as I read him, is that the owner's belief was culpable not because it happened to be false, but because he had no right to hold it on the evidence he had; the sincerity of his belief doesn't get him off the hook. That much seems right to me. But here's the argument I've built out of it.

(1) If a belief is culpable when it is formed by suppressing doubt and ignoring contrary evidence, then what makes a belief permissible is whether the believer has done the epistemic work: actively entertained the possibility of error and looked for what could show the belief false.

(2) So a belief is permissible only if the believer has either actively doubted it and found the doubt defeated, or has a special reason to think active doubt is unnecessary in this case.

(3) There is no general, non-arbitrary way to know in advance of doubting whether a given belief falls into the second category; the only way to acquire such a reason is to examine the belief and the sources that support it, which just is a further exercise of doubt.

(4) For each reason one gives to defeat doubt, the same question arises: have I actively doubted this reason, or am I now suppressing doubts about it? So the requirement applies at every level.

(5) Therefore, on pain of arbitrariness, the only way to discharge the epistemic duty for any belief is to doubt it, then doubt the doubt-defeaters, and so on.

(6) But no finite inquirer can complete this regress for even one ordinary belief, let alone for all of them. So Clifford's principle seems to imply that nearly all of our beliefs are impermissible and ought to be suspended.

(7) That conclusion is absurd: it makes ordinary responsible belief impossible, and it seems to undermine the very practice of inquiry that Clifford himself was defending.

Where does this go wrong? I'd like to keep the shipowner case and the thought that sincerity isn't enough, but I don't want the universal scepticism in (6). I suspect the problem is somewhere around (2) or (3), but I can't make it precise. What's the best diagnosis, and what do I have to give up?
