I've been going around in circles on the toxin puzzle, and a friend built an argument on top of it that feels like it proves way too much, but I can't find the bad step.

Setup, in case it matters: a billionaire tells you that at midnight tonight you'll receive a million dollars if you *intend*, at midnight, to drink a mildly unpleasant poison tomorrow afternoon. The money lands in your account by morning whether or not you actually drink, and you know this. The usual line is that you cant get the money, because you can't form the relevant intention: you know you won't drink tomorrow.

Here's the argument my friend gave:

(1) In the toxin case, you cannot intend at midnight to drink the poison tomorrow.
(2) What blocks you can't be anything about the act itself — the sickness is mild and brief, and people intend much nastier things when there's a payoff. The only candidate left is that you're sure you won't do it: you'll have the money by then, the act has no upside, and you know yourself well enough to predict that.
(3) So the lesson of the toxin case is that certainty you won't φ is enough, all by itself, to make intending to φ impossible.
(4) But then intending to φ must involve thinking of yourself as someone who will φ — some kind of expectation or belief that you'll actually do it.
(5) Now take any normal decision, like deciding whether to accept a job. Before you decide, you don't yet believe you'll accept it; the decision is exactly what settles the question. So the only thing that could supply the expectation required by (4) is the decision itself.
(6) But a decision can't supply its own precondition. If the expectation has to be in place before the intention can form, then you'd need to already believe you'll φ before you could ever decide to φ — and you can't just decide to believe something, any more than you can decide to believe it's raining.
(7) So (4) seems to imply that nobody can ever make a decision. That's absurd.

So either the usual read of the toxin case is wrong, or something in steps (3)–(6) is doing more work than it should. Which step do I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd really like to keep (1), but I dont see how to stop the generalization without it looking like special pleading for the toxin case.
