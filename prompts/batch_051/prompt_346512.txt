I've been in a tenant-union reading group and we keep hitting the same mess: when is a neighborhood's opposition to new housing something we should honor, and when is it just NIMBYism with a conscience? My gut on concrete cases doesn't obviously add up, and I'd like a principle that either captures most of them or tells me which ones I have to give up.

Case one: a wealthy, mostly single-family neighborhood half a mile from a light-rail stop. A developer wants to tear down a boarded-up gas station and build a six-story building with 80 apartments, about a fifth of them set below market rent. The neighborhood association packs the zoning hearing and argues it will ruin the neighborhood's "character" and make parking impossible. My verdict: that's exclusion, the city should just do it. Case two: a low-income, historically Black neighborhood where a developer wants to put up a 40-story luxury tower on the edge of the neighborhood, replacing a strip of local shops. Residents say it'll pull rents up around them and they won't be able to afford any of it. Verdict: they're right to fight it. These two worry me, because both are neighborhoods trying to block housing, and I don't want the principle to be "rich neighborhoods must accept growth and poor ones don't have to," since that would just push all the growth into the places with the least power to stop it.

Case three: a small single-family suburb refuses to allow even duplexes, explicitly to keep out, in one council member's words, "apartment people," while the town's teachers and firefighters commute forty minutes because they can't afford to live there. Verdict: clearly not ok. Case four: a movement to save a century-old low-rise market street with a public market and cheap storefronts from demolition for a 30-story office-and-condo project. Verdict: I'm with the preservationists, even though protecting "low-rise character" for a busy cheap market street feels different from protecting it for single-family homes near transit, and I'm not sure I can say why. Case five: a public housing tenant council resists a "mixed-income redevelopment" that would demolish all their units, move them out for years, and rebuild fewer deeply affordable units plus a lot of market-rate units. Verdict: their resistance is legitimate, no hesitation. Case six, the one I can't settle: a neighborhood of older single-family bungalows—not rich, not especially poor, not on transit—opposes a citywide upzoning that would allow triplexes on their street; they cite their kids' already overcrowded school and the loss of mature trees. I flip between "that's NIMBYism with a sympathetic face" and "no, those are real local harms and they didn't write the citywide policy."

Is there a principle about who has a right to shape the city, or when blocking housing is legitimate, that gets most of these right? State the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or must reinterpret, and apply it to the bungalow case plus this one: a neighborhood that supports a planned new subway station but opposes rezoning the blocks around it for apartments.
