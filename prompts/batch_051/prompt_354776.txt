So I've been traveling a lot for work the past couple years and I've ended up with a pile of gut reactions about local conventions that I can't seem to fit under one rule. I want to know what principle, if any, actually captures what I think, and where I should just change my mind.

Here are the cases, as honestly as I can report them.

Tipping 20% in the US feels obligatory to me, even when service is mediocre. The server's pay is built around tips, and stiffing them feels like a kind of theft. But not tipping in Japan felt right, even though I'm American and could afford it; leaving a tip there felt like it would turn a service into a transaction in a way locals would find insulting. So in one country I tip because that's the local rule, and in the other I happily don't.

Queueing in the UK is the strongest one. Cutting the line at a bus stop would feel deeply wrong even if nobody said anything and there was no real cost to anyone. Yet I've jaywalked against a red pedestrian light in Berlin at 2am with no cars in sight, with locals visibly glaring at me, and I felt basically no guilt. I wasn't endangering anyone and I just wanted to cross.

Haggling in a Moroccan souk felt right and even fun, even though back home trying to haggle at a store would feel pushy and embarrassing. Refusing a drink three times before accepting it, which a local friend said was expected of a guest in his family, I couldn't bring myself to do; it felt like playing a game with hospitality and I kept wondering if I was just being rude in my own way.

There's also the shoes-off rule. In Japanese homes I take my shoes off without thinking, and it feels fine. But a friend in Sweden asked guests to take their shoes off and I felt a flash of annoyance, like the kind of arbitrary thing a host shouldn't impose. Why do I feel differently when the rule is a whole society's rather than one person's?

My tentative rule was something like: a local convention is binding on a visitor when it protects someone or is built into how people are paid, but optional when it's just etiquette or coordination. That fits tipping in the US, and it lets me off the hook for jaywalking, but it doesn't explain why queue-cutting feels more binding than the shoes-off rule, and it makes haggling look like a coin flip.

The hard case I keep going back and forth on: I was in a country where tipping isn't customary, and a young waiter who clocked me as American clearly expected a tip and positioned the whole interaction around it. The local rule says don't; the waiter's expectation say do; and I also worry that tipping there might start exporting a norm that will eventually make workers depend on tips. Is there a principle that gets all or almost all of these, what does it say about that hard case, and which of my original verdicts should I give up if no principle gets all of them?
