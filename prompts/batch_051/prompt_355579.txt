I edit a magazine's ideas section and I'm putting together a big explainer on why serious people can disagree so much about how much history is actually inside famous legends. The three cases I keep coming back to are the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus, because they seem to sit at three different points on a spectrum. Troy has a real excavated city, independent Bronze Age records from the region, and a plausible destruction layer — and yet the story also comes with gods, heroes, a ten-year siege, and a wooden horse, so the overlap between "what probably happened" and "what the epic says happened" is genuinely unclear. Arthur is the opposite problem: there is almost no near-contemporary record at all, and the legend only gets fully dressed up centuries later, so even the question "was there anyone?" is hard, let alone what he did. The Exodus is different again: its a foundational national story, but Egyptian records and Levantine archaeology seem to offer no comfortable place for it at the scale the story describes, which pushes people toward small-group, symbolic, or totally invented readings. Before I write another word, I want the conceptual scaffolding straight.

So give me the desiderata. What does a theory of when a legend becomes history have to be able to do to count as adequate? Write the requirements list: the questions it must keep separate (did a war happen vs. did the war as told happen vs. does a story preserve continuous memory of some real event), the distinctions it cannot collapse (an invented story can still contain real place names; a real event can be dressed in entirely fictional motives and details; a story can be foundational for a people without being historically accurate), and the kinds of evidence it has to be able to weigh fairly — near-contemporary texts, late texts, archaeology, institutional and ritual memory, genre conventions, and silence. For each requirement, say why it's a requirement and which of the three cases puts the most pressure on it. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable for any serious theory and which are just the preferences of particular camps (minimalists vs. traditionalists in biblical studies, say, or literary critics vs. archaeologists in Homeric studies). If some pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — like demanding independent confirmation and also demanding that oral tradition be allowed to preserve a genuine memory over a long gap — say so, and explain whether that means one of them has to give. Skip a full scorecard of named schools; I mostly want the criteria and the tensions between them.
