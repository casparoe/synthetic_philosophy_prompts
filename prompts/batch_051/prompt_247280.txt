I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory and rationality and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on the St. Petersburg paradox that can't just be answered by reciting Bernoulli's log-utility fix. It should force students to seperate three ways of reading the paradox — a mathematical puzzle about divergent expected value, a descriptive claim about what people actually would pay, and a normative claim about what a rational agent should pay — and to see why a fix aimed at one reading can leave the others standing. For instance, log utility makes the original game's expected utility finite, but as long as utility is unbounded you can cook up a faster-growing payoff schedule that again says pay any finite price; and even a bounded-utility answer doesn't by itself settle whether expected-value reasoning is the right norm in the first place. Please write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific mistakes that should loose marks (like treating risk aversion as a complete solution). Treat it as a 60-minute in-class essay, 25 marks total. You're an expert on decision theory and the philosophy of economics; before you draft, brainstorm two or three candidate questions and pick the one that best forces students to confront whether the paradox is about unbounded payoffs or about the authority of expected value.
