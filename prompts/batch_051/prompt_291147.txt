I sit on my university's academic integrity board, and I'm drafting the short written opinion I'll circulate before our meeting Wednesday. The case: two students, Alice and Ben, both bought the answers to a final from the same 'homework help' site. Alice sent Ben the link; he venmo'd her half the money, downloaded the file, and studied it. On exam day Alice submitted answers copied almost word for word. Ben paniked partway through and spent the rest of the exam striping out the stolen material, ending up with mostly his own work. Both got caught because the instructor had already flagged the same answer set circulating. The instructor is recommending an F in the course and a one-term suspension for each of them. One colleague on the board keeps insisting that the wrong is already complete at consent: Ben agreed to the scheme, paid, downloaded, and used it to prepare, so the fact that Alice actually carried it through shouldn't matter much, if at all. He says we punish the outward deed when the old moral insight is that the sin lies in the consent, not in the act. Another colleague says that's a terrible line for a university to take: it would mean the student who backs out at the last minute gets no credit for backing out, and it turns internal states into disciplinary offenses. I honestly see both sides.

Please draft the memo I can circulate. It should recommend a concrete ruling on Ben relative to Alice — same penalty, a lesser one, or something else — give the principle we should use in future cases, and take the strongest argument on the losing side seriously rather than strawmanning it. Our committee is fine with a bare-bones format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no opening or closing paragraph.
