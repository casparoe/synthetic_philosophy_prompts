I'm writing a chapter on whether effort matters in itself or only for its results, and I've noticed that the literature tends to argue for one position or another — effort as intrinsic value, effort as signal, effort as cost — before anyone has fixed the criteria the positions are answerable to. Before I take a side, I want the specification that keeps getting skipped: a full list of desiderata for a theory of effort's value, good enough for moral and prudential evaluation of ordinary striving and achievement.

Produce the list of requirements any adequate theory must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and add anything I've missed where it belongs. It should cover at least the following territory.

Cases a theory must classify correctly: effortless success versus effortful success holding the outcome fixed; present ease that was earned by past effort; effortful failure and wasted effort; effort well invested in a project that later proves worthless or wrong; effort spent in wrongdoing; effort that is never observed or recognized by anyone; and sustained effort built into a skill over years versus a one-off expenditure.

Distinctions a theory cannot collapse: the felt cost of effort, as subjective strain, versus the objective fact of effort or difficulty; effort versus the achievement it partly constitutes; effort as valuable in its own right versus effort as evidence or signal of some other value such as commitment, desert, or character; the value of the agent's effort to the agent versus its value to an observer versus its value simpliciter; and effort that is part of the point of the activity versus effort that is merely a tax on an activity whose point lies elsewhere.

Then the descriptive constraints: a theory of effort's value should not contradict the familiar pattern in which people both avoid effort and yet rate outcomes they reached through effort more highly; the phenomenon in which rewarded high effort makes later effort less aversive; and the fact that effort has real opportunity costs and fatigue limits. It should accommodate these patterns, not merely stay silent about them.

Now the part I most want worked out. I suspect some of these desiderata are in genuine tension and others only look that way from inside a particular camp. Specifically: Can a theory hold both that effort is intrinsically good-making and that the value of an action should not depend on historical properties of how the action came about that are now invisible in its result? Can a theory hold that effort is intrinsically valuable while denying that effort spent in a wrongful project makes that project better in any respect? And can a theory hold that effort should be valued from the agent's perspective while also holding that the value of an activity is exhausted by the value of the states of the world it realizes?

For each pair, say whether the tension is real and exacts a price, or whether it dissolves once a distinction already on the list is respected. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable demands on any adequate theory and which are merely the preferences of a particular camp. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work to eliminate the standing views, and why.
