I spent my working life as a cell biologist and only started reading philosophy of language after I retired, so go easy on me where my terminology is loose. Here's what I keep chewing on. The field I came up in has been naming newly discovered kinds of cell death by pulling from Greek and occasionally Latin: apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and the list keeps going. The English alternative was right there — programmed cell death, cell suicide, inflammatory cell death — but the Greek names are the ones that became standard terms of art in the international literature. I've noticed people assume this is functional: Greek and Latin roots are dead and stable, they decompose into descriptive parts that everyone can parse whatever their native language, and they lend a new concept an air of objectivity. But I honestly don't know whether any of that is true, or whether it's just path dependence plus the fact that people who coin names enjoy showing off their classics.

So here's my question, and I'd like an actual number and the reasoning on the table, not a lecture about how it varies. Consider this claim: for a genuinely new biological concept introduced into the international research literature over, say, the next ten years, a name coined from Greek or Latin roots is more likely to become the standard term within twenty years than an equally precise, equally brief name built from ordinary English morphemes — holding the importance of the concept and the prominence of the originating lab fixed, and assuming no nomenclature committee, journal style rule, or database identifier forces one choice. What's your credence in that claim? Decompose it seperately: how much weight do you give to a real causal advantage from cross-linguistic neutrality; to a real advantage from morphological transparency; to mere prestige or authority signaling; to network effects (classically educated namers tracking the people who gatekeep the field); and to the possibility that plain English names are now better positioned because English is the de facto lingua franca. Give each term a number and one line of justification, then show how you combine them. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, and what evidence — an experiment, a natural experiment from nomenclature history, a computational study of term survivorship, whatever — would move that term the most. I'll hold back my own prior so I don't anchor you.
