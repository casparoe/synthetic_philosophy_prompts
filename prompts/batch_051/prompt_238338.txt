I teach late antique history, and I have spent the past several years working on fifth-century Italy and Gaul, especially land tenure, episcopal authority, and what happens to Roman legal forms once imperial administration recedes. The position I hold—and it dominates much of my corner of the field—is that talk of the fall of Rome misdescribes the end of the western Empire: what happened was not collapse but transformation. Non-Roman kings inserted themselves into Roman structures rather than destroying them, the barbarian settlements were often local reallocations of tax shares and land rights, bishops absorbed civic responsibilities from retreating imperial officials, and Roman law and land categories persisted in modified form through the sixth century and beyond. On this view, the end of Roman rule in the West is better understood as a renegotiation of authority within a still-Roman social world than as a catastrophe. Gibbon’s History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, with its moralizing emphasis on internal corruption and Christian otherworldliness softening the empire for external shocks, strikes me as a cautionary example of how the very category of decline can distort the evidence before one even gets to the sources.

But I want you to play the counterfactual straight against me. If the transformation thesis were wrong—not just incomplete, but wrong enough that it should be abandoned or sharply restricted—what would the western Empire, roughly between 400 and 600, have to look like at the level of settlement density, building maintenance, ceramic and pottery distribution, coin circulation, long-distance exchange, tax records, legal practice, and the self-presentation of the new rulers? Which discontinuities would be too abrupt to be redescribed as adaptation or regional variation? I want you to distinguish three kinds of considerations: evidence that would be decisive against the core claim that transformation is the right master category; evidence that would only force me to restrict the claim to certain regions or processes; and evidence that is often cited against it but that the thesis can legitimately absorb. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the transformation view has become flexible enough to accommodate any fifth-century outcome, and if so, whether that is a serious problem for a historical thesis of this kind or just the ordinary situation of an interpretive framework. I do not need a reading list; I need a map of where the position actually risks something.
