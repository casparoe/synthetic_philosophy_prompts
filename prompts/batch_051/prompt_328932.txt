My friend, a philosopher who works on the Makropulos debate about whether an eternal life could be worth wanting, has given verdicts on several imaginary afterlives. A single perfect day repeated forever with full memory: not worth wanting. An endless stream of superficially varied but purely passive entertainments, with no projects the person chose or cares about: not worth wanting. An unending, wholehearted project whose completion is forever prevented, and which keeps making genuine, non-repetitive demands on the person: worth wanting.

She is now to rule on this case. Priya is offered an afterlife in which, whenever one desire is satisfied or goes stale, a new, fully absorbing desire is automatically generated in her; she is unaware of the mechanism, and every transition feels wholly her own. Her activities are endlessly varied and genuinely engaging. But no single project, commitment, or trait of character persists across the centuries—she is, in effect, a sequence of wholehearted enthusiasts with no stable evaluative core.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on Williams's Makropulos case, think through the candidate principles before committing, and give a committed prediction: would my friend call Priya's afterlife worth wanting? Lay out the two or three principles that fit her earlier verdicts, say what each predicts about Priya, choose the best-supported prediction and defend it, and name one further imaginary case that would distinguish the live candidates.
