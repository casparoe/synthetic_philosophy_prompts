I'm the mayor of a small town on a state line, and I'm trying to decide whether to close the only bridge to the neighboring town during a serious disease outbreak on their side. I'm new to elected office—before this I ran a hardware store—so I have no training in law or political theory. Our town attorney has confirmed that a public-health emergency statute gives me clear authority to close the bridge for up to thirty days and to turn away anyone who can't prove they live here, and that a partial closure with exceptions for medical staff and patients would also fall within the statute. So the legal side is settled; that's not what I'm asking about. What I can't sort out is the other half of it. The two towns function as one place: my town's clinic is staffed by nurses who drive across that bridge every morning, and the neighboring town's only hospital is on our side, so their patients cross to us for care. A full closure would protect us from the outbreak but would cut off their medical access and damage our own businesses, while doing nothing seems reckless. One council member says if the statute permits it and it likely protects us, that's the whole job. Another says no official should use that kind of power unless they can point to something beyond the statute itself that makes it right. Is one of them right? What would it take for an emergency restriction like this to be not just lawful but genuinely justified—and is that even a distinction I can use in practice, with a decision due in a few days?

Please be measured: tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
