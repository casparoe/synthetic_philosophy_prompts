A couple friends of mine got into an argument at dinner last week and I've been turning it over since. The question was whether it would actually be good to be a "moral saint" — the kind of person who lets moral considerations dominate every area of life. I wrote down the main moves from memory afterward:

(1) Dana said a moral saint is someone whose choices about their time, money, friendships, and leisure are always settled by what does the most good or best expresses duty. She said that's not a life any of us should want, because the things that give a life its texture — falling for one particular person, reading a novel that serves no purpose, getting absorbed in a hobby, wasting an afternoon — are exactly the things that look indefensible from the standpoint of maximal moral goodness.

(2) Priya replied that this is a caricature. A saint doesn't have to calculate consequences at every moment; being a saint is more like having a character in which moral concern is reliably the loudest voice. Such a person can still have close friends and private passions, she said — they're just not the kind of person who'd keep those attachments if keeping them clearly meant doing something wrong. The friend who'll help you move is a better friend, not a worse one, because they'd also tell you the truth when it's hard.

(3) Dana came back: okay, but even the moderate saint has to be ready to drop the novel, the friendship, the hobby whenever morality calls. And that readiness changes the character of those goods. Part of what makes a friendship or a passion valuable in the first place is that you treat it as special, as something you're not calmly prepared to trade away for a better outcome elsewhere. If you're always holding something back, you never fully have the thing you're supposedly defending.

(4) Priya said that's exactly backwards. If a friendship is fragile enough that it would crack because you refused to help someone cover up a wrong, it wasn't the sort of friendship worth keeping. And she added: we don't think the off-duty doctor who leaves her dinner to help at a crash scene is missing out on the dinner in any tragic way — we think that's what the dinner was for, in a manner of speaking. So why is the person who is like that all the time suppose to be the one with the impoverished life?

(5) Dana answered that an emergency is the exception, and a life composed entirely of emergencies has no room for the ordinary goods that emergencies exist to protect. Priya shot back that you could say the same about courage in wartime, and that just shows our everyday self-image is the thing that's shallow, not saintliness. Neither would budge.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each numbered turn, say how right or wrong the move is (clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right, or something in between) and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
