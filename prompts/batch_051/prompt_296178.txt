I have an argument that I cannot decide whether to accept, and I want someone to locate the exact step at which it fails, if it does. It concludes that Noether's theorem, appearances notwithstanding, does not explain conservation laws.

(1) Noether's theorem tells us that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system is accompanied by a conserved quantity.
(2) A theorem that pairs two mathematical objects does not by itself explain anything: a correspondence licenses an inference, but it does not state why either side of the correspondence obtains.
(3) A symmetry is the fact that the dynamics of a system are invariant under some transformation; it is not a mechanism, a cause, or an initial condition.
(4) If a symmetry is not a mechanism or a cause, then pointing to one cannot explain why the paired quantity is conserved; it can only redescribe the conservation.
(5) Therefore, the claim that energy is conserved because the laws are time-translation invariant is not an explanation of conservation but a restatement of it, and whatever explanation exists must come entirely from the underlying dynamical laws, with the symmetry contributing nothing.

Premise (1) seems correct to me, and the conclusion follows from the rest in an obvious way, yet physicists and philosophers routinely speak as if symmetry does genuine explanatory work. Which step is false or ambiguous, and on what reading does the argument break? If instead the argument is valid, say clearly what its conclusion amounts to and what it does not refute.
