I'm teaching Mill's The Subjection of Women, and I find myself defending his methodological move as much as his conclusion. The passage I keep returning to is this:

Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality.

The position I want pressure-tested is that appeals to nature in disputes about women's roles should carry almost no independent evidentiary weight, because the burden is on those who restrict liberty or assign unequal roles to show that the difference is required by something beyond custom. What would count against that? I mean specifically the methodological claim, not just the policy conclusion. What empirical findings about sex differences, what successful appeals to nature elsewhere in science or ethics, what examples where a restrictive presumption turned out right, would move me? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position has been insulated by its own burden-of-proof structure.
