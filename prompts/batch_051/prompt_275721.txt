I hold a view about orphan works and abandoned software that I want pressure-tested rather than reinforced. The position is that exclusive rights in a creative work or a piece of code are justified, when they are justified, largely because they make it possible to find a rightsholder and transact with them — to license, buy, negotiate, or refuse. Where the rightsholder is unlocatable, defunct, or has stopped maintaining and enforcing the work for a long period, that justification has lapsed, and the default should therefore shift. More concretely: orphan works and abandonware should not sit under default copyright until the term expires; they should be treated as abandoned, on the model of adverse possession or the abandonment of chattels, so that anyone may copy, redistribute, and build on them. If a rightsholder surfaces later, they can reclaim only prospectively, not retroactively. The underlying intuition is that a right to exclude with no one home is pure deadweight loss — it blocks every living person and benefits no actual owner — and that the law has no reason to protect the vestigial interests of a party who cannot be found and is not acting like an owner.

Tell me what would count against this, treated as a serious legal-philosophical position and not a slogan. I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to be willing to say that the view is well insulated, if that is the honest conclusion, and whether being well insulated would itself be a problem here. In particular, I want to know: which of the claims — the transaction-based theory of what justifies exclusivity, the abandonment analogy, the prospective-only remedy, or the blanket application across media — is carrying the most risk, and what evidence or argument would break each one? Are there results elsewhere, in property theory, in the economics of option value and irreversible uses, or in the empirical literature on how creators respond to copying risk, that a committed defender of my view would have to concede as counting against it, even if not decisively? How, if at all, do the moral interests of authors who do not want to transact but also do not want certain uses — a desire that survives their own unlocatability — bear on the argument? And does the position collapse when it is sharpened: does the concept of abandonment do the work, or is it being redefined to mean whatever produces the result I already wanted? The evidence I personally would find most persuasive would be something concrete about ex ante behavior — for example, that shifting to a use-it-or-lose-it default would measurably change what gets created, funded, or preserved, or a class of cases where a later-surfacing owner was harmed retrospectively in a way my prospective-only rule fails to capture.
