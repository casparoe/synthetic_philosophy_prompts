In my work as a journalist covering science and ideas, I have been trying to get clear on the distinction philosophers draw between original and derived intentionality: the thought that my beliefs and desires are about things in their own right, whereas a sentence, a map, or a computer's output is about something only because an interpreting mind assigns that aboutness to it. I find that when I go case by case I have firm verdicts, but I cannot state the principle that generates them, and I suspect some of my verdicts are in tension.

Here are my verdicts. (1) My occurrent thought that the Seine runs through Paris is about Paris in the basic, underived way; it does not depend on anyone else interpreting it. (2) A sentence in a French guidebook saying the same thing is also about Paris, but only derivatively: if the community of readers disappeared, the marks would no longer be about anything. (3) A dormant memory, not currently conscious, is still about the event it records, and I want to say it has the same underived aboutness as an occurrent thought, not the borrowed aboutness of the guidebook sentence. (4) A photograph of a wedding is about the wedding in a different way again, causal and trace-like, the way smoke indicates fire, and I do not want to call that intentionality at all, or if I do, it is not the semantic kind. (5) A honeybee's waggle dance indicates the direction and distance of nectar, and I am torn: no human interprets it, but the bee does not seem to mean anything; I end up saying it is not intentional in the way my thought is. (6) A large language model that prints "the cat is on the mat" is about cats and mats only because we, its users, treat its output that way; however fluent the output, I would deny that the system has beliefs. (7) When people say a corporation "believes the market will recover," I hear a metaphor rather than a literal attribution.

The two cases that bother me most are the guidebook sentence and the dormant memory. Both are stored structures that produce aboutness only when retrieved or read, and neither is conscious at the moment, yet I insist the memory is originally intentional while the sentence is merely derived. I need a principle that captures all of these verdicts, including whatever separates the memory from the sentence, without simply relabeling my intuitions. If no single principled account can hold them all, tell me which verdict or verdicts I should give up and why those rather than the others. Please commit: state the principle you would defend precisely, run it through all seven cases, and say honestly where it forces me to revise.
