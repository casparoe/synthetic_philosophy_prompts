Got into a long argument last night with a friend about whether the universe has to have a cause — he kept saying the kalam argument leans too hard on the Big Bang and that the contingency version is cleaner — and at the end of it he laid out this argument and told me to say which step fails. I couldn't find the flaw on the spot, but the conclusion feels stronger than the steps really license, so either I'm missing something or I have to stop rolling my eyes at him. Here are his steps: (1) A contingent thing is just something that exists but could have failed to exist — there is a possible world where it isn't there. (2) Pretty much everything we've ever encountered is contingent: tables, stars, people, each one could have failed to exist. (3) So there is a possible world where all the contingent things fail to exist at once — a world with no contingent things in it. (4) But a world with literally nothing in it isn't a real possibility; something has to exist in every possible world, at least the numbers or the laws of logic or whatever makes a possibility a possibility. (5) Whatever exists in a possible world with no contingent things must be non-contingent, i.e., necessary. (6) So at least one necessary being exists. (7) And whatever that necessary being is, that's what we mean by God, so God exists.

He said only step (2) is really up for grabs, and since I'd already conceded (2), I don't see how I can resist (6). But I've got a nagging feeling theres a slide somewhere between each thing possibly not existing and all of them possibly not existing at once, and the jump to God at (7) also feels way too quick. Is the argument valid? If not, take it step by step and say exactly where it fails and what the corrected version of the doubtful step would be — or whether correcting it even gets you to God.
