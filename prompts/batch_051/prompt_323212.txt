I'm a graduate student in political theory who has been asked to give a one-hour introductory lecture on Hobbes's state of nature and the logic of the social contract to a group of advanced undergraduates from outside the humanities, mostly engineering and life-sciences majors. They have no prior Hobbes. Plan how I should teach the argument, not the lecture itself. I need the order in which the concepts must be introduced so the social contract does not feel like a historical anecdote but follows from Hobbes's definitions of the right of nature and the laws of nature. Which one concrete example or scenario should carry the whole argument, and what constraints must it satisfy so it does not secretly presuppose the moral concepts Hobbes is trying to derive? Where should I expect misconceptions: that the state of nature is a historical period, that the sovereign is just a literal king, that Hobbes's problem is that people are selfish, that the contract is voluntary in the ordinary sense—and at which point in the sequence should each be defused? What should I explicitly refuse to settle at this level? What final question would tell me whether they have grasped the relation between the state of nature and the contract rather than merely recalling the labels?
