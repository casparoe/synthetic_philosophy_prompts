So my philosophy club got into Frege's puzzle the other day—the one about Hesperus and Phosphorus, where 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is completely trivial but 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' can be a genuine discovery even though both names pick out the same planet. One kid, Marcus, says this is basically a claim about the mind: before you learn they're the same, using the two names draws on two separate mental name-tags or ways the planet is presented to you, and learning the identity is what merges them. He thinks that merge is a real event in someone's psychology, not just a change in which sentences they'd sign off on. Another kid says no, all we're seeing is that different strings of words get used in different situations, and 'cognitive significance' is just a fancy label for that.

I can't tell if Marcus's thing is even testable, or if its one of those claims that's dressed up to sound empirical but is actually just a choice about how to describe people. Say I wanted to run the best possible test of his claim—that before someone learns Hesperus is Phosphorus, their mind really treats the two names as two separate ways of thinking about one object, and after learning it, it only uses one. What would the experiment look like concretely? Walk me through what result would count in his favor and what result would count against. Then be honest about what even a perfect result would leave open: like, what part of 'the mind merged two name-tags' would still be interpretation rather than something we actually observed.
