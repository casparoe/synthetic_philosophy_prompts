I keep coming back to an argument my brother gave me last summer, and I still cannot find where it breaks. He is an engineer who reads popular physics, not a philosopher, and he presented it as a chain that the physics forces on you whether you like it or not.

(1) Relativity has shown that there is no absolute now: whether two events far apart happen at the same time depends on the motion of the observer, and no single observer's answer is privileged. (2) If only the present moment really existed — with the past gone and the future not yet — then what exists right now would have to be the same for every observer, because existence cannot depend on how fast someone happens to be moving. (3) So only the present being real cannot survive relativity. (4) The alternative is that every event in spacetime, past and future included, is equally real; the universe is one block, and no slice of it is objectively the present. (5) But if next Tuesday's events are already just as real as today's, then what happens next Tuesday is already there, already settled, part of the block. (6) So the sense I have that next Tuesday's outcomes are still open and can be changed by what I decide between now and then is an illusion; the future is fixed.

My brother says the physics commits you to the whole chain, and that my resistance to (6) is just sentiment. I most want to keep (1), because it is experimentally secure, and I most want to keep (6), because giving it up makes deliberation feel fraudulent. The error, if there is one, must lie somewhere in how we move from (1) to (6) — in the physics, in the step from physics to claims about what exists, or in the step from real to settled. Where does the chain actually break, and what do I have to give up in order to break it there?
