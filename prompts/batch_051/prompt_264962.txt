I'm trying to work out, in a principled way, what I actually mean by political freedom, using my own reactions to cases as the data. In each case I'll say whether I judge the person free with respect to the relevant choice; by "free" I mean free in the sense that matters for social and political criticism, not merely unimpeded in some narrow local sense.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A factory worker's contract lets her manager dismiss her at will, for any reason or none. The manager is decent and never threatens or pressures her; she criticizes company policy in meetings and nothing happens. Yet I judge that she is not fully free in speaking up. The fact that her safety depends entirely on the manager's continuing restraint matters to me.

2. A country's laws give a ministry complete legal power to ban any publication, and the current government never exercises that power. A blogger writes whatever she wants, and nothing is ever censored. I judge that the blogger is unfree in this respect, even though nothing interferes with her.

3. A citizen of a well-functioning constitutional democracy with strong rights never votes, never attends public meetings, and takes no interest in politics. Her rights are protected and officials scrupulously follow the law. I judge that she is free in the political sense, even though she exercises no self-rule.

4. An addicted gambler genuinely wants to stop, has tried repeatedly, and cannot resist placing the next bet. I judge that he is unfree with respect to gambling, even though no external agent stands in his way.

5. A woman raised in a strict community genuinely believes she must not work outside the home. If she tried, there would be no legal obstacle, and her family would support her; she simply experiences the idea as something she cannot choose. I keep going back and forth on whether she is unfree or merely has different preferences from mine.

6. A person who is legally free to open a business has no savings, no property, no access to credit, and no prospect except casual dead-end work. I judge that he is not really free to start a business, and that this is a fact about his freedom, not merely about his happiness or the attractiveness of his options.

7. A person with advanced ALS can no longer walk, speak, or type. I do not judge him unfree, at least not in the political sense; I say he is unable, not unfree.

8. An employee can be dismissed only for cause, but the contract leaves what counts as cause almost entirely to the employer's discretion. The employer likes her, so she is safe in practice. I judge that she is less free, with respect to her job, than a co-worker whose job is protected by a precise, independently enforceable contract—even though neither of them is being interfered with right now.

My starting principle was the simple one: a person is free just in case no one interferes with her. That plainly fits some of my verdicts but seems to fail cases 1, 2, 6, and 8. The republican conception—freedom as not being subject to another's arbitrary will, even when that will is unexercised—handles 1, 2, and 8 well, and it says something instructive about 3, but I do not see how it explains why 6 is unfreedom rather than mere lack. The positive conception seems to me to be doing two jobs at once: sometimes it means self-mastery, which fits 4, and sometimes it means effective capacity, which fits 6, and I am not sure those are the same thing.

What I want is the best reflective-equilibrium treatment of these verdicts. Please either state a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, precise enough to generate verdicts on new cases. For each clause of the principle, say which of my cases motivates it. Then tell me what your principle says about a case I have not listed: the slave of a kind master who never interferes and who, by settled habit, would never interfere. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, or which verdicts it would advise me to revise, and explain why those are the ones to give up rather than others. In particular, it matters to me that your account can distinguish case 6 from case 7—both involve an absence of ability, but they feel different to me—and that it come down somewhere on case 5 rather than leaving the ambivalence in place.
