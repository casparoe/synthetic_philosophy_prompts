You are a professional philosopher specializing in animal ethics, food ethics, and the ethics of scarce resources. Think through this carefully. Start by sketching several candidate principles, test each against every case below, and note exactly where each candidate breaks before settling on your final account. If no simple principle fits all the verdicts, say so plainly and explain the trade-offs rather than papering over the gaps.

I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about eating and using animal products in contexts where plant-based alternatives are not reliably available, affordable, or nutritionally adequate. Below are my gut verdicts on eight cases. I am not asking for a general survey of positions on veganism or animal ethics; I want you to extract the principle—or a small ranked set of candidate principles—that best captures my verdicts, state it precisely, and say which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon. I would also rather be told which verdict to drop than be handed an ad hoc patch whose only job is to save every case.

The cases:

1. A family in a wealthy country, with supermarkets full of cheap beans, lentils, and fortified plant foods, buys factory-farmed chicken several nights a week. My verdict: wrong, and not a hard case.

2. A pastoralist household in a semi-arid region keeps a small herd of goats because the land cannot reliably support crops. The household drinks the milk, occasionally slaughters a goat, and sells some animals to buy grain and pay school fees. My verdict: not wrong.

3. A pregnant woman in a low-income country has iron-deficiency anemia. Her staple diet is maize and beans; she cannot afford or reliably obtain iron supplements or fortified foods. She keeps a few hens, eats their eggs, and eats fish whenever she can get it. My verdict: not wrong.

4. A middle-class office worker in a city in a lower-middle-income country has cheap lentils, tofu, vegetables, and cooking oil available year-round and does sedentary work. He eats meat at every meal because he prefers it and always has. My verdict: wrong, roughly as wrong as case 1.

5. A vegetarian family in the same city keeps a cow that pulls the plow and provides manure for the family's vegetable garden; the family also drinks its milk and cooks with ghee. Affordable plant milks are scarce, and getting rid of the cow would sharply reduce the garden's productivity. My verdict: not wrong, or at least much less wrong than buying factory-farmed dairy.

6. A well-off couple in a wealthy country keeps four backyard hens that are healthy, roam the yard, and eat food scraps. The couple eats the eggs. The hens came from a hatchery that kills male chicks. My verdict: uneasy. The eggs themselves feel acceptable, but the hatchery's practice troubles me, and I cannot tell how much it should matter after the hens are already there and well cared for.

7. A person in a wealthy country has multiple severe, medically confirmed food allergies. Eggs and fish are load-bearing parts of the only diet she tolerates well; specialized alternatives exist but are expensive, hard to source, and have sometimes triggered reactions. My verdict: not wrong.

8. A family in the same city as the office worker from case 4 holds an annual festival at which a goat is slaughtered and shared. Refusing to eat would be taken as a deep insult to the hosts and would strain the family's standing in the community; plant foods are otherwise abundant and cheap. My verdict: this feels much less wrong than case 4, and I am not sure it is wrong at all, though I cannot articulate why.

Which principle, or small ranked set of principles, best captures these verdicts? For each clause of the principle, say which case or pair of cases motivates it. Then apply your account to two further cases I have not settled: (a) a poor fishing family that sells most of its catch to buy grains and vegetables but keeps some of the fish for its own meals; and (b) a middle-class family in a wealthy country that is otherwise vegan but eats lamb once a year at a large traditional holiday gathering that it would be socially costly to miss. Finally, tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts your account cannot accommodate, and whether you would revise the principle to keep the verdict or recommend that I overturn the verdict.
