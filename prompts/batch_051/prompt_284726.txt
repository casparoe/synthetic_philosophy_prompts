So here's the situation. I'm the undergrad rep on my university's research ethics committee, and I'm also taking a philosophy of mind class this semester on the moral status of digital minds — so of course this landed on me. Were voting next Thursday on a proposal from a computational cognitive science lab, and I got asked to draft the short memo the committee will actually vote around.

The lab wants to run huge populations of simple reinforcement-learning agents in virtual environments to study avoidance learning and something they call "learned helplessness." The agents use a stripped-down punishment/reward architecture: certain events trigger an internal "aversive" signal (the lab says it's calibrated to pain-like responses in simple animals), and the experiments include inputs that stand in for electric shocks, bodily damage, and having the agent's ability to act switched off — because the whole point is to study how negative valence shapes learning and passivity. They plan to scale this to millions of agents, some of which get terminated or permanently "disabled" mid-experiment. Nobody is claiming the agents are conscious, or that there's a brain in there. The lab's line is that this is code, and should no more need ethics review than a weather simulation. Under our current policy they're technically right, since we only cover human subjects and vertebrate animals.

The committee is split. Some members think the no-brain point is decisive and that adding oversight would be absurd. Others think "it's just computation" is exactly the claim that needs defending here: if you deliberately build systems to instantiate suffering-like processes at scale, maybe precautionary oversight is warranted even when we can't prove the agents are moral patients. This is uncomfortably close to what we've been arguing about in class — whether the badness of pain depends on biology or on the information-processing role — and now I have to make it concrete. The lab has already said it would accept safeguards if we can justify them, so the memo can recommend approve-with-conditions rather than all-or-nothing.

Please write the actual memo, around 700–900 words, that the committee can vote on. It should (1) give a clear recommendation — approve without oversight, approve with conditions, or require full review — with a time frame for any conditions or re-review, (2) state a critereon for when future computational experiments get flagged, so we don't relitigate this from scratch every time, and (3) engage the strongest argument on the losing side fairly rather than just swatting it away. I don't want a literature review on definitions of consciousness; I need the memo to use the arguments to actually move the decision. It also has to be something I can defend to the lab, to my professor if she reads it, and to the committee members who already think the whole question is silly.
