I have been trying to map the landscape of arguments for the thought that greed or acquisitiveness, whatever its moral defects, is a genuine engine of material prosperity and not merely a vice we happen to tolerate. The canonical defenses I already have are these. One is the Mandevillean argument that the craving for luxury and gain sets people to work, multiplies trades, and increases employment, so private vices generate public benefits. A second is the Smithian argument that, under competitive markets, the self-interested pursuit of profit is directed by prices and competition into supplying what others want, so the good result does not depend on benevolent motives. A third is the elementary exchange argument that a voluntary transaction is entered only because each party expects to gain by it, so acquisitiveness expressed through voluntary exchange is positive-sum rather than zero-sum, even before any further effect on productivity. A fourth is the Hayekian argument that the lure of profit mobilizes dispersed knowledge, calls forth innovation, and weeds out failures in a discovery procedure no planner could replicate. A fifth is the doux-commerce argument that habitual buying and selling gives selfish agents an interest in honesty, reliability, punctuality, and peaceable dealings, since cheating or violence is bad for repeat business. A sixth is the accumulation argument that the drive to acquire underwrites saving, investment, and productivity growth, lifting absolute living standards over time.

I think this list exhausts the standard moves, so I am not asking for any of them. I would like a further argument for treating greed or acquisitiveness as an engine of prosperity that is not on this list and is not a mere rewording, special case, or combination of the six arguments above. Give me the best such argument you can construct, state clearly how it differs from each family of argument on my list, and then tell me honestly whether it is any good—that is, whether it adds real independent support or only looks new on the surface.
