I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about self-examination, because the culture around me treats looking inward as pretty much always good, but I know people who have clearly made themselves worse by doing it. Following my gut on cases seems like the only way to make progress, so here are mine.

After a bad date, I walk home replaying the conversation to see where it went wrong, and I end up with two or three concrete things I'd do differently next time. That feels useful. After the same kind of bad date, I replay it for days asking "why do I always make it weird?" and "what's wrong with me?" and end up just feeling defective. That feels clearly harmful. My friend bombed a work presentation and wrote a one-page after-action report: what went wrong, what was outside her control, and one thing to prepare next time. That seemed to make her better at presenting. The same friend, after a later bad presentation, lay awake running through every slide, picturing her coworkers' faces and asking "do they think I'm incompetent?" That was pure rumination and it made her worse. My dad has tracked his mood each night for a year and noticed he feels worst after nights of short sleep, so he changed his bedtime; that worked. My cousin tried one of those apps that pings you every hour to rate how you feel, and after a few weeks she said her feelings had become specimens she was poking at all day; she was relieved when she deleted the app. A CBT workbook had me write down the thought "I'm a fraud" and then list evidence for and against it; it felt mechanical, but it genuinely lessened the thought's grip. But when I tried to "get to the bottom" of whether I was doing a favor for a friend out of real kindness or just to look kind, I went in circles for two days and nearly didn't do the favor. That also felt like self-examination, and it felt bad.

My working theory is that the difference is whether the questions have answers you can act on: "what can I do differently next time?" has an answer, "what's wrong with me?" doesn't. But the CBT thought-record case worries me, because "am I actually a fraud?" doesn't have a clean answer either, and it still helped. So maybe my principle is off. Is there one principle that captures all of these, or close? Which of my verdicts would a coherent principle force me to give up? And what does your principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: every evening, I write down the thing I most regret from that day and then ask "why does it matter to me?"—is that processing, or is it just a machine for producing more rumination?
