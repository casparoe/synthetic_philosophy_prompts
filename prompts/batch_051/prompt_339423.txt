I run an after-school philosophy seminar at my high school, and we are currently finishing a unit on distributive justice and decision rules under uncertainty. Last week I set the following take-home question for the group, and one of the members submitted the answer pasted below. I am responsible for assigning a score out of 10 and returning written comments, and I would rather not rely on a general impression. Please give me a point-by-point assessment of the submitted answer: identify which parts are correct and deserve full credit, and which parts, if any, are mistaken or imprecise, with a brief explanation for each comment. Please also recommend an overall grade out of 10.

The question I set:

A public commission must choose one of three policies, A, B, or C, without knowing which of three economic conditions, S1, S2, or S3, will obtain. Payoffs are measured in units of advantage, with higher numbers better.

           S1   S2   S3
Policy A   10    4    4
Policy B    9   12    3
Policy C    1    4    7

(a) Define the maximin and minimax regret decision rules.
(b) Apply each rule to this table, showing your work, and state which policy each rule selects.
(c) Explain how these two rules relate to the disagreement between Rawls and Harsanyi about how the parties in the original position, behind the veil of ignorance, choose principles of justice.

The student's submitted answer:

Maximin instructs us to identify the worst possible outcome under each policy and then choose the policy whose worst outcome is best. Here the minima are A=4, B=3, and C=1, so maximin selects A. Minimax regret instructs us to construct a regret table by comparing each outcome to the best outcome available in the same state, and then to choose the policy whose maximum regret is smallest. The best outcomes by state are S1=10, S2=12, and S3=7. The regret table is:

           S1   S2   S3
Policy A    0    8    3
Policy B    1    0    4
Policy C    9    8    0

The maximum regrets are A=8, B=4, and C=9, so minimax regret selects B.

For part (c), Rawls uses maximin in the original position. Because the parties do not know which social position they will occupy, they select the arrangement whose worst-off position is best; this means maximizing the minimum level of utility a citizen can end up with, and it yields the difference principle. Harsanyi argues that the parties should instead assign an equal objective probability to being each person in society and maximize expected utility. Since each person's position receives the same probability, expected utility reduces to average utility, which gives Harsanyi's average utilitarian result. Rawls and Harsanyi therefore disagree about whether ignorance permits probabilities: Rawls refuses to assign them, while Harsanyi treats each position as an objective lottery.
