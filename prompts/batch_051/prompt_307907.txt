I spent my career as an experimental physicist, and I have only recently begun reading moral philosophy in earnest. Over the past few months I have been working through the literature on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and the like—are morally binding, and in particular whether a visitor ought to follow a local convention that their own culture lacks. I had tentatively settled on the view that well-established conventions bind, at least to some degree, when others reasonably rely on them. A member of my reading group then set out the following argument, which he claims he has reconstructed from the books he has read. It strikes me as valid, and yet its conclusion seems to overturn almost everything I wanted to keep.

(1) A social convention is a regularity in behaviour to which people in a group conform because they expect others to conform, and on whose persistence they rely when making their choices.
(2) If a person reasonably relies on a pattern of behaviour, and would be set back by its breach, that reliance can ground a moral reason for others to conform to the pattern.
(3) Therefore, for any well-established convention, participants have a pro tanto moral obligation to conform, and this obligation binds visitors and locals alike, since the reliance of those one encounters is the same.
(4) In certain countries, paying small bribes to officials in order to obtain routine permits is a well-established convention in exactly this sense: everyone expects the payment, the officials depend on it for their income, and applicants plan around it.
(5) So in those countries a visitor has a pro tanto moral obligation to pay the bribe.
(6) But no one can have a pro tanto moral obligation to do what is morally wrong, and bribery is morally wrong.
(7) Hence the practice described in (4) cannot be a convention that generates a pro tanto obligation, even though it satisfies the definition in (1) and the test in (2).
(8) Since it does satisfy them, the definition and the test must be too permissive; so everyday conventions, merely as conventions, never generate moral obligations.

He added that the argument is meant as a forced choice rather than a report of common opinion: either we accept that a visitor has a pro tanto duty to bribe, or we deny that conventions bind through reasonable reliance. But the second, he says, is what powers the clear cases—the visitor who ought to tip in a culture where tips are expected, the queue-jumper who wrongs those already waiting, the tourist who ought not to jaywalk where pedestrians and drivers rely on compliance. A great deal turns on which premise I give up.

I want to keep the verdicts about the tip and the queue, but I also find it hard to accept that a visitor acquires any obligation to bribe. Please go through the argument step by step and tell me where it goes wrong, if it goes wrong: which premise you would reject, why it seemed secure, and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step is vulnerable, I would like to know which rejection is least costly, and whether anything salvageable from the argument still supports the claim that some conventions bind visitors.
