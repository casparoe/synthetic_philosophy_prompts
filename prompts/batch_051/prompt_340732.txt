I've been argueing with a friend about something that won't leave me alone. My friend is a big Mill person: he'll tell you it's insane to let a government rule over people who have no say in it, that being subject to the arbitrary will of another is a kind of servitude, and that the only way people learn to care about the common good is by actually participating in governing themselves. Fine. But the same friend is also a very confident defender of ordinary private firms, where the people doing the work have no vote over the person who can set their hours, monitor them, and fire them. When I push him, he says that's different: you voluntarily signed a contract, the owners put up the capital, efficiency needs a chain of command, and anyway the state has a monopoly on force and the boss doesn't. So political autocracy is tyranny, but workplace autocracy is just business.

Then I started reading Mill's later writings on socialism, and it made the question sharper. Mill seems to think the future belongs to worker cooperatives, where the laborers collectively own the capital and elect and remove their own managers, while he's suspicious of state socialism, the kind where a centralized government owns and runs everything. He seems to want the democratizing part of socialism without the central-planning part. But here's what I can't square: if Mill's reasons for wanting political democracy also apply to the workplace (and in those passages they seem to), then on what prinicple is a wage-labor firm with an autocratic boss acceptable right now? And if the principle is "cooperatives are better, but workers should get there by voluntary choice rather than by law," doesn't that same gradualism apply to political despotism too, where we'd find it monstrous?

Act as a political philosopher who knows Mill's later work well; think carefully and start by brainstorming two or three candidate justifications before settling on the best one. I want the strongest principle that says politics must be democratic in a way firms need not be, even though firms are where people spend a third of their lives. Then test it hard: does it survive a small firm in a company town, a giant platform that sets wages by algorithm, or a genuinely competitive labor market where exit is easy? And give me an honest verdict on whether Mill in his late socialist mood would accept the asymmetry, or whether my friend is using Mill for the state and dropping him at the factory gate.
