Earlier this week I had a long argument with a friend about whether two distinct things can be made of the same matter at the same time. She is a one-object person: where I see a statue and a lump, she sees one thing under two descriptions. By the end she had me doubting several of my own verdicts, and I want to know whether any single principle about material constitution and identity over time captures what I actually believe. Here are the verdicts I endorse, as honestly as I can report them.

1. The bronze statue and the lump. On Monday a foundry casts a lump of bronze; on Tuesday a sculptor hammers it into a statue of a fish. Before Tuesday there is a lump and no statue; after Tuesday both exist. The statue is not identical with the lump, because if the sculptor flattens the bronze the statue is destroyed while the lump survives. I therefore count two things in one place.

2. The paperweight and the lump of glass. A factory takes a rough lump of glass and stamps it into a paperweight. Here I want to say there is just one thing: the very same lump has become a paperweight. I can't yet give a principled difference between this case and case 1, and my friend made much of that.

3. Tibbles and Tib. A cat, Tibbles, sits on a mat. Let 'Tib' name everything that is Tibbles except the tail. I take Tib to be a cat too, since lacking a tail doesn't make a cat a non-cat. Before the tail is amputated, Tibbles and Tib are plainly different: one has a tail as a part and the other does not. After the amputation, though, I refuse to say there are two cats on the mat. But Tib lost nothing in the amputation—the tail was never part of Tib—so if Tib existed before, Tib exists after. After the amputation, Tibbles and Tib perfectly coincide. So I seem forced either to accept two coincident cats, or to deny that Tib was a cat, or to say that two things that were distinct have become identical. None of these is comfortable.

4. The continuously rebuilt ship. A wooden ship is repaired over many years until no original plank remains. I say it is the same ship throughout. When the discarded original planks are reassembled into a second ship, I say that second ship is a distinct ship, not the original. Continuity of the working vessel, not preservation of the original planks, carries the identity.

5. The house and its bricks. A brick house is built from a determinate set of bricks, and over decades every brick is replaced. I say the same house persists, and I deny that the house is identical with any particular aggregate of bricks, since the aggregate changes while the house remains.

There is also the case my friend pressed and I kept dodging: a person and her body. Before death, I don't want to say that two things are sitting in the chair when I visit a friend; after death, I want to say that the body remains while the person is gone. That pair of verdicts sounds as if I do believe in a person distinct from her body, coinciding for a lifetime.

Is there one account of what material objects are and how a thing relates to its matter over time that delivers all or nearly all of my verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show how it treats each numbered case, and tell me what it predicts for the person/body case and for one further case I haven't listed. If not, tell me which of my verdicts are the least costly to abandon and what the simplest defensible position is once they go.
