I'm an undergraduate writing a term paper for a seminar on the concept of liberty, and next week I present it to the class. My thesis is that the republican liberty defended by Philip Pettit should not be classified as a third concept of liberty alongside the positive and negative concepts Berlin distinguished, but rather as a reworking of the negative concept: what republicanism substitutes is not self-mastery but a different criterion of the relevant absence — security against arbitrary power rather than mere actual non-interference. My argument has three parts. First, Berlin's negative liberty and Pettit's non-domination are both "absence" accounts; only positive liberty is an account of self-direction, so lumping republicanism with positive liberty mislocates it. Second, the hard case that is supposed to show republicanism is distinct — the slave whose master is benevolent and never interferes — is explained once freedom is taken to consist in not living under another's arbitrary will; the evil is the insecurity of one's status, not only interference. Third, the institutional demands republicans draw from non-domination, such as contestatory democracy and protections against dependence, follow from making that security constitutive of liberty, not from importing an independent perfectionist ideal of civic virtue. I think this yields a cleaner taxonomy: negative liberty comes in an unsecured and a secured variant, and republicanism names the secured variant.

The audience: a senior seminar whose instructor works on liberal egalitarianism and treats freedom as a value to be balanced against equality and the basic liberties; most students are convinced Berlin got the negative/positive distinction right and that negative liberty is the only workable liberal ideal; two students, however, have been reading the Skinner line on neo-Roman liberty and will think I'm dissolving republicanism into negative liberty and losing its distinctive civic character. Prepare me for the questions I will actually get. Give me the objections in roughly the order of how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this audience would put them; for each, a short reply and an honest note on where the reply is weak; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; and the one objection I should hope nobody asks. As you go, make clear which parts of my argument each objection targets. Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
