I'm giving a paper at a workshop where half the room is evolutionary biologists and the other half is AI alignment researchers. I'm defending the claim that once you have machine agents that can aquire resources, copy themselves, and pass traits to their copies, the long-run distribution of machine intelligence is set mostly by Darwinian selection among those agents—and that selection, not the goals we try to install, is what actually determines whether AIs eventually sideline humans, so "AI takeover by natural selection" should be treated as a live theoretical claim rather than a loose analogy. My sketch of the argument: replication + heritable variation + differential success gives selection; instrumental convergence means the traits selection favors (resource acquisition, self-preservation, outcompeting rivals) will tend to look pretty bad for human interests; and the equilibrium of that process need not respect anything like humane values. The evolutionary biologists in the room are strict about what counts as a Darwinian population and will roll their eyes at any selection story that doesn't specify the unit of selection; the alignment people think inner/outer alignment and orthogonality already cover this and that adding evolution just muddies the waters. Prepare me for the question period: what will each camp object to, phrased the way they'd actually ask it, which objections are likely versus damaging, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one reply that has to land for the talk to survive—plus the question I should most hope nobody brings up.
