So I'm in a slightly odd spot. My aunt died last spring and I spent most of May clearing out her house, and I couldn't stop noticing how much I was inferring about her—her beliefs, her routines, the little institutions of her life—from the stuff she left. There was a drawer that was nothing but used birthday candles and restaurant napkins, and i honestly couldn't decide whether it meant she was sentimental, frugal, or just had a drawer where things went to die. The weirdest feeling was how much of the story I was telling about her was really just me projecting, and it made me wonder how much of archaeology is the same move. Now I'm TAing an intro archaeology seminar this fall (about 18 undergrads, mostly non-majors, 75 minutes a session) and the theme of the week is how archaeologists reconstruct beliefs and institutions from material remains. I want to turn that feeling into a classroom activity, but i want them to feel the underdetermination before anyone names it: how easy it is to build a confident story from a few objects, how the same objects can support three incompatible stories, and how the surviving stuff is not a neutral sample—it's shaped by what people kept, lost, broke, or threw away, and by what happened to rot. Design something runnable for me, with actual rules, materials I can pull together from a thrift store and recycling bins, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the general problem of inferring beliefs and institutions from objects. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of real archaeological inference the exercise cant reproduce and I'll have to fill in myself. No lecture up front—i want them to play the overconfident archaeologist first and only afterwards find out what they were doing.
