Here's the way I was taught to split up writing systems: an alphabet is a script where each vowel and each consonant has its own grapheme and you write the graphemes out in the order they're spoken; a syllabary is a script where each distinct syllable gets its own sign, and the sign for /ka/ tells you nothing about the sign for /ki/ — syllable signs are opaque units.

Now imagine a script built for a language with only CV syllables, twelve consonants and three vowels. Every consonant is a base shape, and the vowel is indicated by rotating that shape: upright writes /a/, 90 degrees writes /i/, 180 degrees writes /u/. So /ka/, /ki/, and /ku/ are the same mark in three orientations. A final consonant is a small tick appended to the rotated shape. The system is completely regular, speakers read it fluently, and it clearly represents consonants and vowels seperately — but no vowel can be written on its own, and no consonant either, for that matter.

Is this an alphabet, a syllabary, or neither? Go through the criteria one by one, and where the verdict turns on a word like 'has its own grapheme,' say whether the criterion is carrying weight it can't bear. If the right call is a third category, explain what that third category says about what the script actually encodes — and what it still doesn't capture about the question of how phonemic an alphabet has to be.
