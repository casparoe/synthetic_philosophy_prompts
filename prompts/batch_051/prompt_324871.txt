I am a junior and research lead on my high school competitive debate team, and I keep running into the same problem: other teams build entire cases around vivid hypotheticals, and the vividness seems to do most of the persuading. These scenarios range from a surgeon dividing up one healthy body to save five patients, to a runaway trolley, to a society choosing principles from behind a veil of ignorance. Our coach calls them intuition pumps and says they can be genuinely illuminating or quietly misleading, but I have never found a reusable way to tell the two apart. The stakes are ordinary tournament rounds, but the issue matters to me outside debate too, since I suspect some of my own political views arrived by well-told hypotheticals rather than by evidence. The practical constraint is time: I often have about twenty minutes before a round or late at night while preparing files, I have no formal training in philosophy or cognitive science, and whatever I do has to be defensible when my coach asks me why I answered a case the way I did.

Please give me a practical decision procedure, such as a checklist, a flowchart in words, or a set of tests to run in order, drawn from what is actually known about thought experiments and intuition pumps, that a high school debater can apply under those conditions. The procedure should distinguish cases where a thought experiment is doing legitimate work, such as exposing an inconsistency or testing the scope of a principle, from cases where it persuades by making a bizarre case feel typical or by manufacturing discomfort through framing. It should also distinguish cases where the lesson survives changing the details from cases where the lesson depends on details that could not occur in the real world. Include two or three worked applications, at least one of which should be a thought experiment I currently find persuasive but your procedure says I should reject or be suspicious of. Finally, be explicit about which steps are judgment calls that no procedure can eliminate, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what the procedure cannot do.
