In a comparative ethics course I'm taking, we studied two contrasting professional norms for handling refusal and criticism. One style, which the course associated with Dutch workplaces, treats directness as a matter of respect: if a proposal is weak, a colleague should say so plainly, and to soften the point is to fail to treat the hearer as an equal. The other style, associated with Japanese workplaces, treats explicit refusal and open criticism as crude or harmful: disagreement is conveyed through hesitation, indirection, and polite formulas, and the hearer is expected to infer what has not been said outright.

I'm trying to work out what commitments about truth, deception, and respect each of these practices presupposes, and whether their commitments are actually incompatible. Does the indirect style presuppose that a white lie, or at least a deliberately false implication, is sometimes not merely excusable but required — that truthfulness must sometimes give way to a stronger duty to protect the hearer's standing or the working relationship? Or does it presuppose that such indirection is not deception at all, because the speaker is not trying to induce a false belief but rather declining to announce an unpleasant fact explicitly, within a convention the hearer understands? And does the direct style presuppose the opposite view — that truthfulness simply is what respect requires, so that a softened criticism is a disguised form of contempt for the hearer's autonomy?

I would like to know whether both practices can be justified by a single underlying principle — for instance, one principle about when truth-telling is owed, or one principle about what it is to respect a person — once local conditions are taken into account, or whether each practice rules out the theory implicit in the other. Where they are incompatible, what would have to change in one practice if the other's presupposed view of the ethics of truth were adopted? Please distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice leaves the philosophical question underdetermined.
