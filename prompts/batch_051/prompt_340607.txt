I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, so when I end up in constitutional debates the same comparison keeps walking through the door and it's started to bug me. Someone will say a constitution is basically Odysseus having himself tied to the mast: the people know they'll be weak or panicked or furious later, so in a lucid moment they bind themselves to some rules and hand judges the ropes. The restriction isn't against democracy, the line goes; its democracy choosing to be protected from itself. I've seen the image used to justify entrenched rights, debt brakes, central bank independence, and courts striking down laws a current majority plainly wants.

The image comes from book 12 of the Odyssey, where Circe tells Odysseus how to handle the Sirens (I'm using Samuel Butler's translation). She says: "First you will come to the Sirens who enchant all who come near them. If any one unwarily draws in too close and hears the singing of the Sirens, his wife and children will never welcome him home again, for they sit in a green field and warble him to death with the sweetness of their song. There is a great heap of dead men's bones lying all around, with the flesh still rotting off them. Therefore pass these Sirens by, and stop your men's ears with wax that none of them may hear; but if you like you can listen yourself, for you may get the men to bind you as you stand upright on a cross piece half way up the mast, and they must lash the rope's ends to the mast itself, that you may have the pleasure of listening. If you beg and pray the men to unloose you, then they must bind you faster."

What I'd like is an audit of how far that analogy actually carries. Work out the correspondence explicitly in prose: what maps onto the ship, the crew, the wax, the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, Odysseus's order, the present generation, the future one, and the detail where he begs to be untied and is tied tighter. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the conclusion people draw from it — that judges should enforce these precommitments against a current majority — and which are just scenery. If the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, say so, and offer a better one if you think there is one.

One favor: separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.

And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
