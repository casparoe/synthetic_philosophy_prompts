At a recent workshop on the concept of disease, a colleague defended a naturalist account of disorder by pressing what he took to be a decisive analogy. An organism, he argued, is a machine; disease is to the body what malfunction is to an artifact. A pump that no longer moves water is broken as a matter of mechanical fact, not of normative judgement, and an organ that no longer performs its natural function is diseased in the same value-free sense. The analogy was doing real work: it licensed his further conclusion that disease is an empirical category, and that normativists are mistaking the distress a condition causes for the condition itself. Before I object, I want the analogy audited properly. Set out the correspondence table in full: what in the organism is supposed to correspond to the artifact's designer, blueprint, intended performance, normal wear, and breakdown. Then identify the disanalogies and sort them into those that are merely decorative and those that threaten the argument. In particular, say whether natural selection supplies a non-arbitrary specification of an organ's function in the way a designer's intention does; whether statistical normality can stand in for such a specification without quietly importing a norm; and whether the harmfulness of a condition can be read off the machinery at all. Run the audit against at least one troublesome case, such as a clinically significant departure from species-typical functioning that is asymptomatic, or a condition whose harmfulness appears only against a particular social or environmental background. Finally, say whether the value-free claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if it does not, specify the strongest revised version of the view that remains defensible, and say whether there is a better analogy than the machine for what disease is.
