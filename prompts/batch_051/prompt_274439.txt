So I've been reading around the functions debate in philosophy of biology for a piece I'm writing, and the thing that keeps hitting me is how long its been going in circles — roughly fifty years of very smart people not moving each other an inch. The two camps, as I understand them: the etiological account says the function of a trait is what it was selected for, so a heart's function is pumping blood, not making the lub-dub sound, because pumping is why hearts stuck around. The causal-role account says no, the function of a part is whatever it contributes to some larger capacity you've chosen to analyze — so relative to circulation the heart's function is pumping, but relative to, say, diagnosing by ear, the sound might count. What bugs me is that each side handles cases the other can't: the etiological story gets malfunction and vestigial organs naturally (a busted kidney fails to do what it's for), while the causal-role story gets novel or co-opted traits and physiology where nobody knows the selection history. So my working hunch is that this might not be one debate at all — more like two diffrent jobs ("why is it there?" vs. "what does it do in this system?") that both happen to use the word 'function.' But I don't want to print that without someone pushing on it. What I need is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is the stalemate verbal, substantive, or really about which cases get to count as central? And if it is two projects in a trenchcoat, what would actually make that stick — a distinction both sides could accept, a better word, an argument that one project needs the other? Practically, when a doctor says a kidney is 'not functioning,' which account are they leaning on, and does it matter?
