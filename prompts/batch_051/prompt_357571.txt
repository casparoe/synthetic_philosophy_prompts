Two decision-theoretic camps have been arguing for several years about how to interpret a famous choice problem and have now committed to a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they would issue.

First, fix the problem they are addressing. Urn K contains 100 balls, 50 red and 50 black. Urn U contains 100 balls, each red or black, in an unknown proportion. Four bets are on offer, each paying $100 if a ball of the named colour is drawn from the named urn: RK, BK, RU, BU. The contested pattern of choice is this: RK is preferred to RU, BK is preferred to BU, while RK and BK are treated as equally good, and RU and BU are treated as equally good. Both camps accept that this pattern is robust among careful, reflective subjects; they disagree about its normative status.

Camp 1 is the single-prior expected-utility camp, in the subjectivist tradition. On this view, a rational agent should hold one subjective probability distribution over the relevant states and maximize expected utility with respect to it, so the pattern above must be a mistake, an artifact of the presentation, or the product of some hidden asymmetry between the options.

Camp 2 is the ambiguity-aversion camp, represented by a multiple-prior maxmin expected-utility model. On this view, when evidence is too sparse or ambiguous to justify a single prior, a rational agent may entertain a set of priors and evaluate each act by the worst-case expected utility over that set; the two-urn pattern then follows as a natural response to the missing information.

Write the statement in the following format. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; support each claim from the described scenario or from explicit reasoning.

Section 1 — Numbered agreed claims. Begin with the formal fact that no single-prior expected-utility representation can reproduce the full pattern, and show why using the two-urn description. Then give further signed claims, none of them vacuous: include at least one claim each camp regards as a genuine concession to the other, and at least one substantive claim about what the dispute does and does not turn on.

Section 2 — A disagreements table. Use three columns: (i) the point of disagreement stated neutrally; (ii) Camp 1's wording of its own position; (iii) Camp 2's wording of its own position. Each camp's wording should be one that camp would accept as a fair statement of its side, not a caricature written by its opponent.

Section 3 — Cruxes. Address the following three proposed cruxes in order, and add a fourth only if the statement cannot do without it. For each crux, state a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both camps agree bears on it; record each camp's prediction in advance; and specify what result would make each camp move.

(a) Joint evaluation and the sure-loss argument. If the decision maker is shown the four bets as a portfolio, or is offered the chance to exchange RU for RK and BU for BK for a small fee each, does the pattern survive? Both camps should sign the arithmetic that RU plus BU and RK plus BK each guarantee $100 no matter which ball is drawn, and that accepting both exchanges for fees turns a guaranteed $100 into a guaranteed $100 minus the fees. State whether Camp 1 treats this as a decisive objection, and what Camp 2 must say to avoid the conclusion without changing the subject.

(b) Compound lotteries. Suppose urn U is filled by a known random mechanism: a fair coin is flipped, heads meaning the urn is filled 80 red and 20 black, tails meaning 20 red and 80 black, and the coin is hidden after it determines the composition. Does knowing this second-order distribution eliminate the ambiguity, or does ambiguity aversion persist until the realized composition is itself observed? Record which reduction or non-reduction principle each camp commits itself to, and what persistence or disappearance of the pattern would show for each.

(c) Matching probabilities and complementarity. For an uncertain event, an agent can be asked what known-probability lottery is exactly as good as a bet on that event; call this the matching probability. Both camps should sign that an ambiguity-averse agent's matching probabilities for red-in-U and black-in-U would each be below one-half. The crux is whether their summing to less than one is a violation of a basic coherence condition on belief, or a legitimate numerical measure of how much information is missing. Record which conclusion each camp draws and under what circumstances it would revise it.

Section 4 — Verbal-disagreement note. State which parts of the dispute, if any, both camps now agree were verbal or definitional rather than substantive, and which parts remain substantive. Be specific about which camp is using which sense of any contested term.

Keep the whole statement under 1,500 words. End the document with a last line beginning "Summary:" that gives a one-sentence summary of the joint statement.
