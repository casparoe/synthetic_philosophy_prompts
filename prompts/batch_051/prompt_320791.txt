I am writing a paper on the absurdity doctrine in statutory interpretation — the principle that a court may decline to apply a statute's literal language when doing so would produce a result so absurd that the enacting legislature could not have intended it. A friend from my study group has been pressing the argument below, and I find its conclusion hard to accept because it seems to prove too much: nearly every interpretive school, including many self-described textualists, accepts some version of the doctrine, and the doctrine appears indispensable for handling obvious drafting mistakes. But I cannot locate the step where the argument goes wrong.

(1) Statutory interpretation must recover the meaning the legislature actually enacted, not the meaning a later judge would prefer it had enacted.
(2) The absurdity doctrine may permit a court to depart from the plain meaning of an applicable text only when applying that text would yield a result no rational legislature could have intended — not merely a result the court considers bad policy, unjust, or a probable legislative oversight.
(3) In the hard cases where the doctrine is seriously invoked, the text is by hypothesis clear and the surrounding statutory scheme underdetermines the application; otherwise the case could be resolved by ordinary textual or contextual argument without appealing to absurdity.
(4) So in these cases, the court's judgment that the result is absurd must be driven by its own background conception of what a rational legislature could intend.
(5) But that background conception is not a neutral textual datum; it is the judge's view of what counts as intolerably bad policy, filtered through the judge's estimate of the legislature's competence, rationality, and goodwill.
(6) It follows that the absurdity doctrine, exactly where it does real work, licenses a judge to set aside clear statutory text on the strength of the judge's own view that the text's result is intolerably bad policy.
(7) If judges may set aside clear text whenever they find its result intolerably bad policy, then the distinction between interpreting and amending a statute disappears, and enacted text binds only when the judge already finds its application reasonable.
(8) Thus the doctrine is either a standing license for judicial amendment or a vacuous restatement of what ordinary construction already does; it cannot be the modest corrective its defenders claim.

Diagnose this argument. If it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and describe the resulting position. If it contains a subtle misstep, identify the step, explain why that step looked plausible, and say what a court must be doing when it invokes the doctrine for that step to fail. I would most like to keep (1) and (2); I am willing to give up the either-or framing of (8) if the doctrine admits of a defensible middle characterization. In either case, explain how a defensible version of the doctrine blocks the slide from the modest starting point in (2) to the judicial-amendment conclusion drawn in (6) and (7).
