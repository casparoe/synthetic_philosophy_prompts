So I got called for jury duty last month, and during selection the judge basically told us that being picked at random is the entire point: the law isn't asking for experts, it wants a dozen or so ordinary people who saw the same evidence and have to agree on it. And I believed her, its genuinely moving. But then I went home and started thinking about how the exact same random process would sound completely insane if you proposed it for a mayor or a governor. Nobody would say, let's pull a name off the voter rolls and make her governor, because ordinary people are the whole point. They'd say she has no platform, no experience, nobody voted for her, and you can't hold her accountable. So here's what I want to know: what does that practice actually presuppose? What view of what judging requires — or what governing requires — is the court system quietly committing itself to when it trusts a randomly drawn jury but would never trust a randomly drawn city council? Is the real distinction about competence, or legitimacy, or accountability, or about the difference between applying a law someone else wrote and making the law? And the part that bothers me alot: if the reasons we give for trusting juries are true, shouldn't we take them seriously all the way up? Like, if ordinary people are basically sound enough to decide guilt, why not pick a legislature (or one chamber of it) by lottery too, and call it a day? I think there's a word for that — lottocracy? — and everyone I've mentioned it to reacts like I proposed a coin-flip government. So reconstruct the implicit theory behind the jury system, tell me whether it's consistent with the way we'd all react to a lottery legislature, and say what would have to change in how we justify juries or elections if the line between them turns out not to hold.
