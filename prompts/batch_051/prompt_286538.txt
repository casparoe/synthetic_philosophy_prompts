I'm a novelist, not a geologist, but I've fallen down an Anthropocene rabbit hole because a character in my next book is a stratographer. The thing that keeps nagging me is how geological periods get their boundaries: a committee picks a single physical marker, a "golden spike" in some rock or ice or sediment, and that one spot then defines the official start of the period. For the Anthropocene the candidates run from 1610 (the Orbis spike, a dip in atmospheric CO2 after the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas) to the mid-20th century Great Acceleration, with its plutonium layer from nuclear tests. And it struck me that this is weirdly close to how we periodize literature. We retroactively pick a date — 1922 for modernism, say, because of Ulysses and The Waste Land — and then teach the period as if the boundary were real, even though the poems and novels on both sides obviously bleed into each other. So is this a deep parallel about how institutions impose clean boundaries on messy continuous change, or is it just superficial, since geology has a physical spike you can point to and lit does not? I can't decide, and I'd rather not build a whole novel on a fake analogy. Also, please separate what's actually settled among geologists and literary scholars from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if everyone agrees on it.
