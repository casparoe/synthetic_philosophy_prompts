For a term paper in a class that is covering Marx, I have been reading about his view that under capitalism the labor-saving potential of new machinery is systematically turned against workers: output per hour rises, but the gains show up as layoffs or speed-ups rather than a shorter working day or higher pay. The assignment asks me to design a concrete mechanism that would make automation benefit workers, and then to analyze how the mechanism would actually behave once firms and workers respond to it. Here is the rule I want to analyze.

Any firm with more than fifty employees that introduces a "labor-substituting" technology—defined as software or equipment that does work previously done by its own employees—must, within one year, choose one of two options. Option A: keep the affected workers employed at 90% of their previous pay and 90% of their previous hours, with the released 10% of the time reserved for training or lower-priority tasks. Option B: pay into a central fund an automation dividend equal to 150% of the wages eliminated, every year for five years; the fund is used to subsidize firms that shorten their full-time workweek without cutting weekly pay. Firms self-report whether a new technology counts as labor-substituting, subject to random audits.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral proposal, and explain how it would play out. What does it incentivize among firms—for example, around how they classify technologies, arrange subcontracting, or structure establishments to stay below the threshold—and among workers, technology vendors, and the administrators of the fund? Where would sophisticated firms find ways to game it, and which forms of gaming would be hardest for auditors to detect? What second-order effects would likely appear for wages, for which jobs get automated first, for the pace of adoption, and for the structure of firms? Would the scheme selectively reward automation that genuinely benefits workers, in the way Marx thought machinery should be able to shorten the working day, or would it reward something else? Which failure modes could be fixed by tightening the rule, and which seem built into it? Finally, please be explicit about the weakest link in your analysis: which empirical assumption about how firms or workers behave would most likely flip your overall assessment if it were wrong, and what consideration would change your mind?
