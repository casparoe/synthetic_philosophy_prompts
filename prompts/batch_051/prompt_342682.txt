You are an expert on software copyright and copyleft theory who can also reason like a working programmer. Take the Ship of Theseus and run it on a codebase. Suppose a mature GPL-licensed numerical library, call it 40,000 lines, is maintained by a foundation. At some commit, a clean-room team begins replacing it function by function: for each public function they write tests only from the documented API and the original binary's observable behavior, then reimplement the function without reading the original source. Signatures, public API, and module boundaries are preserved; internal algorithms, data structures, and identifier choices are made independently. After several years every original line has been replaced, while the API, the test suite, and the overall architecture remain. At what point, if any, should the resulting codebase count as, or cease to be, a derivative work of the original for copyleft purposes?

Run the question through both the literal-copying and non-literal-structure analyses, including the abstraction-filtration-comparison test, the merger and scenes-a-faire limitations, and the usual copyleft positions on APIs, linking, and plug-ins. In the classic puzzle the live answers are same-ship, new-ship, and no-fact-of-the-matter; map those onto the derivative-work question and say which answer the legal doctrines, as you understand them, actually select. Start by brainstorming the strongest case for each of the main answers, then take a definite position and defend it against the others. Reason from the doctrines themselves rather than citing case law or commentators.
