I have been developing a measure of civilizational advancement that I call round-trip mastery. Kardashev's scale ranks civilizations by the largest scale at which they command energy flows; Barrow's inverted scale ranks them by the smallest structures they can deliberately manipulate. Both, I think, measure control in only one direction. A civilization that can trigger fission but cannot neutralize its long-lived products, or one that can assemble molecular machinery but cannot disassemble it, possesses only half-mastery at that scale. My proposal is that a civilization is advanced to the degree that its interventions are reversible: for every scale at which it can cause a transition, it can also reliably suppress, undo, or restore the prior state at that scale. I suspect round-trip mastery, not one-way reach, is what genuine capability consists in, since one-way power is largely destructive in disguise. I want an honest assessment: is this a coherent metric or a metaphor dressed up as measurement? What does it get right that Kardashev and Barrow miss? Does it already exist under another name? Give the strongest objections, say exactly where it breaks, and identify the case it most obviously misclassifies.
