A city arts council is considering scrapping its peer-review grant process and replacing it with a mechanism built around Tolstoy's theory of art. Tolstoy held, roughly, that a work is art insofar as the artist, having genuinely lived through a feeling, transmits that feeling to an audience through the work so that the audience is infected by it; and he held that infectiousness depends on the individuality of the feeling, the clearness with which it is expressed, and the sincerity of the artist. The council's proposal tries to operationalize exactly this.

Under the proposal, any resident may submit a work anonymously. A standing panel of four hundred paid residents, refreshed by half every two years and demographically rebalanced to match the city, encounters each work in a standardized room while their skin conductance, heart rate, and facial expressions are recorded. Afterward each panelist reports which emotions, if any, they felt; rates their intensity and clarity on a ten-point scale; and answers one further question: how confident are they, from one to ten, that the maker has genuinely lived through the feeling just transmitted. A work's infection score combines three components: reach (the fraction of panelists reporting at least one emotion at intensity six or above), depth (median intensity multiplied by median clarity), and sincerity (median confidence rating). Works scoring above a fixed threshold receive multi-year grants, with the largest grants going to the highest scores. The council frames this as replacing fashion and professional bias with Tolstoy's own criterion, measured as directly as technique allows.
