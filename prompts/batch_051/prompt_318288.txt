I'm a retired chemist (organic, mostly) and I've only recently started reading philosophy, so bear with me if I mangle a distinction or two. I've been working through the writings on nudges and paternalism, and I keep comming back to a worry. The standard line seems to be that a nudge is the gentle alternative: it steers people toward better choices but leaves every option open, so it should be less objectionable than an outright mandate. But a mandate is at least out in the open. You can see it, argue with it, vote against it. A nudge can work by sneaking past the person's awareness, using defaults or framing or placement to get them to do what the designer wants without their noticing. I'm trying to get clear on whether the usual defense of nudges survives once you stop equating less force with more respect.

So here's what I'd like you to build. Give me a realistic scenario — a workplace, a hospital, a cafeteria, an enrollment form, that sort of thing — with a nudge and a mandate that both target the same behavior. The nudge has to preserve choice in the technical sense: no option removed, no real cost added, no deception about the facts. The mandate has to be a genuine mandate: some announced rule or penalty, not just a firmer nudge. In your walk-through, I want two positions on the table. Position A is the standard soft-paternalism line: the less an intervention limits choices, the milder it is, so the nudge beats the mandate. Position B is a transparency-or-respect line: an intervention is objectionable to the degree that it bypasses or hides from the person's own decision-making, so a hidden nudge can be worse than an open mandate. Please construct the scenario so that Position B ranks the nudge as more objectionable than the mandate, while Position A still ranks the mandate as the heavier interference. The point is to test whether libertarian paternalism can keep its preference for nudges once transparency and respect go on the scorecard — so don't just stipulate that one is worse; make it fall out of the design of the two interventions.

Then do the work. (1) State the target behavior and the two interventions as exactly as you can. (2) Walk through what Position A says about each and what Position B says about each, and show that the verdicts really come out the way I specified. (3) Say which single feature of the case is doing the real work — hiddenness, the particular bias being exploited, the fact that the mandate would be publicly announced, whatever it is. (4) Tell me what happens if you remove that one feature and leave everything else fixed: does the ranking snap back to the usual one? (5) Give the strongest reply a defender of nudging could make — for instance, they could deny that a good nudge needs to be hidden, or insist that the case only works by smuggling in a nudge that is unusually manipulative.

One more constraint: the nudge shouldn't be trivially bad. No lying about the facts, no exploiting a severe cognitive impairment, no mandate that's really just a suggestion. I want a garden-variety, well-intentioned nudge — the kind a real behavioral insights team would actually run — that still comes out badly on the transparency view.
