Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend about whether relying on AI for intellectual work erodes understanding, and I came away less interested in who won than in why the argument could not make progress. It began with a concrete case. I have been using a language model to compress my reading notes, and I have noticed that I can now summarize the main claims of things I read several months ago but cannot reconstruct the chains of reasoning behind them. I called this a loss of understanding. My friend, who uses a model to work through derivations he does not trust his own algebra to complete, said I was romanticizing the inside of my head: nobody can reproduce everything they rely on, and offloading is as old as writing. I replied that writing stores the finished thought, whereas the model is doing some of the thinking and handing me back a conclusion. He answered that I still have to decide which questions to ask, which outputs to accept, and how to combine them, so the hard part remains. We repeated versions of this exchange for hours.

The same impasse seems to run through the wider debate about cognitive offloading and epistemic dependence on AI. One side treats dependence on AI as continuous with earlier technology. The printing press, books, calculators, and search engines all moved cognitive work out of the skull; what mattered was never how much one stores but how well one uses external resources: asking the right questions, checking results, knowing when a tool is applicable. On this view AI does not erase understanding; it relocates it and demands new skills of orchestration and evaluation. The other side says there is a difference in kind between storing or retrieving information externally and delegating the movement of thought itself. When I use a calculator I still have to know which operations to run and in what order. When I ask a model to explain an argument, I outsource the selection of concepts, the framing of the question, and the step-by-step inferential labor, and I receive a fluent conclusion I can affirm without having traveled the route. The result, on this side, is a person who can state conclusions competently but cannot defend, modify, or apply them beyond the summary: a veneer of understanding that passes for the real thing.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are using the word understanding differently, one as a capacity to produce the answer and the other as a certain relation to a subject matter, and that the empirical question of what offloading does to the mind matters less than that difference. But I am not confident. The disagreement might instead be normative, or it might rest on a hidden empirical question, or it might be two different questions smuggled under a single word. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict for either side. Is it verbal, substantive, normative, empirical, or several of these layered together? At what exact step does the argument stall? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, whether a distinction, a new kind of study, or a better question? Or is this a dispute in which no progress should be expected, and why?
