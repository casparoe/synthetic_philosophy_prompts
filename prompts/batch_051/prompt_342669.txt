Two people in my reading group got stuck on Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, specifically the move in chapter 3 where he introduces the skeptical solution. The passage they were fighting about is the one where Kripke says, roughly, that once we give up truth conditions for meaning ascriptions, we should look instead at the circumstances under which we are licensed to assert them, and at the role that whole practice plays in our lives. One of them, Priya, took this to mean meaning facts just are social facts: for a person to mean addition is for their community to endorse or correct them under the relevant circumstances. Dana said no, Kripke is only giving assertability conditions, not a metaphysics, and turning that into social constitution makes the community unable to be wrong. Priya replied that the paradox only gets dissolved if something stops the regress of interpretation, and community agreement is the only thing in the text that actually does that; if you don't read it as constitution, you're left with no fact of the matter again. Dana shot back that this is exactly the problem with the solitary rule-follower discussion later in the book: if Robinson Crusoe in isolation can't be a genuine rule-follower, that looks like a bug, not a feature, and papering over it with group agreement turns normativity into majority opinion. Priya said the whole point of a skeptical solution is that normativity is horizontal, not something metaphysical, so accusing it of being majority opinion is just refusing the solution from the start. They went around on this for an hour and neither moved. Can you adjudicate this point by point: what did each get right and wrong about the passage, what does the community reading actually commit you to, and does the Robinson Crusoe worry land? I'm less interested in who you'd side with overall than in which of the actual moves in this exchange hold up.
