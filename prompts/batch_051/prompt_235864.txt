I am a PhD student in the history of biblical interpretation, and a problem about the shape of my own field has begun to bother me. The dispute between literal and allegorical reading of scripture has been running, in recognizable form, at least since Origen's debates with his critics, and it continues through the Antiochene insistence that spiritual readings must be grounded in history, the medieval fourfold scheme and the Reformation's narrowing of it to the literal sense, down to the present standoff between plain-sense inerrantists and theologians who want to recover figural reading. What puzzles me is not that the two sides disagree, but that they have disagreed for this long while, in many cases, sharing the same text, the same canon, and even the same doctrinal commitments — and neither side finds the other's arguments compulsory. The standard moves are familiar. The allegorical or spiritual reader says the bare literal sense mistakes the letter for the spirit, flattens passages that were always meant to be read typologically or mystically, and refuses the freedom of the Spirit to speak beyond the historical occasion of a text. The literal or historical reader says allegory has no principled brake, lets the interpreter make the text say whatever edifies the interpreter, and dissolves the historical character of revelation into timeless moral or spiritual lessons. Each side can cite both scripture and the tradition in its favor, and each side accuses the other of the same thing — substituting private judgment for the text.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about what any given passage means, nor about the historical facts behind any passage, but about what it is for an interpretation to be faithful to the text. On one view, fidelity consists in recovering a determinate historical or authorial sense that constrains every later use of the passage; on the other, fidelity consists in a use of the text within the community of faith in continuity with the text's own divine purpose, for which the literal sense is one constraint among others rather than the meaning. If that is right, the two sides have largely been answering different questions under the single word 'meaning,' and the debate looks interminable because they are not in fact contradicting one another about the same thing. But I am not sure, and I worry the diagnosis is too tidy — it may itself turn a substantive conflict into a verbal one by stipulation.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict or a history. Is this dispute substantive (about a real property of texts, about which method is truth-preserving, about what interpreters owe the text), verbal (about the word 'literal' and its cognates), or normative (about what a community may do with its authoritative texts) — and on what grounds? Use what each side actually claims as evidence for the diagnosis. And given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction or constraint both traditions could accept, a criterion that would let one side win, a change of question that dissolves the dispute, or an argument that the impasse is structural and should be accepted rather than broken.
