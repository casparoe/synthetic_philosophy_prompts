Consider an asymmetry in how many speakers of English judge two pieces of language. A writer can say that some trait belongs to man in general, call the species mankind, describe a period as man's finest hour and an object as man-made, and many readers treat these forms as neutral: man here is taken to mean human being, not male. But if a writer made the parallel moves with a racial term—spoke of the achievements of white, called the species whitekind (parallel to mankind), described an object as white-made—many of those same readers would take the sentences to be about a racial group, would refuse to extend them to all humans, and would find the locution repellent rather than neutral. A satire collected in Douglas Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas (1985) dramatizes this by rewriting an earnest defense of supposedly neutral masculine words as a defense of supposedly neutral white words; the arguments that sound sober in the original look absurd in the transposition.

Do not settle only the question whether the masculine forms are in fact harmless. The question is whether anything non-question-begging justifies the asymmetry in judgment. Give the strongest candidate justification you can find for treating a sex-based default as more acceptable than a race-based default—candidates might include etymology, the different histories and structures of the two hierarchies, the different ways sex and race cut across the species, a claimed real ambiguity in man that white lacks, or something else—then test that candidate against nearby cases involving other social categories, such as nation, age, religion, or class. Say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. End by stating how confident you are in that verdict and singling out the point you are least sure of.
