Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether the mere fact that we exist is evidence that the universe is teeming with observers. Afterward I wrote down what seemed to be the strongest version of his argument, and I want to know whether it goes through.

(1) I know that I exist.
(2) This knowledge is evidence about the world: it rules out any hypothesis on which no observers exist.
(3) But it does not stop there. For any two hypotheses both of which entail that at least one observer exists, the hypothesis that entails the larger total number of observers makes it more probable that I would find myself existing—it offers more candidate lives for me to have been living.
(4) Therefore, my existence favors hypotheses that predict larger numbers of observers over hypotheses that predict smaller numbers.
(5) Consider two otherwise equal hypotheses: Lonely, on which intelligent life arises roughly once per galaxy, and Fertile, on which it arises on billions of planets per galaxy. Both are consistent with everything I have observed so far.
(6) From (4), my own existence favors Fertile over Lonely, so I should assign Fertile a much higher credence even before I turn a telescope on the sky.

Is this argument valid? If it is, then one's own existence by itself forces a high prior probability that the cosmos is full of life. If it is not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected version of that step would have to state. In particular, address whether the argument confuses two readings of I—a particular possible person whose existence is in question, versus a merely indexical way of referring to whichever observer one happens to be—and whether the disputed step is better understood as a suppressed premise rather than an inference from the earlier steps.
