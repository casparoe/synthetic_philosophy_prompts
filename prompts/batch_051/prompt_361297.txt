You are a specialist in Confucian role ethics and comparative moral philosophy. Work slowly: take the three interpretive approaches I name below one at a time, show how each reads the passage, and only then give your own judgment.

I'm puzzling over an application of Analects 4.18 (trans. James Legge):

"In serving his parents, a son may remonstrate with them, but gently; when he sees that they do not incline to follow his advice, he shows an increased degree of reverence, but does not abandon his purpose; and should they punish him, he does not allow himself to murmur."

Here is a case the text's authors plainly did not foresee. A woman is an infectious-disease physician and the adult daughter of a man who became a prominent anti-vaccination campaigner late in life. Tonight he is the lead guest on a national evening program, and immediately after his segment the producers have invited her to respond, identified only as an epidemiologist. She has privately remonstrated with him for a year; he has not budged and has grown steadily colder, telling her that a daughter who truly honored him would keep her disagreements inside the family. Minutes before the broadcast he sends her a message saying that if she appears and contradicts him, she will have chosen her career over her father. The producer tells her she is the only expert they could book on this short notice, and that if she declines, his claims about vaccines will stand effectively uncorrected.

Does Analects 4.18 permit her to appear and dispute his claims, require her to decline, or leave the matter underdetermined? Give (a) the reading that follows the words, (b) the reading that follows the purpose of the saying, and (c) the reading a modern Confucian role ethicist might defend. Then state which of these you would apply and why, and say what your answer implies about how the saying should be extended to cases where the costs of private, reverent remonstrance fall on third parties.
