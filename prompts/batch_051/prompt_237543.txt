Consider constitutional monarchy as it actually operates in democracies such as the United Kingdom, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, or Japan. These are functioning representative democracies, yet the head of state is hereditary or otherwise non-elected and stands formally apart from, and sometimes above, the elected government. The precise functions vary, but in many of these systems the monarch opens parliament, gives or withholds assent to legislation as a formal matter, plays some role in appointing the government after an election, and serves as the nominal source in whose name courts, armed forces, or public prosecutions act. In several cases the monarch retains, at least on paper, formal powers such as dissolving a legislature or playing a deciding role in government formation, while convention treats those powers as essentially never to be exercised. The arrangement, remarkably, mostly works.
