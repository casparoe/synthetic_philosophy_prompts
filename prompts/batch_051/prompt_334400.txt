Opponent-process theories of colour vision imply that certain colour experiences are, in principle, impossible: because red and green are processed through a single opponent channel, and blue and yellow through another, no stable unitary percept can be simultaneously reddish and greenish, as opposed to a spatial mosaic, a mixture, or a rapid alternation between the two. Suppose a neurotechnology company claims its headset lets users have a genuinely novel forbidden colour experience, such as a stable, unitary reddish-green, rather than merely describing a familiar colour loosely, alternating between percepts, or confabulating to please the experimenters. How could one test the claim that a subject is having a genuinely novel colour experience of this kind? Design the strongest concrete test you can, specify what behavioural, psychophysical, or neural results would count for and against the novel-qualia interpretation, and say exactly what even a decisive positive result would leave unsettled, for example about whether the subject's colour space has genuinely expanded or whether a new stable label has simply been learned.
