I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about originals, fakes, and forgeries, going off my gut on a bunch of cases, and my verdicts are kind of all over the place. I'd like to know if there's one principle underneath them, or whether I have to toss a couple of my verdicts.

Here they are.

A museum finds out that a painting its displayed for sixty years as a Vermeer is actually a forgery from the 1930s. The object hasn't changed, and the pleasure viewers got out of it was real. I still think it's now worth a tiny fraction of what it was worth, and it should probably move out of the main galleries. I don't think that collapse is just snobbery, but when I try to say why I mostly mumble about history.

A heavily restored painting where maybe twenty percent of the original paint surface survives and the rest is modern retouching. I'd still call it the original painting and much rather own it than a brand-new perfect copy. But the copy probably looks more like the painting originally looked than the restored original does, and that doesnt seem to bother me.

A novel I love: I wouldn't feel I possessed it any more deeply by owning a first printing than my beat-up paperback. But I'd pay an absurd amount for the author's handwritten manuscript, even though the text is identical. So the physical original sometimes matters enormously and sometimes not at all.

A newly discovered poem attributed to a major poet turns out to have been written by a scholar a decade ago. The words haven't changed, and a poem doesn't have an original object the way a painting does. But the poem immediately becomes less interesting to me — more a curiosity about the scholar than literature. I can't make that fit the same rule about physical originals.

A violin that has had nearly every part replaced over two centuries of repairs; only the scroll might be from the original maker. I'd still call it the genuine instrument and think its value is reasonable. A new violin made to the same measurements and aged artificially is worth far less. So it isn't about the wood actually being that old, it's about something like continuous history.

A painting that once hung in Napoleon's bedroom. I don't care at all — fun footnote, but it changes nothing about what I'd pay. Yet who made a painting changes almost everything for me. So not all history matters, only some.

My working rule has been: value comes from the actual history of an object's making, not from how it looks or what I happen to know. But that rule says the heavily restored painting is only twenty percent the real thing, which isn't how I feel. It says nothing about why faked authorship poisons a poem. And it treats my verdict on the exposed forgery as wise, while another part of me is convinced the change in my reaction before and after the announcement is just fetishism.

Is there a coherent principle behind all this, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Is the poem case better handled by a different principle than the painting cases, or is there one account that does both? And how should the principle come down on two cases: one where a forgery has become historically important in its own right and maybe belongs in the main galleries as itself, and one where I hang a known forgery in my house because it gives me real pleasure, with no plan to sell it or present it as genuine — is that attachment rational on whatever principle you extract?
