I'm a PhD student in linguistics working on comparative syntax, and I've kind of slid into the view that noun/verb, case, and adpositions (prepositions/postpositions) are natural kinds — not surface universals, but real joints in the space of possible grammars. Languages that look like counterexamples (flexible word classes, no clear adjective class, case that splits along ergative/accusative lines, quirky subjects) would then still be using the same deep categories with different surface settings. I want to know what would have to be true for this view to be wrong, or for it to collapse into the rival view that these categories are just language-particular conventions and cross-linguistic comparison imposes analyst-defined yardsticks. Which typological facts, acquisition results, or syntactic diagnostics genuinely count against the natural-kind claim, rather than just showing the surface is messier? Which would a defender of my view accept as a cost rather than explain away — is the lack of a clear adjective class in many languages more damaging than the old claim that some Salishan languages lack a noun/verb distinction? Are there cases where adpositions or case markers behave so predicate-like that saving the story looks like theory-saving by fiat? What does the view predict that the conventionalist rival doesn't? And has it been insulated from refutation by distinguishing underlying category from surface realization so flexibly that no language could ever count against it — and if so, is that a vice or just what a theory of invisible structure does? I'd personally find a well-documented language where roots freely occur as arguments and predicates with no detectable categorial asymmetry pretty persuasive, more than acquisition data. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether this view risks anything.
