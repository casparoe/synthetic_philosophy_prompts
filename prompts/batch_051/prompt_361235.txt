Here are the criteria I have been using for when an object is a forgery:

(i) it is presented under an attribution of authorship or provenance;
(ii) that attribution is false;
(iii) the false attribution is maintained deliberately, or with culpable indifference to its truth, by whoever is responsible for the presentation;
(iv) the object's value or standing depends on the false attribution; and
(v) the object fails to stand in the appropriate causal relation to the named maker or period.

Now consider this case. An artist dies leaving a large unfinished canvas, a full set of preparatory drawings for it, and a written instruction that her assistant may complete any unfinished work so that it ends up as the artist intended. The assistant completes the canvas over several months, using the artist's own pigments and following the drawings and instruction in every particular; every choice is traceable to a source left by the artist. The estate then exhibits the canvas under the artist's name alone, with the artist's dates and the date of the canvas on the label, and does not mention the assistant. The assistant is paid as a worker, is not identified as an author, and never raises the issue.

Work through the criteria one at a time. Where the answer to a criterion depends on how it is precisified, say which precisification makes the canvas come out as a forgery, and state whether that precisification is the correct one. Then say whether the canvas is a forgery, and whether this case shows that one of the five criteria needs to be revised, dropped, or given a threshold. Finally, say what your verdict implies, if anything, about why the artist's own hand should matter to the value of an original.
