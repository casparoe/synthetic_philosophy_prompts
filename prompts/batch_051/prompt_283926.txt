I've been trying to pin down what actually makes something a tangled heirarchy, in the Escher's-drawing-hands sense, as opposed to just a loop or a feedback system. Here's the working definition I've been using, four conditions:

1. There's a hierarchy in the first place: some level L1 normally governs, produces, or contains another level L2.
2. The tangling: L2 also governs, produces, or contains L1, directly or through a chain, so the above/below relation winds back on itself.
3. The loop doesn't immediately collapse into paradox or infinite regress; something keeps it stable.
4. There's an inviolate level somewhere outside the loop — the paper the hands are drawn on, so to speak — that isn't itself drawn by either hand, and that's what lets the loop be a loop without spinning out.

Now the case I want to test against these criteria, because I honestly can't tell if it qualifies.

Imagine a constitutional order that works like this. The constitution has an amendment clause: parliament can amend any part of the constitution, including the amendment clause itself, by a two-thirds supermajority. So at the level of the text, nothing is entrenched. But the supreme court has developed a doctrine, not spelled out anywhere in the text, that the constitution has a "basic structure" — judicial review, democracy, the rule of law, human dignity, that sort of thing — and that no amendment, even with two-thirds behind it, can touch those. The court claims the power to review amendments and strike them down if they trench on the basic structure. What counts as part of the basic structure is something the court works out case by case; it's an unwritten list that grows. And one of the first items the court put on the list was judicial review itself, meaning parliament cannot amend the constitution to abolish or neuter the court, because then there'd be nobody left to enforce the basic structure.

So you get this loop: the constitution creates the court and gives it its jurisdiction; the court interprets the constitution, including what may not be amended; the court can nullify acts of parliament that are themselves amendments to the constitution; and it justifies all of this by appeal to something that appears nowhere in the text the court is supposedly bound by. Depending on where you stand it looks like the constitution is above the court (it's the source of the court's authority), or the court is above the constitution (it decides, in effect, what the constitution means and what it may not mean), or both.

Here's what I want you to do. Go through my four conditions and say, honestly, whether this case meets each of them. Then give me your overall verdict: is this a tangled hierarchy with an inviolate level, or not? And if it isn't — or if the answer is "it depends where the inviolate level is" — tell me whether that's a problem for my fourth condition. I've got this nagging suspicion that the inviolate level can't be identified from inside the system at all, and that if you try you just get another level of the tangle. Like, maybe the real inviolate level here is just the fact that the whole arrangement keeps working because both sides know how far they can push each other before it breaks — which is less a level than a fact about the people actually running the thing. Does that count, or have I quietly changed the subject from structure to politics?
