My philosophy of language professor is a deflationist about truth. We read Ramsey's "Facts and Propositions", and she keeps stressing the bit where he says that calling a proposition true doesn't add anything to asserting the proposition—"true" is redundant except as a grammatical device for cases where you can't state the thing outright. She's now given us a bunch of rulings and I want to know how she'd rule on one more.

Her rulings so far:
- On "it is true that snow is white", she says that's just the same as saying snow is white; no extra property of truth.
- On "what Alice said about the meeting is true", she says that's the real use of the word: blind endorsement, not naming a property.
- On "everything Alice said was true", she says that's a compressed conjunction of endorsements.
- On "some truths are unknown", she says fine, but that just means there are some true claims nobody has got around to asserting; no mystery.
- On "truth is a real property that explains why true beliefs tend to be useful", she rejects that as reifying something we don't need.

Now the undecided case: "There could be a truth that no actual or possible language could ever state." She hasn't said what she thinks. I'm trying to work out whether her Ramsey-style deflationism commits her to a verdict here. What's the closest analog among her rulings, what plausible rules fit all her verdicts, and which rule does each choice on the new case force her to adopt?
