I'm a retired natural scientist — spent thirty years in a lab before I retired — and I've recently gotten into philosphy of mind, which I'd never had time for. Lately I've been stuck on an asymettry in how we talk about minds, and I can't tell whether it's tracking anything real or is just a habit.

Here's the asymmetry. If I say my thermostat "knows" the room is cold, or that the vending machine "thinks" I've inserted a quarter, everyone treats this as a loose manner of speaking. The machine's states are said to have only derived intentionality: their aboutness is borrowed from us, the designers and users. But if I say my colleague believes the tank is full because she misread the gauge, we treat that belief as the genuine article — original intentionality, not a mere façon de parler. In both cases I'm explaining and predicting behavior by attributing content-bearing internal states to a system, and in both cases the attribution earns its keep in the same way: it lets me predict what the system will do next. Yet we insist there is a difference in kind.

Dennett's two-bitser gets at this. It's a vending machine that takes quarters, and you can ask whether it believes it has been handed a quarter. You can make the case harder by adding a slug that produces the same internal state and the same soda, so the machine is, from the outside, exactly as good as a tired cashier who makes the same discrimination badly. Dennett's question, as I understand it, is what is missing in the machine that is present in the cashier. Searle's answer is that the machine's intentionality is derived and the cashier's is original, but I've never been able to see what that buys you in prediction, explanation, or responsibility, since the two can be made behaviorally identical on the case in question.

So the asymmetry I want scrutinized is this: we happily assign real, original intentionality to human beings, and maybe to some animals, while assigning only borrowed, as-if intentionality to artifacts, even when the explanatory work being done is indistinguishable. Is there a principled justification for that asymmetry, or is it a status claim, a kind of chauvinism about biology that doesn't survive close inspection? I want the strongest defense of original intentionality you can give, tested against the hardest version of the two-bitser case (make the machine's discrimination as fine-grained and flexible as the cashier's, if that helps), and then an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or dropped.

I'd like you to answer as a philosopher of mind who works on intentionality and has some sympathy for both Dennett and Searle. Take your time and think carefully; if it helps, start by brainstorming the best arguments on each side before you commit. But then write me a single continuous piece of prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you'd write a long email. And near the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, or where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. I'm still new to this literature, so go easy on jargon, or at least unpack it when you use it.
