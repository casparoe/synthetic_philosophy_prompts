I have defended the view that metacognition—thinking about one's own thinking, in the sense of monitoring, evaluating, and regulating one's own cognitive processes—is always helpful: that such reflection is never systematically truth-distorting, and that for any task an agent with more accurate insight into its own reasoning will do at least as well as one with less. I am aware of several phenomena that appear to cut against this. The centipede's dilemma: drawing conscious attention to a fluent, well-learned skill, such as a golf swing or a memorized piece, makes experts perform worse. Verbal overshadowing: describing a briefly seen face seems to make later recognition of that face worse, as if putting the impression into words displaced or overwrote the underlying memory. Ironic rebound: attempting to suppress an unwanted thought while monitoring whether one is succeeding appears to make the thought return more insistently once the effort ends. I do not yet see whether these refute the view or merely show that metacognition, like anything else, has costs and boundary conditions. So I want a map of the view's exposure. What would count against it? Give me the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, including things that have arguably already been established, and an honest assessment of whether the view can be qualified so much that it risks nothing—and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind. I would personally be most persuaded by cases in which people with unusually accurate insight into their own reasoning are, in some specifiable task, systematically worse than people whose self-insight is poor. Answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, ordered from most to least decisive.
