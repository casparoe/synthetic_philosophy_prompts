I want to check whether a parallel I keep seeing is real. In the debates over why some nations are rich, the move meant to get around the fact that institutions, geography, and culture are all tangled together is the instrumental-variables strategy: find something that affected which institutions a country ended up with but is claimed to affect present-day income only through institutions. The canonical example is settler mortality, which influenced whether colonizers built extractive or settler institutions. In epidemiology, Mendelian randomization looks to me like the same move one level down: a genetic variant is used as an instrument for a modifiable exposure, such as LDL cholesterol, to estimate the exposure's causal effect on a disease outcome. Both demand that the instrument be relevant, unconfounded, and excluded from the outcome equation, and both are advertised as delivering causal conclusions without a randomized experiment. Is this a genuine identity of structure, with the standard objections transferring point by point—pleiotropy as the epidemiological form of an exclusion-restriction violation, weak-instrument bias on both sides, local average treatment effects in an unrepresentative complier subpopulation—or are they only superficially similar? If the parallel is deep, what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer, and where does it predict something false? For instance, does the fact that a gene's biology can be checked in other organisms or populations, while a historical instrument can never be tested against alternative histories, mark a difference in kind or only a difference in how much evidence we happen to have?
