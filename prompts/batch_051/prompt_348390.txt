I work on decisions that depend on regional climate projections — reservoir sizing, flood defenses, crop choices — where the main inputs come from multi-model ensembles. The habit I want you to assess is this: I discount ensemble agreement by the number of genuinely independent model lineages I think are represented. If three dozen simulations agree on, say, a drying trend, but I can trace them to four or five codebases or research groups, with the rest varying the same shared components or having been tuned against the same observational targets, I treat the result as four or five independent findings rather than three dozen. When I cannot establish the lineage — because the documentation is thin or my deadline is short — I default to treating the whole ensemble as one source of evidence rather than as many.

A colleague objects that this is the inverse of double counting. The models that share structure still differ in other respects, and their spread carries information about the range of possibilities; collapsing them throws that away. She adds that my judgments about independence are themselves not much better than guesswork, and that I will use them to dismiss results I already dislike. I reply that counting near-copies as independent runs is pseudo-replication, and that treating a correlated sample as if it were independent is more misleading than treating an ensemble as if it were smaller than it is.

Is this habit defensible? Under what assumptions about how model errors are correlated is my downweighting roughly right, and under what assumptions am I discarding usable information? Is there a defensible version of the rule — one that deflates redundant agreement without collapsing the ensemble to a single source — and what information would I actually need to tell independence from mere difference, short of becoming a climate modeler myself?
