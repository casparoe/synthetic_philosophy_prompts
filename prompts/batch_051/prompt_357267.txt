You are a professional philosopher who works on argumentation theory and the psychology of reasoning, and you also design serious classroom games. I want you to design a 90-minute activity for a 14-student advanced undergraduate seminar on argumentation, debate, and intellectual virtue. The activity must make students feel, in their bones, the central question of the unit: could argumentation be made into a competitive sport, and would that improve it or corrupt it?

Do not settle the question in the abstract and do not open with a lecture. Build a game whose rules, incentives, and roles force students to experience the tension between two things argumentation can be: a cooperative attempt to track the best-supported view, and a contest in which the point is to win, persuade, or score. The lesson should emerge from what they do and from what they catch themselves doing, before you name it in the debrief. The debrief then names it and maps it onto the philosophy.

Design the activity with these elements:

1. A complete, runnable plan: a room layout that works for 14 students in 90 minutes, materials, roles (judges, scorers, a facilitator, assigned sides, a gallery if useful), exact rules, a scoring system with explicit payoffs and real but small stakes, and a minute-by-minute timeline broken into phases.

2. An incentive structure with real bite. Arrange at least two regimes or phases so that the same piece of reasoning is rewarded differently: for example, one scoring system that pays for epistemic moves (conceding a fatal objection, correcting a mischaracterization of an opponent, surfacing the strongest objection to one's own view) and another that pays for competitive moves (extracting concessions, leaving objections unanswered, winning audience votes). Make the two norms pull against each other concretely enough that students can feel the difference between arguing to find out and arguing to win. Consider assigning some students to defend positions they do not hold, in order to separate the effect of competition from the effect of prior belief, and anticipate that both novice and experienced debaters may be in the room.

3. Predictive notes: tell me what will actually happen when this is run — where students will game the scoring, where a quiet truth-seeker will lose despite having the better argument, where someone makes an excellent point that scores terribly, and where the competitive format unexpectedly surfaces a flaw the cooperative phase missed. Build the debrief around those predicted moments rather than around a predetermined moral.

4. A debrief outline with its own rough timeline, mapping specific in-game moments onto the philosophical anatomy of the question: the internal aim of argumentation, the difference between training a skill and changing what the skill is for, the Goodharting of an evaluation rule, adversarial testing as used in law and science, motivated reasoning and attitude polarization, and the possibility that competition improves preparation and rigor even while distorting what people claim to believe. The debrief should elicit observations from students, not deliver your conclusions to them.

5. Two substantial paragraphs of troubleshooting: the most likely failure modes when this activity is run (one side dominates; a student refuses to argue an assigned side; the scoring becomes so gameable that everyone performs cynically; the winners leave vindicated and the lesson dies), with concrete instructions for the facilitator for each.

6. An honest closing paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce — for example, the way real competitive intellectual life operates over years through reputation, status, identity, and career incentives — and what the facilitator must therefore supply explicitly rather than let the game teach.

Begin by brainstorming at least two candidate structures (for instance, a tournament with two scoring regimes versus a single game whose rules are deliberately ambiguous between truth-norms and win-norms), think through their trade-offs in detail, and then commit to one and present the full, runnable design.
