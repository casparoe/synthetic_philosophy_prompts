I am producing the first episode of a scripted interview podcast in which major historical philosophers are questioned as though they were alive today and had encountered the debates their work later provoked. The first guest is Bertrand Russell, and the episode is to be devoted to the theory of definite descriptions set out in On Denoting. Please draft ten interview questions, each with a one- or two-sentence note on the follow-up to ask if Russell gives the reply most consistent with his published position. Do not include introductory or biographical questions.

The questions should turn on the internal details of the view rather than inviting restatement. In particular, please include at least one question on each of the following, and at least two that bring two of them into a single exchange.

First, the three puzzles the theory is introduced to solve: the apparent failure of substitutivity, as in George IV's curiosity about whether Scott was the author of Waverley; the apparent conflict with the law of excluded middle, given that neither the sentence that says the present King of France is bald nor its ordinary negation seems true even though the law appears to require one of them to be true; and the truth of negative existentials about things such as the golden mountain, whose grammatical subject appears to denote an object that is then said not to be.

Second, the distinction between primary and secondary occurrence of a description, roughly whether a description takes wide or narrow scope with respect to a negation or a verb of thought and speech, and the work this distinction is meant to do.

Third, the Strawsonian objection that ordinary definite descriptions typically presuppose rather than assert the existence of a denotation, so that Russell's analysis assigns truth-values where speakers would treat the question as failing to arise.

Where a question invites a standard Russellian reply, make the follow-up target the weakest point of that reply rather than merely record it; candidates include the charge that scope distinctions are introduced ad hoc, the treatment of incomplete or context-dependent descriptions when more than one object satisfies the predicate, and the question how much logical form can be read off the syntax of natural language. One question may also probe what the apparatus implies about proper names, since Russell's later treatment of ordinary proper names as disguised descriptions grew out of the same machinery.

Make the questions self-contained for a listener who has not recently re-read that paper, and avoid asking for citations, page references, or bibliography.
