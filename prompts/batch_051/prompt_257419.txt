Last week a friend and I argued late into the night about Confucian role ethics. She pressed one objection throughout: a view that defines persons through their roles may be able to describe the obligations that come with being a daughter, a teacher, or a neighbor, but the moment two roles conflict it has no non-arbitrary way to adjudicate between them—unless it quietly imports the detached individual standpoint it claims to have displaced. I resisted the objection but could not produce an ordered way of introducing the view that would let her see it as a genuine alternative rather than a conservative apology for deference and fixed stations.

I have since agreed to lead a single discussion for a reading group of intelligent non-specialists. They will not have read any primary texts, and several will arrive assuming roles are just social scripts that a free self chooses or discards. I am not asking you to write the explanation itself; I am asking for a teaching plan. Please specify: the one prior idea that must be secured before the claim that persons are constituted by their roles can be heard as a claim about what a person is, rather than as a metaphor for social conformity; the order in which the remaining ideas should then be introduced, with a note on what each step depends on and why it cannot come earlier; the single example or case that should do the main structural work, what it must have, and what it must not have; the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or generate, in the order they are likely to appear, and where in the sequence each should be intercepted; what should be deliberately omitted at this level; and one end-of-session check that would distinguish real comprehension from polite discomfort. End with a one-sentence summary of the core idea the session should leave them with, written as \answer{...}.
