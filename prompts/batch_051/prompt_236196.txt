I teach metaethics, and every year my graduate seminar gets to the evolutionary debunking material and every year the same thing happens. The debunker side says: look, the content of our moral judgments is explained well enough by selective pressures on our ancestors—punishing free-riders, loyalty to kin, that sort of thing—and those pressures were aimed at reproductive success, not at tracking mind-independent moral facts. So unless we have some independent reason to think the two happen to coincide, our moral judgments are probably off-track and we should suspend or drastically revise them. The realist side says this only works if you've already assumed moral facts are the sort of thing evolution couldn't track, and then offers third-factor stories: maybe the same fitness-relevant features (pain, cooperation, trust) are exactly the natural supervenience base for the moral facts, so evolution and truth-tracking coincide through a common cause. And then someone always points out that the debunker's argument itself relies on epistemic norms—inference to the best explanation, avoiding circularity, etc.—which are presumably also the product of evolution, so the debunker saws off the branch she is sitting on. Everyone at the table agrees on the evolutionary facts. Nobody has produced a new argument in decades, or so it feels, and the debunker and the realist still each think the other is missing something obvious.

My working theory is that the impasse isn't a factual dispute about evolution or even a first-order normative dispute; its a disagreement about what a belief needs in order to be justified. The debunker is treating evolved moral judgment like a measuring instrument we have no reason to think is calibrated, while the realist is assigning the burden of proof differently: you don't have to show the instrument is calibrated, you need a specific reason to think it is broken. But the symmetry argument makes me doubt my own diagnosis, because it suggests the debunker has a problem that is not special to morality, and maybe the real disagreement is over when a circularity worry is fatal rather than uninteresting. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, is it about epistemic standards, is one side smuggling in a metaphysics of moral facts, or are they answering different questions under one heading? Give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress on that diagnosis—or whether I should expect the same seminar in ten years. And be explicit about what would change your diagnosis, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
