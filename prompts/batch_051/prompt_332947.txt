I run the reliability review group at a company that makes industrial pumps, and the decision I have to make a few times a month is whether two field failures are the same mechanism or different mechanisms. I've started calling this mechanistic anomaly detection. It sounds like a filing question but it determines everything downstream: if they're the same mechanism we open one corrective action and roll out one fix; if they're different, we have to run seperate investigations, and a fix aimed at one may do nothing for the other. The failure modes are unglamorous—cracks, corrosion, cavitation damage, seal leaks—but the stakes are high. Over-lump two mechanisms into one and we ship a fix that covers only one of them and feel falsely safe; over-split one mechanism into ten and we never accumulate the cases that would have made a slow common cause visible. Our current rule is basically same failure code plus same named component equals same mechanism, and I know that's wrong in principled ways: the same corrosion process can show up in different components with different codes, and two completely different mechanisms can kill the same bearing and get the same code. The old-timers on my team swear they can tell by looking at the fracture surface whether an event is the same thing they saw in 2009, and I'm sure that's real knowledge, but it's not a procedure a new engineer can run.

I've been reading bits of the philosophy of mechanisms—people arguing that a mechanism is a stable arrangement of entities and activities producing a phenomenon, and that its identity conditions aren't settled by which component failed or how the damage looks. One distinction I keep chewing on is whether sameness of mechanism should be about the actual physical pathway, or about which variables are doing the work (so two physically different pathways could count as the same mechanism if the same process change would have prevented both), or about the level of description you choose. I want that turned into something my team can actually use. Write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether two anomalies are instances of the same mechanism or different mechanisms, and—for a single new anomaly—whether it should be classified under an existing mechanism or declared novel. The people using it are engineers and technicians, not philosophers; it has to be runnable in about two hours per event, without new lab work, and often before the true causal pathway is known. It should say what to do when the pathway is unknown (which is usual), which distinctions can be safely collapsed and which must survive, and where the procedure stops and a person has to decide because it's a judgment call. Then run it on three cases: (a) two impeller failures, one from cavitation (bubbles collapsing and eroding the surface) and one from corrosion fatigue (a crack growing under cyclic load in a corrosive environment); (b) two corrosion failures that look identical in the field but occur in different alloys; (c) one new failure with a failure code we've never seen, though its crack pattern looks exactly like a known stress-corrosion cracking pattern to the old-timers. Also flag any place where the procedure tells us something we wont like hearing.

After the procedure and the three applications, give me a structured summary as a table whose rows are the steps, with these columns: Step; Question the step asks; What counts as a sufficient answer to move on; What to do when that answer can't be gotten; and What the step does and does not settle.
