I came up through the rationalist-blog ecosystem, so I have long treated akrasia as a mostly solved problem: preferences over future rewards are quasi-hyperbolic, present-you discounts every non-present period at an extra rate, preferences reverse as the future slides into the present, and the standard toolkit follows — commitment devices, bundling decisions, precommitment, restructuring the choice so the desired action is the path of least resistance. On this picture there is no deep mystery about weakness of will. There are time-indexed selves with different discount functions, and the morning self who resolves to write and the evening self who scrolls are two parties in an intrapersonal bargaining game.

I find that view compelling because of its systematic virtues, not its comfort. One extra discount parameter organizes a surprising amount of otherwise scattered data: procrastination, undersaving, workout avoidance, broken New Year's resolutions, the specific shape of deadline-driven work, the reason binding precommitment works while mere resolve does not. It predicts preference reversal rather than treating it as noise, and it replaces a spooky piece of folk psychology — a faculty called the will that mysteriously fails — with a precise model of how valuation shifts with temporal distance. I am drawn to a theory that unifies this much and lets me actually compute with it.

But I have an intuition I cannot shake, and it sits directly in the path of the theory. When I fail to write and open the same notification loop instead, I do not experience myself as one player among several with no privileged position. I experience one agent — me, now — holding a settled judgment that the scrolling is the worse use of the next hour, and doing it anyway. The future self whose preferences the model says I am failing to coordinate with does not, in the moment, feel like a separate person whose values have equal standing. I am the one who acts, and the failure feels local to me, not distributed across a sequence of selves. If the theory is taken at face value, then what I want has no univocal answer across times, and neither the scrolling self nor the planning self is more rational — they simply have different discount functions. That seems to dissolve the very thing the theory was meant to explain: the puzzling thing about akrasia is not merely that a later self wants something different, but that a single agent can judge an action worse and do it anyway within what feels like one act of valuation.

I am not looking for a shallow answer that tells me to make peace with the model because it is useful, or to trust the intuition because it is vivid. I want to know which should give, and in what direction, because I can see at least three exits from the tension and I cannot make them trade off cleanly. First, the intuition might be the thing to drop: perhaps the sense of a single privileged decider is a narrative artifact — an introspective illusion roughly as trustworthy as the sense that vision is continuous — and once it is debunked I should accept that there are only ever time-slices with overlapping interests. Second, the theory might need amendment: perhaps the multi-self model describes valuation well but is silent about normativity, so I should add something it does not contain — a planner-self whose preferences are authoritative, a requirement that a rational agent be time-consistent, or some other device that restores the unity the model flattened. Third, the two might be compatible under the right reading: the single agent and the multiple selves might be two levels of description, one answering who has failed and the other answering in virtue of what mechanism, with the apparent contradiction arising from using one level's vocabulary to answer the other's question.

Walk me through each of the three moves at its strongest, and then give me a negotiated verdict. I want the standards, not just the verdict: what evidence or argument would justify debunking the intuition rather than accommodating it; what functional role a privileged self or a time-consistency requirement would have to serve to be a legitimate addition rather than an ad hoc patch; and what a successful two-level reconciliation would have to establish to avoid merely relabeling the multi-self model while keeping the single-agent story for rhetorical comfort. I am more interested in learning what each move costs than in being told which one to make, though I do want your judgment at the end.
