We have this policy at my company where any safety or alignment work that slows down a launch gets descoped by default. The actual written rule is that a safety intervention has to pay for itself within the same quarter, or it's cut. Every launch review, the same justification comes up: "if we don't ship, a competitor will, and the outcome is basically the same except now the slightly-safer model never ships and we also lose." A bunch of us find this weird becuase the company's stated "safety first" values live in a completely different doc, and nobody can explain how the two are supposed to fit together.

I'm trying to figure out what this practice actually commits us to. Does treating alignment work as a "tax" presuppose a picture of value where only shipped capabilities count and safety is pure cost? Does the "a competitor would do it anyway" move presuppose a specific stance on responsibility — like you're only on the hook for what would be different if you personally acted differently? Or is it not a real philosophical commitment at all, just a game-theoretic constraint that the policy is compatible with under almost any view, while the "safety first" doc is the actual commitment or maybe just marketing? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories here, distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and tell me what would concretely have to change in the launch-review rules if we replaced that presupposed view with one where you can be responsible for participating in a bad equilibrium even when your individual decision wouldn't tip it.
