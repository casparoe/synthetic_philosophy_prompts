Consider a practice that has become ordinary in the regulation of online speech. A state applies its laws against defamation, hate speech, or the glorification of terrorism to a platform headquartered in another country, with servers in a third, where the material was uploaded by an author who lives in neither of those places and where the material is entirely lawful. The enforcing state's only connection to the material is that its residents can read it inside its borders. On that basis, the state orders the platform to remove the material or to geo-block it for users within the state, and it threatens penalties for non-compliance. Yet the same state will usually resist the mirror-image claim: when a foreign state asserts jurisdiction over speech produced by its own citizens and hosted on its own soil, it treats that as extraterritorial overreach and insists that its own nationals' speech is answerable only to its own law.

What theory of speech is this practice committed to? Spell out the view it presupposes about where a speech act occurs, where the harm of speech is located, and whose law governs it. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the outward-reaching and inward-resisting halves of the practice can be derived from one consistent principle. Then identify what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by either of two alternatives: a country-of-origin rule, under which speech is governed only by the law of the place where the speaker, publisher, or server is located, or a single uniform global standard for permissible online speech. Would either replacement still leave room for a state that locates the harm of speech where it is received?
