I've been going back and forth alot between two things that shouldn't have any business being about each other: Locke's chapter on identity and diversity, and the specific way mutation in programs is hard to reason about. The passage from Locke that set it off is this one (An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, "Of Identity and Diversity"):

"…and whilst they exist united together, the mass, consisting of the same atoms, must be the same mass, or the same body, let the parts be ever so differently jumbled. But if one of these atoms be taken away, or one new one added, it is no longer the same mass or the same body. In the state of living creatures, their identity depends not on a mass of the same particles, but on something else. For in them the variation of great parcels of matter alters not the identity : an oak growing from a plant to a great tree, and then lopped, is still the same oak ; and a colt grown up to a horse, sometimes fat, sometimes lean, is all the while the same horse."

Reading that I couldn't stop hearing Python. A tuple or a string is like the mass of atoms: it just is its contents, and change one atom and it's a different value. A list, though, is like the oak — mutate it with .append(3) and it's still the same list even though the contents it holds have changed; the programmer says "same object" rather than "same contents," which is pretty much the `is` versus `==` distinction, and that looks to me exactly like Locke's contrast between the identity of a living creature and the identity of a mass of matter. The hard part of mutation then becomes a Leibniz's-Law-ish problem: `x` is the same object before and after the call, yet the value it presents to the rest of the program has changed, so you can't substitute equals for equals anymore — the same puzzle as a thing that is strictly identical to itself across time while having incompatible properties at different times.

So I want someone to stress-test this. Is it a real structural identity or am I just pattern-matching on the word "identity"? If it's real, does anything transfer in either direction — e.g., does copy-on-write (treating the mutated aggregate as a new value, like Locke's mass) say something about when "creature" identity should be treated as a value, and does Locke's "something else" predict where aliasing bugs live? If it collapses, tell me precisely where the two cases come apart, and give me at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out false. End with a one-sentence verdict on whether the parallel is deep or superficial, written as a last line \answer{...} with a short phrase inside the braces.
