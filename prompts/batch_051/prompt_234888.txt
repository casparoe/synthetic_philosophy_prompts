Analyse the rise and fall of the original Phillips curve as a case study in Friedman's F-twist, the view that theories should be judged by their predictive success rather than by the realism of their assumptions. The original Phillips curve treated the rate of wage or price inflation as a stable, exploitable inverse function of unemployment, with no independent role for expected inflation; for roughly a decade it appeared to fit the data and was used to guide macroeconomic policy, and then in the 1970s stagflation broke the relationship down. Work through the case in both directions. First, what exactly should an F-twist view say about the Phillips curve during its successful period, and what verdict should it pass on the excluded role of inflation expectations? Second, say what the episode does to the F-twist itself: does it support the view that only predictive success matters, does it show that the unrealistic assumption was doing hidden causal work, or does it show that the F-twist needs a further principle about what happens when a model is used to intervene in the system it models? Please answer in British English.
