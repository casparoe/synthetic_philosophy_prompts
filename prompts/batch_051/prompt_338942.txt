Consider Newcomb's problem in its standard form. A Predictor has already made and sealed a prediction about whether you will take one box or two boxes. If it predicted that you will take only the opaque box, the opaque box contains one million dollars; if it predicted that you will take both boxes, the opaque box is empty. The transparent box always contains one thousand dollars. You know all of this, and you know that the prediction cannot be affected by anything you do now.

Standard discussions usually treat the Predictor's reliability as uniform: the same high accuracy whether you are the sort of person who one-boxes or the sort who two-boxes. Vary that assumption. Suppose the Predictor's accuracy is asymmetric across the two choices. Let p be your credence that the Predictor predicted one-boxing conditional on your actually taking one box, and let q be your credence that the Predictor predicted two-boxing conditional on your actually taking both boxes. The values p and q are known from the Predictor's track record and may differ.

First, give the evidential decision-theoretic calculation. What is the expected value of one-boxing, what is the expected value of two-boxing, and what condition on p and q makes evidential decision theory prefer one-boxing? Second, say what causal decision theory recommends and whether its verdict depends on p, q, or neither. Third, describe the region of the (p, q) plane in which the two theories still give conflicting advice, and identify any cases in that region that are surprising — for example, where the Predictor is extremely unreliable in one direction yet the evidential argument for one-boxing survives. Finally, say what this asymmetry does to the two classical arguments: the dominance argument for two-boxing and the evidentialist argument that those who one-box tend to end up with more money. Which argument does the asymmetric version put more pressure on, and why?
