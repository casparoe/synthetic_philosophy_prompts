I recently retired after four decades as an experimental physicist, and one of my retirement projects has been to work through some of the literature on formal epistemology and decision theory. I have been reading about updateless decision theory, which — at least in the introductory treatments I have found — is presented as the advice to select the policy one would have committed to before making one's observation, with Newcomb's problem offered as the motivating case.

I can see, vaguely, why one might want that in a scenario where one's own later reasoning is predicted by another agent. But a deeper worry keeps nagging at me. If the theory really tells me to act on the policy I would have chosen prior to observing the data, does this not amount to a standing instruction to pretend I never saw the data at all? For a scientist, the entire discipline of empirical inference consists in conditioning on what one has observed; a maxim that says decide as though you had not yet looked sounds less like a refinement of Bayesian rationality and more like deliberate self-deception. I would be grateful if you could help me see whether updateless decision theory is actually asking me to abandon ordinary updating on evidence, and if it is not, how the two proposals — choosing as if before the observation, and conditioning on the observation — are meant to fit together coherently.
