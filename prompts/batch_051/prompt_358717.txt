I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether knowing a proposition ever licenses you to set adverse evidence aside. He defended the following chain of thought. Knowledge is factive: if I know that p, then p is true. But then any evidence, actual or possible, that appears to count against p is evidence against a falsehood; it is misleading by definition. If a counterargument to p comes along, I already know it goes wrong somewhere, even before I can locate the error. So I can rationally decline to update on it, and I can do this without examining its merits. He conceded that this sounds arrogant, but insisted the logic was unimpeachable.

I found myself unable to say where the argument fails, yet equally unable to accept its conclusion. I believe we are fallible creatures; I do not think any empirical commitment I hold should be treated as immune to correction; and I take openness to counter-evidence to be not merely courteous but rationally required. If my friend's principle were correct, it is hard to see how a rational enquirer could ever revise a confidently held view, how scientific opinion could change under the pressure of anomalies, or how reasonable people could remain in genuine disagreement. Yet each time I tried to state a principled place for openness, the appeal to factivity seemed to close it off.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the openness intuition to be the thing I give up, and how would the resulting epistemology still make sense of error, revision, and disagreement? Conversely, what would it take to amend or abandon the factivity-driven argument, and which premise is the least costly to revise? And is there a reading on which knowing p and remaining open to counter-evidence are compatible rather than competing?
