So I just spent like two hours arguing with my buddy about the centipede game, of all things, and I need someone to settle the part we kept going in circles on. Quick version: two players alternate, and on your turn you can take a pot of money and end the game, or pass it to the other player, which grows the pot. Backward induction says the first player should take on the very first move, because on the last move that player would take, and you unravel it backwards from there. Except in experiments people pass a bunch, and both players often end up with more than the take-immediately outcome would have given them.

My freind's whole thing was that those subjects are just confused, and if they thought harder they'd take right away. I think that's backwards. But here's what I actually want answered. The textbooks do both at once: they teach backward induction as the rational solution, and they also treat the fact that almost nobody plays it as a human error or bias. What theory of rationality does that practice presuppose? Does the practice actually force you into that theory, or is it compatible with several different ones? And if you instead took the experimental results as evidence about what rationality requires, rather than as evidence about human limitations, what would the textbook story have to change?
