I have spent several years working on the data side of clinical research, and I have never been able to get a straight answer about what the whole system actually believes about evidence. The rule that organizes everything is a sharp one: a finding counts as publishable, grant-worthy, and sometimes even good enough for a drug to be approved only if it clears a statistical threshold, usually written as p < 0.05. A result of 0.049 clears it and a result of 0.051 does not, even when the two studies are otherwise identical. The same journals and regulators that enforce this line also warn researchers not to keep adding participants until the line is crossed, not to run dozens of analyses and report only the successful ones, and not to describe a pattern as predicted when it was only noticed afterward. Yet the same institutions reward surprising positive findings and mostly ignore careful negative ones. And when a much larger, pre-registered study does fail to reproduce an earlier result, the common response is not that the earlier result was false; it is that the failure is not decisive — perhaps the conditions differed, perhaps the effect is real but fragile.

I want to understand what view of evidence — what picture of how evidence accumulates and when it can justify belief — would make these habits consistent. Which of the habits genuinely require that picture, and which are just historical accidents that could be kept or dropped under several different pictures? Does drawing such a sharp line at 0.05 presuppose that evidence arrives in discrete jumps rather than continuously? Does the fear of peeking and multiple comparisons presuppose that the strength of evidence depends on how the data were produced, not just on what the data are? Is the implicit picture coherent, or does the system slide between two or three incompatible pictures depending on whether it is accepting a result, policing how the result was obtained, or explaining away a failed replication? And if someone replaced the implicit picture with a view on which evidence is a continuously updated degree of support — the kind of thing people sometimes call Bayesian, though I care more about the commitments than the label — which of these institutional habits would have to go, and which could remain with a different justification?
