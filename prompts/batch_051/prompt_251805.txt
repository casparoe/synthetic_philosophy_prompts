I'm a backend engineer, not a humanities person, so this might be a dumb way to frame it. I manage a team that includes several engineers at our company's offshore site in India. Recently I read John Stuart Mill's Considerations on Representative Government, specifically chapter XVIII, Of the Government of Dependencies by a Free State. The passage that stuck with me is the one where he argues, as I understand it, that when a more advanced country governs a less advanced one, the arrangement can only be justified as temporary tutelage: it has to be aimed at making the subject people fit to govern themselves, and it should be measured by whether they are improving toward that end, rather than by how convenient the arrangement is for the rulers. I don't have the exact text infront of me, but that's the gist I took from it.

My company has a policy that local engineers at the offshore site are not put in charge of system architecture or major design decisions. Those are done by engineers at headquarters. The official reason is that the local team needs more time to develop the relevant judgment and experience. Every year we say they're improving, but the bar for actually handing over architectural responsibility seems to move. I started wondering if Mill gives a useful test here. Please describe the most important implications of Mill's argument in that chapter for how I should think about my company's offshore policy: under what conditions would our policy be the defensible kind of paternalistic tutelage he describes, and under what conditions would it just be a self-serving excuse for keeping control? Also, does Mill's view imply any specific milestones or exit criteria that should make a difference in practice, or is his argument too tied to nineteenth-century empire to transfer to a company?
