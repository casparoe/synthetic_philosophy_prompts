I'm taking Legislation and Statutory Interpretation, and I want to work through the absurdity doctrine by putting pressure on the case usually cited for it. Take Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457 (1892). The Act of February 26, 1880 made it unlawful to prepay or assist the importation of any alien "to perform labor or service of any kind" in the United States; a New York church contracted with an English clergyman to come serve as its rector, and the government sued for the statutory penalty. Justice Brewer begins the Court's opinion with this concession:

"It must be conceded that the act of the corporation is within the letter of this section, for the relation of rector to his church is one of service, and implies labor on the one side with compensation on the other."

Analyze the decision as a case study in the absurdity doctrine — the idea that a court may depart from what a text plainly says when applying it would produce an absurd result. I want the mapping done carefully: what the doctrine would say the Court was facing and what it was licensed to do; where Brewer's actual reasoning follows the doctrine and where it slides into something else, such as a claim about legislative intent or a policy judgment dressed up as a presumption; and then what the case shows about the doctrine itself. Does Holy Trinity vindicate the doctrine, expose how much discretion it actually authorizes, or reveal that "absurdity" is doing several different jobs at once? Keep the focus on the interpretive principle rather than the immigration context.
