Hey, I'm in a philosophy of mathematics class and we've been on the question of what a proof is, especially with computer-assisted and unsurveyable proofs like the four-colour theorem and Kepler conjecture. For a problem set I have to test a definition against a case. The criteria I wrote down from class are: (1) a proof proceeds by steps that a competent mathematician could check one at a time against accepted rules; (2) a proof is surveyable — someone can hold the whole argument in outline, on at most a few pages; (3) a proof explains why the theorem is true, not just that it is.

The borderline case: a team proves a combinatorial conjecture by encoding its denial as a satisfiability problem, running a SAT solver, and getting 'unsatisfiable'. They convert the solver's certificate into a formal proof object that a proof assistant verifies. The certificate is terabytes and no human ever reads the middle; all a human checks is the encoding and the soundness of the proof assistant's small kernel. So it arguably passes (1) in letter but not spirit, and clearly fails (2) and (3).

Does this qualify as a proof by the three criteria? Go criterion by criterion, and say wether you think the criteria themselves need to change — e.g. maybe surveyability is doing the real work, or maybe it became outdated once verifiers got more reliable than human reasoners. Also, how confident are you in the verdict, and which point in the answer are you least sure of?
