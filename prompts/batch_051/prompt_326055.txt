I chair the scientific advisory board of a private foundation that funds theoretical physics, and we are considering an otherwise strong proposal from a quantum-foundations group. The proposal requests funds for a two-year embedded philosopher of physics whose brief would be to audit the group's interpretive assumptions, run conceptual workshops, and help design experiments that could actually discriminate among competing interpretations of quantum mechanics. The request arises from a specific failure: the group spent a year developing a proposed test of objective collapse models, only to be told by external reviewers that the test could not settle anything between interpretations, because the meaning of the measurement outcome is itself part of the dispute. The board is split. One faction, invoking Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman, says philosophy is dead or useless to working scientists, that the money would be better spent on a quantum-optics postdoc, and that approving this component would set a precedent for funding unfalsifiable commentary. The other faction says those dismissals are themselves philosophical claims asserted without argument, that the group's stalled test is precisely a case of conceptual confusion blocking experimental design, and that the component is less than a tenth of the requested budget. Please draft the board's decision memo: approve the philosophy component as proposed, reject it, or approve it with conditions. Give the board an actionable bottom line, and give an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side before explaining why it does not prevail.

Please deliver the memo as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Point 1 should state the decision; point 2 the main reason for it; point 3 the best supporting consideration; point 4 the strongest objection from the losing side and why it does not prevail; point 5 any conditions or implementation requirements the board should attach.
