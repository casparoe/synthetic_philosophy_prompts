I am a sixth-form student and I volunteer in my school’s peer listening service. We take two forty-minute shifts each week, working in pairs in a quiet room where students can drop in and talk about things that are upsetting them. We are not counsellors; we are trained only in basic listening and in knowing when to refer to a member of staff. The hardest part, I find, is not listening, but deciding what kind of response is actually good for the person in front of me.

I have been reading Schopenhauer’s On the Basis of Morality, and the passage that stays with me is the one in which he argues that compassion is the sole genuine moral incentive, because in compassion another person’s suffering becomes immediately present to me in the way my own suffering is, and I act to remove it without reference to myself. Nietzsche, by contrast, seems to think that much of what calls itself pity is really a disguised way of making oneself feel superior, or a way of doubling the suffering by adding one’s own discomfort to the other’s, or a way of keeping the other person weak by rewarding helplessness.

I want a procedure I can actually run in a twenty-minute conversation with a distressed peer that will tell me whether the response I am about to give is the kind of compassion Schopenhauer describes, or the kind of pity Nietzsche warns against. The procedure must be usable by a sixteen- to eighteen-year-old with no clinical training, under these constraints: I sometimes have to decide in seconds, I cannot prescribe or fix, and I must err on the side of referring to a trained adult.

Please write it as a sequence of questions I can ask myself in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to an adult because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run the procedure on three cases: a student whose parent has just been diagnosed with a serious illness and who wants to talk; a student who has just gone through a bad breakup and is crying; and a student who is being socially excluded by a friendship group and says that no one cares about her. One of the worked examples should be a case where the procedure tells me not to do the thing I would naturally do. Finally, say clearly what the procedure cannot do, and which steps are genuinely judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Please write your answer in British English.
