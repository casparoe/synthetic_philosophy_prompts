What follows is an argument I have been turning over since our class discussion on whether a nation can be guilty of crimes committed before its present citizens were born. I cannot find the flaw, whilst the conclusion strikes me as too strong.

(1) A nation is guilty of a wrong if its officials commit the wrong through the nation's institutions, acting in the nation's name and on the nation's own decisions.
(2) Suppose that, fifty years ago, a state ordered and carried out mass killings through its army and police, with the backing of its own laws.
(3) Then that nation is guilty of mass killings.
(4) A nation persists over time even as its members change: the same state, the same institutions, and the same legal identity continue.
(5) If guilt attaches to the nation, and the nation persists, then later citizens inherit that guilt, just as they inherit its territory, treaties, and debts.
(6) Therefore, citizens born long after the killings are themselves guilty of them.

I suspect step (5) is doing something sneaky, but I cannot say exactly what. Is the argument valid? Is each premise true? If the trouble is in (5), give the strongest reading of (5) on which it is true, and then check whether the argument still reaches (6) on that reading. I am asking about the logic, not about whether the conclusion is morally comfortable. Please write your answer in American English.
