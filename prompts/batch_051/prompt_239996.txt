Last night I spent a long argument with a friend about whether a person's own misconduct can make it misplaced for them to criticize another person for the same misconduct, even when the criticism is accurate. My friend kept collapsing the options to two: either the hypocrite's statement is true, in which case it should work like anyone else's true statement, or we are just dressing up an ad hominem objection in moral language. I want to defend a third position, one that treats standing to blame as a distinct normative status that can be lost through one's own relation to the wrong, and I came away from the argument convinced that this distinction is more basic than people realize.

Next week I have to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates introducing exactly this idea, and I want a plan for the section rather than an explanation of the view. The students have read almost nothing in moral philosophy; the section has to be self-contained. The one thing they should leave with is the ability to see why "the criticism is true, so it does not matter who says it" moves too fast. Please give me: the order in which the components have to be introduced and what depends on what; the single carrying example I should use and what it must have and must lack, so that the whole distinction can keep returning to it; the misconceptions students will predictably form (I suspect they will think standing is a matter of the critic's credibility, or that denying someone standing is just the ad hominem fallacy with better manners, or that the only alternative is to say the hypocrite's criticism is false), and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they understand the distinction rather than merely having heard it; and what I should openly refuse to settle at this level. I can fill in any references myself later; for now, keep the plan self-contained and do not rely on particular authors or texts.
