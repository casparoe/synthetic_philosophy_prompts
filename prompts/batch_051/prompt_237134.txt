An economics journal sympathetic to the F-twist—the view that unrealistic assumptions are acceptable when the model's predictions are accurate—adopts a two-track review rule. On the assumptions track, a referee may object only that the assumptions are mutually inconsistent or that the formal model does not actually embody the assumptions as stated; a referee may not object that an assumption is false of real economies. On the predictions track, a referee may reject only for predictive failure relative to a benchmark the author announces in advance. In addition, an author may designate any assumption as tractability; once an assumption is so designated, a referee may hold the paper's predictive failures against it only by showing that replacing that assumption with a more realistic one would reverse the paper's central result. Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a position: what does it incentivize among authors, referees, and editors; how would the tractability designation be gamed, and what would it come to mean over time; what happens to the supply of models and to the field's ability to notice that a widely shared unrealistic assumption is doing quiet work; and which failure modes could be repaired without simply restoring realism-based review?
