I am a retired research chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have returned to the church in which I was raised, joined its worship and liturgy committee, and begun reading classical theism — the understanding of God as all-knowing and incapable of change. The committee is revising the Sunday intercessions, the prayers in which the congregation asks God for specific worldly outcomes: healing for particular people by name, safe travel for particular people, rain in due season, an end to a war. I keep tripping over a difficulty from my recent reading. If God is all-knowing, none of these petitions tells Him anything He does not already know, including what is best for each person. If God cannot change, He cannot be moved by our asking to do anything other than what He would have done anyway; a God whose actions depended on whether and how we petitioned would be a changing God. Each petition therefore seems to face a dilemma: either we ask an unchanging God to do what He will already do, in which case the petition does no work, or we ask Him to do something He would not otherwise have done, in which case we are asking an unchanging God to change. A younger member of the committee, a philosophy student, argues that the specific petitions should be replaced by prayers that acknowledge God's goodness and ask only that our own wills be aligned with His. The senior members reply that this would abolish intercession and cut the congregation off from bringing its concrete needs before God. The rector has asked me, as the only scientist on the committee, to draft the decision memo. It should recommend one of three actions — retain the specific petitions unchanged; remove them in favor of non-petitionary prayer; or keep petition but reframe it so that it is coherent under omniscience and immutability — and it should give the theological reasoning for the recommendation, treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly, and conclude with language the committee could actually vote on.
