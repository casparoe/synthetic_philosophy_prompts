I coordinate a small restorative justice program in a rural county. We receive referrals from schools, families, and lower courts after incidents involving theft, vandalism, assault, harassment, or broken trust. Before we take a case, an intake worker has to decide whether we are dealing with a moral wrong our process can address, a harm that needs repair, or something we should refer elsewhere. We have no formal training in philosophy, and our current rule is mostly just to ask whether the referred person did the thing. That keeps causing trouble, because two people can do the same thing in very different states of mind.

A few years ago, a volunteer gave me a photocopy of several pages from Peter Abelard's Ethics, also known as Know Thyself. The passage I keep returning to is the one in which Abelard argues that sin does not lie in the outward act itself but in the consent of the mind. As I understand him, he holds that the very same deed can be gravely culpable in one person and comparatively innocent in another, because the outward act, considered apart from the inner consent, does not by itself change a person's guilt or merit. I do not have the exact wording in front of me now, but that is the claim I want to turn into something usable.

Please write a decision procedure for my intake workers, grounded in that Abelardian distinction, that they can actually run in about twenty minutes per referral. They usually have the referred person's account, the affected person's account, and sometimes a short report from a school or an officer. The procedure should separate, in order: what the referred person actually consented to; what they foresaw but did not consent to; what they did from ignorance, compulsion, or panic; and what happened outside their awareness. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the worker stops and assigns a label, and where the case must go to a senior facilitator because the procedure has run out.

Then apply the procedure to two cases. First, a teenager trying to break into a car to steal a backpack smashes a window; the window belongs to someone the teenager knew and was angry at, while the backpack belonged to a stranger. Second, a nurse gives a patient the wrong medication after following a badly labeled chart; the patient is harmed, and the nurse says she was overloaded and trusted the chart. Show how the procedure handles each case, including the step where it gives an answer my staff will not like. Finally, say plainly what Abelard's principle cannot tell us: for example, about blame for negligence, about what repair requires, or about whether some inner consents should be treated as less serious because they were fleeting.
