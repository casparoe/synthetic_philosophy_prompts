Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether it makes any sense to punish corporations rather than the people who run them. He kept pressing the claim that when a company is convicted and made to pay a large fine for misconduct that identifiable managers actually committed — a pollution cover-up, a fraudulent accounting scheme — the proceeding functions like a scapegoat ritual. The community, he said, transfers its indignation onto a substitute that cannot feel guilt or shame, performs a costly penalty on it, and then treats the moral accounts as settled even though no natural person has borne the guilt. I resisted the comparison, but the structural similarity stuck with me. It reminded me of the rite in Leviticus in which the high priest lays his hands on a goat, confesses the people's sins over it, and sends it into the wilderness, while a second goat is slaughtered as a sin offering — a procedure that seems to treat guilt or impurity as something that can be moved from one bearer to another and then removed by destroying or expelling the substitute.

I want to know what view of guilt and responsibility such a practice would have to presuppose. Is it committed to the idea that guilt is transferable in some robust sense, or only that guilt can be represented and thereby socially discharged? Could the modern corporate fine be explained entirely by deterrence, compensation, and the difficulty of localizing fault in complex organizations, with no residue of substitution logic at all? And if someone held the opposite view — that guilt attaches only to the agent who acted and cannot be shifted — what exactly would have to change in the way legal systems punish wrongdoing inside organizations? I am less interested in the history than in the implicit theory: which claims about guilt the practice strictly needs, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where it remains philosophically underdetermined.
