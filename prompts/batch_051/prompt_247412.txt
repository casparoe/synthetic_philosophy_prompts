Here is an argument I put together for a seminar and now find suspicious, because the conclusion is plainly false. (1) If musical works exist, they are repeatable: one and the same work is present, in full, in each of its performances. (2) Whatever is repeatable is a type, and its occurrences are tokens of that type. (3) A token is a token of a type only if it possesses every property constitutive of that type; an object that lacks even one constitutive property is simply not a token of the type. (4) The constitutive properties of a musical work include at least the pitches and rhythms prescribed by its score, in their prescribed order. (5) Almost every live performance departs from the score in some small way, and many of these departures concern exactly those prescribed pitches and rhythms. (6) Therefore almost every live performance fails to be a token of the work-type it purports to instantiate. (7) So almost no actual performance of a sonata is, strictly, a performance of the sonata, and our ordinary talk about performances of works is massively in error. I would like to hold on to (1) and (2), and I am reluctant to reject (3), since that seems to give up the only crisp account of what it is for a particular to be an instance of a type. Which premise should a Platonist about musical works reject, and what does rejecting it commit her to? Does adding recordings make the problem easier or harder, given that a recording seems to be both a repeatable sound-pattern and a particular artifact on a shelf?
