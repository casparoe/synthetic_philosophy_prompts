I run a philosophy-and-science-fiction reading group, and next month we are reading Ray Bradbury's story "The Veldt." I want to lead the discussion through a question the story seems deliberately built to raise: whether the nursery is best treated as a virtual environment, and whether what happens inside it can count as genuinely real harm. The obvious moralizing reading—that the story warns against letting technology raise your children—is fine as far as it goes, but I want the group to push past it. Taken on its own terms, what does the story commit itself to about the reality of virtual objects and virtual harms? The nursery's lions eventually kill the parents, so the simulated threat has perfectly real consequences; on the other hand, the nursery is often spoken of as a therapeutic projection the parents expect to switch off, and the children treat it as something between a game and a place.

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should take the philosophical debate seriously without reducing the story to an illustration, and it should not distort the story to score a point. Where does Bradbury seem to side with the view that virtual objects are fully real—real patterns with real causal powers—and that harms to and within them are genuine harms? Where does he instead treat the virtual as derivative, ersatz, or merely imagined? And does the story end up showing that the distinction between virtual and real harm is less stable than the philosophers assume? Please also say what the story adds to this debate that an argument alone would miss. Assume we have all read it; don't summarize the plot. Keep your answer to at most 600 words.
