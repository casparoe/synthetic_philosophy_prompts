For a course on classical Chinese philosophy I have been reading Mengzi and Xunzi side by side on human nature, and one thing about the surrounding discussion has started to bother me: the debate is usually presented as Mencius saying human nature is good while Xunzi says it is bad, yet it has continued for more than two thousand years without settling into any resolution both sides could accept. On a careful reading, the positions look less like flat contradictions than like answers to different questions. Mencius builds his case from examples such as the spontaneous alarm at seeing a child about to fall into a well, and he treats the virtues as present in us in incipient form, as sprouts that need to be nourished; when someone turns out badly, the explanation is that the sprouts were stunted or failed to develop. Xunzi, by contrast, identifies human nature with what happens without cultivation—unregulated desire issuing in contention and disorder—and locates goodness in deliberate artifice, ritual, and teaching rather than in the natural endowment itself; he even faults Mencius for not distinguishing what is born in us from what we acquire through effort. Read this way, Mencius's xing (nature) seems to name the direction in which an undamaged constitution tends to develop, while Xunzi's xing names the unassisted output of the raw material before cultivation.

What I cannot decide is how to classify the disagreement that remains once the two are read charitably. It might be merely verbal, a boundary dispute about how much of a developed moral disposition may be folded into the word xing. It might be substantive but turn on a hidden empirical question about whether untutored human beings really do show incipient moral responses. Or it might be a deeper disagreement about what an account of human nature must anchor itself in: what we start from, or what we can become under the right conditions. My own hypothesis is that the impasse is largely a boundary problem over the extension of xing plus a difference in explanatory starting points, but I worry this hypothesis is too convenient. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and say what would count as progress—a distinction, an empirical finding, a reframing of the question, or a reason to expect no convergence at all?
