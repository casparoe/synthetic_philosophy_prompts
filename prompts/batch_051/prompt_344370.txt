I want you to act as a philosopher of biology who works on information and representation in molecular biology. Last night two friends of mine got into an argument I couldn't referee, and I'd like you to umpire it point by point.

Maya said it's literally true that DNA contains a code: the genetic code is a mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids, the way Morse code is a mapping from dot-dash patterns to letters, and the sequence gets copied across generations, so DNA stores information in a perfectly ordinary sense. Dan said that's all metaphor. A DNA molecule is just chemistry; a codon doesn't 'mean' glycine until the ribosome, the tRNAs, and a bunch of enzymes interpret it, so the code is really in the whole translation machinery, not in the DNA strand itself. Maya replied that this is true of every code: Morse code is just marks on paper until someone applies the decoding convention, but nobody thinks calling it a code is misleading. Dan came back that Morse has an actual sender and receiver with intentions, while cells have none; so DNA only gives you causal correlation, and the word 'code' smuggles in intentionality. Maya then shifted ground: in information theory a code is just a rule mapping sequences from one alphabet to another, no intentions required, and the genetic code satisfies that, so her original claim doesn't need intentionality. Dan answered that on that deflated sense smoke 'codes for' fire and a barometer 'codes for' pressure, so saying DNA contains a code becomes trivial and stops being interesting.

Please think carefully, and start by distinguishing the senses of 'code' and 'information' in play before you rate anything. Then go through the exchange move by move — Maya's opening, Dan's reply, and each later response — and rate each from clearly right to clearly wrong, with a sentence or two of justification. Finally say who is more right overall and whether there's a better position neither of them saw.
