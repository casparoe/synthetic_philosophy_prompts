I have become convinced of modal realism, roughly in the form defended by David Lewis. I take possible worlds to be concrete universes, each a maximal whole, all equally real, causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another; I take the word actual to be an indexical that picks out whichever world one happens to inhabit; and I handle de re modality — what an individual could have been — through counterpart relations rather than through a single object's appearing in many worlds. My reasons are theoretical rather than intuitive. The view seems to me the only fully reductive account of modality: it analyzes possibility, necessity, and counterfactual dependence without taking modal notions as primitive, and it supplies worldly truthmakers for modal truths. I am unmoved by the incredulous stare and by the charge of ontological extravagance, since I regard parsimony as a comparison across many theoretical virtues, not a single head count.

What I want is an honest map of the view's exposure. Do not simply rehearse the standard objections; rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate those that a committed modal realist would themselves concede as counting against the view from those they could deflect in good faith. Take each of the load-bearing commitments — the concreteness of worlds, their causal and spatiotemporal isolation, the indexicality of actuality, counterpart theory, and the principle of recombination by which worlds are generated from duplicates of parts of other worlds — and say what would have to be true for that commitment to fail, and whether its failure would refute the whole position or only a local component of it.

I also want to know where the real pressure lies, rather than where critics have merely been loudest. Identify the empirical and formal pressure points — in the interpretation of physics, in the semantics of natural language, in set theory or in the treatment of world-sized domains — and say which of them are live and which are only logical possibilities. Say what modal realism commits me to that actualist or ersatzist rivals — views on which possible worlds are abstract representations rather than concrete universes — do not commit me to, so that I can see where the view genuinely sticks its neck out. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position as I have stated it is so insulated that it risks nothing at all, and whether that is a problem for a metaphysical theory of this kind.
