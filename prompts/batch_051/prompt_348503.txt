Act as a decision theorist who regularly turns formal results into procedures practitioners can actually run, and think carefully; begin by stating precisely what Good's theorem does and does not imply before operationalizing it.

I run logistics planning for a regional food distributor. Several times a week I make quantity and supplier decisions under uncertainty, and I can usually purchase information first: a demand forecast, a quality audit, a price report. My CFO keeps asking why we buy information that never changes what we do, and I have no disciplined test for when information is worth paying for. Please give me a reusable decision procedure grounded in the result known as Good's theorem — that for an ideal Bayesian decision-maker, free information cannot reduce expected payoff. I need a checklist I can run in about fifteen minutes per decision: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with 'don't buy the information' or 'buy it,' and where it hands off to a person because a judgment call or a value question is being made. It should say what to do when I cannot put numbers on the relevant quantities, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a $900 demand forecast before committing to a large order, where the forecast could alter our order size but only at the margin; (2) a supplier audit whose results cannot change this quarter's supplier decisions because the contract is fixed, even though our quality manager badly wants the reassurance; (3) a market-price report before a launch decision, where the relevant executive has said no conceivable report outcome would change their mind. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot capture — for instance, information bought for reassurance or as political cover, and situations where the information would be ignored or misused.
