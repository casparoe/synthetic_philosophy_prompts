I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about language death, and we ended up holding three judgments that seem hard to reconcile. We both accepted that when an endangered language loses its last fluent speakers, something of real value is destroyed, not only for the community but for anyone who regards linguistic diversity as a form of human wealth; it is the sort of loss that could justify public money and institutional effort. We also both felt that parents who, under economic or social pressure, deliberately raise their children only in the dominant language and let the heritage language lapse within a generation have done nothing blameworthy, even when this is exactly how most languages die, quietly and without coercion. And yet we both thought that a state which pursued the same outcome through schooling or language policy, whether by teaching only the majority language or by making the minority language unavailable in public life, would be committing a serious wrong.

So we were left with an asymmetry: the same gradual loss of a language can look like a public misfortune when seen as an outcome, like an innocent private decision when produced by scattered families, and like an injustice when produced by policy. What, if anything, justifies this pattern of judgment? Is the relevant difference coercion versus choice, intention versus foreseen side effect, the dispersed agency of many versus the concentrated agency of the state, or something else? Give me the strongest principle you can construct, test it against harder cases—for instance, a state that merely incentivizes the dominant language, or parents whose economic alternatives are so poor that switching is close to forced—and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. I also want to know what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
