For the past several meetings my moral philosophy reading group has been circling the same impasse over Scanlon-style contractualism and the aggregation problem, and I want to understand the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. The contractualist side holds that an act is wrong when it would be disallowed by any principle that no one affected could reasonably reject, and that this blocks treating the reasons of distinct individuals as a pooled sum: a justification must be answerable to each affected person separately. They take it as fixed that one person's severe pain cannot be justified by the fact that millions of others would go on enjoying a broadcast — Scanlon's transmitter-room case — and they trace this to the separateness of persons, the idea that one person's loss is not made good by another person's gain. The aggregationist side grants that verdict but insists it cannot be generalized. A view that refuses aggregation, they argue, cannot explain why one should save five drowning people rather than one when their claims are equal, or why it can be right to impose a small cost on one person for a very large collective benefit. The contractualist then tries to earn those results without aggregating, by arguing that the number of equal claimants matters through the equal standing of each person rather than through a sum; the aggregationist replies that this is aggregation under another name. Everyone in the room accepts the same verdicts in the standard cases, and everyone can predict each move before it is made. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement and not merely a verbal one: the two sides take different classes of cases as non-negotiable data — equal-claim rescue cases for one side, cases of one person's serious harm against many small conveniences for the other — and then build theory outward from those starting points. Is that the right diagnosis? Is there a hidden formal or conceptual question here, such as exactly which operation is meant by aggregation and which of those operations, if any, the contractualist must forbid? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal characterization, a new kind of case, or an argument that the debate should be relocated rather than continued?
