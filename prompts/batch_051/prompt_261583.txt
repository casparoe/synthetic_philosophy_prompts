I keep a loosely structured dream journal, and it has made me notice something about my own reports. When I wake and say that I was chased through a station or that I argued with a dead relative, the report does not feel like the retrieval of an episode I lived through while asleep. It feels like a construction: a rush of images and emotions that surfaces as I return to consciousness, with gaps that I immediately and automatically fill, and with transitions I seem to invent on the spot. Because of this, I have adopted a standing epistemic rule: I treat my waking dream reports as evidence about what I am doing at the moment of reporting—assembling a narrative—and not as evidence that a corresponding experience occurred during sleep. The rule is motivated by a discriminative problem. From the inside, I cannot distinguish between two possibilities: a dream that was genuinely experienced during sleep and later remembered, and a dream that is first assembled at or after waking and then backdated in my autobiography as though it had occurred an hour earlier.

A friend objects that this is an absurd degree of distrust in my best, and perhaps only, evidence: whatever a dream report is, its being caused by an earlier dream is precisely what makes it a dream report rather than a waking fantasy. I reply that the report may be the dream's entire existence, and that no act of introspection could compare the report with an earlier episode to confirm that the two match. Is my rule defensible as a policy about my own mental life? Under what conditions would a waking report count as evidence of an experience during sleep, and could I ever know from the inside that those conditions held on a particular night? Is there a more moderate version of the rule that preserves suspicion of backdated narratives without refusing, across the board, to take my own mental life at its word?
