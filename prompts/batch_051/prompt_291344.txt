I've been defending what I think is the only defensible version of the precautionary principle: it isn't a rule that requires proof of safety before acting, but a rule about reversibility and option preservation — don't take moves that permanently close off future courses of action under deep uncertainty, and beyond that, uncertainty is just another cost to manage. I want to know what would count against this. In particular: are there cases where an irreversible commitment under radical uncertainty is clearly rational, not because you know enough to be confident but because the act of deciding itself changes what's possible (burning the boats so you can play equilibria you otherwise couldn't)? Are there cases where keeping options open is itself the risky move — where reversibility is cheap but the merely possible future keeps corrupting present choice? And is the strong reading that treats hazards as guilty until proven innocent maybe right for some classes of tail risk, even when consequentialist-looking considerations can't see it? Rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which are live rather than just logically possible, and say whether the reversibility view has just been insulated so it can't loose.
