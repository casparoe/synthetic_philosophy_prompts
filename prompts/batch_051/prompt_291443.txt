I'm a retired chemist who got into political philosophy a couple years back, and I keep getting hung up on the idea of performance legitimacy — roughly, that a government earns its right to rule, or at least gets treated as legitmate, by delivering prosperity, order, decent administration and the like, rather than by coming to power through free elections. The two cases everyone reaches for are Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew and China after 1978. I'd like you to analyze Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew through that lens, with China brought in only as a quick contrast if it helps.

Start by pulling apart two versions of the claim: the explanatory one (people accept the regime because it performs) and the justificatory one (the performance makes the regime morally entitled to rule). Keep them separate, because the literature I've read slides between them constantly. Then do the case analysis on the explanatory side. Who is the relevant agent — Lee himself, or the PAP as an institution? What counts as 'performance': GDP growth, public housing, corruption control, public order? Which of those was actually doing the legitimacy work, and what evidence would you look at to tell? Say where the theory goes quiet or misdescribes: preventive detention and press controls, defamation suits that bankrupted opposition politicians, the fact that the PAP also kept winning real elections, and the way the premiership later passed to Lee's son.

Then do the justificatory side. If a country becomes rich, safe and well-run under authoritarian rule, does that ever add up to justifying the absence of meaningful political competition? Or does that conclusion need a hidden premise — that staying quiet counts as consent, or that outcomes are all that matter — that someone has to argue for? Finally, turn the case back around on the theory: does Singapore confirm performance legitimacy, complicate it, or show that 'performance' covers too many different things to explain or justify anything on its own? I dont need citations; I want a tight argument, and I want you to actually take a side.
