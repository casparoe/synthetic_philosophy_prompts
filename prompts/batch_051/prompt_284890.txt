I'm not a philosopher or anything, so forgive me if I use the wrong words for this. I've been thinking about humility alot — everyone talks about it like it's obviously a virtue, but I keep coming back to the idea that there's a kind of humility that's really just ducking responsibility for your own judgment. Basically, always assuming other people know better than you, or making yourself small so you never have to stick your neck out. My book club is doing The Remains of the Day next month, and Stevens seems almost like a test case for this. He erases himself in the name of being a good butler: he defers to Lord Darlington about everything, goes along with getting rid of the two Jewish maids without pushing back, and misses his father dying because he can't step out of the role for one night. He treats dignity as if it means having no opinions of your own, or at least never letting them show. Is that actual humility, or is it moral cowardice wearing a humble costume? And it isn't only his employer — with Miss Kenton he can't even admit to himself what he feels, so the modesty seems to go all the way down into self-deception. Could you read the novel through that tension? I'd want you to be fair to both sides: the places where his humility (intellectual, moral, social — I dunno if those are even the right categories) actually functions like a virtue, and the places where it curdles into something that lets a decent man serve a bad master. What would someone who defends humility as a virtue say Stevens gets right, and what does the novel show that those defenses tend to miss? Also, does the book seem to think there's a line between good humility and giving your whole self away, or is it more pessimistic — like, in his world, dignity and self-deception might be the same thing? Since I'm leading the discussion I want to avoid turning it into a moral lesson, so be honest about the spots where the book resists a clean philosophical reading too.
