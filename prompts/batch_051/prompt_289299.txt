I serve on my university's animal care and use committee. We are about to consider a proposal from the veterinary school to run a terminal-surgery practical for third-year students: healthy dogs, purpose-bred for the exercise, would be anaesthetised throughout a session in which students practise abdominal procedures on living tissue, and would be killed without ever regaining consciousness. The school's case is that no felt pain is involved, that synthetic models and cadavers cannot reproduce tissue handling or the consequences of surgical error, and that the realistic alternative is supervised practice on client-owned patients, which transfers risk from healthy animals to animals whose owners have entrusted them to the school. The committee's review framework is Kantian in the following specific sense: we treat the standing principle as the view that we have no direct duties to animals, but that we owe duties with regard to them because cruelty towards animals dulls the sympathetic dispositions on which our duties to human beings partly rest. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before our meeting. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions; give an actionable bottom line and a time frame for any conditions; and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, the memo must confront the fact that the usual indirect-duty objection, that a practice will coarsen students, is not straightforwardly available here, since the animals feel nothing and the students know it, while the school is invoking a duty to human beings, the owners whose animals would otherwise bear the risk of novice error, on the other side. It should also say plainly whether the framework as stated can condemn painless, acknowledged instrumental use of animals, or whether a refusal would require the committee to rely on a further principle it has not yet adopted. The student body is itself divided: some argue the practical instils professional discipline and a duty of care, while others argue that treating healthy dogs as disposable instruments is exactly the sort of habituation the Kantian principle is meant to guard against. Throughout, separate what is settled within the Kantian framework as stated from what is genuinely contested among those who have studied the position, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Write the answer in British English.
