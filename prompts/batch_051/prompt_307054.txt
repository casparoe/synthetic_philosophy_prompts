You are a political philosopher working on the comparative ethics of corruption, patronage, and gift economies. Before committing to a verdict, brainstorm the mixed and intermediate cases this raises.

A colleague who studies politics in a clientelist setting defends its central practice this way: a politician pays school fees, hospital bills, and funeral costs for constituents, gives short-term jobs on public projects, and sponsors communal events, while expecting recipients to attend his rallies, display his posters, and mobilize votes for him. International anti-corruption bodies call this corruption. My colleague says they are mistaking gift exchange for corruption: these favors create the same diffuse obligations, standing relationships, and cycles of reciprocity as wedding gifts or helping a neighbor bring in a harvest, so the anti-corruption judgment merely imposes a Western, legalistic standard on a legitimate moral economy.

How far does that analogy actually hold? Spell out the intended correspondence in a table (gift, giver, recipient, obligation incurred, time horizon, sanction for non-reciprocity), then identify where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from those that do not, and say which features of ordinary gift exchange the analogy quietly imports that the political relationship lacks. Finally, state whether the conclusion — that this practice should not count as corruption, and that the Western standard is the problem — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what better analogy you would use instead.

End by saying what would change your verdict: which further empirical findings or arguments would push you the other way, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
