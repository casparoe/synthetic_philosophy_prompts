So I cover early religion and psychology for a magazine, and i'm doing a piece on why oath-swearing still feels binding even to people who don't believe there's anyone watching. I want to open with a poem, because honestly the whole subject is about words that won't let go of you.

Could you write a sestina from the point of view of Zeus right after he's sworn by the Styx to grant Semele whatever she wants? In the version I'm working from, he's already realized what she's going to ask for — to see him in his true form — and he knows it'll burn her up, but he can't unswear it. The old story (Hesiod, and Ovid retells the Semele bit) has a very precise punishment for gods who break Styx oaths: they lie breathless and speechless for a year, and then they're cut off from the food and company of the other gods for nine more. But here's the thing that interests me: Zeus is the one who usually punishes oath-breakers, so who punishes him? Thats the puzzle I want the poem to sit inside.

Form-wise, a proper sestina: six six-line stanzas where the same six end-words keep rotating in the fixed pattern, then a three-line envoi that uses all six. Pick six end-words that do the philosophical work — maybe swear, Styx, word, held, ask, burn, something like that. The repetition shouldn't just be decoration; the form should be the binding. Every time the speaker tries to turn the thought a different way, the same words come back round and force the oath back into his mouth.

Take a definite position on what actually does the binding here — since there's no higher god to enforce it, what makes the oath bind Zeus? Is it the water itself, the fact of having said the words in that form, the kind of being he'd become if he broke it, something else? Build the poem around that, don't just gesture at it. Then give me a short note after the poem on the places where you've taken liberties with the myth or leaned on a particular reading of what makes oaths bind.
