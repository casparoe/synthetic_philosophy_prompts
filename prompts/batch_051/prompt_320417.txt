I'm taking a research ethics course this term, and I want to get clear about what the institutional practice around randomized controlled trials actually presupposes. As we've learned it, the requirement of clinical equipoise has two faces: an ethics board will approve a randomized trial only while the relevant expert community is genuinely uncertain which arm is superior, and the trial is supposed to be stopped early once the accumulated data would resolve that community-level uncertainty. Yet this structure treats randomization as permissible as long as no expert consensus identifies a worse arm, even if a particular patient would, given her own values, strongly prefer one arm; and it treats assignment to the arm that later turns out worse as not itself a wrong, because it was not knowable at the time. What theory of the researcher's obligation — and of what it is to wrong a research subject — does this practice presuppose? Is it committed to something like a therapeutic duty that is relative to the epistemic state of the expert community, rather than to the patient's own interests or to consent? Please reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it is merely compatible with, and explain what would have to change if that view were replaced by a purely consent-based or expected-benefit account of trial ethics.
