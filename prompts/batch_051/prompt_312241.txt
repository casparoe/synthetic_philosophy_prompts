I'm trying to run an adversarial collaboration between two people who have been arguing for about a year over whether markets corrupt or civilise character. Call one the Civilising side: they hold that commerce is a school of the virtues, because honest dealing with strangers, keeping promises you could get away with breaking, delaying gratification, and cooperating across suspicion all get trained by repeated exchange. Call the other the Corrupting side: they hold that markets train us to see other people instrumentally, crowd out intrinsic motivation, and turn goods that ought to be governed by love, duty or citizenship into priced commodities, so that over time people become more mercenary outside the market too. I'm drafting the document for them, and I want you to write it.

They've already settled some common ground, which you can take as given and tighten rather than reopen: functioning markets need some prior background of trust and contract enforcement that they cannot produce entirely from zero; reputational pressure in repeated exchange (think buyer-seller feedback systems) makes strangers behave more honestly; competition builds some traits while eroding others; and prices do not merely allocate goods, they also express and sometimes reshape the meaning of what is exchanged. The fight is about what these facts add up to.

I suspect the real cruxes are these: whether the honesty of the merchant is the moral virtue of honesty or only a prudent simulation of it; whether market habits transfer to non-market life or stay compartmentalised; and whether pricing care, blood or other non-market goods corrupts their value or merely re-describes it. Use those if theyre actually the live ones, replace them if not.

Write it in British English, and structure the document like this:

1. Agreed claims (about 400 words, numbered): the substantive claims both camps can sign, worded neutrally so neither side flinches, and not vacuous.

2. Disagreements table (about 500 words): a table with columns for (a) the question stated neutrally, (b) the Civilising side's own wording, (c) the Corrupting side's own wording, and (d) whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.

3. Cruxes and predictions (about 400 words): for each crux, a concrete case, natural experiment or piece of evidence both sides would accept bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — include at least one prediction that would genuinely embarrass each side if the evidence went the other way.

4. Pressure points (about 200 words): the places where each side's position is most strained, phrased so each side accepts the wording.

No references or reading lists needed, just the document.
