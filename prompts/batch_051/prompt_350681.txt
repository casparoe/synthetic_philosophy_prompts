I am a retired natural scientist—an experimental physicist by training—who has recently begun reading philosophy of causation. I have been puzzling over causation by omission, and I have collected my own intuitive verdicts on a series of cases. I cannot tell whether they add up to a single coherent view. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in causation and metaphysics, and to reason carefully: begin by sketching two or three candidate principles, test them against my cases, note where each fails, and only then present the best account you can defend.

In what follows, when I say that an omission caused an outcome, I mean causation in the factual, worldly sense, not legal liability or moral blame. I recognize that these may not come apart cleanly; that is part of what interests me.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A nurse is assigned to a hospital patient and has a standing written order to administer an antibiotic at 8:00 a.m. She forgets. By evening the patient is in septic shock. I judge that the nurse's omission caused the shock.

2. A retired nurse is visiting a relative in the same hospital. She happens to read the bedside chart, sees the 8:00 a.m. antibiotic order, and notices at 9:00 a.m. that no one has administered it. She says nothing to the staff. The patient goes into septic shock. I judge, with some unease, that the visitor's omission did not cause the shock.

3. A city engineer submits a report warning that an aging earthen dam must have its spillway gates replaced. The city council receives the report but never allocates funds for the replacement. The gates fail during the next heavy rain, and downstream homes are flooded. I judge that the council's omission caused the flood.

4. A birdwatcher walks along the same dam every weekend, notices water seeping through the earthen wall, and never reports it. The wall fails during the next heavy rain, and downstream homes are flooded. I judge that the birdwatcher's omission did not cause the flood.

5. An engineering firm discovers that a safety valve it sells sticks open in roughly one test out of every five hundred but issues no recall. In a customer's plant the valve sticks open, releasing a toxic gas. I judge that the firm's omission caused the release.

6. A shift supervisor at the same plant notices during a routine check that the valve is sluggish to close and could have shut the system down for maintenance, but he does not. The valve sticks open the next day, releasing a toxic gas. Here I find myself unsure whether the supervisor's omission caused the release, and I keep reversing myself.

I am struck by the contrasts between cases 1 and 2 and between cases 3 and 4. In each pair, the underlying physical situation before the outcome looks nearly identical except for who failed to act and what role they stood in. That suggests to me that role or expectation is doing causal work, but I do not want to conclude that causation by omission is just a disguised moral judgment.

Is there one principle that captures all five of my confident verdicts and also yields a determinate verdict in case 6? If so, state it precisely and show how it handles each case. If the only principles that capture my confident verdicts import normative notions such as duty, professional role, or reasonable expectation, I am willing to accept that, but then I need an account of how those notions enter causation without collapsing causation into blame. If no reasonably simple principle captures all five, tell me which of my confident verdicts you would overturn and why, and what your preferred principle says about case 6.

Please write your answer in American English.
