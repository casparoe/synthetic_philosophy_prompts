For a while now I have been collecting examples that seem to raise one underlying issue, and what I want first is the specification that any adequate account of it would have to satisfy, not a survey of existing accounts. Consider four. (i) In the real numbers, "the square root of 2" names a unique, distinguished object, because the ordering of the reals selects the positive solution; in the complex numbers nothing selects i over −i, since complex conjugation is an automorphism fixing every real number and carrying every arithmetic statement about i to the corresponding true statement about −i. The label is arbitrary, but harmless. (ii) A finite-dimensional vector space has no privileged basis, and facts such as dimension, rank, and the eigenvalues of an operator are formulated so as not to depend on any basis. (iii) A function defined on equivalence classes—multiplication of fractions viewed as names for the same rational, say—is legitimate only if the recipe returns the same output on every representative; failing that, the would-be definition is defective. (iv) Some constructions obtain an object only by making choices along the way (one representative from each equivalence class, one orientation, one coordinate system), and later statements can turn out to depend on those choices even when they appear to be about the object itself.

These four sit differently: in the first, a convention works because a symmetry connects the available options; in the second, invariance is built into the relevant notions; the third is the textbook idea of a well-defined map; and the fourth is where the failure is easy to miss. I want the desiderata for a theory of well-definedness and canonical choice, with the following purpose: letting a working mathematician judge whether a label or a choice is harmless, and why. What must such a theory get right? Which cases must it handle—including any you would add—and which distinctions must it refuse to collapse, such as the distinction between "the labels are arbitrary but the structure is preserved" and "no unique object has been picked out at all"; between canonicity up to a unique isomorphism and a merely conventional label; and between a choice whose downstream claims are invariant under every admissible alternative and one whose downstream claims genuinely differ? Which facts of mathematical practice may it not contradict—for instance, that the symmetry between i and −i is exactly what makes that convention harmless, and that dimension is basis-independent? For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a nicety.

Then separate the desiderata that are non-negotiable from those that reflect the taste of one philosophical camp, flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single desideratum that does the most to exclude otherwise plausible accounts of what "canonical" means. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the deepest difficulty any such theory has to resolve.
