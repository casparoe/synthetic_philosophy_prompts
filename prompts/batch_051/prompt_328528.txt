You are an expert in the philosophy of punishment and in the design of legal institutions. Your task is to analyze a proposal, not to defend or attack punishment itself. Think carefully: begin by brainstorming the actors whose incentives would change, then work through each stage of the mechanism, and only then assess the system's likely failure points.

A jurisdiction proposes replacing ordinary judicial sentencing for serious felonies with a two-stage institution. In Stage 1, a Desert Panel of five lay assessors, chaired by a retired judge, hears the full facts of the offense and the offender's history. Its sole task is to set a ceiling: the sentence this offender deserves, understood as a backward-looking judgment only, with no consideration of deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, or any other future benefit. The Panel writes a short explanation of its ceiling, but the ceiling and the explanation are not made public; they are transmitted to the Prediction Panel and to the offender. In Stage 2, a separate Prediction Panel of criminologists and statisticians receives the ceiling, the explanation, and a de-identified case packet containing the offense of conviction, the offender's criminal history, and the inputs required by the risk model. The Prediction Panel must set the actual sentence somewhere between fifty and one hundred percent of the ceiling. It is forbidden to consider desert or the moral gravity of the offense; it may rely only on risk-assessment models estimating how sentence length affects the offender's probability of reoffending, and how many crimes incapacitation would prevent. It never sees the offender's name, race, sex, or any directly identifying characteristic. Each final decision is published with the model's estimates, the sentence, and the sentence expressed as a percentage of the ceiling, but the ceiling itself remains unpublished so the public cannot know how much of the deserved sentence was withheld.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Analyze what it incentivizes among offenders and defense lawyers, prosecutors, Desert Panel members, Prediction Panel members, and the firms that sell risk-assessment models. How would each group game it? What happens to the expressive function of punishment when the sentence is visibly produced by two panels working under contradictory instructions? How is the public likely to respond to a hidden desert ceiling and a mandatory prediction discount? Does the institution actually separate desert from prediction, or does it push desert judgments into the risk models and risk judgments into the Desert Panel? For example, how will Desert Panel members understand the task of setting a deserved sentence once they know another panel will cut it by as much as half? Identify the points where the system is most likely to break down, explain whether those failures could be fixed without collapsing the two panels into one, and conclude by saying whether the institution would be stable or would drift toward one of its two underlying rationales.
