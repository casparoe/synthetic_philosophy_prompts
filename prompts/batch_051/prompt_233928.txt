Over the past month a debate in my household has hardened into a standoff over whether my sister should stop flying to academic conferences for climate reasons. My father keeps reaching for some version of "What if everyone did that?"—if everyone stopped flying, airlines and the communities dependent on them would collapse—while my sister replies that her single choice tips no relevant threshold and that the slogan merely describes her action at an uncharitable level of generality. I have read some Kant, but I find I cannot articulate what the universalization test is actually meant to isolate once one steps beyond Kant's own treatment of maxims.

I would appreciate an explanation of the conceptual geography here, written for someone in my position. In particular: (1) how Kant's contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-willing tests differ from the later universalizability debates associated with R. M. Hare and Peter Singer; (2) how a Hare-style universal prescriptivist would handle the act-description problem—the fact that "what if everyone did that?" yields different verdicts depending on which description one generalizes over (say, "everyone flew to conferences in the institutionally ordinary way" versus "everyone knowingly contributed a marginal share to a collectively harmful practice"); and (3) how Singer's drowning-child argument differs from a generalization test, and why it is supposed to be resistant to the reply "but what if everyone gave that much?" I am especially interested in whether a Hare-style universalizability requirement is best understood as a merely formal consistency constraint on prescriptions rather than a substantive decision procedure, and what follows for my family's argument if that is the right way to understand it.
