Last night I had a long argument with a friend about generics, and it made me realize how unprepared I am for the question period after my upcoming talk. I will defend the claim that generics like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" are true in virtue of something about the kind itself — roughly, whether the property is part of the kind's explanatory or characteristic profile — rather than in virtue of statistical prevalence among members of the kind. The audience is a language-and-cognition workshop: mostly formal semanticists who want to analyze generics by positing a hidden generic quantifier or a contextually supplied frequency parameter, plus several psychologists who study how people acquire such statements and suspect that apparent truth is really a measure of how striking or dangerous the property is. Prepare me for the question period: what objections will each group raise, phrased in their own terms and ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; which objections have real force and should be conceded up front; which should be resisted, with what reply, and where that reply is weakest; which single reply has to land for the talk to survive; and what is the one objection I should hope nobody asks?
