I am writing a position statement for a seminar on causation in early Islamic philosophy. The view I want to defend is broadly al-Ghazālīan occasionalism, but I want to avoid the familiar consequence of making ordinary causal talk false or scientific practice irrational. Below is the set of commitments I currently hold. Before I commit any of this to paper, I would like a consistency audit: which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, how severe each genuine tension is, and what would have to be given up in each case. Please rank the tensions by severity and say what the cheapest repair is for each rather than treating them all as equally serious.

1. Occasionalism about the natural world: God is the only cause. No created thing—fire, cotton, or anything else—has causal powers, and no connection among created events is necessary in itself.
2. Deflationary realism about causal talk: ordinary sentences such as "the fire caused the cotton to burn" are true, but they do not attribute an inherent power to fire; they report that burning follows contact with fire in accordance with God's customary order.
3. The customary order is counterfactual-supporting: if the same cotton had been placed in the same fire, it would have burned.
4. What makes such counterfactuals true is neither a disposition in the cotton nor a law of nature distinct from God's will but the stability and wisdom of God's custom.
5. Miracles are possible at any point: because the customary order is freely sustained by God, God could have omitted any element of it and could at any moment suspend any element of it.
6. Induction is rationally in good order: past regularities provide a good but fallible reason to expect future regularities, grounded in God's wisdom and in the observed stability of God's custom, not in a principle of the uniformity of nature that would bind God.
7. Scientific practice needs no revision: conservation principles, equations, and the other well-established regularities of physics are best read as descriptions of God's customary manner of acting, not as discoveries of creaturely powers.
8. Citing ordinary causes is explanatory as well as true: "the fire caused the burn" explains the burn, even though the fire has no productive efficacy.

Please go through these carefully. I want to know where the strain actually is before I decide which commitment, if any, to revise.
