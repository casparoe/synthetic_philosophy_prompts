For a piece I'm planning on how cost-effectiveness reasoning has moved from policy conferences into bedside triage, I'd like a satirical sidebar. Write a deadpan parody of a patient admissions handbook for a hospital that has fully, proudly implemented QALY-based priority-setting: every proposed treatment is scored by expected quality-adjusted life years gained per dollar and compared against a single published threshold, and the hospital describes this as the fairest possible system because no one gets special treatment. The handbook should be written in the flat, reassuring, procedural voice of actual hospital literature — sections on intake, how your quality-of-life score is measured, how the threshold works, how to appeal — and it should never break character or wink at the reader. The satire should exaggerate only what the practice already contains, so a health economist recognizes the real machinery: a generic-preference utility questionnaire, time-trade-off and standard-gamble valuations, discounting, the accountancy of statistical lives. Without stating them outright, the piece should lodge the standard objections: that the system quietly values the old and disabled less; that a threshold attaches a price to an identified life while pretending it prices only statistical ones; that purportedly objective utility weights encode strangers' judgments about which lives are worth living; and that an appeals process run by the same cost-effectiveness committee is no safeguard at all. Aim for roughly 700 words, and let the unease come entirely from the deadpan.
