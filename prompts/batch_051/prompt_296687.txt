I work as a software engineer and have never studied philosophy formally, so I want this stress-tested from the ground up rather than defended. I've arrived at a position about psychopathy and moral knowledge, and I want to know what would count against it.

The position is this. When a psychopath says, in an interview or a task, that what he did was wrong, he is not reporting the same kind of thing an ordinary person reports. He has the label. He can attach the word "wrong" to the right cases, often with no worse accuracy than anyone else. But the label is decoupled from whatever it is that makes a classification a piece of knowledge rather than a correct output. He is like a lookup table that returns the right value without possessing the concept. What he lacks is not the ability to classify the act; it is the thing the classification is supposed to be a report of.

I keep coming back to a passage in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII, chapter 3 (H. Rackham's translation), because it seems to me to state, for a different phenomenon, the exact distinction my view depends on. Aristotle is writing about lack of self-restraint, not psychopathy, but the machinery is what matters:

"But persons under the influence of passion are in the same condition; for it is evident that anger, sexual desire, and certain other passions, actually alter the state of the body, and in some cases even cause madness. It is clear therefore that we must pronounce the unrestrained to 'have knowledge' only in the same way as men who are asleep or mad or drunk. Their using the language of knowledge is no proof that they possess it. Persons in the states mentioned repeat propositions of geometry and verses of Empedocles; students who have just begun a subject reel off its formulae, though they do not yet know their meaning, for knowledge has to become part of the tissue of the mind, and this takes time. Hence we must conceive that men who fail in self-restraint talk in the same way as actors speaking a part."

Read that as a statement of my position, with the psychopath in the place of the person Aristotle describes. Now: what would count against it? I want a map of the view's exposure, not arguments for it. Which experimental results, developmental observations, or conceptual arguments would show, if true, that psychopaths do know that what they do is wrong in the sense that matters? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and, for each, say whether a committed defender of my view would have to concede that it counts against her or whether she could dismiss it, and why. Also say what my view predicts that its main rival does not — the rival being something like: psychopaths know exactly what they are doing, they simply do not care — and what that rival would count as evidence against mine. Finally, tell me honestly whether my distinction between having the words and having the knowledge has been qualified so far that no observation could disturb it, and, if it has, whether that is a defect or a legitimate feature of a claim about something as internal as knowing.
