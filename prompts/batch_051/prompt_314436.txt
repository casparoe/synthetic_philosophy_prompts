I have come to regard the common prior assumption as more than a modeling convenience in the theory of games. The position I hold is this: for ideally rational agents who share a common space of possible states and a common algebra of propositions, any difference in their degrees of belief is rationally permissible only insofar as it reflects a difference in information. Consequently, if such agents' posterior probabilities over the shared space are common knowledge, those posteriors must coincide; any residual disagreement must be redescribed as a difference in private evidence or as a lapse of rationality, and it cannot be traced to an original and rationally defensible difference in priors. I want you to tell me what would count against this position, not by surveying the literature but by mapping the position's exposure. Where does it lean on assumptions it cannot justify from within—the shared state space, the common algebra, common knowledge of rationality itself? Which formal results, models, or conceptual distinctions should most unsettle a defender of the view? Which defeaters are decisive, and which are merely awkward? What would a careful defender concede counts against the position, as opposed to dismissing the objector as having changed the subject? Does the position risk becoming unfalsifiable when any disagreement can be re-described as a difference in private information, and if so, is that insulation a defect in a normative claim of this kind? I am especially interested in whether purported cases of disagreement under fully common evidence—in scientific theory choice or philosophical controversy—can be made precise enough to count as genuine counterexamples, since those strike me as the sharpest possible challenge. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the position survives them.
