I'm a clinical psychologist at a university counseling center, and I keep seeing the same pattern: a student comes in unhappy, and their own theory about why they're unhappy is that they haven't optimized their happiness enough. They've read a couple pop psych books, they keep a gratitude journal, they track their mood in an app, they schedule social time because loneliness is bad for you, and the whole project has started to feel like a second job that makes them more miserable. I have a vague memory from undergrad that the "paradox of hedonism" is about exactly this—that making happiness the direct target can be self-defeating and that happiness ends up being a byproduct of doing other things for their own sake. But I don't trust my memory of the arguments, and I also don't want to become one of those clinicians who tells a probably-depressed student to just stop trying so hard. So I want something I can actually use in an intake appointment, not an essay, and something I can explain to my supervisor when she asks what the procedure is based on.

Give me a decision procedure—a checklist or a set of questions in order—for deciding whether a given student's low mood is a "hedonism paradox" case where I should steer them away from direct happiness-optimization and toward engagement with something they find worthwhile for its own sake, or instead a more standard depression/anhodenia case where I should treat the low mood first with something like behavioral activation. The procedure has to be usable in about fifteen minutes with a student I've never met, by someone who hasn't read the philosophical literature. For each question, say what kind of answer counts as a signal in either direction. Flag the steps where you're making an empirical claim about what actually makes people happier rather than a conceptual point, and the steps where the decision is really a values call about what kind of life the student should be helped toward. Also tell me where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to clinical judgment instead of pretending the checklist decides.

Then run the procedure on three intake cases: (1) a student who spends an hour-plus a day on happiness interventions, can list their "wellness routine" in detail, but can't name anything they do purely because it's absorbing or worthwhile; (2) a student with classic anhedonia who says they've lost interest in the things they used to love and nothing sounds worth doing; and (3) a student whose mood is basically fine by any measure but who worries they're failing at happiness because their friends look happier on social media. Push the procedure hard enough that you include at least one case where it tells me something I'd rather not hear, and end by saying what the whole approach can't do.
