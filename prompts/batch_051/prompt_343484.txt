I maintain a small proprietary desktop application, and I have been trying to work out whether distributing it can be reconciled with a command-line engine I rely on, which is released under a reciprocal copyleft licence. The licence contains this clause: "You may distribute the Program, or any work derived from it, only under the terms of this Licence. A work is derived from the Program if it incorporates any part of the Program, or if it is designed to be linked with the Program so that the two operate as a single functional unit in the same address space." My application never links to the engine's code. It is separately compiled and distributed; at startup it looks for the engine on the user's machine and, if present, launches it as a subprocess, serialises a representation of the user's document, sends it over a local socket, and receives back a list of edits that my interface then presents as if the application itself had produced them. The application is marketed as a polished front end for the engine, and without the engine installed it has no useful function, but I ship only the application: the user downloads and installs the engine separately, under its own terms.

I am less interested in predicting litigation than in understanding what the clause, and the idea of a derived work that it codifies, would have to mean in order to reach my arrangement. Give me the readings one by one. On the plain words, does a private protocol between two separate processes count as "linked so that the two operate as a single functional unit in the same address space"? On a purposive reading, would exempting this design simply reward moving the boundary between programs to a socket, or is the fact that I do not distribute the engine decisive? What would the drafters most plausibly have said if shown this architecture and asked whether it fell within their clause, given that the address-space language was presumably aimed at dynamic linking rather than meant as a formal loophole? What would an ordinary developer reading the licence reasonably take the terms to permit? And where the readings diverge, which should govern: the wording as publicly relied upon, the purpose of the copyleft condition, or the expectations of downstream users? Does the boundary between one program and another lie in the compiled code, the running process, the protocol, or the user's act of assembly, and what would it take for a definition of this sort to track that boundary rather than pick one of several arbitrary cut points?
