I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and in the literature that tries to explain religion as a natural phenomenon I keep running into a comparison: a religion is like a pathogen, and religious beliefs and practices spread the way infections do. On this picture, a doctrine or a ritual is the infectious agent; a congregation, a region, or a generation is the host population; preaching, child-rearing, and media exposure are routes of transmission; and the religions that thrive are simply the ones whose traits make them most contagious. The analogy is being made to do real work, because it is used to license a strong conclusion: that the spread and persistence of a religion can be explained without ever asking whether its teachings are true, and that what wins out is fitness for transmission rather than truth.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. First, give the correspondence table in full: what maps to the pathogen, the host, infection, the incubation period, immunity, mutation, virulence, and the basic reproduction number. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks down. I am especially interested in which features of infectious disease get quietly imported when someone leans on this analogy — for instance, that transmission is a matter of passive exposure, that hosts are largely helpless recipients, that variants arise by blind mutation rather than deliberate revision, that virulence and transmissibility trade off in a predictable way, or that the pathogen is a separate organism from the host it infects — and whether those features are actually present in the religious case. Finally, tell me whether the strong conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along. If the analogy is misleading, say what a better analogy would be and how it would change the conclusion. Please write the answer in American English.
