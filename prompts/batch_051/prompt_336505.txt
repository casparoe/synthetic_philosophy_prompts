I am a novelist at work on a book in which the protagonist, a media theorist by training, publishes a short essay on the value of privacy early in the narrative. The essay matters to the plot: colleagues quote from it, an antagonist attacks it during a public panel, and the protagonist is eventually forced to live with the consequences of having defended its central claim more strictly than she practices it. I want the essay to read as something a smart, philosophically engaged non-specialist could plausibly publish and be sincerely proud of, but I also want it to have the kinds of pressure points a trained philosopher would immediately push on, so that the panel scene does not become a straw-man debate.

Here is the essay's argument as I have sketched it. It opens by claiming that privacy is usually misdescribed as a right of control over personal information. This custodial picture, the essay argues, treats information too much like a possession and therefore misdescribes what is bad about violations: the harm of being overheard, observed, or gossiped about is not always that one has lost control of a piece of information, and can occur even when no piece of information is later misused. The protagonist's positive proposal is that privacy is better understood as a relational achievement — the condition in which a person can vary how much of herself is available to different others, and to the same other at different times, without that variance being collapsed by surveillance, rumor, or forced disclosure. Its value, she argues, lies less in keeping secrets than in preserving the ability to rehearse, revise, and abandon versions of oneself; privacy is what allows a person to be different things to different people without thereby becoming a liar. On this view, constant observation is objectionable even for a person with nothing to hide, because it flattens the contexts in which self-revision is possible.

I would be grateful for comments on three points. First, are the two moves against the control-based account genuine vulnerabilities, or is the protagonist attacking a straw man — in particular, can a sufficiently refined control account absorb the observation that a violation may involve no subsequent misuse of information? Second, does the positive relational account overgenerate — would it, for example, condemn ordinary accountability, workplace evaluation, or being known well by a small community? Third, what should the single strongest paragraph of the antagonist's reply be, so that the protagonist is challenged by something better than a mediocre objection she can dismiss in one line?
