I've been trying to convince myself that a parallel I noticed between evidence law and the pragmatic encroachment debate in epistemology is more than just a shared fondness for the word 'stakes.'

The mapping in my head goes like this. Pragmatic encroachment says a fixed body of evidence can be enough for knowledge in one practical situation and not enough in another, because the cost of being wrong raises the evidential bar. That's structurally the same claim as the law's nested burdens of proof: the same evidence can support a civil judgment under preponderance of the evidence while falling short of clear-and-convincing for fraud, or beyond-a-reasonable-doubt for a conviction. Preponderance, clear and convincing, beyond reasonable doubt — as the severity of what the state can do to you goes up, the probability the factfinder needs goes up. The Simpson case sits there as the clean illustration: acquittal and liability on roughly the same evidence.

So you could line the columns up: subject's practical situation ↔ defendant's (or society's) exposure; knowledge ↔ finding of liability or guilt; evidential standard ↔ burden of proof; stakes ↔ severity of the sanction. Both sides deny that what the evidence supports is fixed by the evidence alone.

Where I get stuck is whether the parallel runs deeper than the columns. Two disanalogies feel important, but I can't tell wether they're fatal. First, legal burdens are set ex ante by fixed rules and apply uniformly across cases, whereas pragmatic encroachment is usually framed as highly particular: your stakes right now can cost you knowledge on one Tuesday and hand it back on Wednesday. Second, the legal standard governs what a jury is permitted to do — return a verdict — not whether the jurors themselves know the defendant did it. A juror can be quite sure someone is guilty, and yet the system tells her she shouldn't vote to convict. If anything, that sounds more like contextualism — a shift in what a speaker may properly say with 'proven' or 'knows' in a legal context — than like encroachment on whether the underlying state of knowledge obtains.

So: is there a genuine identity of structure here, with the law as pragmatic encroachment rendered as an institution — or is the analogy superficial, and does the legal case actually pull toward the contextualist side? And is there anything the parallel predicts that comes out false on one side or the other, the way a good structural analogy should let you test it?
