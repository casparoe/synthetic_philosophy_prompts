I write novels, character-driven stuff, and I've been stuck for months on a question about human nature that I keep feeling in my work. I'm drawn to something like Xunzi's view: our inborn nature is basically self-seeking—desires, appetites, the works—and anything decent in us is artifice, the result of ritual and training and culture doing their slow work on raw material. It's got the virtues I like in a theory: it's parsimonious, it explains why people backslide the moment institutions loosen, and it gives a clean story about why moral education has to be actual work rather than a matter of letting something unfold. But I can't shake Mencius's intuition, the child at the well: that there's an untaught, pre-calculative alarm at another's suffering, and it shows up before self-interest can get its hands on the steering wheel. As a novelist this lands hard. Whenever I've written a character as purely self-interested, readers find them fales or inhuman, and whenever a hard character unexpectedly flinches at someone else's pain, the scene suddenly has a pulse. It feels like the flinch is the thing that's real, and the self-interest is the story we tell about ourselves afterward.

Here's what I want. Give me the strongest version of the Xunzi side—the case that the intuition should be debunked or demoted, explained as early-installed training that presents itself as raw nature, or as genre convention, or whatever the best version is. Then give me the strongest version of the Mencius side—the case that the intuition is tracking something the theory leaves out and the theory should be amended or abandoned. Then tell me which you'd give up, or whether there's a reading on which both survive without being a verbal dodge. What would it take, concretely, for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes? And if I've set the whole thing up wrong—strawmanned Xunzi, or loaded 'nature' so the two aren't actually contradicting—say so plainly rather then trying to reconcile politely. American English, please.
