I'm helping my younger sister fight a benefits sanction, and we keep going in circles about a single clause that the decision cites. I'm not anti-conditionality in general—I actually think it makes sense that someone can't just refuse ordinary work and stay on benefit—but this one feels different, and I can't tell whether I'm saying that for good reasons or just because she's family.

The clause reads:

"Where a claimant has been in receipt of an out-of-work benefit for more than thirteen weeks, the Department may require the claimant to take up any position which is offered to them, provided the position is suitable. A position is suitable if (a) the claimant can reasonably be expected to perform it; (b) the hours are consistent with any caring commitments the claimant has already disclosed; (c) it pays at least the national minimum wage; and (d) it does not require the claimant to relocate."

She was offered courier work with a same-day parcel platform. They call her a self-employed contractor: no guaranteed hours, paid per drop. To get work at all she has to book four-hour "availability blocks" a week ahead; during a block the app can assign her a route at any moment, with pickup within the hour, and she must accept 90% of what it sends or lose the account. Her daughter is seven, and she's disclosed the school runs: no work from 8:15 to 9:10, and none from 3:00 to 3:40. The only block that fits the school day is 10 to 2, but the depot's own figures show that block averages below minimum wage per hour because it's overstaffed and pay is per drop; to earn the advertised rate you have to book the 6:30–10:30 or the 3:30–7:30 blocks, which collide with the school run. The benefits office says the job is suitable: the contract has no fixed hours "so the hours are obviously consistent with childcare," the headline rates are above the minimum, and she isn't asked to move. My dad says she's being picky, and that the rule is there precisely to stop people treating the dole as a lifestyle.

I don't want a menu of possible readings and a polite "it depends." Please answer as an expert on social security law and statutory interpretation, and actually commit. Think carefully—start by brainstorming the strongest arguments on each side—then come to a definite view. Structure it like this: (1) The plain words (about 150 words): apply the language of the clause to this position, on its face. (2) The purpose (about 150 words): what the suitability test is for in a system that conditions benefit on taking work, and which reading best serves that purpose. (3) What the drafters would have said (about 150 words): if the people who drafted the clause had been asked at the time about platform courier work with no rota, how they would most likely have resolved it. (4) The reasonable claimant's reading (about 100 words): what someone in my sister's place would have taken the clause to promise when she disclosed the school runs. (5) My verdict (about 200 words): does the clause require her to take this job, what should happen at the tribunal, and which approach should win if they pull apart. Also say whether the clause itself needs amending to handle this kind of work, or whether the existing words are enough.
