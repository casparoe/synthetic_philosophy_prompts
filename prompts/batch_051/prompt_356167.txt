I am a PhD student in political theory trying to determine whether an entitlement or historical theory of justice can capture what I actually think about particular cases, and I would like help reconstructing the principle behind my verdicts. My reactions are these. (1) In the familiar Wilt Chamberlain example, where many fans each freely pay an extra quarter to watch him play and he began with a legitimate holding, his resulting wealth is just, even though the final distribution fits no favored pattern. (2) If a thief sells a stolen violin to an innocent buyer, the buyer acquires no title and the original owner's claim remains. (3) If someone wins a lottery in which every ticket was purchased voluntarily, the prize is just. (4) If the owner of a justly acquired factory increases profits by dumping effluent that foreseeably sickens people downstream, the increase is not just. (5) If I inherit a house that my grandfather acquired by fraudulently excluding a Black family from purchasing in a redlined neighborhood, with enduring effects on that family's wealth, then my entitlement is compromised and I owe something even though I did nothing wrong. (6) If the state builds a road at public expense and my justly acquired land triples in value, I am unsure whether I am entitled to the whole windfall. Is there one principle, or a well-motivated refinement of the entitlement account, that captures all of this? Which of my verdicts does the cleanest such principle force me to revise? And what does the principle you propose say about a further case: a gift from someone whose wealth derives from a long chain of otherwise voluntary exchanges that began in an unjust dispossession?
