I'm a science journalist writing a feature about why consciousness evolved, and in the course of reporting I've found myself caught in a debate that seems more interesting than the usual consciousness-is-an-illusion talk. One philosopher I interviewed defends epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that felt experience is real — seeing red and feeling pain involve genuine subjective qualities — but that these qualities never do any causal work. He compared them to smoke rising from an engine: visible, but not part of the drive train. A second philosopher pressed him with what he called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If felt qualities are causally inert, he argued, then my belief that I have them, my memory of having them, and my public reports about them are all produced by physical processes that would run in exactly the same way whether or not any felt quality accompanied them. The first philosopher replied with a common-cause analogy: we often treat one event as evidence for another when the two share a cause even though neither causes the other, much as a shadow can indicate the sun's elevation without the shadow's doing anything to the sun. The second philosopher responded that the analogy fails, because on the epiphenomenalist's own picture a physically identical counterpart of me — a zombie twin, in the philosophical sense — would issue the same reports with no felt qualities at all, so the report process cannot discriminate the two cases.

I want my article to do more than quote both sides; I want to understand which position is actually in better shape. You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in consciousness and epistemology. Please write the analytical core of my piece: an essay that weighs the strongest versions of the self-stultification objection to epiphenomenalism and of the common-cause or tracking reply, and reaches a definite, justified conclusion about whether epiphenomenalism can coherently account for our knowledge of our own phenomenal states. Start by brainstorming the most charitable formulations of each position and identifying the key assumptions each side needs; then defend a verdict and address the strongest counterargument to it. Think through the dialectic carefully, and do not end on a merely non-committal note — if your conclusion is conditional, specify the conditions precisely.
