I keep returning to the epistocracy debate and suspect both sides are grading proposals against different hidden criteria. Before I read any further arguments in either direction, I want the specification of what an adequate defense of apportioning political power by competence actually has to accomplish. Write the desiderata for a theory of when, if ever, some adults may justifiably hold more or fewer voting rights than others on the basis of competence, where the theory's purpose is to license an actual change to the franchise in a modern democracy — one that would have to be justifiable to those it disenfranchises, not merely produce better aggregate outcomes. Among the phenomena the requirements must cover, make sure to include rational ignorance about politics, the demographic skew of political knowledge, correlated bias among experts, the expressive or status value of an equal vote, the gap between competence on facts and competence on values or interests, the problem of who appoints and checks the competent, and the historical record of restricted franchises. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that puts it on the list. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp, flag any pairs you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most work against the strongest current version of epistocracy. I want a committed list — make your own calls rather than surveying the options — and I care more about the criteria and their motivating cases than about a scorecard of authors.
