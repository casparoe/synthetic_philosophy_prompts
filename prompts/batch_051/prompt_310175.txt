I have been thinking about a problem in inverse reinforcement learning and preference inference that I have been calling capability masking. The basic observation is that observed behavior never reveals preferences on its own, because what an agent does is always a joint product of what it wants and what it believes it is able to do, or what it is in fact able to do. A rat that never explores a closed corridor is not thereby telling you that it does not care what lies behind the door, and a person who never applies to a highly selective job is not thereby telling you that she prefers not to have it; each is also encoding a belief, or a fact, about feasibility. This makes every trajectory through an environment what I have taken to calling a joint encoding of desire and perceived capability.

The principle I have been trying to work out is that a sound inference from behavior to preferences must therefore co-infer two things at once: a reward function and a model of the actor's perceived action space, where the perceived action space includes both the options the actor believes are available and the outcomes the actor believes are achievable by her own efforts. I call violations of this principle capability attribution errors: the mistake of attributing to preference what is really a difference in perceived feasibility. I suspect that much of what looks like divergent preferences across people, or across cultures, is actually shared preferences run through systematically different feasibility beliefs, and that standard inverse reinforcement learning goes wrong precisely because it takes the observer's action space as the choice set rather than the actor's.

I am aware this could be a reinvention of something in revealed preference theory or in regularized inverse reinforcement learning, and that it might be too strong. I would like an honest assessment. Is the distinction coherent, and what is it really claiming beyond standard IRL? Does it collapse into ideas that already exist under better names? Give me the strongest counterexample: a case where treating the full, objectively available action space as the choice set is actually the right inference, or where my principle would systematically mislead. And tell me where it breaks down when the actor's feasibility beliefs are themselves something the actor is choosing, or when those beliefs are simply wrong.
