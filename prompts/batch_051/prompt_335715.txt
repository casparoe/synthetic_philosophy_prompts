I find myself torn between two ways of answering the question of when the Anthropocene begins, and I need help working out which should give way. The first is the stratigrapher's way. On this view the Anthropocene ought to be treated exactly like any other geological unit: a boundary is legitimate only if it can be nailed to a physically traceable, globally synchronous marker in rock, ice, or sediment — a golden spike, for instance a sediment layer of plutonium fallout from mid-twentieth-century nuclear testing. This method has real virtues. It yields a determinate, non-arbitrary answer, it holds every geological unit to the same evidential standard, and it keeps a scientific classification from dissolving into a political argument. Yet I have an intuition I cannot shake. The Anthropocene is not, at bottom, a story about sediment chemistry; it is a story about human beings becoming a planetary force — agriculture, colonization, industrialization, the Great Acceleration — and those transformations happened unevenly, over centuries, and were caused by some societies far more than others. Choosing a single marker feels like substituting a technical criterion for the thing the concept was coined to track, and I suspect the choice of a start date cannot help being an ethical and political act about whose transformation of the Earth counts as the beginning. Give me the strongest version of each position and a negotiated verdict. Should the formal stratigraphic view yield? Should the intuition yield? Or is there a reading on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing the Anthropocene as a formal chronostratigraphic unit from the Anthropocene as a historical and ethical concept — and would that distinction be a genuine resolution or a dodge? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Please write in American English.
