I am working out what I actually believe about choices in which a person cannot know in advance what the outcome will be like from the inside, and I want to know whether there is a principled account that captures my pattern of verdicts.

My gut reactions to particular cases run as follows. Deciding to try an unfamiliar fruit on the strength of other people's descriptions is straightforwardly rational; the fact that no description can tell me what durian tastes like does not make the choice epistemically problematic, since at worst I taste something unpleasant and stop. A patient with severe, treatment-resistant depression who has read the testimonials of people in a psilocybin trial, and who knows there is a non-trivial chance of a personality shift lasting several months, is rational to say yes, even though the testimonials cannot tell them what the relevant part of the experience will be like. A couple deciding to have a first child can read about parenthood, talk to parents, and weigh expected costs and benefits; I do not think the decision is irrational, but I also do not think they are doing an ordinary expected-value calculation, and the thought that you cannot know what parenthood is like until you are a parent seems to me more true in this case than in the durian case, for reasons I cannot yet articulate. Suppose a device were available that reliably ends severe chronic pain but also reliably makes people much less intellectually ambitious and much more easily contented, so that after using it they do not miss their old projects and do not regret the change. I have the strong intuition that it would not be rational for me to accept this offer, and that the fact that my post-treatment self would be satisfied does not license my choice now; this is the verdict I find hardest to reconcile with the first-child case. Someone who has studied another country's language and culture in depth but never lived there is rational to emigrate on that basis, even while knowing that their values and habits will shift in ways they cannot foresee from here, though this feels like a decision made at the far edge of what studying can tell you. And a fussy eater who refuses ever to try a new food because he cannot know what it will taste like until after is not making a deep point about transformative choices; he is just being irrational.

Is there a coherent principle behind all of these verdicts, or a small set of candidate principles that each capture most of them? In particular, what precisely distinguishes the pain-relief device from having a first child, if both change your preferences in ways you cannot fully anticipate, and the later self in both cases is happy with the change? Does the principle you propose preserve the rationality of the parent, the emigrant, and the chronic-pain patient, or does it force me to give up one of those verdicts?

Finally, what does your principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a congenitally Deaf adult who deeply values their place in Deaf culture and is deciding whether to get a cochlear implant, which would give them a sense they have never had, may alter their relationship to the community that matters to them, and cannot be tried and reversed if it changes how they feel?
