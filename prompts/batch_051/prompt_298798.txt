I have been trying to work through the nonidentity problem, and I keep reaching for a distinction that I suspect is either a genuine contribution or a piece of disguised consequentialism. I would like an honest assessment. I call it the Seat Principle. A seat, as I define it, is not a person but a position in a plan, family, or population: a position that will be filled by some possible person or other depending on what we choose, but whose occupant is not fixed in advance. The seat "child of this parent, born now, into these circumstances" and the seat "child of this parent, born five years later, into those better circumstances" are different seats, even though we cannot point to the occupant of either before the choice. My central claim is that when a choice helps determine who will exist, what is made worse off is the seat, not the person who eventually fills it. In the familiar case of a parent who conceives now rather than waiting, the actual child is not harmed, because waiting would have meant that this child never existed; but the seat the child occupies is worse than the alternative seat, because whichever child fills the alternative seat would very likely have a better life. The wrongness of the choice consists in knowingly filling a worse seat when a better seat was available.

A second example: a government adopts a cheap industrial policy whose long-run environmental effects will partly determine which people come to exist two generations from now. Those future people will probably have lives worth living, so if wrongdoing requires making some particular person worse off, it is hard to locate the harm. On my view, the seat "generation shaped by this policy" is worse than the seat "generation shaped by the cleaner alternative," and that is what makes the policy wrong. The attraction, as I see it, is that we avoid both horns of the nonidentity problem: we do not have to say the actual child or the actual future people are worse off than they would otherwise have been, and we also do not have to go wholly impersonal by ranking whole possible populations against one another, because a seat is still the shape of someone's life.

My worry is that this is exactly the impersonal comparison wearing person-centered language, since a seat is not a moral patient and cannot literally be harmed. So my questions are these. Is the Seat Principle coherent enough to add a real position to the debate, or does it collapse into a view that already has a name? Give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. In particular, I want to know whether the view goes wrong in cases where the relevant life is not worth living, and in cases where the number of future people differs depending on the choice.
