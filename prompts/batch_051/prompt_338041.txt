I help run a small nonpartisan democracy-monitoring outfit. Every few weeks we have to decide whether a specific government action — a new election law, a court-packing bill, a takeover of a public broadcaster, an NGO "foreign agents" law — belongs on our public erosion tracker as democratic backsliding or not. The tracker gets quoted in the press and attacked by governments as a partisan label, so the call has to be defensible. We're policy people, not political philosophers; we get about half a day per case, and each entry has to be signed off by another analyst and a director.

I want you to turn whatever political theory actually knows about democratic backsliding, democratic erosion, and the line between illiberal moves and ordinary policy disagreement into a written procedure we can run on a single case: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a yes or no, and where it hands off to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. Flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as method.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) an elected government expands the constitutional court from 12 to 15 seats and fills the new seats with its own appointees, on the stated ground that the previous government had packed the court; (2) a government passes a law requiring NGOs that take more than 15 percent of their funding from abroad to register as "foreign-funded" and label all public materials that way, while large domestic corporate donors face no equivalent rule; (3) an incumbent government lowers the voting age to 16 for the next election, in a year when 16- and 17-year-olds poll heavily for the incumbent. For each verdict, say which fact about the case or which different reading of the concept would most likely change it, and where the whole procedure is most likely to misfire.
