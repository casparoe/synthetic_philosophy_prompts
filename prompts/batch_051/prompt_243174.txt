In Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus, the argument that death is nothing to us is sometimes carried by a general principle stated in a single sentence:

"Whatever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation."

I want to test whether that principle is doing real work in the argument or only looks plausible because we silently supply the examples. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The case should involve a person facing a future event E such that:
(1) when E is present, it causes the person no annoyance;
(2) the reason it causes no annoyance when present is not that the person has changed their mind, been compensated, or finds E pleasant, but that E permanently removes the person's capacity to be annoyed at all;
(3) E is not the person's biological death — the person remains alive in a biological sense but will never again be aware;
(4) the person's dread of E is rationally fitting, in the sense that they can give reasons for it that do not reduce to the unpleasantness of the feeling of dread itself;
(5) the case is mundane and realistic: no afterlife, no science fiction, no perfect predictors, and no appeal to the grief of others; a hospital case or an accident will do.

The scenario itself should be no longer than a paragraph.

Then do the check. State the event; state the person's reasons for dreading it; state the sense in which E causes no annoyance when present; show that Epicurus's principle, applied strictly, would have to classify this dread as groundless; and identify which feature of the case forces that verdict and what would change if that feature were removed. Finally, say what an Epicurean could do to stop the case from counting — by biting the bullet, by denying that the principle is meant to cover such cases, or in some other way — and whether the case refutes the principle, refutes its application to death, or merely relocates the familiar dispute.
