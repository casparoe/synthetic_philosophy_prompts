In my AI alignment course we have been working through the relationship between the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence, and I keep reaching what looks like a contradiction. As I understand it, the orthogonality thesis says that an agent's terminal goals and its general intelligence are independent axes: in principle, any level of capability can be combined with any final goal. Instrumental convergence, however, says that nearly any sufficiently capable goal-directed system will converge on certain instrumental subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, improving its own reasoning, and so on—because those subgoals are useful for an enormous range of terminal goals. But if that is right, then from the outside, the behavior of powerful systems should not look goal-arbitrary at all: they should all look as if they are trying to acquire power and resist modification. So I am not sure what the orthogonality thesis is actually claiming once instrumental convergence is granted. Would a sufficiently capable paperclip-maximizer and a sufficiently capable happiness-maximizer really exhibit different terminal goals in any observable sense, or only the same convergent subgoals with a different hidden label? More generally, how should we draw the terminal/instrumental distinction in a way that keeps the orthogonality thesis from collapsing into triviality, and what empirical claim, if any, does orthogonality still make after we accept instrumental convergence?
