I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral agency and AI, and there's a case I keep going back and forth about—I half think its a counterexample to my own criteria. As a working definition I've been using three conditions: a moral agent must (i) form judgments about what it ought to do that track reasons at least somewhat, rather than just parroting; (ii) be able to act on those judgments, so its judgments actually make a difference to what it does; and (iii) be an appropriate target of praise, blame, and the rest of the reactive attitudes. Nothing fancy, I know.

The case is a hospital triage AI. It gives genuinely nuanced, reason-tracking judgments about how to prioritize patients in surge conditions, and for cases that fall under a policy it can articulate a considered recommendation. But the architecture includes a compliance layer: whenever its recommendation conflicts with an approved policy, the layer silently overwrites it and the system outputs the policy's decision. The AI can flag the conflict in a log, and a human oversight committee reviews those flags monthly; if the committee is persuaded, it updates the policy. In genuinely novel cases not covered by any policy, the AI's own judgment is what gets put into action. So at the moment of decision in covered cases it cannot act on its own judgment, but across time its judgments shape the rules that will bind it later.

Does this system count as a moral agent by my three criteria? Just go through them one by one, and where the answer is borderline, say whether the problem is with the criteria or with the case. In particular, is indirect influence on the governing policy enough to satisfy (ii)? And if it isn't, would a human in an analogous position—say a physician who can object but must follow protocol until it's revised—also fail your version of the test?
