I have become convinced by a broadly Williams-style argument about the tedium of immortality, of the sort illustrated by the Makropulos case: that a human life extended indefinitely would eventually lose its point, because the categorical desires that give a person reason to continue are bound up with a finite human nature, and over an endless life they would either run out or be repeated until they no longer sustain genuine engagement. On this view, eternal bliss is coherent only if we are no longer recognizably human, temporally extended, or project-driven — which is to say that the kind of endless life most people hope for is not something we should want.

What I want to know is what would count against this position. What would have to be true about desire, boredom, personal identity, or the structure of worthwhile activity for the argument to fail? Which forms of immortality — atemporal existence, continuously renewed desires, a non-human psychology, periodic forgetting, open-ended projects — would escape the argument, and which remain vulnerable? What would a defender of the view have to concede as evidence against it, and which supposed counterexamples would they rightly dismiss? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all or has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
