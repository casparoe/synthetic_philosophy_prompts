Consider the following argument, which I have encountered only in compressed form and want assessed carefully rather than shrugged off.

(1) Peirce holds that a proposition is true just in case it is the opinion that would be supported by all the evidence and agreed to by all inquirers at the ideal limit of inquiry.
(2) Some propositions are such that no possible inquiry, however prolonged or well conducted, could ever settle them — for instance, whether Caesar sneezed an even number of times over the course of his life, or whether the number of stars that have ever existed is even or odd.
(3) For any such proposition p, there is no consideration that could rationally drive convergence; so the final opinion, if it is formed only on evidence, must remain silent on both p and not-p.
(4) From (1) it follows that neither p nor not-p is true.
(5) But bivalence says that for each such proposition, either p is true or not-p is true.
(6) That yields a contradiction, so Peirce's account of truth must be false.

Assess this argument closely: is it valid as stated, and if not, which step exactly fails? If it is valid, which premise is the best candidate to reject, and what is the strongest Peircean response? In particular, address whether rejecting (5) for these cases is a defensible price rather than an ad hoc retreat, and whether drawing a sharp distinction between a definition of truth and a criterion for recognizing truth would let Peirce escape the argument. Would James's version of pragmatism be better or worse off against this same argument?
