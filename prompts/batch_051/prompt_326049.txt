I am a software engineer, not a philosopher. My work involves designing resource allocation systems under scarcity, so I keep thinking about Garrett Hardin's essay Lifeboat Ethics: The Case Against Helping the Poor. The part I want to focus on is the passage where Hardin replaces the spaceship metaphor with a lifeboat analogy. He asks us to imagine a lifeboat with a fixed carrying capacity, with passengers already aboard and many more people in the water around it. He says the lifeboat has no captain to assign places, so the passengers themselves must decide what to do. His conclusion, as I understand it, is that the passengers cannot admit everyone without destroying the boat, and that treating the earth as a shared spaceship obscures that constraint.

Here is my question. Hardin writes as though the distinction between who is in the boat and who is in the water is simply given. The boat's passengers have a certain stock of food and a safety margin, while the swimmers outside are there because they have exceeded their own carrying capacity or failed to govern their own reproduction. But in the decades since the essay appeared, it has become much harder to treat the two groups as causally isolated. The passengers' economies have done much of the cumulative fossil fuel burning, resource extraction, and pollution that now drives climate displacement, ocean acidification, and crop failures in poorer countries. The swimmers are not outside an unaffected boat; the boat's ongoing operation has helped make the water rise around them. Moreover, the boat's prosperity partly depends on supply chains that draw materials and labor from regions where the swimmers live.

Does Hardin's lifeboat argument survive that changed assumption? I can see two possibilities. One is that the causal connection does not alter the physical logic of the lifeboat: the boat still has a fixed carrying capacity, and taking everyone aboard still ruins it. On that view, the past and present causation only means the passengers have additional obligations, such as reducing emissions or transferring resources, but it does not create an obligation to let swimmers aboard. The other possibility is that the boat/water distinction itself was doing much of the moral work, and once the passengers are implicated in producing the swimmers' condition, the metaphor no longer licenses refusal. On that view, the passengers have a duty to repair the damage, and perhaps to accept people aboard as a temporary measure even if it reduces the boat's safety margin.

I want to know which of those readings is more faithful to what Hardin's argument actually needs, or whether there is a third option that keeps the finite-capacity constraint while accepting the changed moral facts. Please analyze the structure of his argument under the new assumption rather than simply giving your own view on immigration or climate policy. Which premise does the new development bear on, and can the argument be repaired, or does it only become narrower in what it supports?
