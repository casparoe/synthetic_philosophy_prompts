I keep tripping over a thing about Latin and Italian. People talk as if Latin is dead and Italian is a separate living language, and they'd think it was just being clever to say Italian is modern Latin. But you couldnt actually find the moment of death or birth. Each generation understood its parents and its children, and nobody in the chain from a Roman street to a modern one ever noticed the language break in two. The changes are all tiny and gradual, but the labels we use are sharp and binary. And we are not consistent about this: if English drifts in the same way across a few generations we still call it English, but when Latin drifts across many generations the later thing gets a new name and the earlier thing gets called dead. My question is whether there is a principle that justifies drawing a line somewhere in a continuous chain, or whether the line is just a useful filing system and Italian realy is just modern Latin. I would like the strongest possible defence of the ordinary way of talking, tested against nearby cases like Scots and English, or the Italian dialects, and then a straight verdict on whether the line should be kept or given up. Also, please write the answer in British English.
