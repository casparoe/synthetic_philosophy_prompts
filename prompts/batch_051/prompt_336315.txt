Theres a mismatch in how states deal with old wrongs that I keep turning over, and I'd like it worked up as a philosophical question. A government will issue an official apology for something done a century or more ago — colonial land-taking, forced labour, that kind of thing — and it will even pay compensation to *descendants* of the victims, many of whom weren't alive when the wrong happened. Then in the same statement, or in the political fight around it, the same state insists that no one living today is morally guilty of the original wrong: guilt, it says, is individual and cant be inherited. That looks inconsistent, or at least like its smuggling in a picture of what a country is — a single thing that persists across generations, can say sorry, and can owe money, but apparently is never guilty as a thing. I want to know what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Specifically: does it require treating the nation as a group agent that can be guilty or answerable as a group, or can it get by with a thinner notion of responsibility-without-guilt, or can the whole thing be cashed out just in terms of the benefits present citizens still receive from the old wrong? Reconstruct the implicit theory of collective responsibility that the practice needs, say whether the apology, the cash to descendants, and the denial of personal guilt are actually coherent together, and mark which parts of the practice are underdetermined enough that several rival theories would license them. Then tell me what would have to change if you swapped in a strict individualist view where duties and debts can only ever attach to living persons — would the apology have to go, or could it survive but mean something different? I don't know the jargon here, so define terms as you go. And please separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's still genuinely contested; don't present a contested view as the consensus.
