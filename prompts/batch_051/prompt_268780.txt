Two friends got into an argument I couldn't settle. A bus driver and a cab driver both text while driving down the same street on different nights. Each takes their eyes off the road for the same stretch, at the same speed, in the same visibility. The bus driver hits and kills a pedestrian. The cab driver hits no one. Everyone agrees the only difference was whether a pedestrian happened to be in the crosswalk.

Maya said extra blame for the bus driver has to be a mistake. This is a textbook moral-luck case. The two did the same thing, with the same beliefs and the same degree of risk; the result was set by something neither of them controlled. So holding the bus driver more blameworthy violates the control principle: someone can only be assessed for what was up to them.

Dev said that's too clean. The bus driver's action, as actually performed, included killing someone. And the bus driver now has reasons the cab driver lacks — to apologize, to make reparations, to face the family. Those are moral reasons, not just bad feelings, so outcomes can't be morally inert.

Maya came back that reparations are forward-looking. The world is different, but the drivers aren't more or less blameworthy for what they did. If the pedestrian hadn't crossed, the bus driver would be in exactly the cab driver's shoes. You can't make someone a worse person by luck.

Dev: but she is a different person now in one respect. She has to live with having killed someone, and whether she owns it or waves it off becomes part of her character in a way the cab driver's character hasn't even been tested. Anyway, blame isn't just a private measure of a person; it's how we hold people to account for what actually happened in the world. If we bracket the death entirely, we're not taking the family seriously.

Maya: that's the point. How she responds afterward is about who she becomes after the fact, not about whether her conduct at the time was worse. If we let the outcome set how much we blame her for the act itself, we're letting a coin flip determine what she deserves.

Dev: maybe what the two deserve isn't the only question. Even if they committed the same wrong, the bus driver has a different thing left to do now — answer for a death. It's not a coin flip to say she, and not the cab driver, has to answer for it. The coin flip just explains why she's the one who has to.

I couldn't decide. Walk me through who's right about which specific points, where each of them slides past a distinction the other is drawing, and who is more right overall.
