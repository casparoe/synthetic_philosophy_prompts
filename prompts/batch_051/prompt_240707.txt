I am an attorney for a municipal transit authority that has just approved a pilot in which autonomous delivery vans will operate on designated streets without a human driver. The relevant provision of our state's motor vehicle code reads, in full:

"Section 9-31. Duty of operator. (a) Every operator of a motor vehicle shall exercise due care to keep the vehicle under proper control and shall be responsible for any collision caused by a failure to exercise that care. (b) For purposes of this section, 'operator' means the person who has actual physical control of the vehicle's movement and direction."

The vans are Level 4: on the pilot streets they perform all dynamic driving tasks themselves. A person in our control room monitors a bank of vehicles and can send a stop or pull-over command remotely, but cannot steer, brake, or accelerate the vehicle by hand. Last week one van misclassified a child in a costume as road debris, swerved, and struck a parked car. There is no dispute about the facts: the vendor had pushed a software update two days earlier, the monitor's response met our internal protocol and was nonetheless several seconds too late, and no human chose the swerve.

I have been asked to advise the board on how section 9-31 applies. Is anyone in this situation an "operator"—the remote monitor, the transit authority as the vehicle's registered owner, or no one? How do the main approaches to statutory interpretation—the plain words, the statute's evident safety purpose, what the drafters would likely have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader in the monitor's position would understand—resolve the question, and which should govern? If the honest answer is that no one is an operator, I want to know whether that shows a defect that a short amendment would fix, or whether it exposes a deeper problem about assigning legal responsibility for decisions made by an autonomous system.
