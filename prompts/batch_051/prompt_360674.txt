I've been trying to pin down where the self-torturer argument actually breaks, if it does. Imagine a guy with a implant that has 1000 settings, 0 to 1000. Turning it up one notch adds $1,000 a year but also a tiny increase in constant pain, so small he can't notice any difference between adjacent settings; at 1000 he's in terrible pain and would pay all the money to be back at 0.

Here's the argument I keep seeing:

(1) For every n from 0 to 999, the pain difference between setting n and setting n+1 is imperceptible, and the extra $1,000 is clearly worth having, so the guy rationally prefers n+1 to n.
(2) Rational preference is transitive: if you rationally prefer A to B and B to C, then you rationally prefer A to C.
(3) By chaining (2) through all the preferences in (1), the guy rationally prefers setting 1000 to setting 0.
(4) But he rationally prefers setting 0 to setting 1000 — at the top he'd give all the money back to be at 0 again.
(5) So (1), (2), and (4) can't all be true, and since (1) and (4) look unproblematic, the culprit must be (2): rational preference isn't transitive.

Is this actually a valid argument? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and what the correct version of that step should be. If it is valid, then is "rational preference" just a messier notion than people pretend?
