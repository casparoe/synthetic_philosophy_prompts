I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I've been stuck on an argument I reconstructed after reading the epilogue to Schrödinger's What Is Life? (I think the epilogue is called On Determinism and Free Will). Schrödinger is drawing on Kant and Vedanta, and at one point he makes a move that I keep going back to. He claims, roughly, that in immediate experience you never come across a multiplicity of consciousnesses; you only ever find consciousness, singular, and the idea that there are many separate minds is an inference we make from the fact that there are many bodies spread out in space. Then he adds that space is only a form of our intuition, not a property of things in themselves, so the separation we project onto the world is something we ourselves bring, not something that belongs to consciousness as it really is. He even treats the old Vedantic claim that the individual self is ultimately identical with the universal ground as something that follows directly from this, not as mystical excess.

So I tried to formalize his line of thought for a chapter I'm writing, because if it works it would unsettle a lot of our talk about agency and responsibility. Here's my reconstruction, numbered:

(1) All I ever directly experience is my own consciousness; I never directly experience another mind.
(2) The only basis for believing in a plurality of consciousnesses is the observed plurality of living bodies spread out in space.
(3) Space, as Kant argued, is a form of intuition rather than a feature of things in themselves.
(4) So the spatial separation of bodies is not a feature of reality as it is independently of consciousness; it is contributed by our cognitive apparatus.
(5) Therefore the plurality of consciousnesses is not a feature of consciousness as it is in itself, but only of how it appears under spatio-temporal conditions.
(6) Hence consciousness itself is one, and the distinction between my consciousness and yours is ultimately an appearance, not reality.

I keep think step 4 or 5 is doing something sneaky, but I can't put my finger on it. Maybe the transition from 'space is a form of intuition' to 'bodies aren't really separate' equivocates between transcendental and empirical senses? Or is Schrödinger's argument actually fine and the conclusion just sounds more radical than it is? I'd like to know exactly which step, if any, fails and what the correct version of it would be.
