I am a novelist, not a scholar, and I am building a speculative novel around a temple whose priests perform rites of substitution: a guilt, pollution, or curse that would otherwise fall on a person, a household, or an entire city is transferred to a substitute — an animal, an effigy, a designated exile, or in rare cases a volunteer — and the substitute is then killed, burned, or driven out. I want this institution to run on a real logic rather than invented fantasy plumbing. Acting as a philosopher of ritual with a working knowledge of the anthropology of sacrifice and the scapegoat mechanism, think carefully, begin by rehearsing the two or three rival accounts of what a substitution is actually doing, and then give me a decision procedure my temple's ritual officers could actually follow, not an essay about the topic.

Assume the officers are not scholars, that the temple runs this procedure constantly — weekly expiations and emergency rites for famine, plague, or unsolved killing — that a botched rite exposes the community to disaster or the officers to blame, that the rite must be run under time pressure, and that there is no magical test revealing whether guilt has actually moved; the procedure may rely only on what an officer can know from the petition, the offense, the community's standing, and the rite's observable structure. The procedure should decide, in order: whether the proposed transfer is one the rite can perform at all, or whether the guilt is the wrong kind to be transferred; what class of substitute is appropriate or forbidden and what makes a creature or person eligible to carry the guilt; how officers can tell afterward whether the transfer should be counted as having taken; and where the procedure must stop and hand the question to a person because it is a matter of values rather than rules.

Please separate what is genuinely settled in the philosophy and anthropology of sacrifice from what is contested — in particular, whether guilt transfers literally as a kind of stain or load, or only expressively through identification and performance, and whether the scapegoat must be innocent or may be culpable — and do not present one contested account as the consensus.

Then run the procedure on three sample petitions: a confessed killer asks that his guilt be laid on a ram so he can remain in the community without paying the blood price; an entire city, after an unsolved murder has left no one to punish, wants to send a scapegoat into the wild to end the pollution; and in a famine with no identifiable offender, the priests propose burning an effigy and releasing a voluntary exile. Include at least one case in which the procedure produces an answer I will not like — for example, by refusing the rite I had plotted — and say exactly which feature of the case forces that answer. End with an honest note on what the procedure cannot do and where an officer's judgment, or the novelist's invention, cannot be eliminated.
