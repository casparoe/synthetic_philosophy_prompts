I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so bear with me if I mangle the terms. I own the internal docs for a platform team of about fifteen people: architecture decision records, runbooks, onboarding notes, a wiki full of "why did we do it this way" pages. Every few months we have an incident or a re-litigated decision where someone says "wait, there was a reason for this," and the reason was written down the whole time but nobody remembered it, including the person who originally wrote it. Meanwhile other stuff on the same wiki spreads instantly: half-true shortcuts, a hack that becomes folklore, some misremembered number. It sticks for years no matter how many times we correct it.

Someone on a forum used the word "antimeme" for ideas that resist being remembered or spread. I don't know if that's a real term or a sci-fi thing, but it's the most useful word I've found for what keeps biting us.

I want to turn that idea into a procedure I can actually run each quarter on our docs and decision records to catch these antimemes early and decide what to do about them. Please write it as a checklist or decision procedure a non-philosopher could execute in maybe two hours per quarter without reading any background theory. Give me the questions to ask in order, what answer counts as "this is probably an antimeme," where the procedure says "stop, this isn't worth worrying about," and where it has to hand off to a person because it's now a judgment call rather than a fact. Also be straight with me about where the procedure will misfire. Changes to our docs have to go through a pull request and the team lead approves, so whatever the procedure outputs needs to justify itself to someone even less philosophical then me.

Then run it on three cases from our place: (1) an ADR explaining why we use a self-hosted message queue instead of a managed service, accurate but dense, and every new team member reopens the decision; (2) a runbook line saying "do not skip this step even though it looks redundant," about draining connections before a failover, which people skip anyway; (3) a wiki page about a known bug in an old service, a bug we absolutely do not want becoming team folklore because people keep "remembering" it in mangled form and misapplying it. For that third one I'll be honest: I kind of want the procedure to say "rewrite it everywhere and make it impossible to miss," but I suspect that's wrong or impossible. Tell me if it does.
