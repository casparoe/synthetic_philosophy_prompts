I'm putting together a set of accounts of why political communities rise and fall cyclically — meaning theories where the decline is already built into the rise and the whole thing can in principle start over, not just "everything ends eventually." The passage below from Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah (Franz Rosenthal translation, the section where he says dynasties have a natural life span like individuals) is my anchor, because it's the most concrete statement I know of an actual mechanism rather than just a mood:

"We have stated that the duration of the life of a dynasty does not as a rule extend beyond three generations. The first generation retains the desert qualities, desert toughness, and desert savagery. (Its members are used to) privation and to sharing their glory (with each other); they are brave and rapacious. Therefore, the strength of group feeling continues to be preserved among them. They are sharp and greatly feared. People submit to them. Under the influence of royal authority and a life of ease, the second generation changes from the desert attitude to sedentary culture, from privation to luxury and plenty, from a state in which everybody shared in the glory to one in which one man claims all the glory for himself while the others are too lazy to strive for (glory), and from proud superiority to humble subservience. Thus, the vigor of group feeling is broken to some extent. People become used to lowliness and obedience. But many of (the old virtues) remain in them, because they had had direct personal contact with the first generation and its conditions, and had observed with their own eyes its prowess and striving for glory and its intention to protect and defend (itself). They cannot give all of it up at once, although a good deal of it may go. They live in hope that the conditions that existed in the first generation may come back, or they live under the illusion that those conditions still exist. The third generation, then, has (completely) forgotten the period of desert life and toughness, as if it had never existed. They have lost (the taste for) the sweetness of fame and (for) group feeling, because they are dominated by force. Luxury reaches its peak among them, because they are so much given to a life of prosperity and ease. They become dependent on the dynasty and are like women and children who need to be defended (by someone else). Group feeling disappears completely. People forget to protect and defend themselves and to press their claims. ... In the course of these three generations, the dynasty grows senile and is worn out."

From that passage I get one entry for my list: (2) Ibn Khaldun — the rough desert life produces group feeling ('asabiyya) strong enough to win a dynasty, but the settled luxury the dynasty then enjoys dissolves exactly the cohesion that made conquest possible, over roughly three generations, until a fresher group of outsiders replaces it.

The rest of my working list so far:

(1) Polybius's anacyclosis — kingship, aristocracy, and democracy each rot into their corrupt double (tyranny, oligarchy, mob rule), and each corrupt form gives birth to the next good one, round and round, unless a mixed constitution catches the wheel.
(3) Vico's corso e ricorso — every nation passes through an age of gods, an age of heroes, and an age of men; the refinement of the age of men collapses back into barbarism and the whole corso begins again, though not identically.
(4) Spengler — a culture is a kind of organism with a more or less fixed life course from youth through maturity to senility, with "civilization" as the petrified terminal phase before a new culture takes over.
(5) Toynbee — a civilization grows by creatively responding to challenges, and breaks down when the creative minority hardens into a dominant minority; the cycle closes becuase the universal church nurtured by the internal proletariat becomes the chrysalis of the next civilization.

I want one more entry with a genuinely different causal structure from all five. Not a re-description of moral decay, luxury, institutional rot, or loss of creativity, and not just bolting two of the above together — it has to name a different engine of recurrence. Then tell me whether that engine, unlike Ibn Khaldun's, could produce a cycle in a commercial society that never had a desert or nomadic reservoir to draw on, or whether it also needs an external pool of tough outsiders to restart the loop.
