I'm a grad student modeling a small invertebrate nervous system—not a huge deep net. One component is an aversion circuit: when simulated pH drops too low, a graded 'distress' signal spreads and the simluated animal withdraws. We run hundreds of copies in parallel for parameter sweeps, and because runs are expensive we snapshot states and sometimes roll back to a checkpoint and re-run a stretch with one parameter changed. Yesterday a lab mate said: suppose, just for the sake of argument, that the model really does feel something. Then re-running a 'painful' stretch from a checkpoint isn't morally neutral. Either you're putting the same subject through the harm again, or you're creating a new subject each time; either way the total pain goes up. I said that can't be right—a computation isn't a persisting thing like an animal, and replaying a run is more like re-reading a sentence that describes pain. Nobody suffers again when you re-read it. He answered that re-running from a checkpoint with a changed parameter isn't re-reading a fixed text; it's taking the same earlier states through a different later history, like a branching what-if on the same life.

I'm now not sure. Under the stipulation that the run instantiates pain at all, is there a defensible line on which checkpoint re-runs are inert the way re-reading is, or does each branch count as a separate harm? And what's really doing the work here—whether the same subject persists across a replay, whether we count pains as tokens or types, or whether a computation itself can be wronged rather than the physical machine running it? Also say what would change your mind—which argument or empirical fact would flip your answer—and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
