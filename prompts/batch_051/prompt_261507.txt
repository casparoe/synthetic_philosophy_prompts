I am in a high school philosophy of mathematics seminar, and we recently spent several days on the indispensability argument. As I understand it, the argument says that because our best scientific theories unavoidably quantify over numbers, functions, and other mathematical objects, we have the same reason to believe in those objects that we have to believe in electrons or genes. The version of the argument that interests me goes further: mathematics is not merely a calculational tool, since there seem to be cases where a mathematical fact genuinely explains an empirical phenomenon. The two examples we discussed were cicada broods emerging every 13 or 17 years, where the arithmetic fact that prime-numbered cycles minimize overlap with predators' cycles is said to explain the broods' periodicity, and the hexagonal cells of a honeycomb, where the geometric fact that a hexagonal tiling minimizes total edge length for a fixed area is said to explain the bees' wax economy.

Lately, though, I have been reading about how much of cutting-edge science is shifting toward machine-learning models that are trained rather than derived. These models can make remarkably accurate predictions about things like protein folding or climate variables, but they consist of billions of fitted parameters, and no one can write down a law or explain the phenomenon in compact mathematical terms. Scientific success in these cases seems to come from a black box whose internal mathematics is mostly linear algebra and optimization, not from an explanatory mathematical pattern that anyone can articulate.

What I am trying to figure out is what this shift does to the indispensability argument. Does the argument survive when the best available science no longer arrives packaged as mathematical laws or explicit models? Does the mathematics inside the black box now inherit the burden that the traditional argument placed on explanatory mathematics, or has the development undercut a premise on which the argument depends? I am less interested in whether machine-learning predictions are reliable and more interested in the structure of the argument itself. I would like someone to identify the specific premise the development presses on and say whether the argument can be repaired, and in what form.
