I run the analysis desk at a political-risk firm that advises clients on moving cargo, staff, and capital through unstable countries. My analysts publish numeric probability estimates — a port will close for at least a week this quarter, a government will impose capital controls, a strike will interrupt a client's operations — and those numbers are revised weekly, defended to a partner, and then acted on by clients. The weekly revision is the problem I want you to solve.

Much of what triggers a revision is certain evidence: an official decree, a verified satellite image, a signed regulation. There, conditioning on the thing observed is straightforward. But a large share of our information is uncertain. A local contact sends a one-line message whose meaning is unclear; an analyst leaves a meeting less sure of someone's intentions without being able to name the sentence that changed their mind; an automated monitoring system fires an alert that does not distinguish among several underlying conditions; a diplomat hints at a policy shift while denying one. My analysts currently improvise, and because each improvises differently, the same input produces different numbers.

Formal epistemology has an account of belief revision under uncertain evidence — often called Jeffrey conditionalization or probability kinematics — in which incoming information shifts your probabilities over a partition, a set of mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive possibilities, without making any single proposition certain. I want that account translated into a single procedure my desk can run in fifteen minutes per estimate, with no one on the team needing to read the philosophy. I am asking you to commit: give me the procedure you would actually insist on, with the exact questions in the order they are to be asked, the rule for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the choices you made where the formal account leaves something underdetermined — not a menu of candidate approaches with their trade-offs.

The procedure should include:

- a routing question that decides whether an input gets treated as an observation to condition on (we are certain the report was received even if we are not certain the report is true) or as a case for the uncertain-evidence procedure, with criteria precise enough that two analysts will route the same input the same way;
- for the uncertain-evidence path, the ordered steps: fixing the partition, eliciting the new probabilities over it, holding the appropriate conditional probabilities fixed, and computing the revised estimate;
- an explicit rule for what to do when an analyst can give only a range, or only a direction such as "more likely than not" or "I'm less sure now," rather than a number;
- the point at which the procedure must stop and hand the matter to a human because the remaining inputs are judgment rather than arithmetic, and how that handoff is to be recorded;
- a short, honest statement of what the procedure cannot do and where it will systematically misfire.

Then run the finished procedure on the two cases below and show the arithmetic.

Case A. An analyst gives 20% to the proposition H: "Port K closes for at least a week this quarter." She receives a one-line message from a local agent reading "trouble at Port K." She is certain the message was sent, but she does not treat it as directly telling her H or not-H. Instead she thinks of the message as shifting her probabilities over a three-way partition of what it reflects: E1, a labor action that would close the port; E2, a customs slowdown that would not close the port; E3, rumor or no significant disruption. Before the message she sets P(E1) = 0.15, P(E2) = 0.25, P(E3) = 0.60, with P(H | E1) = 0.90, P(H | E2) = 0.14, P(H | E3) = 0.05. After reading the message she sets P'(E1) = 0.40, P'(E2) = 0.35, P'(E3) = 0.25. Show how she should revise P(H), and what your procedure instructs if she can only say E1 became "more likely" rather than committing to 0.40.

Case B. An automated monitoring system fires an alert concerning one of our clients' facilities. The desk knows from the system's record that on days when a cyber intrusion is actually underway it fires 60% of the time; on days when a labor action is actually underway it fires 60% of the time; and on days when neither is underway it fires 55% of the time. The alert itself says only that it fired. An analyst currently gives 20% to a labor action at the facility this week, 10% to a cyber intrusion, and 70% to neither. Show how those three numbers should be revised, and state explicitly which path the routing question sends this case down.

Finally, say which of the two cases delivers an answer the analyst is likely to resist because it moves a number less than intuition demands, and explain whether the procedure should overrule that intuition or the analyst's resistance is justified.
