Last night I got into a long, messy argument with a friend about whether the fact that different cultures seem to weight harm, consent, authority, loyalty and purity differently means there’s no objective moral truth. He kept bringing up the WEIRD psychology thing — western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic — and claiming that if even our most basic moral instincts are shaped by the culture we grew up in, objectivity is out the window. I kept feeling like I needed a concrete case to think through instead of the big slogan-y back-and-forth, so I want one built to order.

Build me a thought experiment in an ordinary-life setting, no trolleys, no magic, no science fiction, with one agent and two mutually exclusive options, engineered so that a harm-and-consent folk morality and a role-and-community folk morality come apart completely: each framework requires one of the two options and forbids the other. The two frameworks should agree on every descriptive fact about the case — how much harm actually occurs, whether consent was real, what the role obligations are, who finds out, everything — and they should still disagree about what the agent should do. The case is for me to test my friend’s claim that this style of cross-cultural disagreement refutes moral objectivity, so don’t cheat by making one side ignorant, selfish, or obviosuly misapplying its own standards, and don’t rig it by putting a clause into one framework that already settles the verdict: the disagreement has to survive both sides being fully informed and arguing in good faith, and the verdicts should fall out of what each framework treats as basic wrong-making features.

Then do the check. State the two options and the descriptive facts each framework cares about. Show in detail why each framework gives its verdict, and say which feature of the case is doing all the work — what happens if you remove just that feature and keep everything else fixed. Also tell me how a defender of whichever framework ends up looking worse from a WEIRD point of view would attack the case: where would they say the deck got stacked. Finish by saying what this case does to my friend’s argument: is the disagreement best explained as one side misapplying its own standard, as two standards tracking different features of one situation, or as a real head-on conflict, and which of those readings would actually threaten moral objectivity.
