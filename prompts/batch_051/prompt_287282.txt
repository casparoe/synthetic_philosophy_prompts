So I'm a PhD student in law and religion, and in three weeks I'm giving a faculty seminar talk arguing that the German church-tax model is the least bad way for a liberal state to relate to organized religion — better than English establishment, better than American tax-exemption pluralism, and less self-deceiving than French laïcité. My rough argument is this: once you grant that the state inevitably has some fiscal and legal relationship with religious bodies (charity status, exemptions, chaplaincies, state churches, concordats), the real question is which arrangement is transparent and treats believers and non-believers like adults. The German system wins because it's explicit, individually opt-out-able, and keeps churches publicly accountable, whereas the American system delivers much the same subsidy through the back door of the tax code, and the French system pretends there's a wall that doesn't exist in practice.
