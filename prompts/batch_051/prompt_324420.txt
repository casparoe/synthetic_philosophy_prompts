Here are my verdicts on some risky choices, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them. The classic St. Petersburg game pays $2^n if the first heads occurs on the nth coin flip. I would not pay more than about $25 to play it, even though its expected value is infinite. If the game is capped at $1,000,000, I would pay about $20, which feels barely different. If a billionaire offers to play the uncapped game against me, I still would not pay more than $40, even though the billionaire can cover enormous payoffs. Yet if someone offers me a one-shot choice between a certain $100 and playing the uncapped game, I take the certain $100. I also would pay $200 to insure against a 1% risk of a $10,000 loss, even though the expected loss is only $100; but I would not pay $100 for a 1% chance to win $10,000. Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, such as expected utility with diminishing marginal utility, bounded utility, risk aversion, or something else? Which of my verdicts would that principle force me to revise? What does it predict for a game that pays $n^2 rather than $2^n, and for a game that costs $50 and has a 1% chance of paying $1,000,000?
