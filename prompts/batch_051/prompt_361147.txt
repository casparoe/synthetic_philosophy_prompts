Write an 850-word parody of a judicial opinion in which an administrative law judge must decide whether two populations of a rare wildflower—one in Larkspur Valley, one on Copper Ridge—count as a single species under a statute that protects species but does not define the term. The opinion should be deadpan and formally structured, with numbered paragraphs and at least one footnote, and it should read as a judicial tour of the species problem. The judge should work through the main candidate species concepts and hit the familiar snags: the interbreeding or reproductive-isolation criterion runs into fertile hybrids and introgression; the smallest-diagnosable-lineage criterion runs into gene trees disagreeing with species trees and turns every slightly distinct population into a new species; the ecological-niche criterion runs into one lineage occupying several niches and distinct lineages converging on one; and the morphological-similarity criterion runs into cryptic forms and conspicuous polymorphism. The footnote should mention ring species and horizontal gene transfer as opposite ends of the same difficulty, and should quietly observe that the statute's drafters simply assumed species were natural kinds. The holding should sound legally crisp and biologically arbitrary. Aim the satire at the practice of forcing a settled administrative species category onto a phenomenon that resists it; a philosopher of biology should recognize each snag, and a general reader should still follow the joke. Begin with the piece itself, with no preface or commentary.
