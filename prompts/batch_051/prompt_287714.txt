I have been arguing with two friends about the COVID-19 emergency measures, and I have realized the disagreement turns on what a legal system implicitly commits itself to when it does what it did. In normal times, constitutional democracies guarantee individual liberties, subject the executive to judicial review, and insist that all state power must trace back to written law. But during the pandemic, many of these same states closed their borders, confined people to their homes, restricted assembly and worship, and gave the executive broad power to impose or lift rules with only light parliamentary oversight. Some of this was done under pre-existing emergency statutes; some was done by decree and approved by courts only after the fact. Courts mostly upheld these measures, often saying that judges should defer to the executive's assessment of an unfolding public-health emergency, while also insisting that the measures remain necessary, proportionate, and temporary. In several countries, emergency powers were then renewed, extended, or converted into longer-term statutory arrangements well after the initial crisis had passed.

One friend, a civil-liberties lawyer, calls the lockdowns and border closures a kind of legal after-the-fact rationalization: the state did what it thought necessary and the courts then supplied legal cover. The other, a public-health official, says this is overheated, because emergency statutes already authorized nearly all of it, so the practice was inside the legal order the whole time. I would like you to reconstruct the philosophy that best explains the overall pattern.

Specifically: what theory of the relationship between legality and legitimacy does this pattern of practice presuppose, if any? Does it presuppose that necessity, survival, or the public interest becomes an independent source of legitimate authority that law merely ratifies afterward, or does it presuppose that legality already contains emergency powers, so legitimacy never leaves the law? Does the pattern presuppose a particular epistemic picture about when courts should defer to predictive, risk-based decisions under uncertainty? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would justify the same practice. Finally, tell me what would have to change in constitutional design and in judicial review if a legal system adopted, consistently, the view that legitimacy is exhausted by legality and that emergency powers are just ordinary statutory powers with special triggers. Please answer in American English.
