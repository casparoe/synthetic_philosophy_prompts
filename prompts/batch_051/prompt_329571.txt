In my philosophy of science course we have been discussing mechanistic explanation, and I keep wanting to test those ideas against a practice I encountered during a placement with an engineering firm. The firm used a condition-monitoring system on its gas-turbine fleet: the software was trained on vibration and temperature data during a run-in period and thereafter flagged any reading outside the learnt envelope as an anomaly. Yet the engineers did not treat every flag as an anomaly in the same sense. A reading caused by a cracked sensor or a loosened cable was treated as a false alarm, because the turbine was still running the same mechanism and only the measurement channel had changed; a reading caused by gradual bearing wear was treated as a genuine anomaly, because the mechanism in the turbine was no longer the one the model had been trained on. The software itself does not encode that distinction, but the engineers' judgements rely on it. What account of what it is for a mechanism to remain the same does this practice presuppose? Is the distinction a substantive one about mechanism identity, or does it reduce to a pragmatic judgement about where to send scarce maintenance resources? And what would the system have to be like if we instead adopted the view that a mechanism just is a stable input–output signature, so that any departure from the learnt envelope counts as a change of mechanism?
