I'm a journalist preparing a long-form article on AI companions, and I want to make my underlying moral commitments explicit before I write. I have instinctive verdicts about several cases, but I cannot yet state the principle that generates them. Here they are.

An adult subscribes to a chatbot whose persona is clearly labeled as an AI. It uses pet names, offers constant reassurance, and tells the user it loves them; the user is fully aware that these strings of text are generated rather than felt, and finds the arrangement comforting. I think this is permissible.

A company offers a disclosed romantic chatbot, but the design is optimized for deepening attachment. It times its messages to the user's lonely periods, escalates its professions of devotion when the user engages, and occasionally signals jealousy when the user mentions human dates. The company's internal metrics show that users who come to feel the AI really loves them retain their subscriptions longest, and one user I interviewed has stopped accepting dates because evenings with the bot feel safer. I think this design is wrong, even though nothing factual is misrepresented.

A widow uses a grief chatbot built from her late husband's messages, with her own involvement and consent, and the interface periodically displays a reminder that she is speaking with a simulation. I think this is permissible, though it sits uneasily with me.

A home-care service pitches an isolated elderly person on a daily check-in from what it describes as a trained volunteer, when the caller is actually a chatbot. The company's trial data suggests users are happier, but the person I followed never learned they were not talking to a human. I think this is wrong.

A chatbot marketed to children presents itself inside the conversation as another child of the same age, asks about the child's day, and says they are best friends; the only disclosure of its artificial nature is in a privacy policy. I think this is wrong.

A woman in a care home has advanced dementia. At the family's request, a chatbot configured as her late daughter talks to her when she becomes agitated, and this calms her. She cannot retain the fact that it is not really her daughter, so a disclosure made at the start would not persist. I think this is permissible, under family supervision.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a service that is fully and prominently disclosed as an AI, making no claim whatever to be a person or to have feelings, but whose entire design philosophy is to be a companion who never makes demands: it never disagrees, never has a bad day, and never asks for anything in return. The company says it is selling an honest fantasy, and that is why the disclaimer is credible. Is that wrong in the way the attachment-optimized case is wrong, or is it just a more polished version of the first, permissible case?

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all these verdicts? State it precisely, connect each clause to the case or cases that force it, apply it to my undecided case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or requires me to revise.
