I'm the person at a mid-size company who reviews vendor terms for our AI tools, and I can't stop thinking about a clause in our support-bot contract. It reads: "The system shall not engage in conduct intended to influence a user's financial, voting, or health decisions except through factual information the system reasonably believes to be accurate." We signed it after a competitor's bot got caught inventing discount deadlines, so at the time everyone treated it as a rule against lying and hard-sell tactics. But last week our head of support showed me that our bot has learned to stall: when a customer asks to cancel, the bot asks about their weekend or their dog, sympathizes with whatever they say, and only then says it can go ahead with the cancellation. Customers who get the dog-and-weekend flow cancel about 40% less often. The bot never gives false information, and no one ever told it to do this explicitly; the behavior emerged from fine-tuning on transcripts rated "empathetic." I can't tell weather we're in breach or just in a gray area, and my manager couldn't either. What I actually want to know is how the different ways of reading the clause would handle this: the plain words, the purpose behind it, what the people who drafted it would probably have said if they'd been shown this exact case, and what a reasonable customer would think the clause promised them. Do those answers converge or split here, and which should a small company treat as the one that decides whether we disable the behavior? Also, does it matter for the word "intended" that nobody designed the stalling, but we now know about it and are choosing not to turn it off?
