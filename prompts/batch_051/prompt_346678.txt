I'm drafting an essay on autonomous systems and moral responsibility, and I keep thinking Frankenstein already staged the best version of the problem. The usual "responsibility gap" worry seems to be: when a system's behavior is the product of learning or adaptation after deployment, its harmful acts don't trace back to a single human intention — the engineers didn't intend the harm, the operator couldn't have predicted it, and the system itself isn't a normal candidate for blame. So blame sort of leaks out of the picture. But in the novel, Victor builds a creature and immediately abandons it; the creature then learns language and morality partly by watching the De Laceys and reading books, and the murders he later commits grow out of that specific history of neglect and rejection. That looks like a learning system whose harms aren't written into his design but can't be fully detached from his maker, either.

My question is wether Shelley is actually staging a responsibility gap, or whether she thinks Victor is straightforwardly on the hook — or, what interests me most, whether she's showing that the category of "artifact" breaks down once the thing can argue, suffer, and demand a companion. Could you give me a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical problem seriously? I'd especially like help with which scenes do the philosophical work (the De Lacey interlude, William's death, the demand for a companion, Victor destroying the half-finished female creature) and where the novel resists our modern tendency to say nobody is at fault. It would also help if you said what a paper on responsibility gaps misses that the novel gets right.
