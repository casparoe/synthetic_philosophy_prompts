I've been thinking on my own about a puzzle deep inside the puzzle of consciousness. There is the hard problem — roughly, why any physical process should feel like something from the inside — and there is the meta-problem — why we are so firmly convinced that the hard problem is real and that no physical description could ever capture what seeing red is like. I've been trying to show that deflationary answers to the meta-problem, answers that treat that conviction as a systematic illusion produced by the brain, end up undermining themselves. Here is the argument I've built, numbered so you can point at a step.

(1) A deflationary solution to the meta-problem says our problem intuitions — the sense of an unbridgeable gap between physical facts and the feel of experience — are produced by processes that do not track the truth about consciousness.

(2) Put another way, such a solution says that first-person access to our own mental lives misleads us about the character of consciousness and its relation to the physical world.

(3) But the only evidence we have that the problem intuitions exist, and that they have the force they do, comes through first-person access: my own sense of conviction, and other people's reports of theirs.

(4) If first-person access misleads us about the character of consciousness and its relation to the physical world, then it cannot be trusted to tell us that the problem intuitions exist, or what they are like.

(5) So a deflationary solution undermines the evidence for the very data it was supposed to explain.

(6) Therefore, no deflationary solution to the meta-problem can be coherent; any acceptable solution would have to treat the problem intuitions as basically trustworthy, which leaves the hard problem itself standing.

I can't find the hole in this, but I suspect there is one — something about sliding from being misled on one narrow point to being misled on every point that matters. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, say exactly what move it makes, and tell me whether the argument can be repaired or whether the whole thing collapses. I'd like a definite verdict, not just a menu of possible readings.
