I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary political theory workshop and need a Q&A prep sheet. The paper defends an updated version of Aristotle's argument in Politics Book I, chapter 2, where he claims that the polis exists by nature and is prior to the household and the individual because the whole is a condition of self-sufficiency; he compares a person without a polis to a hand or foot separated from the body, which would be such only in name, and he says that one who can live without a polis because already self-sufficient is either a beast or a god. My update holds that individual self-sufficiency is not an ideal for persons, and that a qualified ideal of civic autarky — a political community able to set its own terms of cooperation rather than depending on outsiders for its basic conditions of agency — remains defensible even in a materially interdependent world. I distinguish civic autarky from economic isolationism and from national self-sufficiency as a policy of import substitution, and I close with the passage: I read it as giving a criterion for when an association counts as a political community, not as a license for autocratic closure.

The audience has three camps. The first are liberal individualists, mostly sympathetic to Stoic and Kantian ideas of self-legislation, who will take any claim that the individual is subordinate to the community to be a threat. The second are historians of economic thought, who know the history of autarky as a policy program — from Athenian grain policy to twentieth-century autarchic fascism — and will suspect me of writing an apology for the polis without facing those costs. The third are virtue ethicists and neo-Aristotelians, who accept the priority of the political community but think my extension from the polis to modern civic autarky is anachronistic and that I concede too much to the nation-state. One of them is likely to press me on whether the passage commits me to saying that stateless persons or members of failed states are not fully human in the relevant sense.

Give me the objections I am actually likely to get from this audience, ordered by likely order and damage, phrased the way each camp would phrase them. For each, give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, with particular attention to where my interpretation of the Aristotle passage does more than decorative work. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks and explain why it is the dangerous one.
