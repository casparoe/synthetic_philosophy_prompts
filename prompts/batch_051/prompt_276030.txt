I'm a PhD student in cultural psychology, raised partly in Germany and partly in the US, and I'm trying to work out what I actually think about rule-following vs. individual conscience by going case by case. My gut keeps flip-flopping, so I want to know if there's a single principle behind my verdicts, or whether I should drop some of them.

Case 1. It's 3 a.m. and I'm at an empty intersection in Berlin. No cars in any direction, no one around. I wait for the little red man. I don't cross. I feel good about this, and not because I'd get fined. I don't think my American friend who crosses is doing something terrible; I just think waiting is the better default.

Case 2. An official at the border refuses to wave through a traveler whose visa lapsed two days ago and whose father is dying in hospital; the next appointment would take a week. Policy is clear, but the official in practice could bend it. Verdict: not fine. That's rule-following as cruelty, and the official should use judgment.

Case 3. My American neighbor decides the noise rule "doesn't apply in spirit" to his 1 a.m. saxophone practice because his neighbors are "chill." They're not. Verdict: not fine. His conscience exemption is self-serving rationalization, and I want the building management to enforce the quiet-hours rule.

Case 4. A delivery guy double-parks on a quiet Berlin side street for two minutes to bring medication up to an elderly woman. A passerby glares at him. Verdict: fine, and I'd probably glare back.

Case 5. At home I sort my trash carefully even when no one would find out if I threw a batery in with the normal rubbish. But I'd never report a neighbor who's sloppy about it; maybe I'd leave an anonymous note, at most. Verdict: strict with myself and light with others feels exactly right.

My tentative principle is something like: rules matter because they're shared goods that make social life predictable, and my own compliance is my contribution to that good; but enforcement against someone else has to be justified by a real person being hurt or inconvenienced, not by order in the abstract. The problem is I can already see that case 1 might be "order in the abstract" and I still endorse it, so the principle may just be flattering my German side when it comes to my own behavior and my American side when it comes to other people's.

Is there a clean principle that gets all five verdicts, or close? If not, which verdicts should I drop and why? What does the surviving principle say about two cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) a German colleague who won't let a disabled student move a lab bench and set up her equipment until the safety form is processed, even though it's an obvious accommodation; and (b) an American postdoc in a German lab who leaves shared equipment dirty on the argument that "we're all adults, no rule should be needed here"? And does the final principle actually pick a winner between the German default of internalized order and the American default of individual conscience, or does it just redescribe my own back-and-forth more carefully?
