After a career as an analytical chemist, I have been spending my retirement reading philosophy, and I am struck by how much institutional design turns out to be quietly committed to substantive philosophical views.

The practice I have in mind is physician-assisted dying as it is legally structured in jurisdictions that permit it but deliberately stop short of voluntary euthanasia. I will describe the features so that nothing turns on the details of one statute. A mentally competent adult with a terminal illness and a prognosis of six months or less may request a lethal prescription. The request must be repeated over time with a waiting period; a second physician must verify the diagnosis and prognosis; the patient must be able to self-administer the medication; and the law does not extend to someone with decades of severe but stable chronic illness, to someone whose suffering is primarily psychiatric, or to a patient physically unable to take the drug themselves. Voluntary euthanasia, in which a clinician administers the drug at the patient's request, remains a criminal act.

What philosophical commitments does this arrangement presuppose? That is: what view of autonomy, well-being, the value of life, and the moral difference between a patient ending their own life and a clinician doing so at their request is required for the policy to make internal sense? I would like you to separate the parts of the policy that the underlying theory strictly presupposes from the parts that are better explained as compromises or pragmatic limits that several rival theories could accept. In particular, does the six-month terminal-prognosis cutoff presuppose that only proximity to natural death can make an intentionally shortened life permissible, or is it compatible with a broader view on which suffering alone might matter? Does the self-administration rule presuppose a genuine moral difference between assisted suicide and euthanasia, and how defensible is that difference? And what would have to change in the law if its underlying views were replaced, first, by the view that autonomy by itself can justify assistance in dying whenever a competent person chooses it, and, second, by the view that human life is an inviolable good that may never be intentionally ended regardless of the person's wishes?

Please structure your response in the following four sections, in this order:

1. What the policy presupposes (roughly 350 words). Reconstruct the implicit theory of autonomy, well-being, the value of life, and the patient/clinician distinction that the policy's main features seem to require.

2. Presupposition versus accommodation (roughly 200 words). Distinguish what the underlying theory strictly requires from what is best explained as political or pragmatic compromise that rival theories could also accept.

3. Internal tensions (roughly 250 words). Identify the strongest inconsistencies and underdeterminations in the policy, especially around the terminality cutoff and the self-administration requirement.

4. Rival theories and their consequences (roughly 200 words). State what the law would look like under the autonomy-only view and under the inviolable-life view, and say precisely what would have to change.
