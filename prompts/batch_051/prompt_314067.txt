I've been reading rationalist and EA-adjacent blogs for years, and I keep noticing an inconsistency in myself and basically everyone around me when it comes to micromorts. Take driving. On any honest accounting, an ordinary person clocks up a decent number of micromorts every year—accumulated one-in-a-million chances of dying—just doing normal errands, and over a lifetime that's a real chance of dying in a crash, yet nobody treats it as especially reckless. We drive to get coffee, we take the scenic route because its prettier, we treat driving as the neutral background activity. But show the same person some other source of the same order of risk—a medical scan with a tiny cancer risk, a food additive, living near a small industrial plant, a vaccine side effect on the order of one in a million—and suddenly it's a completely different thing, sometimes refused outright or only accepted for huge compensation. The standard rationalist line is that the second reaction is affect heuristic plus scope insensitivity, and that our comfort with driving doesn't actually license the other judgments. The standard normal-person line is that of course the features of the risk matter: voluntariness, control, dread, who benefits, whether it's natural, whether it accumulates slowly or hits all at once. My question is weather any of those features actually justifies treating one micromort from driving as fine and one micromort from, say, a food additive as something to avoid at real cost. I want the strongest principle you can find that makes driving's micromorts special while excluding the medical, dietary, and environmental cases, and I want it tested against nearby cases: passenger versus driver, motorbikes, living under a flight path, a one-in-a-million vaccine side effect versus the drive to the clinic. Then give me an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up and treat micromorts as roughly fungible? And if they are fungible, tell me what that implies for whether all this driving is actually worth it. Oh, and please write in British English, and no formatting—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just continuous prose, like an email.
