I am drafting the theoretical chapter of a dissertation in moral psychology, and the chapter defends a set of views about guilt and shame that I have arrived at from several directions at once—philosophical accounts of the reactive attitudes, anthropological work on honor and shame, and the cross-cultural psychology of self-conscious emotions. I can argue for each commitment on its own, but I have not subjected the set as a whole to a consistency check, and I want an audit rather than a survey of the literature. My current commitments are these:

(a) Guilt is the more reliable moral emotion of the two. It tracks the act rather than the self, orients the wrongdoer toward repair and apology rather than concealment, withdrawal, or retaliation, and—unlike shame—does not depend on the presence or imagined presence of an audience.

(b) This ranking should not be run together with the familiar contrast between individualist guilt cultures and collectivist shame cultures. The distinction is not proprietary to any one form of social organization, and there are shame-based practices—for example, forms of public accountability in which a person is brought to acknowledge a shortcoming before the community—that have genuine moral content.

(c) Guilt and shame are not natural kinds with fixed functional profiles across contexts. How a transgression is appraised, and which of the two emotions it elicits, is substantially shaped by local emotion scripts, so the same underlying violation might be parsed as a guilty act to be repaired in one setting and as a shameful failure of character in another.

(d) It is nevertheless legitimate to make comparative normative judgments about entire moral-emotional cultures, and to say that some are worse than others: a culture that systematically routes wrongdoing through shame-as-character-stain is, in that respect, worse than one that routes it through guilt-as-repairable-act.

(e) At the same time, when an individual's shame-proneness is the product of the culture they were raised in, it would be unfair to respond to them with full-blown blame; the criticism is properly aimed at the cultural script rather than at the person shaped by it.

My question is not whether each of these is individually defensible, but whether they form a jointly tenable package. I need a diagnosis that separates the real tensions from the merely apparent ones, orders the real tensions by severity, and tells me what would have to be revised and what the least costly revision costs: which commitment gives way, or what qualification to one of them is the cheapest way to make the set coherent. If some apparent conflict dissolves under inspection, say so and name the distinction that dissolves it, rather than treating every pair as a problem.
