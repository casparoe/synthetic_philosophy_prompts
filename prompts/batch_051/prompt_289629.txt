I am trying to get clear about what I actually think concerning envy and resentment, and in particular whether envy is ever a signal of an injustice rather than merely a personal vice. I have collected my verdicts on a set of cases below. They strike me as mostly sound, but I doubt they all follow from one principle, and I would like to know which of them survive the attempt to make them coherent.

1. A coworker and I are finalists for the same promotion. She is plainly better on every relevant measure — output, skill, client relations — and she gets it. I notice a sharp little dislike toward her. My verdict: this is envy, it is a vice, and it signals no injustice.

2. A different coworker is promoted over me because he belongs to the manager's club, and the manager explains that he was a better fit, although my performance reviews are stronger. The feeling I have is nearly indistinguishable from the first case — the same knot in the stomach, the same wish that he will fail. My verdict: this is not envy, or not primarily; it is resentment, and it accurately tracks an injustice.

3. I learn that a coworker with the same title, seniority, and performance is paid 25 percent more than I am, purely because he negotiated while I accepted the first offer. My verdict: the discomfort I feel is envy rather than a signal of injustice, but it does carry useful information about my failure to negotiate.

4. A worker near the bottom of a company whose chief executive is paid four hundred times the median salary says she envies the executives. My verdict: her envy is excusable, and it points to a genuine structural defect, even though in practical terms she would be better off not dwelling on it.

5. I envy a friend who just won a major award for which I was also shortlisted; by every standard I accept, she deserved to win. My verdict: this is envy proper, and it is not excusable.

6. I envy a different friend who won the same award through a selection process we both know is arbitrary, reputation-driven, and only loosely connected to merit. My verdict: here the feeling seems closer to resentment at an injustice, and I do not feel the same self-reproach as in case 5 — though I cannot say why the fairness of the process should change my assessment of a feeling that feels the same from the inside.

I keep going back and forth on a further case. I envy a colleague who received a very expensive treatment for a rare illness quickly through a family connection, while strangers equally ill wait years on a public list. I believe the healthcare system is unjust. But my feeling is entirely personal — it fixes on his good luck, not on the queue. Is this envy, resentment, or both? Is it a signal of injustice, or a vice wearing a disguise?

Is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures most of my verdicts and explains the tensions among them? Please state the principle precisely, identify which of my six verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to overturn, and apply it to the healthcare case.
