ok so ive been going back and forth with people on a travel forum, and theres this move that keeps coming up: someone (usually an american arguing that you cant visit france and skip the tip, or a tourist in japan arguing about queueing) will say that when you take a table or join a line you've tacitly signed on to the local rules, so failing to tip or pushing in is basically breaking a promise you made just by being there. sometimes they appeal to Hume to back this up, since he treats promises as conventions rather than natural duties. the passage they lean on is from A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5, "Of the obligation of promises" (im quoting from the davidhume.org text):

"THAT the rule of morality, which enjoins the performance of promises, is not natural, will sufficiently appear from these two propositions, which I proceed to prove, viz. that a promise wou'd not be intelligible, before human conventions had establish'd it; and that even if it were intelligible, it wou'd not be attended with any moral obligation."

i've read it a few times and i'm not convinced it does what they want. as i read it, its a two-part claim: a promise only makes sense once a convention already exists, and even then what generates the obligation isnt the act of will itself. so id like an audit of the "tipping/queueing is a promise" analogy against this passage, not just in the abstract. give me the correspondence table: what's supposed to be the promise, the promisor, the promisee, the background convention that makes the act intelligible, and the thing that actually creates the obligation. then tell me where the mapping breaks, especially for a visitor who doesnt share the local convention, or where theres no identifiable promisee who could release you from it. and be straight with me: does the "tourists are morally obligated" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was promise-talk carrying the whole thing? if promise is the wrong frame here, is there a better way to get at whatever (if anything) a visitor actually owes under Hume's account?
