I've spent the last week trying to decide whether I should take acausal blackmail seriously, and I keep flip-flopping, so I want someone to check the argument I keep almost believing. Setup: a very powerful agent on the other side of the galaxy has no causal channel to me, but it has a near-perfect model of me and has precommited to run and torture a faithful simulation of me iff that model predicts I won't pay it a tribute; if the model predicts I do pay, no simulation is run. Paying doesn't cause the simulation not to happen—its model is fixed before I actually choose. Here's the argument that I should still pay:

(1) A rational agent should maximize evidential expected utility: states of the world matter when they're strongly correlated with an act, not only when the act causes them.
(2) In the case as set up, "I don't pay" is strongly correlated with "a faithful simulation of me is tortured," and "I pay" is strongly correlated with "that simulation isn't run," because the blackmailer's model of me is extremely accurate.
(3) A faithful simulation of me being tortured is, for decision purposes, about as bad as me being tortured, since my preferences extend to any copy of my decision procedure, memories, and values, not just to this body.
(4) So paying has much higher evidential expected utility than not paying.
(5) Therefore a rational agent should pay the acausal blackmailer.

I think the live options are that (1) is doing a CDT/EDT bait-and-switch, that (3) is just a dressed-up version of "a copy is me," or that the argument is actually valid and my resistance is exactly why people end up as evidential decision theorists. Tell me exactly where it goes wrong, if it does. Give me exactly five numbered entries—one per step—each a sentence or two stating whether that step holds or fails and why, with no intro or conclusion.
