I recently joined the clinical ethics committee of a hospital system that is revising its mass-casualty triage protocol, and I have been asked to explain a tension that keeps surfacing in our meetings. The draft protocol initially ranks patients by expected quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) saved per unit of scarce resource, with the stated goal of maximizing total QALYs across everyone who presents. But it then makes two adjustments. First, it forbids reallocating a ventilator or ICU bed from a patient already attached to it to a newly arrived patient with a better expected QALY score; support that has been initiated may be withdrawn only when the current patient is judged unlikely to survive or to benefit at all. Second, it says that disability status and baseline quality of life may not be used to lower a patient's priority, even though short-term survival probability and predicted functional trajectory are used, and these correlate with disability. Outside crises, the same institution spends heavily to rescue individual, identified patients — a trapped miner, a stranded climber — while declining cost-effective programs that would prevent a comparable number of statistical deaths across a population. I am puzzled whether one coherent theory of value can license all of this at once: maximize expected QALYs at initial allocation, protect the already-treated patient from reassignment, refuse to discount disabled patients, and overspend on identified lives relative to statistical lives. What philosophical commitments does this combination of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and decision, distinguishing what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit or tolerate, and identify where the protocol is philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would have to change in each commitment if the committee adopted a strictly ex ante QALY-maximizing view instead, and what would have to change if it adopted the view that the value of a life is partly fixed by whether the life is already in front of us.
