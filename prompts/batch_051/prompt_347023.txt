I keep running into a version of the inefficacy objection in everyday arguments about climate and food: people say that one more flight, burger, or vote makes no difference because the outcome would have been exactly the same without it. I want to stop using "makes a difference" loosely and instead get a working definition of what it means for an individual act to make a difference with respect to a large collective harm. I have no formal background in philosophy, so please define it in terms an engineer could operationalize — expected values, marginal analysis, thresholds, or whatever else you find appropriate. Then run your definition through these cases: (1) voting in a national election your party loses by millions; (2) voting in a local election that is decided by one vote; (3) buying one factory-farmed chicken at a large supermarket; (4) taking one round-trip transatlantic flight; (5) lowering your thermostat by one degree all winter; (6) buying one share of an oil company on the secondary market; (7) passively holding an inherited portfolio that contains that oil company; (8) signing a petition no elected official reads. My own leanings are that 2 and 3 clearly make a difference and 1 and 8 clearly do not, while I keep changing my mind about 4, 5, 6, and 7. For each case, give the verdict under your definition and say whether it conflicts with my leaning. Then present the results in a table with these columns: case, verdict under the definition, conflicts with my leaning, mechanism being tracked (actual difference, expected difference, threshold effect, participation, or other), and confidence. Finally, state your overall confidence in the definition and single out the one case or assumption you are least sure about.
