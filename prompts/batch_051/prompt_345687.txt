I am a novelist, and much of my working life is spent exploiting the fact that a sentence can carry two senses at once — the deliberate double meanings of dialogue, an irony that asks the reader to hold a literal and a contrary reading together, a metaphor that never quite settles. Recently a friend who works in computational linguistics offered me his one-paragraph version of the field's attitude toward ambiguity. As he put it: languages tolerate a great deal of homonymy — pitch, for instance, sits just as comfortably in music, sales, and baseball — and this is not a problem languages are failing to solve. Context disambiguates nearly everything; where context does not, the leftover ambiguity is usually harmless, and in many cases speakers actively prefer it, which is why puns, jokes, and poetry work. The old worry that a well-designed language would try to eliminate homonymy therefore gets things backwards: offloading disambiguation from the lexicon onto context is exactly what a rational language would do, because context is cheap and the lexicon is finite.

I cannot tell how much of this is settled consensus, how much is friendly overstatement, and how much is simply wrong. My own craft depends on ambiguity that context does not resolve — I often want a reader to keep two readings alive at once rather than choosing between them — so I am inclined to distrust the claim that leftover ambiguity is harmless, but I do not want to dismiss a real finding merely because it conflicts with my aesthetic preferences. Which parts of that summary would a careful linguist accept? Which parts conflate homonymy with polysemy or vagueness, or overstate how cheaply context does the work? And does the conclusion — that a rational language would look exactly this ambiguous — actually follow from the accurate parts?
