I've ended up convinced by the "misconceptions as theories" picture: when a kid adds 1/2 + 1/3 and gets 2/5, or insists that heavier things fall faster, the error usually isn't noise. It's the output of some small, internally coherent alternate theory — multiplication always makes things bigger, motion needs a force to keep it going, and so on. So the job of teaching is to get those naive theories out in the open and confront them, not just patch the bug. What I want to know is what would actually count against that picture. Not the strawman "students are just careless" — the hard cases. If the theory view is right, how consistent should a student's error be across contexts, what should happen to the error after a good lesson, and what should studies that track a kid's changing explanations week by week show? Spell out which patterns would be fatal, which would be merely awkward, and which ones a defender would try to absorb. Then give me a real verdict, not a menu: either the theory view is in good shape, or its been protected so thoroughly that it can't lose — and that itself would matter.
