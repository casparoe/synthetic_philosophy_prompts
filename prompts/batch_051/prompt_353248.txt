I'm an ML engineer who ended up in a reading group that's mostly philosophers and biologists, and for the last year we've been stuck on whether natural selection is a good analogy for gradient descent. It got personally relevant for me because I'm about to write a design doc proposing an evolutionary outer loop for part of our training pipeline, and I can't tell weather I'm building on a real structural insight or just on a metaphor.

The two camps, as best I can state them. One side says both processes are optimizers in the same abstract sense: mutation and selection do noisy search over a fitness landscape, SGD does noisy search over a loss landscape, and the differences are implementation details, so lessons transfer. The other side says evolution is a stupid optimizer: it has no gradient signal, no credit assignment across generations, a fitness function that shifts as the population changes it, and it gets stuck in local optima and path-dependence in ways SGD mostly doesn't; so the analogy misleads more than it explains.

What puzzles me is that everybody knows the same biology and the same ML, and nobody has moved. So before I write this doc, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden empirical question about how similar the two processes really are, a verbal dispute about the word optimizer, a difference about what an analogy is supposed to do for us (explain, predict, license design choices), or something else? My working hypothesis is that the two sides are using optimizer in two different senses—one broad, one narrow—and most of the fight is over the word. But I'm not confident.

Please approach this as a philosopher of science who works on analogies and models. Start by brainstorming the live options for what the disagreement could be, then commit to the one you think is right and defend it. Don't give me a survey of the two positions; I already know them. Say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis, and be honest if the answer is that we shouldn't expect progress.
