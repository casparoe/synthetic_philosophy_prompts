I sit on the risk subcommittee of our university's endowment investment committee. Each quarter we populate our risk dashboard by having each member score scenarios — a recession, inflation above a certain level, a court ruling against the carbon border tax — on a scale of 0 to 100, then averaging the individual scores. Joint and conditional scenarios are averaged the same way, though not always over the same members or the same quarter. At last week's meeting, a new member showed that this independent averaging leaves the dashboard internally inconsistent: one page assigns a probability to a compound event such as "recession and inflation," while the base-rate and conditional figures on other pages, combined the way our own worksheet instructs, imply a different probability for that same event. He argued that anyone could therefore offer the committee a small set of bets, each priced in line with a published dashboard number, such that the committee loses money no matter which scenario obtains. He wants the full investment committee to suspend every allocation decision that relies on the dashboard until a working group rebuilds the scenario book. Several of my colleagues say this is a parlor trick: we are nine people, not one mind; the dashboard is a planning summary rather than a set of odds we have committed to cover; and nobody is obligated to accept a bet against it.

Draft the short opinion I'll circulate to the full committee by Thursday morning. It should recommend a concrete course of action — suspend, do not suspend, or suspend only certain uses — with a time frame, and it should engage the member's case on its strongest reading rather than just rehearsing the obvious reply. In particular, I want the memo to address squarely whether a Dutch book aimed at an aggregated dashboard has the same force against a nine-person committee that it has against a single reasoner, given that the dashboard really does guide actual allocations. Please also separate what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material: for instance, the arithmetic of the inconsistency can be treated as settled, but the claim that coherence is a requirement for any group that acts on its published numbers should be presented as contested if that is what it is, rather than as consensus.

Begin with the document itself, with no preface or commentary; lead with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasoning.
