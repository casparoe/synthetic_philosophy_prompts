I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy in earnest. I have been working through Karl Jaspers' The Question of German Guilt, and I find myself both impressed and unsettled by his taxonomy. As I understand him, he distinguishes four kinds of guilt: criminal guilt, which a court adjudicates for individual acts; political guilt, which falls on all citizens of a state for the deeds done by that state, irrespective of what any particular citizen did; moral guilt, which concerns what one has personally done or failed to do; and metaphysical guilt, which rests on the idea that each human being shares in responsibility for all wrongs by virtue of solidarity with humanity. The category that troubles me is political guilt. It seems to be collective in a strong sense: it attaches to citizens as citizens, not as the particular agents of particular deeds, and it can apparently outlive the generation that committed the wrongs. The claim that a polity can be guilty in this way, and that later generations inherit that guilt, looks to me as though it might be either a morally important truth or a confused way of speaking. I want to know whether there is any empirical content to it. So my question is this: How would one test the claim that a nation can bear guilt across generations in something like Jaspers' political sense? I want a concrete design: what would you observe or measure, in whom, over what timescale, and with what comparison or control? What pattern of results would support the claim that this is a real property of collectives rather than a metaphor or a social convention? And after the test came back positive, what exactly would it have shown, and what part of the claim would remain untested? I am especially interested in where the operationalization does violence to the concept.
