I am a working programmer with a mostly practical background, and I have been trying to pin down a pattern that makes mutable state hard to reason about. I can point at it with two examples.

First: I bind one name to a list, then bind a second name to that same list, and then build a third list whose contents are equal to the first. At that moment the second and third lists are indistinguishable if I only look at what they contain. Yet one operation through the second name changes the first list as well, while the same operation through the third does not. Two things that were extensionally "the same" behave differently, and the difference lies entirely in how each was produced, not in anything observable in their present state.

Second: I write a function that, run in isolation, gives the same output for the same input every time. But when I call it twice with the identical argument during one run of a larger program, the two calls return different results, because earlier code modified some state the function reads without mentioning it among its parameters. The meaning of the call is not fixed by the call itself; it depends on where in the program's history the call happens to sit.

I suspect these are two faces of one thing: once state can change, equality is no longer a property of present contents, and the substitution of equals-for-equals stops being a safe move; reasoning has to be carried along a timeline instead of by replacing expressions with equal expressions. Is there a name—or several names—for exactly what is going on here? I keep running into the terms side effect and aliasing, but I cannot tell whether what I am describing is the same as one of those, a special case of them, or the more general phenomenon of which they are both instances. I am not satisfied by a bare label. I want the carving: what part of this, if any, is aliasing; what part is side effect; what exactly is lost when substitutability fails; and whether there is a single term that covers the whole phenomenon. If no established term does, then give it one and mark where it ends and its nearest neighbors begin.
