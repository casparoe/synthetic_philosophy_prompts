I've been arguing with my friend Cam about when you're allowed to flat-out state something as a fact, and Cam has this quietly consistent track record that I can't quite reduce to a rule. Here are the judgments I've collected. Cam thinks it's fine to say your biopsy came back clear when you've read the lab letter yourself and that's what it says. Cam thinks it's wrong to tell someone a train is cancelled because the departure board flickered and you had a strong hunch, even when you genuinely believe it and the train really was cancelled. Cam thinks it's fine to tell a friend the grant deadline is Friday because you checked the funder's site an hour ago and have no reason to think it changed. Cam thinks it's wrong to pass along an office rumor about who's getting fired as if it were established fact, even when the rumor later turns out to be true. Cam thinks it's fine to state there's coffee in the kitchen because you saw the pot there five minutes ago.

Now the case I actually care about: someone buys a ticket in a huge, fair lottery, knows the odds, and before the draw says to a friend, "I lost" (the draw hasn't happend yet, and as it happens their ticket does lose). What would Cam say about that assertion?

I'm less interested in the bare yes/no than in the rule Cam seems to be running, the other rules that fit the track record just as well, what each candidate rule predicts about the lottery case, and which further case—invented or realistic—would be the cleanest way to tell the surviving candidates apart. Also, give me your best guess about which rule Cam would actually endorse, and say why it isn't just a fancy restatement of "don't lie."
