A classic line of argument for the preservability of personal identity across whole-brain emulation proceeds by gradual replacement. Replace a single neuron with a functionally equivalent artificial unit, tuned to reproduce that neuron's input–output behavior, and the person (it is argued) survives the change. Since each individual replacement seems survivable, and survival composes across steps, the person survives once every neuron has been exchanged; an emulation reached as the endpoint of such a sequence would therefore not be a mere copy but the same mind. I am trying to determine whether this argument has any real force for the procedures most often proposed for whole-brain emulation today, rather than only for a thought-experimental sequence that may never be feasible.

The procedures actually envisioned look almost nothing like the gradual sequence. A brain would have to be preserved, scanned or sectioned post mortem, and reconstructed as a computational model; the process is discontinuous, destructive, and completed before any whole-brain emulation is running. There is no stage at which the biological brain and the emulation are simultaneously active, causally interacting, or jointly underwriting a stream of consciousness, and the reconstruction may operate at less than full synaptic resolution. Does the gradual replacement argument survive once the assumption of continuity between stages is replaced by this one-step destructive reconstruction? Which premise is doing the real work—the safety of single-neuron replacement, the functional-equivalence criterion, the transitivity of survival, or the continuity of the process itself—and can any weaker conclusion be salvaged, for example about our obligations to the original person, the emulation's status as a successor self, or what a prudent person should believe before choosing the procedure?
