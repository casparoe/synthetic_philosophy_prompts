You are an expert on epistemic game theory and the theory of distributed computing. I want you to evaluate a parallel I have noticed between two results and tell me whether it is deep or superficial.

Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two Bayesian agents share a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for a given event become common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal; if they still disagree, at least one of the assumptions—common prior, common knowledge, or Bayesian rationality—has failed. In distributed computing, a standard family of results points the other way. The two-generals problem shows that no finite exchange of messages over an unreliable channel can give two parties common knowledge of their plan, and a related impossibility result says that no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system even when at most one process crashes. So one field treats common knowledge as the condition that makes persistent disagreement impossible, while the other treats common knowledge as something real systems cannot achieve, which is precisely why agreement is hard.

Is this a genuine structural parallel about the role of common knowledge in convergence, or a loose analogy riding on the word agreement? Start by reconstructing the formal structure of each result carefully. Then state the mapping between the two sides, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and identify at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false.
