I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I got nerd-sniped by the St. Petersburg paradox a few weeks ago and ended up with my own fix, and I want to know if it's a real idea or just a dressed-up mistake. The game as I keep seeing it: flip a fair coin until it lands tails, and if the first tails is on the nth flip the payoff is 2^n dollars. The expected payout is infinite (1/2 times 2, plus 1/4 times 4, and so on), but nobody would pay more than maybe twenty bucks to play. The usual move is diminishing marginal utility—a dollar matters less to you when your rich. That always felt like patching the math after the fact rather than explaining what's actually wrong.

So I've been calling my version "ergodic expectation," though I might be misusing the term. The ensemble average in the usual EV calculation is an average over infinitely many parallel copies of you each playing once; but you only have one bankroll and one life, so for anything you actually do repeatedly the relevant quantity is the growth rate of a single bankroll over time. For the St. Petersburg game, a player who keeps reinvesting and paying anything close to the arithmetic EV gets wiped out almost immediately, while maximizing expected log wealth survives and compounds. My central claim is that the paradox doesn't need utility curves; it needs the right kind of average for the process. Expected value isn't broken, it's just the wrong statistic for multiplicative quantities like wealth, which should be evaluated by geometric growth rate rather than arithmetic mean.

I know this smells like the Kelly criterion. My question is whether "ergodic expectation" is a real concept and whether someone has already worked it out under another name. Does it actually dissolve the paradox, or did I just sneak in logarithmic utility and call it math? And where does it break? I can already see one edge case: for a single one-shot game offered at $5, my rule says don't take it, which feels too conservative.
