Exam question:

To what extent did Mandeville and Adam Smith hold that private greed is a public benefit? Draw on the Fable of the Bees and Smith's idea of the invisible hand, and address whether "vice" and "self-interest" come to the same thing for the two thinkers.

Student answer:

Mandeville's Fable of the Bees (1705, expanded 1714) argues that what makes a society rich is precisely what moralists call vice: luxury, pride, envy and the appetite for fashion keep demand high and put people to work. When the bees are made virtuous, demand collapses, industry dies and the hive is ruined, hence the subtitle, Private Vices, Publick Benefits. Smith's famous remark in the Theory of Moral Sentiments that we do not expect our dinner from the butcher's benevolence but from his regard to his own interest looks like the same thesis. For Smith, the invisible hand converts each person's pursuit of private gain into an outcome that benefits everyone, even though nobody intended it. The only real difference between the two authors is rhetorical: Smith prefers to say self-interest where Mandeville says vice, but in a commercial society these amount to the same thing. It follows that Smith, like Mandeville, is committed to the claim that luxurious spending, and in principle even fraud, is a public benefit so long as it keeps money circulating and gives people work.
