Suppose a country brings in a 'derelict works' law covering books, films, recordings and software. If no one has made a verifiable claim to a work for ten years — no sales, licences, renewal notices, takedown requests, or replies to a rights enquiry — anyone can list it on a public derelict register, and if no owner objects within a year it becomes derelict. While a work is derelict, anyone may preserve it, teach from it, or use it commercially; commercial users must pay 5% of gross revenue into an escrow pot. If the owner turns up later, they get the pot and full control resumes, but uses made before they reappeared cannot be sued over. For software, whoever lists it must deposit a complete copy (source code if it can be found) with the national archive, and any patches they release while it is derelict have to be made public. If 25 years pass with no owner, the work enters the public domain and the escrow money goes to the archive. Walk me through what this would actually do rather than whether it's a good idea: who benefits first, how people game it, where it fails, what second-order effects it has on publishers, archives, pirates and developers, and whether the failure modes can be fixed without scrapping the scheme. Answers in British English, please.
