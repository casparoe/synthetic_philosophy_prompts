I am putting together a short course on the ethics of influence in clinical and counseling settings, and I keep going back and forth about a case I want to use. On the first day I give students a working definition of manipulation: an act of influence is manipulative to the extent that it is (a) intentional; (b) carried out through non-rational channels, such as biases, emotional pressures, automatic responses, or the structuring of options rather than through explicit argument; (c) hard for the target to detect or track as it is happening; (d) one whose mechanism the target would not endorse if it were fully explained to them; and (e) likely to move the target toward a choice they would not make under their own settled values. Influence that fails to meet these conditions, I tell them, is legitimate persuasion or permissible choice architecture.

The case is motivational interviewing, a goal-directed counseling style often used with ambivalent patients — for example, someone who says they want to quit smoking but keeps finding reasons to defer quitting. The clinician asks open questions and then selectively reflects and develops the patient's own change talk, meaning their statements that favor quitting, while deliberately not arguing with or directly correcting their sustain talk. Resistance is met by rolling with it rather than confronting it head-on. The patient knows the general point of the program, but not the fine-grained conversational strategy. Some of the technique's effectiveness appears to run through consistency and salience effects rather than through the patient's critical weighing of reasons.

Go through (a) through (e) honestly and say, for each, whether motivational interviewing as I have described it satisfies the condition, and where my criteria are silent or underdetermined. Then address whether the real difficulty lies in the case or in the criteria. In particular, does the fact that the patient already endorses the general goal do all the work in keeping motivational interviewing on the permissible side, and is the rational/non-rational distinction in (b) doing any independent work beyond what the transparency condition in (c) and the endorsement condition in (d) already capture? If you were to amend the criteria so that the verdict is less equivocal, which condition would you change, what would you replace it with, and what would the amendment cost for neighboring cases such as advertising, parenting, or political canvassing?
