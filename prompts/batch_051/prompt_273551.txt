In ordinary moral judgement we treat a broken promise as a real wrong, but a broken oath as something in a different category. A minister who abandons a campaign pledge is accused of cynicism; an official who violates an oath of office is said to have struck at the constitutional order itself. A witness who lies to a journalist is merely dishonest; a witness who lies after swearing to tell the truth commits perjury, even if the oath took thirty seconds and invoked a deity in whom the witness has no belief. The ancient Greek version of this intuition is the oath sworn on the Styx: the gods themselves were bound by it, and a god who swore falsely upon it was deprived of breath and shut out of the company of the gods for years, not because the river administered a penalty but because the oath was thought to carry its own sanction. This combination of attitudes is intelligible and yet puzzling. If the underlying commitment—the intention, the expectation created, the harm of breach to the other party—is the same in the promise and in the oath, what exactly does the added formality contribute?

I would like you to examine whether the asymmetry between promise-breaking and oath-breaking is justified. Reconstruct the strongest candidate principles that might ground the difference: for example, that oaths make the commitment public in a distinctive way; that they stake the swearer's honour or credibility as a single asset that breach destroys; that they waive in advance the usual excuses; or that they deliberately invite a heavier social or supernatural penalty. Then test each candidate against hard cases: an oath sworn by a person who privately regards the invoked authority as meaningless; an oath extracted under threat; a bare promise that creates exactly the same public expectation as an oath; and an oath sworn on a dead or fictional power such as the Styx. In each case, say whether the candidate principle still delivers the judgement that oath-breaking is weightier. Finish with an honest verdict: is the special binding force of oaths a defensible moral technology, or a piece of theatre whose authority we have kept without examining it?
