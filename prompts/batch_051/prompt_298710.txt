hey — I'm a PhD student in political science, writing on the fiscal side of the open borders debate, and I've been trying to design a scheme that separates labor-market openness from welfare-state membership. I want someone to stress-test it: not whether it's just or unjust, but how it would actually behave in practice.

The scheme: a rich welfare state opens its labor market to foreign workers pretty much freely (work permit tied to a job offer, renewable, portable across employers). But instead of enrolling those workers in the ordinary welfare state, it credits the payroll taxes that would normally fund unemployment insurance, public health insurance, and the pay-as-you-go pension into an individual social-insurance account for each migrant. The migrant can draw on the account for the same contingencies — unemployment, sickness, old age — under roughly the same eligibility rules and replacement rates as citizens. If the account runs dry, they don't fall back on the standard safety net; instead they can take a repayable emergency loan from the state, recovered out of future wages or contributions. After seven years of contributions, or once the account balance passes some threshold, or upon naturalization — whichever comes first — they convert to full ordinary welfare membership and any remaining balance gets swept into the general fund. Citizens and permanent residents can also top up any migrant's account as a tax-deductible donation.

What I want to know is how this actually plays out. Who self-selects into migrating under these rules, and do employers end up preferring these workers because they're effectively cheaper or easier to shed in a downturn? Does it make natives more or less supportive of immigration, and does it entrench a permanent second tier even with the conversion threshold? Where does it get gamed first — employers churning workers before the seven-year mark, under-the-table work, migrants paying into each other's accounts, a market for tax-deductible sponsor donations? In a recession, when lots of accounts go empty at once, does the scheme protect the welfare state's fiscal base or fall apart exactly when it's needed? And overall: is this a genuine step toward open borders, or a guest-worker caste with extra steps? Give me the mechanisms, and tell me wheter the failure modes are fixable.
