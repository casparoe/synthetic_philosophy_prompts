I keep encountering proposals that either define possibility in terms of probability or treat probability as a measure over an antecedently given space of possibilities, and I want a standard against which to judge them. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of how modal and probabilistic notions relate. The list should specify: the cases it must get right (an event that is possible but has probability zero, such as any particular point being chosen from a continuum; an event with probability one that is nonetheless not necessary); the distinctions it must not collapse (logical, metaphysical, nomic, and epistemic possibility; objective chance versus rational credence; possibility versus positive probability); the inference patterns it must preserve or explain (what impossibility entails about probability, what positive probability entails about possibility, and which modal principles survive when "probably" is read as a probability threshold, such as monotonicity but not agglomeration); and the structural question it must settle, namely which of modality and probability is prior, and how the answer avoids circularity. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular camps, and flag any subsets that cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether the demand that every genuine possibility receive positive probability can be reconciled with standard probability over continuum-sized spaces. Finally, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the usual reductive proposals.
