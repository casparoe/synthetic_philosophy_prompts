I was arguing with a friend about Nozick's experience machine, and he kept insisting that the thought experiment refutes not merely hedonism but any view that makes well-being the only thing of ultimate value. I pushed back: the familiar machine is rigged to tempt a positive, pleasure-maximizing hedonist, since what it offers from the inside is a life of successes, loves, and satisfactions. It may not engage someone whose suffering-focused ethic gives the reduction of suffering strict priority over the production of positive goods. My friend then demanded that I build a version of the machine that such a view would actually find maximally tempting, and say whether Nozick's objection survives the change, so I am trying to work through that rigorously.

Consider the following variant, which we can call the anesthetic machine. Unlike the original, it does not even offer the illusion of writing the great novel or having deep friendships. The only programs available are, from the inside, indistinguishable from a life of complete and permanent relief: no pain, fear, boredom, frustration, humiliation, or unsatisfied desire, but also no achievement, novelty, deep satisfaction, or interesting relationship, with just enough positive affect included to keep any of the simulated lives from tipping into negative states. You know before deciding that none of the contents are real, that your family and friends outside will be harmed by your choice, and that you will never actually do or make anything of value.

Does the standard anti-experience-machine argument retain its force here? In the original case, the usual diagnosis is that we would not plug in because we want to live in contact with reality and to actually be the authors of our lives, not merely to have experiences as if doing these things. But a negative utilitarian can reply that the original machine tempts us with fake positive goods, whereas the anesthetic machine offers only the removal of genuine bads; if suffering and happiness are asymmetric, the two cases may come apart. Work through what a careful negative utilitarian should say about the anesthetic machine, and whether the standard objections to the original machine — authenticity, the value of real relationships and achievements, the sense that a simulated life is beneath us — apply with the same force, less force, or perhaps even greater force. Does this variation expose a genuine difference between suffering-focused views and ordinary hedonistic utilitarianism, or does it show instead that even a negative utilitarian cannot treat the desire to live in reality as merely another preference to be outweighed by the absence of pain?
