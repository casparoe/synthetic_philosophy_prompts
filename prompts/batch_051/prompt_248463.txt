Say that a state policy is religiously neutral in the French laïcité sense, as opposed to being a form of cultural assimilation, just in case it satisfies four conditions. First, its justification is a general state interest — such as equal citizenship, the prevention of coercion of citizens by public officials, or the neutrality of the state toward users of public services — and not the promotion of the majority's religion or culture. Second, it is symmetrical: the rule applies the same standards to all religions and to nonreligion, and neither names nor singles out any religion. Third, it is limited in scope: it regulates state officials only while they are performing official duties and leaves private and associational religious practice substantially untouched. Fourth, it is proportional: it is the least restrictive means adequate to that general interest.

Now consider this invented but realistic law. A state forbids public-school teachers, judges, and police officers from wearing conspicuous religious symbols while on duty: headscarves, turbans, kippot, and large crosses are enumerated, while small religious jewelry, such as a pendant cross, a Star of David, or a small crescent, is expressly permitted. The law's stated rationale is the state's duty of neutrality toward the public and the need to prevent officials from imposing religious pressure on pupils, litigants, and citizens. In practice, enforcement falls almost entirely on observant Muslims and Sikhs who cover their heads or wear turbans, and to a lesser extent on observant Jewish men who wear a kippah.

Does this law qualify as religiously neutral by the four criteria, or is it better described as a disguised form of assimilation? Work through the criteria one by one; where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say so. Finally, say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision — in particular, whether a conspicuous/discreet distinction does principled work or merely tracks the majority's default practices.
