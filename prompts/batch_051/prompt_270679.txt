I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether a brain interface could ever produce a forbidden color, and I want to work through the question more carefully than we did. My friend treated the classic opponent-process argument as decisive; I suspect it turns on an assumption that a future technology could quietly remove, but I am not sure how much would survive. I would like you to think it through with me.

Here is the classic argument as my friend defended it. Human color vision is organized around opponent channels: a red-green channel, a blue-yellow channel, and a black-white channel. Within the red-green channel, red and green are opposite directions of a single underlying variable, and they cancel each other; this is why hue-cancellation procedures always find a point that is neither reddish nor greenish, and why the argument takes it that no stimulus presented to the eye has ever produced an experience that is reddish and greenish in the way that purple is reddish and bluish. From this the argument concludes that reddish-green and bluish-yellow are not merely absent from experience but impossible for a human visual system: forbidden colors, hard limits on the space of possible color experience.

Now suppose a mature brain interface can drive color-coding neurons directly, bypassing the eye and the early opponent processing. It can activate the population whose activity normally accompanies a red percept at the same time, and in the same region, as the population whose activity normally accompanies a green percept, and it can hold that pattern stable for minutes. Suppose the subject has normal color language, can compare the resulting state with familiar reds, greens, browns, and olives, and robustly reports a single new color: not an alternation between red and green, not a muddy mixture of the two, but a color that seems to present both red and green at once, as purple presents both red and blue.

I want to know what this does to the classic argument. Does the argument survive the interface, or does it survive only in a weakened form, or does it die? Which premise is the interface actually putting pressure on — the premise that opponent channels constitute the machinery of color experience, the premise that a single opponent channel cannot be driven in both directions at once, or a background assumption about the routes by which color experience can be caused? Is the classic argument best read as a claim about the structure of possible experience, or as a claim about what normal human vision can extract from light? If there is a repaired version, what is the strongest claim that remains true and still does the explanatory work the original argument was supposed to do? I am interested in the fate of the argument, not in forecasting the technology.

When you answer, act as an expert in the philosophy of perception who is comfortable with color neuroscience. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the different readings of the classic argument before settling on the reading you will assess. Distinguish impossibility for a normal visual system under ordinary stimulation from impossibility for any system. Also say what would count as evidence that the subject is genuinely having a reddish-green experience rather than misdescribing a novel, ambiguous, or mixed one. Finish by stating precisely what, on your view, survives of the classic argument.
