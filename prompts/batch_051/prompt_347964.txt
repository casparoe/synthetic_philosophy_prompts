I'm a retired physiologist and I've fallen in with a reading group on early modern philosophy. We've spent three weeks on Descartes and non-human animals, and it's turned into one of those rows where both sides think the other is being wilfully dense. One of us holds what I'll call the beast-machine reading: Descartes means it flat out — animals are unthinking, unfeeling mechanisms, no more sentient than a chiming clock. The other holds the feels-but-doesn't-reflect reading: Descartes denies animals reason and language, not sentience; a dog genuinely feels the kick, it just can't form a judgement about the feeling. We've agreed to stop bickering and write an adversarial-collaboration statement. Could you draft the document for us?

Structure it like this. First, a numbered list of substantive claims about Descartes' actual commitments that both readings can sign — these should be load-bearing, not truisms. Second, a short disagreements table: a neutral statement of each point at issue, then each camp's own wording of it's position, phrased so its defenders would say yes, that's us. Third, two or three cruxes — sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move at least one side — and for each crux a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written in advance. Finally, say which of our disagreements you take to be genuine and which are merely verbal.

Work in one distinction I suspect is doing a lot of the work: feeling as a bodily process (nerves, animal spirits, brain events) versus feeling as a mode of consciousness — and whether Descartes kept those two seperate in his own thinking. I also think Descartes wobbles between confidently denying thought in brutes in some places and, in his late letters, more cautiously saying we cannot prove there is none — the document should say how much each reading can lean on that wobble. Keep the whole thing under 700 words, use British spelling, and don't hedge: actually make the calls about what's agreed, how each side should be worded, which cruxes matter, and which disagreements are only verbal, rather than laying out the options.
