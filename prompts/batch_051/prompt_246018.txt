Im in a jurisprudence course on the rule of law right now, and weve been going through Fuller's eight ways to fail at making law and Raz's point that law has to be capable of guiding behaviour. I'm trying to write my term paper on whether algorithmic decision-making by the state can really satisfy the rule-of-law ideal, and I keep returning to a toy constitution the tutor gave us. It has one clause, and it says: 'No person shall be subjected to any penalty, burden, or adverse official decision except pursuant to a law that is public, general, and prospective.'

The case I keep getting stuck on is this: the revenue authority uses a machine-learning model to select which small businesses get audited for underreporting. The enabling statute is on the books, public, general, and prospective — it just says the authority may audit where there is 'a risk of noncompliance.' But the actual selection criteria are the learned weights of the model; even the engineers can't say in advance what feature of a given business made it high-risk, and the weights aren't published. The people who drafted that clause plainly weren't picturing trained models.

How should the clause apply to that case? Work through the approaches we've been using in class — the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if you'd asked them, and what a reasonable business owner reading it would think — and say which should win. The thing I'm actually stuck on is whether the answer changes if the model is a very long but human-readable decision tree (so the rule could in principle be stated, just not usefully), versus a black-box neural net. I care less about the practical legal answer than about what this shows about the rule of law as an ideal, and whether satisfying the ideal is a property of the written law alone or of the whole decision system behind it.
