I'm not any kind of philosopher, just hoping you can settle something that's been chewing at me. I volunteer once a week at a small farm sanctuary near my town, mostly mucking out enclosures and collecting eggs. Most of the hens are rescues from battery-cage operations and they still lay. The sanctuary doesn't sell or donate the eggs; the staff hard-boil a lot of them, crush them shell and all, and feed them back to the hens to replace the calcium laying costs them, and whatever can't be used goes into the compost. A newer volunteer, Dana, asked if she could take some of the leftover eggs home and eat them. The manager shrugged and said it's compost otherwise. That kicked off a running argument between Dana and Rita, a long-time volunteer who's been vegan for around twenty years. I can't stop thinking about it and neither of them will back down.

The way I remember it, the argument went like this. (1) Rita said Dana shouldn't eat the eggs, because using an animal as a source of food is exactly the mindset the sanctuary exists to undo; the hens aren't machines and their bodies aren't ours to harvest, even if no one is hurt. (2) Dana said that makes no sense for eggs that are genuinely going to waste: no hen is harmed, the sanctuary isn't going to acquire more hens because she ate an egg, and calling it exploitation stretches the word until it means nothing. (3) Rita came back that the eggs aren't pure waste -- they get fed back to the hens precisely because laying is so hard on them -- and even if a few eggs really are surplus, taking them still presumes a standing right to whatever an animal's body produces. (4) Dana said if that's the rule, then Rita should also object to manure, compost, and the wool the sanctuary sometimes has to shear for the sheep's own health; but Rita happily eats vegetables grown in sanctuary compost. So either the rule is 'never use anything that comes from an animal,' and Rita is inconsistent, or the rule is 'don't cause harm,' and surplus eggs are fine. (5) Rita said eggs aren't like manure, because eggs are food and eating them keeps a person's taste for eggs alive -- which quietly props up the same demand that keeps battery cages in business -- and in any case the hens can't consent. Dana replied that hens can't consent to being composted either, and a victimless act can't become wrong just because it might lead some unrelated person to do a different, harmful act later. I'm paraphrasing, but that's the shape of it.

You're a professional philosopher who works on animal ethics and the morality of using animal byproducts. Please think carefully, and start by writing out the strongest version of each side's position before you judge it. Then go through the exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it's clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, and justify it in a couple of sentences. Then say who is right about what overall, what the actual crux is, and what both of them are missing. Don't just pick a winner based on the last word -- I want to know where the argument really turns, and whether the 'victemless use' point or the 'keeps demand alive' point is doing the real work.
