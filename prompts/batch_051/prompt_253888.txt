In my decision theory course we discussed the idea that, while you are in the middle of deliberating, you cannot treat your own possible actions as ordinary events to be predicted: assigning a probability to an act seems either to settle that you have already chosen it or to reduce your will to an external chance process. I am worried about the formalism, though. When we compute expected utility, we use probabilities of outcomes conditional on performing an act, P(O | A). But a conditional probability is normally defined only with respect to a probability for the conditioning event A. If I cannot assign P(A) while deliberating, then where does P(O | A) get its meaning? It seems that either deliberation must license self-probabilities after all, or the expected-utility formula is not really using conditional probabilities in the ordinary sense. I'd like a committed answer: does rational choice theory require probabilities over my own actions, and if not, what exactly are the act-conditional probabilities in expected utility calculations? Please take a definite position and defend it rather than just surveying interpretations.
