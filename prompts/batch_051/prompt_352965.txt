Two members of a reading group I sit in on got locked in an argument over what exactly Beauvoir means by her well-known claim that one is not born a woman but becomes one. Both made arguments the rest of us found persuasive at different moments, and since neither would concede anything, I want the exchange adjudicated turn by turn rather than simply resolved in favor of one side.

The first held that the claim is best read as an anticipatory distinction between sex and gender: a child is born a female of the species, and only through upbringing, expectation, and custom is that child made into a woman. On this reading, womanhood is a social product rather than a biological destiny, so anatomy cannot settle what a woman is or must be. The second replied that this reading places too much daylight between the body and its meaning. For Beauvoir, the body is a situation: the sexed body is not inert material on which culture inscribes womanhood, because the biological is always already lived and interpreted, and is never encountered in a raw, pre-social form. That is why The Second Sex spends so much time on puberty, menstruation, and pregnancy — not to reduce womanhood to biology, but to show that these facts are experienced only through meanings one did not create. The first came back by pointing to Beauvoir's existentialist vocabulary of transcendence, project, and bad faith: if becoming a woman were simply something done to someone, there would be no place for the woman who assumes her situation or refuses it, and the analysis would collapse into the very determinism Beauvoir is trying to escape. The second answered that the possibility of refusal does not make the category optional. A woman who refuses marriage or motherhood refuses as a woman, from within a situation whose meanings are not of her making, and that refusal is itself interpreted through those same meanings. So, the second concluded, the becoming is imposed on a person before it is taken up by that person: the element of choice is real but secondary, exercised only within constraints one did not author.

Please adjudicate this turn by turn: assess the sex/gender reading, the body-as-situation objection, the comeback from existentialist agency, and the reply about refusal, saying in each case whether the move is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right and why. Then say what each person has on balance established, and identify the distinction the argument keeps circling but never quite names.
