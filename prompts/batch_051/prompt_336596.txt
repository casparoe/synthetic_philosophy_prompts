I teach a course on value theory and I keep coming back to an argument a student made at office hours last term. I've turned it into numbered steps because I can't decide wether it's a neat refutation of hedonism or a bit of sleight of hand. You're a professional philosopher working on practical reason and the theory of value, so I'd like you to think through it carefully and give me a rigorous ruling.

(1) If hedonism is true, pleasure is the only thing that is good for its own sake; everything else is good, if at all, only as a means to pleasure. (2) But if pleasure is the only thing good for its own sake, then the sensible way for a hedonist to make decisions is to ask, for each option, how much pleasure it will produce for her, and to pick the option with the highest expected pleasure. (3) Yet people who actually deliberate this way—treating every choice as an explicit calculation about their own enjoyment—reliably end up with less pleasure overall than people who throw themselves into friendships, crafts, and other projects for their own sake. (4) So hedonism directs its adherents to adopt a deliberative habit that, by the theory's own lights, makes them worse off; the theory is practically self-defeating. (5) And a theory of value that's self-defeating in this way can't be right. (6) Therefore hedonism is false.

I have a nagging suspicion that step (2) is sliding between two different things—something like what makes a life good versus how you should actually think when deciding what to do—but I'd like a precise diagnosis rather than a vibe. Before you deliver your verdict, maybe brainstorm the strongest replies a smart hedonist could make against each step. Then tell me exactly which step fails (if any), what the corrected version of that step would say, and whether the conclusion still follows from a repaired version of the argument. Also flag any step that's ambiguous between two readings and say how the argument fares under each reading.
