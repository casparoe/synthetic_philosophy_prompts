I am a novelist, and I have a standing policy about the legal thicket that surrounds my work: I do not read it. When my agent sends me a publishing contract, I sign it. When a platform presents me with updated terms of service, I click through. When the tax code or a copyright rule is said to have changed, I wait for someone I trust to tell me the part that matters. My reasoning is not laziness, or at least not only laziness. These documents are drafted so that an ordinary careful reader cannot reliably recover the rules that will actually be applied to them; the binding content lives in cross-references, definitions, and enforcement practices that are not on the page. Reading them would not make me know the rules. It would only make me feel as though I did, which is more dangerous. And I cannot put every document in front of a lawyer without spending my working life on legal fees. So I rely on the reputation of the counterparty, the assumption that most terms are standard boilerplate, and the fallback belief that a court would not enforce a genuinely surprising clause against an author who could not have been expected to find it. Is this a defensible response to the fact that the law binds me to rules I cannot realistically know, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle — free-riding on a legal system I assume will protect me from its own fine print? Under what conditions does the policy fail, and is there a version of it that keeps the benefits without treating my ignorance as a shield?
