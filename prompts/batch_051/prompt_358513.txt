Most people, and the laws of armed conflict, hold two positions at once. Outside war, an aggressor who attacks someone has no right to kill the person defending herself; if he kills her, he commits murder, and only she is permitted to use lethal force. Inside war, however, the soldier fighting for an unjust cause is treated as no less entitled to kill the soldier defending a just cause than that defender is entitled to kill him; the two are regarded as moral equals so long as each obeys the rules of engagement. The very same fact—that one side's cause is unjust—strips the private aggressor of any permission to kill, but appears not to strip the unjust side's soldier. What principle, if any, generates both judgments? Find the strongest candidate justification and press it against the hardest cases: the volunteer who enlists in a war he knows is unjust, the mercenary, the conscript who could refuse at modest cost, and the just-side soldier whose own forces commit atrocities. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept in full, narrowed, or abandoned.
