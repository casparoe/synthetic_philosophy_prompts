Title: The Etymological Fallacy Is Not One Fallacy

Note 1. The etymological fallacy is usually defined as the error of taking a word's earlier or original meaning to settle its correct present meaning. The stock example: decimate derives from a Latin word for removing one in ten, so it cannot properly mean any large-scale destruction.

Note 2. That single label runs together three distinct claims. (a) The historical claim: word W earlier meant M, or descends from a form meaning M. (b) The authoritative-origin claim: because W earlier meant M, current uses that depart from M are corruptions of W's real meaning. (c) The interpretive claim: when reconstructing the meaning of an older text or a deliberately technical vocabulary, the earlier sense M is the right object of analysis.

Note 3. These claims fail or succeed for different reasons. (a) is an ordinary empirical claim and may simply be true. (b) needs an additional essentialist premise, namely that words possess a true meaning independent of how a linguistic community actually uses them. (c) is often sound, because there the target is not present-day meaning but meaning at a time, or within a stipulated terminology. Many bad appeals to etymology are bad, then, not because etymology is irrelevant, but because the arguer slides from (a) to (b) while speaking as if only (a) were at issue.

Note 4. The word really does much of the smuggling. "Decimate really means to reduce by one tenth" is false as a claim about present-day English, and defensible only as a claim about the word's source or about a particular historical usage. On this diagnosis, the fallacy is a conflation of questions: evidence that answers one question is treated as if it answered another.

Note 5. I would also resist the mirror-image overcorrection: the rule that etymology is never relevant to meaning is too strong. Etymological facts are relevant when the question is historical, when a term is explicitly stipulated to carry a source meaning, and sometimes in legal or technical contexts whose point is continuity with an earlier usage. The useful corrective is not "ignore origins" but "say which question the origin is being offered to answer."
