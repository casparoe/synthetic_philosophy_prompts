I'm in a political philosophy class on whether you can opt out of the state, and we've been working with a checklist my professor gave for what makes something a state. His list is roughly Weberian but not strict: a state is a political association that (a) has a more or less permanent population, (b) governs a definite territory, (c) makes law through some authorized procedure, (d) claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of force inside that territory, and (e) is not subordinate to any other political authority as to these functions.

The borderline case I keep going back and forth on is basicaly a charter city, and I'd like you to run it through the checklist carefully. Imagine a host country passes a statute creating a special administrative zone, and the zone is then governed for 99 years by a private development corporation under a charter that is supposed to be very hard to revoke. The corporation passes a civil and commercial code for the zone, runs its own courts whose rulings the host state agrees to enforce, and hires a private police force that has statutory arrest powers inside the zone. It funds itself through fees on residents and a cut of the zone's taxes. The host state formally retains the power to dissolve the zone, but the charter says it may do so only for material breach, and even then the corporation gets binding arbitration and enormous compensation. People and firms who move in sign a Resident Agreement in which they consent to the zone's rules and waive some ordinary legal avenues they'd have in the rest of the country.

So is that thing a state according to the five criteria, or not? Go criterion by criterion, and where the answer is genuinely indeterminate under the criteria, say so instead of forcing a verdict. The three spots that snag me: does the monopoly-on-force criterion require that the same body that makes the rules also employs the enforcers, or can you satisfy it by controlling the rules while subcontracting the force? Does "definite territory" require the entity to own the land itself, or is a long lease plus exclusive administrative control enough? And does the host state's retained power to dissolve the zone automatically defeat the "not subordinate" criterion, or should we look at how hard the charter actually is to revoke in practice? Then say whether this case shows that one of the five criteria should be revised or dropped, or whether the real lesson is just that "state" is a family-resemblance cluster notion and no checklist will draw a clean line. If you've got room, also say whether your verdict flips if the same governance package is placed on a permanently moored floating platform outside any state's territorial waters, and what that second case tells us about whether the territory criterion is earning its keep.
