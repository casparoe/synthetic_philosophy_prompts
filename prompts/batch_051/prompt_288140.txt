Population axiology has been stuck for roughly four decades, and I want a diagnosis of the stalemate itself rather than another theory. The question looks simple: when two possible worlds differ in how many people exist and how well off they are, which world is better? Totalists rank worlds by summed welfare and thereby inherit the repugnant conclusion: for any world of, say, ten billion people living extremely good lives, some vastly larger world whose inhabitants have lives only barely worth living must be judged better. Averagists rank worlds by mean welfare and escape that conclusion, but they are left saying that adding a person whose life is worth living yet below the current average makes things worse, and that a solitary person of maximum welfare is better than a vast population only slightly less well off. Person-affecting theorists hold that merely bringing a happy person into existence is no improvement at all; this blocks the repugnant conclusion but leaves different-number comparisons undefined and imports the non-identity problem, the puzzle of how a choice that determines which people will exist could wrong anyone. Critical-level views rank worlds by the welfare that exceeds some positive threshold; they avoid the repugnant conclusion only by courting unpalatable verdicts about adding lives that fall just short of the threshold. What strikes me is that all sides seem to accept the same fixed stock of case judgments — that the repugnant conclusion should be resisted, that adding lives that are worth living and harm no one need not make a world worse, that a thoroughly miserable population cannot be better than a flourishing one — and yet a formal impossibility result shows that a familiar list of such adequacy conditions cannot be jointly satisfied. The field nevertheless proceeds as if its task were to find the one theory that honors all of them. My question is structural rather than adjudicative. Is this a substantive moral disagreement about which case judgments to abandon, a verbal disagreement about what ranking populations by betterness even means, a disagreement about where to set the zero level of a life worth living, or an artifact of imposing jointly inconsistent conditions on a single ranking? My own suspicion, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is partly caused by conflating several senses in which one population can be better than another, but I want that suspicion examined rather than confirmed. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: isolating the adequacy condition that is doing the work, a non-arbitrary account of the zero level, reframing the question around pairwise choices or policies rather than whole worlds, or accepting that the original question has no well-formed answer and should be replaced?
