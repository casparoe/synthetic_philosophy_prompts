After retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I find I want explicit reconstructions of what a practice assumes before it can be called justified. I have been working through John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, and a passage in Chapter IV, "Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual," keeps coming to mind when I read about deplatforming disputes. Here is the passage:

"The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law. As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it, and the question whether the general welfare will or will not be promoted by interfering with it, becomes open to discussion."

The contemporary practice I have in mind is this: a university rescinds a speaking invitation, an online platform removes an account, or an employer dismisses someone after a public campaign against the person's speech, and the institution involved defends the move as protecting the interests of its members — their safety, dignity, or inclusion — not as suppressing dissent. What does that defense presuppose? In particular, does it treat social sanction as Mill's "punishment by opinion," and does Mill's test actually license it? Or does it presuppose a different view of what social penalties are for — for instance, that they express communal condemnation or maintain a norm, rather than prevent harm to the interests of others? And where does the passage leave the matter: would it classify deplatforming as a legitimate exercise of society's jurisdiction over conduct that affects the interests of others, as a social penalty it would reject because it exceeds what those interests warrant, or as something on which Mill's principle is genuinely undecided?
