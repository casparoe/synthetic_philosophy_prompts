I have a rule I use whenever I need a mathematical result I didn't prove myself, and I'd like to know whether it's defensible. I rank evidence as follows. If a theorem has been formalized in a proof assistant such as Lean, Coq, or Isabelle, and the proof script typechecks, I treat the result as settled — even when the underlying proof term is far too large for me ever to read. If a theorem has only a human proof in a reputable venue, I treat it as true but with a small, live error bar: I hedge anything I build on it, and my confidence drops as the proof gets longer. This puts me at odds with a mathematician I work with. He is comfortable citing the four-colour theorem on the strength of the mathematical community's longstanding verdict, even though the classical proof rests on a computer case-analysis far too large for any one person to survey. Yet he thinks my rule is naive: a proof assistant is itself a program, programs have bugs, and the larger risk is not a gap in the proof but a gap between the formalized statement I reviewed and the theorem I actually intended to use.

I adopted the rule because I have watched careful code review miss concurrency bugs and off-by-one errors too often to believe that paragraph-by-paragraph human proof review is more reliable than a typechecker. But I cannot tell whether I have found a sound division of cognitive labor or simply replaced the fallible humans I distrust with a different set of fallible humans, one layer down.

Is this ranking of evidence defensible? Under what assumptions about the error rates of proof checkers, human referees, and my own reading would a machine check rightly outrank a human proof? And is there a version of the rule that keeps the reliability of running a proof while taking seriously the gap between the formal statement and the theorem I think I am using?
