I am giving a department colloquium next term defending the thesis that public argumentation would be epistemically improved if it adopted selected institutional features of competitive sport — codified competitive formats, formally trained and certified judges, public performance rankings, and systematic coaching with post hoc review — rather than remaining the informal, mostly unranked practice it now is. My argument in brief: (i) competitive sport produces measurable improvement in skill because it creates repeated, externally judged, scored performance with structured feedback; (ii) argumentation outside formal debating largely lacks that feedback loop, so recurring patterns of bad reasoning go uncorrected; (iii) the familiar vices of competitive debate, such as point-scoring and audience-directed sophistry, are defects of particular formats and incentive structures, not of adversarial scoring as such; and (iv) the beneficial design features can therefore be borrowed while deliberately declining the vices. The audience is my own department: mostly analytic epistemologists, including several working in social epistemology on disagreement and group inquiry, one political philosopher who works on deliberative democracy, and a historian of rhetoric. They are not debate coaches, and I expect them to be suspicious both of the competition metaphor and of the claim that scoring can be aligned with truth-seeking.
