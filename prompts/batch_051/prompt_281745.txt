I am writing a novel set in a volcanological observatory, and one of the central characters, Dr Lena Sorg, has spent thirty years building models of a restless caldera. I want her intellectual temperament to be exact, so I have drafted a series of episodes in which she must choose between competing models that fit the available data equally well. Four of those decisions are now settled. Early in her career, she chose a model that unified the deep tremor with the caldera's slow inflation by positing a compressible reservoir whose existence predicted a shift in the gas plume, later observed, over a mathematically tidier model with fewer fitted constants that dismissed the tremor as sensor noise. A decade later, she again chose the bolder account: a single two-layer magma chamber that unified the isotope anomaly with the caldera's inflation cycle and predicted a ground-tilt signal subsequently confirmed, over two independent mechanisms that fitted the same data but offered no novel testable consequence. She later defended a model with an exotic rheology that unified the caldera's behaviour with a regional deformation pattern and predicted a slow wave that was afterwards found, against an orthodox model that left the regional signal unexplained and made no new predictions. Most recently, she chose a model that unified the post-eruption gas chemistry with the subsidence curve and correctly foretold a specific tremor burst, over a rival that modelled the two signals separately and more simply.

She now confronts a fifth choice. Two models remain. The first is a single master mechanism that unifies all four earlier phenomena with fewer free constants than any rival has required; its one distinctive consequence, however, lies just below the resolution of present instruments and may never become testable. The second model leaves the four phenomena causally separate, is unlovely and heavily parameterised, but has just passed a risky novel test, having predicted a subtle seismic signal that was later observed.

I need to know which of the two she would choose if she is to remain consistent with her four earlier decisions, and why. Specifically: identify the rule or rules of theory choice that fit those four settled decisions; state what each live candidate rule would predict for this fifth case; give your best prediction of her choice, with the reasons for favouring that rule over its rivals; and describe one further case, distinct from the fifth, that would force the leading candidate rules apart if their verdicts on the fifth case were somehow inconclusive. Please answer in British English.
