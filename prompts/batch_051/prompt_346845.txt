For several months my political-psychology reading group has been stuck on the same question: once you accept the moral foundations findings about how self-described liberals and conservatives differ in the moral intuitions they rely on, does any of that bear on who is right politically, or is it merely a description of the disagreement? The empirical pattern is no longer disputed among us. People on the left lean heavily on harm and fairness; people on the right draw on those as well but weight loyalty, authority, and sanctity much more heavily. The standard gloss is that the left relies mainly on the individualizing foundations while the right draws on the binding foundations too (liberty sometimes appears as a sixth foundation, but it cuts across the two camps in a more complicated way). What we dispute is whether this asymmetry has any normative or epistemic upshot.

One camp says it has none. For them, the fact that conservative moral judgment is driven more by loyalty, authority, and sanctity is a fact about the causes of belief, and no collection of such facts licenses an inference about what actually matters. Moving from a claim about which intuitions a group's psychology treats as moral to a claim about which considerations really are moral is a psychologized version of the naturalistic fallacy. On this view, the literature can explain why left and right talk past each other, but it cannot enter an argument about who is right.

The other camp says that is too quick. They grant that the findings do not directly establish that the binding foundations track anything genuine. But they hold that the findings do real dialectical work: a familiar style of left-leaning argument implicitly assumes that harm and fairness exhaust the moral domain, and the moral foundations evidence shows that this is exactly what is at issue. To treat loyalty, authority, and sanctity intuitions as noise or contamination rather than as data, one needs an independent argument for why the considered moral judgments of a substantial share of the population get no justificatory weight — and that argument is never supplied. Read this way, the literature functions like a debunking-or-vindicating device whose upshot depends on how much weight intuitions are permitted to carry in the first place.

Everyone is reasoning from the same studies, and no one has moved. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on whether the left or the right is correct. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is largely a collision of two projects sharing one word: one side is doing descriptive moral psychology, the other is doing moral epistemology, and moral foundation does double duty as mechanism-that-produces-judgment and consideration-with-justificatory-weight. Is that diagnosis right, or is the stalemate hiding a substantive question — for example, about the justificatory weight of other people's moral intuitions, or about the epistemic impropriety of leaning only on intuitions one's own subculture shares? Is there also an empirical question in the background, such as whether binding-foundation intuitions are stable under reflection or applied only parochially, whose answer would move the substantive question?
