I have been thinking about a strange asymmetry in the way we treat animals, and I cannot find a principle that clearly accounts for it. Most people would be horrified at the idea of a dog being operated on without anaesthetic, and in many countries that would be a criminal offence. Yet the same people will drop a live lobster into boiling water, swat a wasp, or have an insect infestation dealt with by poison, and think little of it. The lobster has a nervous system, shows strong escape and avoidance responses, and takes some time to die in the pot; the wasp writhes when crushed. So the asymmetry is not between creatures that respond and creatures that do not. It is between kinds of animals we are willing to count as conscious sufferers and kinds we treat as almost mechanical.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry. The answer I usually give myself is that dogs and pigs are more complex, or more obviously conscious, than lobsters and insects, and that a line has to be drawn somewhere. But the line as ordinary practice draws it looks suspiciously convenient, and I suspect it may track how much an animal's face and behaviour happen to resemble ours rather than anything about its actual capacity to suffer. If the justification is complexity, I would like to know which specific feature of complexity does the moral work, and whether it really places the line where our practices place it, or only roughly where our sympathies already fall.

Please set out the strongest possible justification for the asymmetry between the way we treat, say, a dog and the way we treat a lobster or an insect, and then test that justification honestly: does it survive cases that fall just on either side of whatever threshold you propose? If the asymmetry cannot be justified, say so, and say what a more consistent practice would look like. Please write your answer in British English.
