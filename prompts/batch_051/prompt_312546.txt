For my high school philosophy club I am trying to reconstruct the implicit rule a friend of mine uses when she decides whether something is funny. Over several months she has given these verdicts. She found the following funny: a cartoon in which a character steps on a rake and the handle smacks him in the face, though he is perfectly fine afterward; a clever pun that turns on a real double meaning; an absurdist one-liner told in a perfectly calm study hall, whose images are surprising but involve no target and no taboo subject; and a long joke whose punchline requires reparsing the entire setup as a different grammatical structure. She did not find the following funny: a classmate who, as he does at least once a week, misread an ordinary sentence aloud and then sat down in embarrassed silence; and a joke whose only content was a predictable, mean-spirited stereotype. Next week I plan to show her a video of a baby being tickled by his father, shrieking with laughter and reaching out for more. I want you to predict her verdict on that video. In particular, say which theories of humor are still consistent with her record—superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation, or some other rule you can articulate—what each surviving theory predicts about the tickling video, which theory you think she is actually running and why, which earlier case is the closest analog, and which further case would best settle which rule she is using. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each item a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
