I'm a sociology PhD student, and I sit on my university's interdisciplinary social-science seed-funding committee. I'm suposed to circulate a decision memo before our Thursday meeting, and I'd like to use a draft from you as the basis.

The proposal we're stuck on is a cross-national study of the "gender-equality paradox": the finding that in countries that score higher on gender-egalitarian norms and policies, women are more likely to choose fields like nursing and teaching and less likely to go into STEM than women in less egalitarian countries. The team wants to combine administrative data on university enrollments with new survey measures of personal values, interests, and perceived discrimination to test competing explanations. Methodologically it's solid, pre-registered, with big samples, so this isn't a quality-control vote.

The committee is split. Half says we should approve it: the paradox is exactly the kind of counterintuitive, robust finding social science should study, and blocking research because some people dislike its possible implications is motivated censorship of descriptive science. The other half says we should reject it or attach conditions: results like this are catnip for people who want to argue the STEM gap is self-chosen and innate, and our committee shouldn't subsidize a study whose main practical effect is to give a veneer of rigor to that story. One member floated approving only if the team commits to a companion paper on policy implications and includes a "uses and misuses" appendix in the report; some think that's a reasonable compromise, others think it turns scientists into PR consultants.

Draft me the memo. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions; give reasons our committee could actually defend; engage the strongest consideration on the loosing side rather than strawmanning it; and be something I could plausibly circulate as-is.
