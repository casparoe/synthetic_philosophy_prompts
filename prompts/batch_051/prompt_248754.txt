For the past several years I have been defending a position I call the lien model of collective guilt, and I would like an honest assessment of it. The central claim is that the guilt of a state is best understood on the model of an encumbrance that runs with property, not as a property of individual persons. A lien attaches to a parcel of land: a later purchaser acquires the land already burdened, even though she did not create the debt and is not at fault for it. I hold that something analogous is true of citizenship. Citizens are not guilty in the criminal or moral sense for wrongs their state committed before they were born, but the state they now vote in, hold office in, and claim the protection of is encumbered by those wrongs, and anyone who takes up the state's powers takes up the encumbrance along with them. As a result, a third-generation citizen of a state with a genocidal past can owe reparations, or owe support for an official apology, without being blameworthy, just as a purchaser can owe a debt she did not incur. On my view, refusing to acknowledge the encumbrance is a genuine wrong, but it is more like trying to convey property free of a known lien than like confessing to a crime.

I am aware the view sounds close to several existing positions: Jaspers's category of political guilt, meaning the liability citizens bear for what their state does in their name; Arendt's distinction between individual guilt and collective responsibility; and beneficiary-based arguments for reparations, which hold that those who benefit from an injustice can owe repair even though they did not cause it. But I think the lien model adds something those positions lack. It locates the normative burden in the institution rather than in the psychology, causal biography, or benefit of the individual citizen, and it explains why the obligation persists even for citizens who sincerely wish the wrongs had never occurred and who would return every benefit if they could.

I would like you to do three things. First, reconstruct the view in its strongest form, correcting ambiguities and overstatements. Second, say whether it is genuinely distinct from the three positions I mentioned or whether it collapses into one of them under another name. Third, give the three strongest objections to the view and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Please be candid rather than encouraging, and write in American English.
