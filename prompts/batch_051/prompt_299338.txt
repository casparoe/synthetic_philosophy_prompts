I've been rereading Darwin's *Descent of Man* for a little discussion group on cultural evolution, and there's a passage early in the chapter comparing mental powers where Darwin says the formation of different languages and of distinct species are curiously parallel, both developed through a gradual process. He even suggests that words and grammatical forms have been preserved through something like a struggle for existence, with the better or easier forms surviving and others dropping out. I'd always thought the memetic analogy started with Dawkins, so it's strange to see Darwin already gesturing at something like it.

Anyway, our group is now stuck on a conceptual issue. If we take Darwins language analogy seriously, what exactly is the thing that gets selected in cultural evolution? I keep going back and forth between three candidate units: the publicly observable practice or utterance (a song performance, a handshake, a particular way of tying a knot), the mental representation or rule inside a person's head that produces that behavior, and an abstract pattern or type (the song as a repeatable pattern, independent of any particular performance or brain). The group can't even agree on whether it matters.

So my question is: could you explain, in a fairly careful way, what difference it makes for a Darwinian theory of culture to locate the replicator at each of those levels? In particular, which level has to have offspring resembling parents closely enough for cumulative change to get going, and what would count as the selection pressures at each level? Also, is language a special test case here, since Darwin seems to treat words themselves as the evolving individuals, but words don't have minds or bodies? I'm not after a reading list, just a clearer conceptual picture of what's at stake.
