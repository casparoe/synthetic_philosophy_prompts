I run a Kierkegaard seminar for undergrads — 18-ish students, one 90-minute block. Next week we start on his attack on the daily press and the public, and I want to open with an activity rather than a lecture, so they meet the phenomenon in what they do before we read him. The thing I want them to feel is: how a room full of actual, concrete people can turn into an anonymous "public" that seems to have opinions but is no one in particular; how commenting and comparing replace committing and acting; and how that flattens real qualitative differences between people. For Kierkegaard this flattening is the levelling the daily press runs on, and the counter-move is becoming what he calls a single individual — someone who exists in his or her own action instead of hiding out in the crowd. Design the whole thing: roles, rules, materials, a 90-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the public / levelling / single-individual triad. The labels should not come out until the debrief, and there should be no warm-up lecture. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the view a classroom can't reproduce and I'll have to supply afterwards. If there is a way to rig it so that one student ends up the only person forced to actually do something while everyone else opines about it, even better.
