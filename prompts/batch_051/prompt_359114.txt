I'm writing an essay on whether 'religion' names a single coherent kind of thing, and I want to use Yann Martel's Life of Pi as a test case that puts pressure on the usual definitions. In the novel Pi practices Hinduism, Christianity, and Islam at once, despite the objections of his parents and the local religious leaders, and the closing interview offers two tellings of his ordeal at sea — one involving animals and one without — then asks which is the better story. Read the novel through the dispute between essentialist accounts of religion and family-resemblance or functionalist accounts. Does Pi's pluralism assume that the different traditions share a common core or converge on a single aim, or does it suggest that what holds the category together is mainly the use to which such stories are put? Where does the novel support a functionalist reading of religion as a set of better stories that make suffering bearable, and where does it resist being reduced to that? I'd like the reading to let the novel complicate the category itself, rather than simply illustrate one side, and to end by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate.
