I'm a software engineer, so my route into philosophy is usually through things that look like system design. Lately I've been chewing on Dennett's ladder of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — because it reads like a description of four increasingly elaborate generate-and-test loops. As I understand it: Darwinian creatures only get to test designs through natural selection; Skinnerian ones add online trial-and-error learning; Popperian ones keep an internal model and run cheap simulations so their hypotheses die in the simulator rather than in the real world; and Gregorian ones add language and cultural tools for building sharper internal models. I want a thought experiment built to a spec, because I'm using it in a talk for other engineers.

Build me a one-paragraph scenario in which a Skinnerian creature reliably outperforms a Popperian creature on a task that, on the surface, looks like it should reward foresight and planning. Constraints: the setting should be ordinary and near-future realistic — a trading desk, a warehouse robot, a live-service game economy, something along those lines — not a rigged world that's deliberately out to punish thinking. The Popperian creature's weak performance must not come from it holding an obviously wrong model or a daft hypothesis; it should come from the costs of building, updating, or trusting an internal model, where building such a model is still a trade-off a reasonable designer would make in general. No perfect predictors, no exotic setup, and the payoffs should be the kind a normal person or company would care about.

Then do the checks. Say what exactly the Skinnerian creature does in this environment and why it wins; what exactly the Popperian creature does and where it's model-based loop loses; which single feature of the case is doing the real work; what happens if you remove that feature; and how a defender of the Popperian design would attack the case without simply denying the details. Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1 is the scenario; items 2–5 are the checks in the order I listed.
