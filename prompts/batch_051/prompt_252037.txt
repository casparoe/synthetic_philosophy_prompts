I got into an argument with a friend about love that I can't shake, and I'd like someone to actually work through it rather than just pick a side. We noticed that people apply very different standards to parental love and romantic love. A parent who loves their kid no matter what — even when the kid grows up to be selfish, dull, or cruel — is treated as a good parent, and a parent whose love visibly tracked the kid's qualities would seem almost monstrous. But in romance, if someone says 'I don't love you for any reason, I just do,' we find that hollow or even insulting: surely they should be able to say what they love about you. Yet if they can produce a sharp list — your sense of humor, your looks, your drive — we get suspicious in the other direction: they love a bundle of traits, and would trade you in for someone with more of the same traits. So romantic love is asked to be both responsive to reasons and not responsive to reasons at the same time, while parental love gets excused from the demand for reasons entirely.

I've since read around a bit, and some people distinguish between love as appraisal — a response to value that's already there — and love as bestowal, where the loving itself creates a value the person didn't otherwise have, at least for you. The two ordinary judgments seems to flip between these pictures depending on context, and I can't tell whether the flip is justified or just convenient. So: is there one coherent account that handles both the parent case and the romantic case, or is one of our ordinary judgments simply wrong? And in the romantic case, can you hold both that love ought to be answerable to reasons and that loving someone for a list of qualities isn't really loving them? If you had to give one of those up, which would you drop, and what would that do to the other cases?
