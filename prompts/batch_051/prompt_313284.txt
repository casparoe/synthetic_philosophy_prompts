In discussions at the intersection of machine learning and evolutionary biology, the analogy between natural selection and gradient descent is often leaned on and made to do real work: fitness plays the role of an objective (or of a loss with its sign reversed), mutation and recombination play the role of stochastic weight updates, a population plays the role of a batch of candidate models, and both processes are said to hill-climb on a high-dimensional landscape. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that evolution is an optimizer—effective but blind, greedy, and badly conditioned—and, on some accounts, to license the further conclusion that intuitions and formal results can be transferred in either direction. Audit this analogy. Give the correspondences explicitly, then separate the disanalogies that matter for those intended conclusions from the ones that do not. Attend in particular to what the gradient-descent side quietly imports that the selection side may lack: a fixed and explicit objective; a computed direction of steepest descent rather than sampling of nearby variants; a training/test split; and a loss landscape that stays put during optimization. Then say which of the licensed conclusions survive the disanalogies, which collapse, and whether a better analogy could do the same work with less distortion.
