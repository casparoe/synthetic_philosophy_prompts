I'm trying to figure out what makes a generic statement like "ducks lay eggs" true, and I've collected my gut reactions on a bunch of cases. Act as a philosopher of language who works on generics. Start by brainstorming candidate criteria — statistical majority, high prevalence, what's normal or characteristic for the kind, what the kind is "for", what's striking or dangerous, and so on. Then take my cases one at a time and say which candidates survive and which die. At the end, state the best principle (or small set of principles) precisely, including which clause is doing which work; tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed; and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I want the reasoning, not references, and I'd rather have a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a gerrymandered one that saves them all.

My verdicts:
"Ducks lay eggs" — true, obviously, even though only a small minority of ducks are laying eggs at any given time, since drakes, ducklings, and out-of-season hens don't.
"Mosquitoes carry malaria" — true, even though the vast majority of individual mosquitoes never carry the parasite.
"Lions have manes" — true, even though only adult males do.
"Ducks are female" — false, even though roughly half of ducks are female. That already bothers me, because more ducks are female than are laying eggs at any moment, so prevalence can't be what's moving me.
"Lions are male" — false, and it feels like "lions have manes" and "lions are male" should land the same way but they don't, and I can't say why.
"Turtles are long-lived" — true, even though most turtles die young. It says something about the kind's design or potential, not about the average individual turtle.
"Dinosaurs are extinct" — true, but "extinct" isn't a property any individual dinosaur has, so this must be a different kind of case.
"Sharks attack bathers" — I want to say true, because it's exactly the sort of thing we warn people about, but it's extremely rare and most sharks never encounter a bather, so I can't defend the verdict.

I keep going back and forth on "humans are right-handed": about 90% of people are, which is a much bigger share than the share of ducks that lay eggs, but it feels like a statistic about the population rather than a fact about the kind. Should my principle count it true or false?

My working hunch is that a generic is true when the property is characteristic of the kind — something the kind does, or is for, or that belongs to its normal life. But that can't be the whole story: "mosquitoes carry malaria" isn't characteristic of mosquitoes in any sense that flatters them, and "turtles are long-lived" is a capacity most individual turtles never realize. So repair the hunch or replace it. And tell me straight: is there a principle that gets all of my verdicts without bolting on an ad hoc "dangerous to humans" clause? If not, which verdict should go first?

Also apply it: what do you get for "mosquitoes carry dengue," "dogs have four legs," and one other case of your choosing?
