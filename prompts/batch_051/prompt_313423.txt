I am enrolled in a philosophy of medicine course this term, and our unit on the concept of disease has left my study group going in circles. The debate is between naturalism and normativism. On the naturalist picture, diseases are value-free departures from species-typical biological functioning: a part or process is diseased when it fails to perform its function in the statistically normal way. On the normativist picture, no purely biological criterion can mark off disease from health, because calling a condition a disease is ineliminably evaluative — it says the condition is bad for the person and ought to be treated or prevented. We got stuck on two examples. The first is chronic, symptomless hypertension: someone can feel perfectly fine, yet we consider them diseased because their blood pressure is far above the statistical norm and raises the risk of stroke. The second is tooth decay: in many populations it is extremely common, and it involves clear harm, but a simple statistical account of "normal functioning" seems to have trouble explaining why a condition that most people have should count as a departure from health.

Please write a dialogue between two students, one defending naturalism and one defending normativism, in which they work through these examples and try to show that their own account handles the hard cases better. I would like both speakers to be equally competent, and each should respond to the specifics of what the other has actually said rather than restating a canned position. Most importantly, commit: each character should take a definite stand on what disease is and on how the examples come out, and give their reasons, instead of presenting the options or softening into "both sides have a point." If the dialogue ends with their disagreement sharper and better defined rather than resolved, that is exactly what I want.
