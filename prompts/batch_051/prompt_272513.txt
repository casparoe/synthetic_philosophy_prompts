I am a retired geochemist who has recently turned to philosophy, and I have been working through John Dewey's *Democracy and Education* (1916) on my own. In Chapter XI, "Experience and Thinking," Dewey writes: "Experience as trying involves change, but change is meaningless transition unless it is consciously connected with the return wave of consequences which flow from it. When an activity is continued into the undergoing of consequences, when the change made by action is reflected back into a change made in us, the mere flux is loaded with significance. We learn something." I follow the surface of this, but I suspect I am missing what it commits him to. As a scientist, I instinctively want to translate the "return wave of consequences" into something like a feedback loop, and then the passage seems almost trivially true; but Dewey plainly thinks it is doing heavier philosophical work, since it underlies his view that genuinely educative experience is experimental rather than merely passive or customary. As a professional philosopher working on American pragmatism, please help me with this. First expound the passage closely, laying out each of its conceptual pieces and what depends on what; then test whether the claim that this "return wave" is what turns mere flux into significance is defensible or whether it overreaches; and finally explain how this account of experience feeds Dewey's broader experimentalism about inquiry and, eventually, about democratic social life. Write in American English, and give me continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were writing me a considered letter. Before composing your answer, take time to think through the passage sentence by sentence.
