I've been trying to figure out what actually makes a statement racist, and my gut reactions to a set of cases are pulling me toward different principles, so I'd like someone to work through them with me and tell me what, if anything, ties my verdicts together.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A white stand-up comedian uses a racial slur on stage while mocking a bigot; the bit is plainly anti-racist, and the audience reads it that way. I don't think the statement is racist. (2) A white reporter reads aloud in court from an exhibit, repeating the same slur exactly as it appeared in a defendant's message. I don't think that is racist either. (3) A white man angry at his Black neighbor over a property dispute calls him that slur. Clearly racist. (4) A politician campaigns on "restoring law and order in our cities," never mentioning race, but everyone in the room knows which images the phrase is meant to summon. I think it's racist even though the words are bland on their face. (5) My elderly aunt says of a Black family moving in next door that "those people are just different from us." She means no harm and would be hurt to be called racist, but I think the remark is racist. (6) A white friend tells a dinner-party joke that turns on a lazy stereotype about a racial group; no member of that group is present and nobody is offended, but I still think the telling is racist. (7) A Black friend uses the slur in easy conversation with other Black friends. I don't think that's racist. (8) A white teenager at a party raps along to a song and sings the slur because it's in the lyrics. I keep going back and forth on that one.

My tentative principle is something like: a statement is racist when what it conveys in context, to a reasonable hearer, given tone, history, and who is speaking, marks a group as inferior, dangerous, or not fully one of us — regardless of the speaker's private intentions. But that principle strains against (6), where the message depends heavily on who is listening, and it makes me wonder whether I can keep both (4) and (6) while also keeping (1) and (2), where private intent and surface words seem to point in opposite directions.

Is there a single principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures most of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon or qualify.
