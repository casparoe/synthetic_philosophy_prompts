In a course I'm taking on ritual and social explanation, we've been looking at the recurring family of rites in which a community's guilt or blame is placed on some substitute — an animal, an object, a person — and the substitute is then destroyed, expelled, or sent away: the biblical goat sent into the wilderness, the Athenian pharmakos driven out before a festival, and various New Year customs in which a village's accumulated wrongs are written on an effigy or vessel and burned or set adrift. A classmate has been arguing that these rituals are not merely superstition or theater. Her claim is that something is actually transferred and discharged in them: by concentrating collective guilt in a substitute and eliminating it, the community really lowers the amount of resentment, blame, and punitive energy circulating among its members, and this real effect is part of why the pattern recurs.

I don't want to dismiss her claim as unfalsifiable, but I also don't know how to get empirical traction on it. What would be the strongest test of the claim that scapegoat-like rituals genuinely transfer, discharge, or redirect guilt or blame? Design the best experiment, field study, or cross-community comparison you can, and specify exactly what result would count in favor of the claim, what would count against it, and what the test could not settle even in principle. I'm especially stuck on how to separate the strong hypothesis that guilt or blame is actually relocated onto the substitute from the weaker hypothesis that participants merely feel, and publicly present, it as relocated; I don't see what behavioral measurement could distinguish those. Be as concrete as possible about how you would operationalize the central notions, and be explicit about the residue no test can reach.
