I am a doctoral student working on the epistemology of machine learning, and I recently read a popular science piece that explains the philosophical significance of the no-free-lunch theorems as follows:

'Wolpert and Macready's no-free-lunch theorems show that, averaged over all possible tasks, every learning algorithm performs exactly as well as every other; any advantage an algorithm enjoys on some tasks must be paid for by an equal disadvantage on others. There is therefore no principled reason to prefer one algorithm or model to another, even for a specific, well-defined problem. The result is a formal vindication of Hume's problem of induction: just as Hume argued, no amount of past data can justify any prediction about the future, since for any hypothesis that fits the data there is another that fits it equally well but predicts the opposite.'

I suspect the passage is broadly competent but that at least one step is too quick, and I do not want to repeat its claims in my own work without knowing where the reasoning gives way. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a learning theorist would accept, which they would qualify, and which they would reject, and then say whether the final claim about Hume follows from the mathematical result?
