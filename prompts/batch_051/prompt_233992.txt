I have been trying to get clear on the old distinction between vicious and benign infinite regresses, and I keep coming back to a parallel that may be either illuminating or idle. The standard characterization seems to be that a regress is vicious when each item in the chain owes its status to the next in a way that never discharges the debt: the first item is supposed to be secured by the second, which is secured by the third, and without a final non-dependent term nothing is actually secured. The familiar cases are justification regresses, where a belief's justification keeps deferring to the justification of the justifying belief, and the regress that appears when a rule of inference is treated as just another premise that must itself be applied. By contrast, the regress produced by iterating a truth predicate — p, that p is true, that it is true that p is true, and so on — is usually classed as benign, because the first item is already fully in place and the rest of the chain seems to trail along for free.

What struck me is that this looks a great deal like the distinction in programming language semantics between recursion that must be well-founded to define anything and corecursion that may be infinite provided it is productive. On one side, a recursive equation with no independent base case fails to compute anything; it is bad circularity. On the other side, a lazy stream defined by saying that its first element is zero and each later element is the previous element plus one is infinite and entirely legitimate, because every finite prefix can be produced in finite time. The usual test on the computational side is productivity or guardedness: each step must yield a determinate finite piece before deferring to the rest. My question is whether the philosophical distinction simply is this one. Is a vicious regress just a non-well-founded recursive definition that is not productive, while a benign regress is a productive corecursive definition? Or is the mapping doing real work only in some cases and merely pattern-matching on the word infinite in others? If the parallel were deep, I would expect criteria like productivity, guardedness, or the existence of a well-founded order to transfer to philosophical cases and to say something precise about when an infinite chain of reasons, truth-ascriptions, or causes is acceptable. But the truth-predicate regress is classed as benign even though it is not obviously productive in the computational sense, and I cannot tell whether that is evidence against the parallel or evidence that philosophers have been using too coarse a notion of benignity. What exactly would have to be true on the philosophy side for the computational results to transfer, and where does the analogy most clearly come apart?
