I am a professor of moral philosophy, and I want you to draft a short agenda-setting memo of about 900 words. The intended reader is the programme director of a private foundation that will fund one three-year postdoctoral project on universalizability and its legacy: the idea, running from Kant through R. M. Hare and Singer, that a moral judgment, maxim, or principle is defective unless the agent could consistently will, prescribe, or accept it for all relevantly similar cases. The foundation is not interested in Kant exegesis, and it will not fund empirical or historical work. It wants a conceptual project: something a philosopher can make real progress on by argument alone. Do not include a bibliography or propose a reading list; the director cares about the shape of the problem, not the state of the literature.

As you choose the candidate questions, keep in mind at least these live tensions: the dependence of universalization tests on a choice of act-description; the difference between asking what would happen if everyone did something and asking what would happen if everyone were permitted to do it; threshold and tipping-point cases in which no single act changes the outcome; and whether universalizability is merely a formal consistency test or can yield substantive moral conclusions.

Please structure the memo in exactly the following four sections:

1. Framing and criteria (roughly 150 words). State, in order of importance, the criteria you are using to rank open questions — for example, how many other questions depend on a given question, tractability with purely conceptual tools, neglectedness, and how much an answer would change downstream work — and justify that weighting in one or two sentences each.

2. Candidate questions (roughly 200 words). Present four to six genuinely open questions in this area, each in one or two sentences. They should be specific enough that a specialist would recognize the problem, and they should collectively cover the logical form of universalizability, its moral substance, and its application to collective-action cases. Do not answer them here.

3. Ranking and defence (roughly 400 words). Order the candidate questions from most to least worth the three years. For each, explain why it sits where it does: whether it is upstream of other questions, whether it is likely to be tractable by argument alone, whether the literature has left it underworked relative to its importance, and what a settled answer would actually change.

4. What would move the ranking (roughly 150 words). Say what developments over the next two or three years — a new argument, a decisive counterexample, or a result from a neighbouring field such as formal epistemology or decision theory — would most plausibly change your ordering, and which question you would promote or demote as a result.
