Last night my friend and I got in a long argument about Roko's basilisk, and I came away convinced the problem is that people (us included) never actually feel the pull of the acausal move — we just rule it out on principle and then talk past each other. Quick version for anyone who hasn't run into it: the idea is that a future superintelligence that wants to exist could precommit to punishing people who, after learning about it, didn't help bring it about. The tricky part is that the threat isn't causal — your action now can't reach the future AI — but the argument says a decision theory that cares about the right kind of logical correlation should still treat the threat as live, so even knowing about it is supposed to put you in a bind. My friend keeps calling it Pascal's mugging with better special effects; I think that's exactly the cheap dismissal I want to test.

So I want to stage a low-stakes version instead of arguing about it in the abstract. I run a university philosophy club, about 15 undergrads, 90 minutes, mostly people who've had one course in decision theory or formal epistemology but nothing fancier. Design a game or role-play that makes participants feel the force of basilisk-style reasoning without ever mentioning Roko's basilisk by name (I don't want the people who've heard of it to pattern-match, and I don't want to creep anyone out). I want them to come away feeling both why "I'll just ignore this because the future agent doesn't exist yet" is harder to say than it looks, and exactly where the reasoning loses its force. The idea has to show up in what happens, not in a lecture afterwards. Give me rules, materials, roles if any, a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, and a 90-minute timeline. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the pieces of the argument: the precommitment, the acausal threat, the incentive to actually follow through on punishing, and the place where the "this is just Pascal's mugging" objection should bite. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the classroom game cannot reproduce about the real basilisk argument, where I'll have to supply the missing peice myself. Also, I have a hunch the payoffs should be private rather than announced to the group — is that right, or am I overthinking it?
