I'm a PhD student in social philosophy and I'm partway through a chapter that has to say whether what we owe each other is fundamentally an attitude of acknowledgment, or just the right actions. The move I keep bumping into — in the recognition literature and in arguments with friends — is the invisibility picture. Recognition is being seen; misrecognition is being looked through, treated as if you weren't there. People use this to argue that actions alone can't be enough: you can hand someone every formal right and still look through them, so what we owe must include the attitude itself, not just it's behavioral output. I can't tell how much of that conclusion is actually being earned by the picture.

I want an audit of the analogy. Please also push on two pressure points, because they're the ones my committee will push on: seeing is largely passive and involuntary, whereas recognition is something we hold people to; and being seen is also the mechanism of hostile gazes, surveillance, and being sized up, so the visual side might quietly import a kind or neutral perceiver that the target situation doesn't include. And if there's a better analogy for what the recognition people are trying to get at, I'd like that too.
