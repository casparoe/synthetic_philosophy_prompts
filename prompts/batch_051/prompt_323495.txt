I'm a retired physical chemist — most of my career was statistical mechanics and stochastic simulations, so I thought I had a decent grip on probability. Since retiring I've gotten into philosophy of chance, and the thing I keep chewing on is the Principal Principle: roughly, if you know the objective chance of an outcome is x, your rational credence should be x, at least when the rest of your information is admissible. There's a case I cant get out of my head.

Suppose a coin is known to be fair, so each toss has chance 1/2 of coming up heads, and it's going to be tossed a million times tomorrow. The fair-coin chance theory assigns the all-heads outcome a probability of one in 2 to the millionth — tiny but positive. Now take a broadly Humean view of chance, where the chance facts are just a compact summary, a best system, of the whole pattern of outcomes. On that view the all-heads history is trouble: if all-heads actually happened, the best system would presumably not say the coin was fair — it would say it was heavily biased toward heads. So the very chance theory you're deferring to seems to assign positive probability to a course of events in which that theory is false.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about what to make of this. Renata thinks the Principal Principle needs no repair: once you know the chance of heads is 1/2, you should set your credence in all-heads to one in 2 to the millionth, and any information about what the chance theory would have been in the all-heads world is just inadmissible — using it is peeking at the outcome instead of respecting the chance. Samir thinks the principle has to be amended: a rational agent who is certain of the chance theory can't coherently assign positive credence to a history that would falsify that theory, so the right rule is to defer to the chance conditional on the chance theory itself, which in the all-heads case sends the credence to zero.

I want real engagement, not two talking points. Have them actually respond to each other, and make sure they get into at least these pressure points: whether Renata can give a principled account of inadmissible evidence that doesn't beg the question against Samir, whether Samir's amended rule only makes sense if you've already bought into the Humean picture of chance, what each of them would do when offered a bet on all-heads at astronomical odds, and whether Renata's position ends up treating chance as an unanalyzable primitive whose only job is to be deferred to. Don't let either one win too easily. And skip the reading list — I just want to watch them think.
