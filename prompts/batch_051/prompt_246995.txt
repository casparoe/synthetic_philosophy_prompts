I've been going back and forth on Endō's Silence (the Scorsese adaptation works too, but I'm thinking mainly of the novel) and whether it actually takes sides in the transcendence versus immanence debate. Rodrigues, a Portuguese Jesuit in seventeenth-century Japan, spends the whole story waiting for a God who will break in from outside: some sign, a rescue, an answer to the persecution. It doesn't come, or not in the way he expects. Then, when he is about to step on the carved image of Christ and apostatize, he hears Christ tell him to do it—that this is what the incarnation was for. I read that as the novel opting for an immanent God: the divine shows up in the act of betrayal, in weakness, in the trampled image, not as a transcendent power that saves from above. A friend of mine reads it the other way. She says the silence is what preserves transcendence, because a God who appeared whenever a suffering person demanded it would just be one more thing inside the world, and the voice is probably Rodrigues rationalizing.

Read the novel or film through that debate and tell me which reading is better supported. Where does the work push toward immanence, where does it pull back and insist on transcendence, and does the ending settle the argument or leave it open? I'd like a reading that is honest about the parts that resist each interpretation rather than turning the book into an illustration of one side. I'm not asking about Endō's biography or intentions, just what the work itself can support.
