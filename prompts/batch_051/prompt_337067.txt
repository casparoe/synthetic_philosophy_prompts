I am developing a continuing-education module on uncertainty for research scientists at a public-health and environmental agency — mostly epidemiologists, exposure modelers, and hydrologists. The point of the module is to get these scientists to state explicitly what they mean when they attach a number to an uncertainty, and to stop switching between incompatible meanings mid-analysis. I plan to present four interpretations of probability and need to decide how much weight each should carry. The four are: frequentism, on which a probability is a limiting relative frequency in a specified class of repeatable trials; subjectivism, on which it is an agent's degree of belief constrained only by coherence; propensity, on which it is an objective tendency of a chance setup to produce an outcome, applying even in a single trial; and logical-epistemic probability, on which it is the degree to which a body of evidence supports a proposition, as in logical or objective Bayesian accounts.

These are the situations in which my scientists actually report probabilities: (a) the probability that a parameter lies in a 95% interval after conditioning on data under a specified model; (b) the probability that a deterministic but chaotic flood model produces an overtopping event in the next thirty years; (c) the probability that a specified radionuclide decays within a stated time window; (d) the probability that a one-off historical claim is correct — say, that a particular disease cluster began with one contaminated shipment; (e) the probability that changing an analysis decision, such as how to adjust for a confounder, would reverse a study's conclusion; (f) a calibrated forecast probability of an extreme weather event next season, averaged across several models; (g) a laboratory measurement reported with a 95% uncertainty interval around the measured value; and (h) the probability that a particular past dam failure was caused by a design error rather than operator error.

For each of the four interpretations, rate on a 0 to 5 scale how naturally it accounts for each of these eight probabilities, where 5 means the interpretation gives an answer a practicing scientist could state without quietly borrowing from another interpretation, and 0 means it has nothing coherent to say or can only mimic another interpretation by force. Then produce an overall ranking of the four interpretations, with ties permitted only if explained. State what fraction of the module each interpretation should receive if the goal is to make these scientists recognize which interpretation they are implicitly using and to avoid mixing incompatible ones. Finally, identify the two or three situations that are most diagnostic — the cases where the choice of interpretation changes what a scientist should say or do, rather than merely changing the philosophical gloss — and explain why those cases do the most separating work.
