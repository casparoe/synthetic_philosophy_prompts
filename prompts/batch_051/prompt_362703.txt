I have spent the past several months on a team trying to specify how a large public AI assistant should handle questions about which citizens deeply disagree concerning the good life—whether it should answer substantively, defer to the user's tradition, refuse, or redirect. The framework I keep being pushed toward is the public-reason strand of liberal neutrality. On that view, political power is distinctively coercive, and its exercise is legitimate only when it can be justified by reasons that citizens can accept despite holding different comprehensive doctrines. The state therefore should not rank rival conceptions of the good; it should maintain a procedural and rights-protecting framework and remain neutral among visions of human flourishing, leaving each person free to pursue her own ends within it.

What unsettles me is that this argument was built for institutions that can often abstain. A legislature can leave a question undecided; a court can settle a procedural matter without endorsing a way of life; a constitution can remain silent on the good. A general-purpose assistant cannot abstain at that level, because it must act or answer constantly and at fine granularity. Refusing a contested question is itself a consequential move; answering it embeds a ranking of considerations; even advising someone simply to follow their community presupposes that deference to that community is the right procedure for the question. We are forced to write down an objective, and no objective looks neutral—it looks like a partly comprehensive doctrine about what matters, now given wide influence through daily advice.

I am interested in what happens to the argument, not in a policy recommendation. Does the public-reason argument for liberal neutrality survive this development? Which premise does it bear on, and is that premise true in a setting where the system gives pervasive but not coercive guidance? I can see three verdicts: the argument still goes through, so the assistant should be kept deliberately thin, treating only overlapping-consensus and procedural matters as legitimate content; the argument needs repair, because its picture of abstention never applied to omnipresent advice; or the argument is inapplicable, and the real problem is value aggregation under pluralism rather than neutrality. I want to know which premise the argument needed to be true, whether it remains true in this setting, and which of the three moves is most defensible. Start with a one-sentence bottom line and keep the whole answer under 600 words.
