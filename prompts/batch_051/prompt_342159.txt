I co-host a tiny podcast that's mostly read by rationalist/ACX types, and next episode I'm interviewing an evolutionary biologist who defends a Grafen-style version of the handicap principle as a general account of honest signaling, including human cases like luxury goods and conspicuous piety. Her whole line is that the rationalist-sphere habit of saying "signals are honest because they're costly" gets the formal result backwards: in the models the cost has to fall differentially on low-quality signalers, and in equilibrium a signal can be quite cheap for the high-quality ones. The waste isn't what does the work.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that actually push her rather than let her restate this position? I'd like at least one question each on:

(a) the handicap/index distinction, and why she files stuff like luxury consumption or religious display under handicaps instead of indices (where the signal is just physically tied to the quality and no strategic cost is needed);

(b) how she'd respond to the worry that many human "costly signals" are only kept honest because an audience punishes fakers, which looks like a different mechanism from the intrinsic cost in the formal models;

(c) the investement-vs-waste problem: once the signal is working, the cost is buying receivers useful information, so what exactly is being wasted, and what's left of the rationalist instinct to debunk expensive displays;

(d) what evidence could count against the handicap account of some concrete behavior.

And for each of those, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely give — not a generic "can you say more?"

Also, can you work in one non-sneering version of the self-referential point that taking a public anti-signaling stance is itself a signal, possibly a countersignal? I want her to actually engage with that rather than roll her eyes at it.
