I'm a novelist, two drafts into a fantasy book where oaths genuinely bind — break one and the thing you swore by comes for you, automatically, no god needed to enforce it. The problem is I keep changing my mind about what the binding force actually is, and its wrecking the magic system. I've collected five accounts and dont need any of them repeated:

(1) witness-and-punishment: the oath calls a god or an ancestor as witness, and binds because that being will punish perjury. It's third-party enforcement.

(2) the self-curse: an oath is a conditional curse on yourself, like 'may the Styx drown me if I lie,' and binds because you've set a curse going when you speak; the penalty is already in motion.

(3) the speech-act account: swearing is like saying 'I do' or 'I bet you five' — under the rules of an existing practice, the words themselves put you under the obligation. No curse metaphysics needed.

(4) the staking account: you swear by something precious — your name, your sword, the Styx — and that thing is collateral. Break the oath and you forfeit it. Binds because you put something you value on the line.

(5) the credibility account: an oath is a commitment device, a way to make your word trustworthy by raising the cost of breaking it. The binding is calculative, not sacred.

Give me a sixth account, genuinely different from those five — not a rewording, not a special case, not a mashup. What I'd really like is one that explains why the *gods* swear by the Styx and can't break it, since several of my five go flat for immortals: you can't take collateral from a god, and a reputational cost only bites if the god cares. So tell me what the binding force would be on your account, and whether it actually handles the Styx case better than mine. If you think it's no good, say so.
