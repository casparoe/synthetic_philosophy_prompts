I want to test a parallel I keep noticing between archaeological inference and latent-variable modelling, and to know whether it is structural, merely analogical, or actively misleading.

An archaeologist works backwards from a small, biased sample of durable remains—postholes, middens, potsherds, grave goods—to claims about things that left no direct trace: descent rules, status hierarchies, exchange obligations, cosmological beliefs. The inference has two unknowns at once: both the hidden institutions and beliefs, and the mapping from those hidden things to the material record—that is, how a given ritual, rank order or marriage rule would deposit itself in the ground. The same evidence is compatible with many pairs of the two.

That looks formally like the identifiability problem in factor analysis and unsupervised learning. The same observed covariance matrix can be generated by indefinitely many combinations of latent factors and loadings; the usual response is to impose a constraint—orthogonality, simple structure, a sign convention—that the data themselves do not choose. In archaeology, the analogous constraints would be interpretive rules of thumb such as 'ceremonial objects are deposited deliberately, domestic objects are discarded casually' or 'elite residences cluster around ceremonial centres.' In the statistical language these read as identifying restrictions that are already a theory of the culture being reconstructed.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. State the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the covariance matrix, to the latent factors, to the loadings, and to the identifying constraint. Then say what would have to be true on both sides for results like rotational indeterminacy, or the existence of many different models that fit the same data equally well, to transfer and to teach us anything about the hard limits of reconstructing past beliefs. And tell me which of the parallel's predictions turn out false. In particular, I cannot decide whether archaeology has a source of constraint that factor analysis lacks—namely, that the generating process was produced by intentional agents, so one can reason about what it would have been reasonable for them to do—or whether that is just the investigator's prior wearing a different hat. If it is only a prior, does it make the reconstruction of past beliefs more reliable than the statistical machinery suggests, because the priors encode genuine knowledge of human institutions, or less, because they are unacknowledged and insulated from testing?
