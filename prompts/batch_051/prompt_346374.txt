I am a programmer rather than an academic, but I have been thinking about a resemblance between two things that do not usually come up together, and I would like to know whether it is a real structural parallel or only a loose analogy.

Consider the prisoner's dilemma: two players would each do better if both cooperated, yet each has an incentive to defect whatever the other does. Suppose, though, that instead of simply choosing a move, each player must submit, in advance, a piece of computer code that returns "cooperate" or "defect" and that can also inspect the other player's code before returning an answer. One can then submit a program whose rule is roughly this: cooperate if and only if the other program, when run against mine, would cooperate; otherwise defect. Against such a program, defecting would yield at best the payoff of mutual defection, while cooperating would yield mutual cooperation. In that setup, cooperation seems to become a stable outcome without any outside party forcing the players to keep their word. I am describing this informally and do not know how the formal results are usually stated.

That mechanism reminds me of a legal contract. A contract also makes the parties' actions conditional on one another: I perform only if you perform, you perform only if I perform, and an external enforcer such as a court stands behind that conditionality. The program-based arrangement appears to get the same result, conditional cooperation as a stable equilibrium, while dispensing with any external enforcer. The court's role seems to have been replaced by transparency: each side's decision procedure is visible to the other and fixed in advance.

My question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Is a contract best understood as a special case of this program-based mechanism, with the court simply making the conditionality credible? Or does the analogy break in important ways, for instance because a real contract must also handle ambiguity, renegotiation, breach, and the enforcer's own incentives, all of which the program setup quietly assumes away? What would have to be true of two parties in the non-code world for a binding agreement to be nothing more than mutual inspection of decision procedures? And is there something the analogy predicts that turns out false, such as some feature a contract has that the program setup cannot reproduce, or some assumption of the program setup that contracts do not need?
