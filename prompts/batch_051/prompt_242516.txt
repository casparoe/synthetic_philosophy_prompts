I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the metaphysics of time. My home framework is the contemporary analytic debate: McTaggart's A-series and B-series, tensed versus tenseless theories, presentism, eternalism, the growing block, and the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism; I move comfortably in that vocabulary and can tell when a claim is being forced into it. I have recently been reading Dōgen's treatment of being-time (uji) in translation for a dissertation chapter, and I keep finding that my usual categories at once illuminate and deform it.

As I understand him, Dōgen holds that time is not a container or medium in which beings occur; that being and time are not two things; that each existent is itself a time-being; that the now does not pass or flow; that examples like firewood and ash present them as distinct time-beings rather than successive states of one persisting substance; and that practice and realization are themselves time rather than events located in time. I want a systematic translation of these claims into the analytic vocabulary listed above, claim by claim, as faithfully as possible, followed by a residue report on what does not survive the translation. My working suspicion is that the deepest resistance lies in a presupposition shared by A-theories and B-theories alike — that moments are items arrayed in a series — but I want that suspicion tested, not confirmed.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The first four items should each translate one strand of uji into the analytic vocabulary and mark where the fit is tight and where it is forced. The fifth item should be the residue report: which parts of the view have no expression in the target vocabulary, and whether that residue is better explained as a limitation of the analytic framework or as a sign that Dōgen's claims are doing something other than what the analytic debate is about. Do not smuggle the assumptions of the target vocabulary into the translation and call it fidelity; I want the failures of fit stated as sharply as the successes.
