I work in probabilistic machine learning and think natively in priors, posteriors, reparameterization, measure, and invariance. I recently encountered Bertrand's paradox in a philosophy-of-probability reading group, and my first reaction was that it is just an under-specified prior: if a flat prior over one parameterization is not flat after a nonlinear change of variables, then "choose a chord at random" picks out no unique distribution. I want that reaction tested rather than merely confirmed. The paradox, as it was presented to me: in a circle, what is the probability that a chord chosen "at random" is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Three classical procedures give three different answers. Choosing two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference gives 1/3, because the relevant arc between them must exceed 120 degrees. Choosing a radius uniformly and a point uniformly along it, then taking the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point, gives 1/2, because the point must fall within half the radius of the center. Choosing a chord midpoint uniformly in the disk gives 1/4, because the midpoint must fall in the concentric disk of half the radius. Each procedure is a way of being indifferent, yet the principle of indifference seems to sanction all three, so it appears to assign 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 to what is verbally the same event.

Restate the paradox and the underlying principle of indifference (the rule that equal ignorance over a partition of possibilities should be represented by a uniform distribution over that partition) in my vocabulary — prior distributions on parameter spaces, flat and improper priors, reparameterization and the Jacobian, invariance principles, and, if you think it earns its keep, Jeffreys prior — as precisely as you can. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the paradox map cleanly onto the under-specified-prior diagnosis, which parts you had to fudge or distort to force into that vocabulary, and which parts of the philosophical worry have no Bayesian counterpart at all. In particular, I want to know whether the paradox collapses into "there is no privileged parameterization, so the problem is ill-posed until you choose one or impose an invariance principle," or whether something survives the translation — a sense in which "indifference" or "complete ignorance" was doing work that prior-selection machinery still does not capture. End the response with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in a short phrase whether Bertrand's paradox is best classified as an ill-posed problem that a prior-selection or invariance principle can settle, or as a genuine ambiguity that no formal rule for choosing priors fully eliminates, or a third option if you think that dichotomy mislocates the issue.
