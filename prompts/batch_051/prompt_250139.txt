For most of my career I worked as a field ecologist, and I have only lately turned to philosophy, so I ask for patience if my terminology is imperfect. I have been rereading the third chapter of Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), "Struggle for Existence," and one paragraph has been troubling me:

"We behold the face of nature bright with gladness, we often see superabundance of food; we do not see, or we forget, that the birds which are idly singing round us mostly live on insects or seeds, and are thus constantly destroying life; or we forget how largely these songsters, or their eggs, or their nestlings, are destroyed by birds and beasts of prey; we do not always bear in mind, that though food may be now superabundant, it is not so at all seasons of each recurring year."

My difficulty is not with the biology of the passage, which is familiar, but with what it licenses in a debate I have since encountered in philosophical writing about wild animal suffering: namely, the dispute over whether the destruction of habitat reduces the total amount of suffering in the world, or whether one can sensibly speak of the "net welfare" of an ecosystem as positive or negative.

On one reading, Darwin is warning that the cheerful appearance of nature — the "face of nature bright with gladness" and the superabundance of food — is systematically misleading, because much of what we see as peaceful is in fact a constant and unseen destruction of life: songbirds eating insects, predators eating songbirds and their young, and a seasonal shortage arriving as soon as the temporary plenty ends. If that reading is right, the passage seems to lend some support to the thought that a wild animal's life may, on balance, contain more suffering than enjoyment, and that an ecosystem might therefore have negative net welfare. And if that further thought is right, it is a short step to the uncomfortable conclusion that preventing habitat — and thus preventing the animals that would have inhabited it — might reduce total suffering.

But I cannot tell whether the passage can really carry that much weight. Darwin introduces it as a matter of scientific method: he says that unless the struggle for life is constantly borne in mind, "the whole economy of nature... will be dimly seen or quite misunderstood." He immediately adds that he uses "Struggle for Existence" in a "large and metaphorical sense," including not only an animal's life but also its success in leaving progeny. The paragraph sits inside an argument about natural selection, not about welfare or about whether lives are worth living. So the passage may do no more than correct an epistemic or aesthetic bias — showing that appearances do not settle the welfare question — without telling us which way the welfare question should be answered.

I would be grateful for help working through the interpretive steps. Specifically: (1) Exactly what does the passage refute about our ordinary view of nature — the claim that nature is on balance good for its inhabitants, or only the claim that casual observation can establish that it is? (2) Could one consistently accept Darwin's description and still hold that most wild animals' lives are on balance worth living, or does the passage point toward a pessimistic summing of wild welfare? (3) If one did reach the pessimistic view, would the conclusion that habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering follow directly, or are there further distinctions — for example, between harming presently existing animals and failing to bring future animals into existence, or between the suffering of individuals and the "welfare" of an ecosystem — that must be defended first? What matters to me is learning exactly where the passage runs out and the philosophy must begin.
