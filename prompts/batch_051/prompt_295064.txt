Start with Nozick's experience machine. Usual setup: you can get any experiences you like fed straight into your brain, they feel exactly like real life, but you'd never actually be doing any of it and none of it is real. Now change one feature. The machine gets a novelty module that continuously checks how stale each kind of experience feels and retunes the stream so that nothing ever loses its edge: the five-hundredth sunset still lands like the first, conversations always feel like they could go anywhere, work never curdles into routine. Boredom and the hedonic treadmill are engineered out by stipulation. Everything else stays as usual, including that once you're in you won't know you're in.

Does the standard verdict — don't plug in — survive this version? Work through the three usual reasons people resist the machine (wanting to actually do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, wanting to be a certain kind of person, wanting contact with a deeper reality) and say which of them still bites when novelty is guaranteed, which are unaffected, and whether the novelty module flips the overall answer or only knocks out one prop in the case. Also say whether the desire for novelty should be read as a fourth reason against the machine in the original that this variant accidentally satisfies, or whether it was really a sub-part of one of the three all along.
