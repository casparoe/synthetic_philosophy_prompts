Question: Davidson famously denies that metaphors have a special metaphorical meaning. What exactly does that denial amount to, and how does it contrast with the conceptual metaphor theory associated with Lakoff and Johnson? Which of the two views gives a better treatment of novel poetic metaphor, and why?

Student answer: Davidson is targeting the idea that a metaphorical sentence carries, alongside its literal meaning, an extra encoded metaphorical meaning. He thinks a sentence like my lawyer is a shark means only what it literally says, that the lawyer is a carnivorous fish, and any further effect, like noticing that the lawyer is aggressive or relentless, is just a causal effect the words have on a hearer, not part of the sentence's meaning. So far Davidson is close to Grice: he treats metaphor as a kind of conversational implicature, where the speaker intends to communicate something beyond the sentence meaning and the hearer works it out from context. Because this extra effect is non-semantic, Davidson is committed to saying metaphors cannot genuinely convey insight or improve understanding; they are decorative rather than cognitive. Lakoff and Johnson reject this whole picture because for them metaphor is not primarily a deviant or special use of language but a basic feature of thought. We understand abstract domains through concrete bodily domains, so arguements are war, time is money, and so on, and these mappings structure how we reason rather than merely ornamenting speech. On their view Davidson's mistake is treating metaphor as one-off and open-ended, when in fact metaphorical thought is highly systematic. But Davidson seems right about one thing: a fresh poetic metaphor often has no fixed paraphrase, and its effect is open-ended. So his account handles novel poetic metaphor better than Lakoff and Johnson's, which is built mainly for conventional mappings and has little to say about one-off poetic creativity.
