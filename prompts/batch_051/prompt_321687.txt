I've been going back and forth with a friend about a view he calls tranquilism. The claim is that the only thing bad in itself is craving — tanha, in his half-Buddhist vocabulary — and the only thing good in itself is the absence of craving, where suffering is just what craving feels like from the inside. On this view, any state with no craving and no suffering is exactly as good as any other, regardless of what the person in it is doing, knows, or is in contact with.

My first response was Nozick's experience machine: people don't plug in because they want to actually do things, to be a certain kind of person, and to stay in touch with reality. He said I'd rigged the example by making the machine a simulator of pleasures and accomplishments. His version is a "stillness machine" that doesn't simulate anything: it directly and permanently removes craving and the felt push of suffering — no boredom, no FOMO, no itch to unplug, but also no interests, projects, or curiosity once you're in.

So vary the classic thought experiment in exactly that way: the machine now produces the state tranquilism calls the sole good, rather than an illusory life you'd value. Does Nozick's argument survive the change? Is the tranquilist forced to say you should plug in — and that refusing is itself just another craving to be let go of? What does each of Nozick's original reasons amount to under tranquilism: real objections, or just more suffering to be extinguished? And does the fact that, from the outside, the plugged-in person can't be gotten to object tell us anything, either for the view or against it? I'd like an analysis that says exactly where the original anti-machine reasoning still bites and where, on tranquilism, it simply stops applying.
