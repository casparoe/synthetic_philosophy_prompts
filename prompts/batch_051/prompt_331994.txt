I recently read the preface to Leibniz's New Essays Concerning Human Understanding, where he resists Locke's claim that the mind begins as a blank sheet. His argument, as I understand it, is that no collection of particular experiences can by itself produce the knowledge of a necessary truth: the senses can tell you what has happened in the cases you have observed, but they cannot tell you that something must happen in every case. He illustrates the point by saying that unless we already carry in us the principle that a sufficiently similar course of things will have similar outcomes, the mere fact that the sun has risen on many mornings gives us no certainty about tomorrow. The senses, he grants, are the occasion on which such truths become active, but not their source.

This matters to me because I have been working on systems that learn from data, and I recently noticed that I have developed a personal version of Leibniz's view in practice. My policy is this: when a general rule is needed to make sense of any finite sample of observations, and no amount of additional data would by itself settle whether the rule holds beyond the observed range, I treat the rule as a starting point rather than as something the data taught me. Moreover, once I classify a rule that way, I stop treating apparent counterexamples as evidence against it, and instead assume they must be misleading or misdescribed.

I find this policy comfortable, and it has saved me from chasing noise. But I am unsure whether it is genuinely Leibnizian or just a way of immunizing my favorite assumptions. Is a policy like this rationally defensible? Under what conditions does an inductive system, human or artificial, legitimately need something built in rather than learned from examples, and how would someone following this policy detect that she has crossed from rational nativism into unfalsifiable dogmatism?
