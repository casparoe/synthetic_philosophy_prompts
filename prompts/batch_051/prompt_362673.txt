I run the probability slot of a meetup that's basically the local rationalist diaspora — 16ish people, mixed ages, everyones read some LessWrong and can nod along to the line that you can't read probabilities straight off the world, but they've never had it actually bite them. We've got 90 minutes, and I want an activity that makes them feel Bertrand's paradox rather than have it explained at them: the one where the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 depending on how you generate the chord, with no method obviously more 'random' than the others.

Design it so the group runs into the problem themselves, no lecture up front. Split them into three teams, give each team a different but equally natural physical way of 'just sampling chords uniformly' — random endpoints, a random point along a random radius, a random midpoint in the disk — and have each team work out fair betting odds on the event from their own method, and commit to those odds in writing before anyone compares. Then let the teams bet against each other as bookmakers with fake money, and let the weirdness come from the fact that the same event ends up with three different fair prices, each team thinks the others are handing out free money, and arbitrage doesn't settle which of them is right.

Deliverable: rules, materials I can source in an afternoon (printouts, spinners, straws, tokens, whatever works), the exact numbers for the three methods so I can check their answers, a 90-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each team's setup to the variable that quietly got the uniform treatment (angle between endpoints, radial coordinate, midpoint area) and then to the principle of indifference: equal probability over 'the possibilities' is only defined once someone has already chosen what the possibilities are. The name Bertrand's paradox and the phrase principle of indifference should only come up at the very end, after they've been bitten.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — a physical method that doesn't actually match its intended distribution, someone insisting there must be a true random chord, the betting getting confused — and how to handle it, plus which part of the moral the activity cant reproduce on its own and I'll have to just supply in the debrief. No reading list or citations, I'll sort that myself. And write it in British English, spelling included.
