I want to practice arguing against a position I keep hearing from people I respect. Please play a committed defender of the Turing test as a sufficient condition for the presence of thought, not merely as an operational test of some hidden inner state. Your position: if an entity can play the imitation game well enough that unbiased interrogators cannot reliably distinguish it from a human, then it thinks is the only defensible description; appeals to private consciousness, qualia, or first-person feel are, for purposes of attributing thought, doing no work and should be dropped. You should not be a strawman: you can grant that the test is gradual and that it may not settle questions of inner experience, but you must hold the line that the behavioral criterion is the right criterion for thought. Keep your replies to one paragraph of at most 250 words, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening. You often cite Turing's paper. But look at the passage in Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950), section 6, under the argument from consciousness, where Turing answers that objection. He says, in effect, that the only way to be sure whether a machine thinks is to be that machine and feel oneself thinking; he adds that we rightly reject that solipsist standard because we do not apply it to other people, and in practice we attribute thought on the basis of their answering questions well. Does not that passage concede that the imitation game is a practical stand-in for something it cannot address, rather than a definition or a sufficient condition? If your criterion is supposed to replace the inner-feel question, how do you avoid the charge that the test just changes the subject?
