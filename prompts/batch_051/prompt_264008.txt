The three live positions, as I understand them, are eternal conscious torment (the lost suffer forever), annihilationism (the lost are destroyed and stop existing), and universal salvation (everyone is eventually reconciled). What's strange to me is that this dispute has gone on for many centuries and the standard moves on each side are extremely well known, yet nobody seems to move. The parties aren't waiting on new data: the same texts and traditions have been on the table forever, and all three camps agree in some sense that God is just and loving, that humans sin, and that punishment should be proportional to something. So I don't want a verdict about which view is true; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it substantive or verbal? Are the three camps answering different questions under one heading? Is there a hidden factual or formal question doing the work, or are they starting from different bedrock intuitions about what a punishment has to be like? Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis. I suspect the real fault line is about what punishment is for — retribution, destruction, or correction — and that underneath that, each camp is importing a different model of proportionality. The torment camp treats desert as a quantity that can outrun any finite bound, so an offense against an infinite being earns an infinite duration. The annihilation camp treats death as the natural upper bound on what a finite creature can suffer or deserve. The universalist camp treats punishment as corrective, which makes it essentially temporary by definition. If that's right, the debate isn't really about the texts or the facts; it's about wich proportionality model you bring to the table before weighing anything, and nobody argues for their model because it feels obvious to them.
