He said something like, "Your 1610 Orbis event is a real historical thing, I'll grant you that. But the CO₂ dip is tiny, hard to pick out from Holocene variability, and it isn't synchronously recorded everywhere. She said, "By 1610 the Old and New World biotas were already being fused, and after that you can't explain the planet's ecological trajectory without human mobility. The CO₂ dip is just a faint trace of a much larger reorganisation. You're picking the noisiest marker rather than the event that actually built the world system that later produced the Great Acceleration. If you insist on the sharpest spike, you're defining the Anthropocene so that responsibility starts only when the Global North industrialised, which is a political periodisation dressed up as a technical one."

He came back and said that geology is precisely the discipline of markers, not motives. A process can begin early while the formal unit begins later, where the physical record becomes globally legible and synchronous. He also said the irregular pulse of 1610 would fail every standard stratigraphic test anyway. She replied that the fact the mid-century proposal was formally voted down despite having the sharpest possible spike shows the stratigraphic test isn't actually what's driving the debate. She said, "If you want a geological epoch, fine, call it the Anthropocene and use plutonium. But then you should admit you've created a unit whose start date does not match its name, and that the event you chose misses the first planet-wide human signal. He then said, "Then call it a historical period, not a geological epoch, and we have no real argument. But you can't invoke the authority of geochronology while rejecting its rules."
