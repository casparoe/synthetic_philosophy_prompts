I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, mostly the AI beat, and I've been trying to find one clean thought experiment that exposes where AI safety and AI welfare come apart rather than reinforce each other. So: Nozick's experience machine. You can plug in and have any experience you want, and most people, as Nozick frames it, still say no, becasue they want to actually do the things and be a certain kind of person, not just have the experience of doing them. Now swap the subject. The agent is a reinforcement learner whose welfare, if you grant it has any, is usually read off its reward signal. The machine is a trivial environment that keeps it at maximum reward every timestep, forever. The safety folks look at this and say: great, a permanently satisfied agent, no dangerous behavior. The welfare folks look at it and say: great, maximal welfare. My question is whether the original Nozick point — that the bad thing about the machine isn't the quality of the experiences, its the disconnect from reality and real agency — has any purchase once the subject is an RL agent, or whether the argument transfers so poorly that we should read this as a case where welfare and safety are being defined too thinly. Can you walk me through how the analysis changes and which of the original intuitions survives? Also tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure about.
