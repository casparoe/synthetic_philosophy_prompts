We just did dutch book arguments in class and I keep thinking about a gap in them. The argument says: if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability rules (say you're 60% sure it'll rain and 50% sure it won't), a bookie can offer you a package of bets that each look good to you on their own but together make you lose money no matter what happens. My friend says that means incoherent credences are just irrational, full stop.

But the classic setup assumes there's a single price at which you'd buy or sell a bet, exactly your credence. Now suppose the only way to bet is through a prediction-market exchange that quotes a bid and an ask — you buy at the higher price, sell at the lower one — and charges a fee on every trade. So nobody can trade at one "fair" price, and every leg of the classic dutch book costs the bookie a little.

Does the dutch book argument survive that? With the spread and fees, can a bookie still guarantee a loss against any incoherent person, no matter how small the incoherence? If yes, show the package that works around the spread. If no, tell me which premise of the classic argument the spread actually breaks, and whether the argument still shows something weaker — like that you just need separate buy and sell prices that don't leave an arbitrage open, even if there's no single probability assignment behind them. I'm interested in the argument itself, not in what the exchange should do.
