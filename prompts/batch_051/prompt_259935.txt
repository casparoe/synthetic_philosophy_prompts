A friend of mine is a committed strong longtermist: she holds that because the number of people who could live in the future is so large, the dominant question about any donation is what it does to the far future, not what it does this year. I have been writing down her verdicts as I prepare to make my own annual giving decision. So far she has ruled as follows.

Between a highly effective deworming program and an orchestra's renovation fund, she takes the deworming program without hesitation. Between deworming in one region and an international network for early detection of emerging pathogens whose main payoff is decades out, she takes the network. Between unconditional cash transfers to very poor households and a concrete biosecurity measure with a well-modeled one-percent chance of preventing a catastrophic lab accident, she takes the biosecurity measure; she calls the cash transfers serious and real but says the expected value is not there. Between a clean-water program with strong near-term evidence and a climate mitigation fund whose benefits accrue mostly after 2050, she takes the climate fund, because future people count equally and there are far more of them. And between an existential-risk charity with a concrete mechanism for reducing the chance of nuclear war and a vague project that merely promotes concern about AI risk, she takes the former; her objection to the latter was not that its probability was small but that it gave her no stated probability at all, so there was no expected value for a decision rule to use.

Now I have to choose between two grants. Option A is a proven seasonal malaria prevention program expected to prevent roughly two hundred child deaths next year, with replicated impact estimates. Option B is a project that tries to improve the research culture of a major AI lab; a single modeling exercise estimates that it lowers existential risk by one in a hundred million, and that same model assigns a large enough value to the far future that the expected number of lives at stake exceeds Option A's two hundred. The estimate comes from one unvalidated model, and the mechanism is exactly the diffuse cultural influence she is usually suspicious of.

What should I predict she will say? I can see at least two longtermist rules that fit her record so far but diverge on this case: an undiscounted expected-welfare rule that lets any nonzero probability of affecting the far future dominate, and a threshold or robustness rule that refuses to act on tiny, mechanism-poor probabilities and otherwise defaults to well-evidenced near-term good. Which rule better explains her verdicts, are there others that fit just as well, and what case would most cleanly settle which one she actually holds?
