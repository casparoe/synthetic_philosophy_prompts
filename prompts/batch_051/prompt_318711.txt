I cover mathematics and the history of ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about the number one: what it means to say that something is one thing, and how that apparently simple notion sits at the boundary between arithmetic and metaphysics. In drafting the essay I keep returning to the definitions that open Book VII of Euclid's Elements, because they look elementary and turn out to be anything but. The translation I have been consulting gives the passage as: "1. A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one. 2. A number is a multitude composed of units." A little later the same definitions add: "11. A prime number is that which is measured by a unit alone."

The sentence I find genuinely hard is the first one. It appears to define a unit in terms of being called one, which sounds circular, and it gestures at 'the things that exist,' which sounds more like ontology than mathematics. I would like help understanding exactly what Euclid is doing there: is a unit a primitive object, a role something plays, or the very possibility of counting? The second definition then makes a number a multitude composed of units, which seems to push one outside the class of numbers altogether—and that, in turn, changes how one should read the eleventh definition, where a prime is what is measured by a unit alone. A modern reader might expect that definition to make one a prime; evidently it does not. I want to understand where in these lines the exclusion of one is actually established, and whether it follows from a principled distinction between a unit and a number or from something more contingent.

My broader question is whether this old concept of a unit really holds together under scrutiny. When later foundations build the natural numbers from sets or from a successor operation, do they preserve what Euclid was pointing at, quietly drop it, or answer a different question altogether? I would be grateful for a close, sentence-by-sentence reading of the quoted definitions, together with an honest assessment of whether the notion of a unit they articulate can survive being translated into modern terms.
