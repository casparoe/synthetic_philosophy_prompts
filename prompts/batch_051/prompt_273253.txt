I'm giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop on work and meaning. The audience will be mixed: about half academic philosophers who know Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" ("Vom Sinn des Lebens"), and half people from industry who have never read him and are suspicious of philosophy. Write me a five-minute speech, no more than 750 words, built around the passage in that essay where Schlick identifies meaning with youth—understood not as a chronological age but as a manner of acting—and says that work becomes meaningful only when it is transformed into play, done for its own sake rather than for an external end. The speech should let that passage do structural work: it should refuse the two consolations the audience will expect (that meaning comes from achieving external goals, and that meaning is a final state one reaches), and instead present meaning as a quality of activity itself. It must not become a lecture or cite the text, but the philosophers should see you have read the passage carefully, and the others should leave with a usable distinction between work-for-an-end and work-as-play. Close by giving the audience something they can do with the distinction during the workshop.
