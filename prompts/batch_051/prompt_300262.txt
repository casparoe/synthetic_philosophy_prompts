I've been roped into a dispute at my mums allotment site and I'm trying to work out what the rules actually require, not just which side I like more. The rulebook has had the same pest-control rule since it was last revised in the mid-nineties, before you could easily order live insects online. Rule 6 says: 'No member shall use any chemical insecticide, fungicide or herbicide on the plots. Control of insect pests is permitted only by the following methods: physical barriers, traps, hand removal, and companion planting.'

A plot-holder, Ed, had a really bad aphid infestation on his broad beans. He ordered a tube of ladybird larvae (the ordinary native sort) from an online supplier and tipped them out onto his beans, and within a fortnight the aphids were basically gone. Now the committee says he's broken Rule 6, because releasing predators is a way of controlling insect pests and it isnt on the list. Ed says the whole point of the rule is to keep chemicals off the site and the list was never ment to be exhaustive. He also points out that companion planting is partly recommended because it attracts ladybirds, so he's just done directly what the rule encourages indirectly. The committee says that's completely different, because he's deliberately introduced a load of extra predators instead of just making the plot welcoming to them. Both of them think they're the genuinely ecological one.

I don't care about the drama, but I do want to know how Rule 6 should be read. Work out what each approach gives: the plain words of the rule; the purpose of the rule; what the people who drafted it in the mid-nineties would probably have said if someone had asked them about mail-order ladybird larvae; and what a reasonable member reading the rule today would take it to mean. Then tell me which interpretation should win and why. If the committee is right, is there a principled difference between attracting predators and introducing them, and is that a sensible line for a rule like this to draw? Please answer in British English.
