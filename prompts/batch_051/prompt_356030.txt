I am about to work on the moral equality of combatants — the thesis that combatants on both sides of a war are symmetrically permitted to kill opposing combatants, whatever the justice of their side's cause — and I want the standard of adequacy fixed before I commit to a view. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the moral situation of combatants in war must meet. The account may end up vindicating the moral equality thesis, rejecting it, or replacing it with something finer-grained; state the requirements so that they are neutral on that outcome but constrain each of those possible verdicts.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and put it in a form precise enough to test a theory against. The list should at least cover the following sources of pressure:

- the symmetry between opposing combatants that the laws of war and much ordinary moral judgment take for granted;
- the contrast with ordinary individual self-defense, where an unjust attacker and a just defender are not symmetrically permitted to kill each other;
- the distinction between jus ad bellum and jus in bello, and the question whether in bello permissions can float free of ad bellum justice;
- the distinctions among liability to be killed, permission to kill, and blameworthiness, and between justification and excuse;
- the combatant/noncombatant distinction and the difficult cases — medics, munitions workers, child soldiers, irregular fighters;
- the epistemic position of the individual soldier, who often cannot tell whether the war is just;
- the retrospective position: the soldier who learns after the war that his side was unjust, and must now say what he did;
- the unjust combatant who otherwise fights lawfully and with restraint.

Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp (for example, Walzerian defenders of the traditional view versus revisionist critics of it). If any subset of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say which subset and why — in particular, whether symmetry and the individual-self-defense requirement can both be met without collapsing the unjust soldier's permission into a mere excuse. Keep any scorecard of existing views short; the requirements themselves are the deliverable.
