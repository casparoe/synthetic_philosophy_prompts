An economist I follow said on a podcast that people who agonize over choices are doing rationality wrong. His example: in the secretary problem, the optimal rule is to look at a bunch of candidates without committing, then grab the first one who beats everything you've seen so far. Then he glided from that to renting an apartment, choosing a restaurant when your starving, and even picking a movie, saying these are all really search with a stopping rule, so satisficing—set an aspiration level and take the first option that clears it—is not settling, its what a rational agent with scarce time and attention should do. I half-believe the conclusion, but I think the analogy is carrying it. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the secretary pool, the initial sample, the one-way nature of the decision, the aspiration level. Then show where the mapping breaks. I especially want to know whether the disanalogies that matter (you can often go back or recall earlier options, the number of options isn't usually known in advance, the cost of a bad pick is wildly uneven, and real search changes what you know about your own preferences) actually distroy his conclusion, or whether the conclusion survives them for reasons the secretary analogy can't see. And if the optimal-stopping frame is doing more work than it should, what would be a better analogy that does the same job honestly?
