Last month I joined the board of a small foundation that funds both early-childhood care and the preservation of regional poetry presses. At my first meeting I suggested that we should direct money to whichever programs produced the most value, and I was surprised when the other members pushed back. They insisted that the goods we serve are irreducibly different and cannot be ranked against each other on a single scale. I have been turning this over since, because it seems to threaten the whole practice of allocative reasoning. If our values really are many rather than one thing in disguise—if the good of caring for a child and the good of keeping a literary tradition alive share no common substance or single underlying value—then on what basis can one allocation possibly be more choiceworthy than another? Does this kind of value pluralism force us to conclude that hard choices are arbitrary, or at least arational, with no right answer to be found? And if a pluralist replies that a decision can still be reasonable without a common unit of measurement, hasn't she quietly introduced a higher-order value—something like choosing well, or acting coherently—that does the weighing, and thereby conceded that there is one value above the many after all? I cannot locate the flaw in this reasoning, but I doubt it is something a serious pluralist would accept.
