I am a retired atmospheric physicist, and over the past few years I have been reading philosophy of science. A claim that climatologists routinely lean on has started to look to me more like an assumption than an established result: that agreement across a multi-model ensemble is evidence that a projection is robust, and therefore deserves confidence. My worry is that members of an ensemble share parameterisations, modules of code, calibration datasets, and even authors, so agreement may just be the same hypothesis counted several times rather than independent confirmation.

Please approach this as a philosopher of science who works on climate modelling, uncertainty, and confirmation. Begin by reconstructing my worry in its strongest, most charitable form, and distinguish the relevant senses of robustness — at minimum: agreement within the existing ensemble, agreement across genuinely independent modelling centres, and stability under alternative structural or parameter choices. Then design the best concrete test you can — observational, computational, statistical, or a combination — that would tell us whether the robustness of a given projection is doing genuine confirmatory work or is better explained by common ancestry and shared idealisations. Take a concrete projection, such as Arctic amplification or changes in the frequency of extreme precipitation, to illustrate. Say exactly what result would favour each reading, and then be candid about what the test could not settle even in principle.
