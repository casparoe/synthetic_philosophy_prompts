I spent most of my career in a lab and only recently got into philosophy, so forgive me if this is a rookie question. Something from a lifetime of travel keeps nagging at me, and I want you to help me work out what the practice actually commits us to.

The practice is this: we routinely expect visitors and out-of-towners to pick up our local everyday conventions, and we treat their failure to do so as a genuine wrong, not just a faux pas. In much of the States, if a visitor sits down at a restaurant and leaves a five percent tip, the server is hurt and the rest of the table is mortified; someone usually says you have to tip, that's how it works here. Yet Americans who travel to places where tipping is not the custom—and can even be taken as an insult—are expected to stop tipping while they're there, and get the same that's-not-how-we-do-it-here lecture if they dont. Queueing is similar. In Britain a queue seems to have almost legal force; push in and you've done something wrong, and nobody cares that where you're from that's normal. But the same Brit who is furious at a queue-jumper will happily cross against the light in a foreign city, and wont expect a visitor to pick up on subtler local conventions—how close to stand, how loudly to talk on the train. So we don't outsource all our manners to the locals; only some of them.

What I can't figure out is what theory of moral obligation this pattern presupposes, if any. Does treating local conventions as binding on visitors commit us to the idea that conventions generate obligations just by existing in a place, the way some people think positive law does? Or is the real commitment something else—not harming people who've built livelihoods around a convention, or not free-riding on a practice you benefit from? In much of the US a server's hourly wage is set on the assumption that tips make up the difference, so the tipping case might pull toward a reliance or harm story rather than a conventions-bind story. But queueing in Britain isn't like that; nobody's wages depend on it, yet the indignation is just as moralized. So which of these views actually explains the pattern, and can it explain why tipping and queueing get the treatment while standing-too-close does not? And here's the rub: if a visitor understands the local convention perfectly well but thinks it's a bad one—say, a tipping culture built on a minimum-wage carve-out that lets employers pay servers almost nothing—is the practice still committed to saying they should follow it? Lay out the implicit theory, mark clearly where the practice strictly presupposes it as opposed to merely fitting with it, and say what would have to change if that presupposition were dropped.
