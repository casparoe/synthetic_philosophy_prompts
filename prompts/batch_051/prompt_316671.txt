For a piece I am writing on how the social sciences argue about explanation, I recently spent time with a researcher who defends methodological individualism, the view that adequate explanations in the social sciences bottom out in facts about individual people. He illustrated the stakes with a bank run: should we explain it by citing the bank's fragile funding structure, or by citing the many depositors who each decided to withdraw? He gave me the following argument, which he regards as decisive, and I have reconstructed it from my notes as faithfully as I can:

(1) Any institution, market, demographic trend, or other social structure is composed of individual people, their actions, beliefs, habits, and relations, and of nothing over and above those individuals.

(2) If X is composed of Y and of nothing over and above Y, then every fact about X — including facts about the effects of X — is made true by facts about Y.

(3) Therefore, every fact about a social structure, including facts about its effects, is made true by facts about individuals.

(4) A social-scientific explanation is adequate only if it states the facts that made the explained outcome the case.

(5) Therefore, an adequate social-scientific explanation must state facts about individuals; explanations that cite structures, institutions, or other aggregate entities are legitimate only as shorthand, and only insofar as they can in principle be replaced by individual-level explanations.

I find each step individually hard to reject, yet the last sentence of (5) feels as though it reaches further than the premises allow, and I cannot tell whether that is a real defect or merely my discomfort with the conclusion. I would like a committed ruling. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which numbered step fails, and on what reading of that step? If it is valid, is it sound, and how much of the intended conclusion does it actually establish? Do not merely lay out the options; tell me where you come down and why.
