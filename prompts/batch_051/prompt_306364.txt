I’m a teaching assistant leading a two-hour seminar for second-year undergraduates who have read a bit of Hume on sympathy but have not read Adam Smith before. Next week’s text is The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and my goal is to get them from “the impartial spectator is just a fancy name for conscience” to understanding Smith’s actual claim about the mechanism of self-judgment. The assigned passage is from Part III, Chapter I, Of the Consciousness of Merited Praise or Blame: Smith describes how, when a person tries to examine his own conduct, he divides himself, as it were, into two persons — the examiner and judge, and the person whose conduct is examined. The examiner is the spectator whose sentiments the agent tries to enter into by imagining how things look from that point of view; the second is the agent, the person he properly calls himself. Smith then holds that the judge cannot be in every respect the same as the person judged, any more than cause and effect can be in every respect the same.

Plan how I should teach that passage and, through it, the impartial spectator. I want the order of ideas, not an explanation of them: what must be in place before this passage carries its weight, and why; what it is doing that the earlier sections on sympathy alone do not do; and how to make the concepts visibly stack up. Pick one everyday example that can carry the whole session — not a morality tale, but something where a student can feel both roles — and say what the example must have and what it must lack. Then give me the misconception map: the predictable readings students will bring or form (the impartial spectator as a literal internal observer, as just other people’s actual opinions, as a conscience that speaks directly, as emotionless calculation), the point in the sequence where each is best headed off, and how to do it without pre-empting the insight. I want them to leave with a working handle on why Smith says the judge and the person judged cannot be exactly the same, and why that asymmetry is supposed to be a feature of self-knowledge rather than a bug. End with a question or short exercise I can use in the final ten minutes that would show whether they understand the mechanism rather than just the vocabulary.

Do not assign secondary literature; this is all from the primary text and the discussion.
