Okay here's the thing. I'm writing something on technological determinism and I keep seeing the debate about whether artifacts have politics run two different claims together, and I want a case that pries them apart. So construct a thought experiment for me.

Here are the two claims as I want them understood. One: an artifact has politics in the arrangement sense when its design itself advantages some identifiable group over another — the same function could have been served by a different design with different winners, but this design settled the contest the way it did. Two: an artifact has politics in the inherent sense when operating it at all seems to require, or strongly favor, a particular structure of authority — the classic example being a centralized nuclear grid versus rooftop solar panels.

Now the spec. Build me one artifact, realistic and boring — a workplace system, an app, muncipal hardware, something like that — and it has to meet all of these:

- It has politics in the arrangement sense: the design itself advantages a specific group and disadvantages a specific group, and this isn't just a matter of how the thing happens to get used.
- It does not have politics in the inherent sense: nothing about running it normally requires or pushes toward any particular authority structure, and it would work fine under quite different political setups.
- It's mundane — no sci-fi, no nukes, no AGI, no social credit systems. Think time-clock, not death star.
- The disadvantage has to live in the artifact, not the function. If any reasonable redesign with the same function would still disadvantage the same group, that's cheating — then the function is doing the political work, not the artifact.

After the scenario, do the check. First, what does a hardline neutralist say — that it's just a tool and the politics is in the rules around it? Second, what does the arrangement-sense theorist say — that the design settled the contest? Third, and this is the part i actually care about: does the neutralist's reply get its force by quietly adopting the inherent sense, arguing that because the thing could run under any authority structure it isn't really political, and is that a good move or a bait-and-switch? Then tell me which single feature of the design is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how the side that comes out looking worst would revise the example to get their conclusion back.

Finally, give me a table summarizing the verdicts, with columns: Position; Verdict; One-sentence reasoning; Feature of the case the verdict hangs on. Rows for: hardline neutralist, arrangement-sense theorist, inherent-sense theorist, and your own assessment.
