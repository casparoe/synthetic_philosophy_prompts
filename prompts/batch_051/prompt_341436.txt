I'm drafting the framing chapter for a seminar on obedience, authority, and deference, and I'd like an audit of the view I seem to have ended up defending before I commit to it in print. I hold all of the following:

(1) The Milgram-style obedience experiments, including their close replications, show that ordinary people can be led by situational pressures—the physical proximity of the authority figure, incremental escalation, the diffusion of responsibility—to act against their own independent moral judgments.
(2) Because conduct in such settings is so strongly situationally driven, individuals caught up in institutional wrongdoing are usually less blameworthy than our punitive instincts suggest; the primary locus of moral failure is the system rather than the person.
(3) I am also persuaded by the Arendtian line that unthinking deference—declining to examine whether a superior's instruction could be one's own action—is itself a serious moral fault rather than a mitigating condition.
(4) I think the military-law position is right that a manifestly unlawful order is no defense for the subordinate who obeys it.
(5) Separately, I think epistemic deference—trusting well-credentialed experts and scientific consensus on matters outside one's own competence—is an intellectual virtue, and that public life would be improved by more of it, not less.

Please go through these as a set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and which stand or fall together? For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and state the cheapest revision that would resolve each; for the merely apparent tensions, say why they dissolve. I want to know which of these commitments is doing most of the work and where the view would buckle under pressure.
