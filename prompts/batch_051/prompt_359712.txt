For a small research collective working on emergency governance, write the list of adequacy conditions that any moral-political theory of designated-survivor authority must meet. By a designated survivor I mean the practice, most familiar from presidential systems, of keeping one official in the line of succession physically separate when the entire senior leadership is gathered, so that a single catastrophic attack cannot eliminate every person constitutionally entitled to exercise executive power. The question is not what current law happens to say but what a normatively adequate account of that surviving official's authority would have to establish: when they may act, in what sense their action is legitimate rather than merely lawful, and what limits apply to an official who was never elected to the office they now occupy and who may have to act within minutes, possibly including the use of force, under profound uncertainty.

For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration behind it, and mark explicitly whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (proceduralist, consequentialist, democratic-participatory, and the like). Pay particular attention to which apparently hard requirements are really only the preferences of one camp. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable once catastrophic succession is taken seriously — for example because decisiveness, democratic authorisation, and fidelity to the existing constitutional order cannot all be preserved at once — say so, and say which requirement should give way under which circumstances. The criteria themselves are the deliverable, so do not spend the answer scoring existing proposals against them.

The purpose that fixes adequacy is this: the document will inform a group deciding what succession arrangements to recommend for a large democratic state faced with the sudden loss of most or all elected leadership. The hardest cases I want the criteria to expose are ones like these: whether continuity of government is a value in its own right or only an instrument for protecting the governed; how a chain of popular authorisation survives a discontinuity in personnel; what distinguishes a designated survivor from a usurper beyond the bare existence of a positive rule; and why the justification for emergency authority does not simply lapse or regress if the survivor's own would-be successors are killed as well.

Please write the answer in British English.
