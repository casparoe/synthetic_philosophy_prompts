I'm preparing a section of a course on well-being and public policy, and I want a piece of sustained satire to open the discussion of libertarian paternalism before the students read the critical papers. Write a mock memorandum to the Minister, around 700 words, from the deputy director of a fictional national Behavioural Insights Unit, in a tone of placid bureaucratic confidence. The memorandum should propose, as the logical completion of the unit's work, the replacement of the remaining coercive instruments of the state — fines, bans, mandates — with pure choice architecture: opt-out defaults, reordered forms, framing, cooling-off periods, reminder letters, and the strategic arrangement of options on shelves and screens. At every point the deputy director must insist that none of this is paternalism, because every intervention is transparent and aimed at what each person would, on reflection, want for themselves.

The satire should follow that assurance to its natural conclusion. The memo should quietly extend defaults from organ donation and pension enrolment toward more consequential domains, while treating the low opt-out rates under existing defaults not as a problem for the liberty defense but as a feature: a way to achieve mandate-level compliance while retaining the moral cleanliness of choice. The jokes should come from the actual machinery — status quo bias, the power of defaults, the difference between coercive and non-coercive influence, the requirement that nudges be transparent rather than hidden, and the gap between what people say they want, what they do, and what the unit decides they really want. A specialist in the nudge literature should recognize a fair reductio of the claim that a nudge must be freedom-preserving simply because one could in principle opt out, and someone new to the material should still feel the slide. Begin with the memorandum itself, with no preface or commentary.
