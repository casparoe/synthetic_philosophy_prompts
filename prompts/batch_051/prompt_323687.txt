I keep loosing the thread on a parallel I noticed, and I can't tell whether it's deep or just me pattern-matching. Here it is.

The Allais paradox, run the standard way, gives people two choices. Choice one: a sure $1 million, or a gamble with an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. Most people take the sure million. Choice two: an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing, or a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing. Most people take the second gamble. That reversal violates the independence axiom, which in rough form says that if you prefer one option to another, swapping a component the two options had in common for a different component they still have in common shouldn't flip the preference. The usual gloss is that people overweight what's certain: a certainty effect.

Now the parallel. Independence is a cancellation principle: what's common to both options is supposed to drop out of the comparison. Deontological constraints look to me like a systematic refusal to let a shared item cancel. Say two possible acts produce the same aggregate outcome — five saved, one dead either way. A simple additive consequentialist says that settles it, because the deaths and saved lives cancel out in the comparison. A deontologist may still think one act is permitted and the other forbidden, because it matters whether the one death is a means to saving the five or a side effect of doing so. In decision-theoretic jargon, expected utility assumes separability across states; deontology is denying an analogous separability across persons, refusing to let the shared parts of an outcome drop out. Both the Allais chooser and the deontologist treat a 'same on both sides' component as non-detachable, in a way the official theory says they may not.
