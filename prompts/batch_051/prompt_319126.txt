In my decision theory seminar, we have been working through the money-pump argument for transitivity. The setup is familiar: suppose an agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and yet C to A. A trader can lead her through the exchanges A for B, B for C, and C for A, charging a small fee at each step, so that she returns to A having lost money even though each trade, considered on its own, was consistent with her preferences.

I am having trouble pinning down what the argument actually establishes. Below are five candidate diagnoses of what the money-pump argument shows. Please rank them from most to least adequate, and after the ranking, give a paragraph defending your top-ranked candidate and a sentence or two explaining what pushes each of the others up or down.

1. The argument shows that intransitive preferences are practically irrational because they make the agent exploitable.
2. The argument shows nothing about the rationality of the agent; each individual trade is rational given her preferences, so the failing belongs to the trader who exploits her.
3. The argument shows only that an intransitive agent needs a supplementary policy, such as refusing any trade that would complete a preference cycle; with that policy she can keep her preferences and avoid exploitation.
4. The argument shows that apparent preference cycles are better redescribed as diachronic changes of mind, so the real lesson concerns dynamic consistency rather than synchronic transitivity.
5. The argument collapses once alternatives are individuated more finely in time: the agent is not literally returning to the same A, but to a temporally later counterpart of A, so no genuine cycle occurs.

Please treat the candidates charitably, including the ones you rank lower.
