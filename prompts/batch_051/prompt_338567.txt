As part of my reporting for a science and ideas outlet, I sat in on a long argument after a panel about mindfulness, peak performance, and the ancient Daoist ideal of wu-wei, or effortlessness. The two parties were a cognitive psychologist who studies goal pursuit and a taijiquan instructor who teaches wu-wei through movement. Neither would let me identify them, so I'll call them Rao and Lin.

Rao said he had recently tried, as a personal experiment, to spend an afternoon not striving. Lin asked how he did it. Rao said he cleared his calendar, put his phone in another room, and kept repeating to himself something like: "Don't engineer anything. Just respond to what comes." Lin immediately said he had guaranteed his own failure, because he had turned non-striving into a task. What followed lasted an hour and went roughly like this:

(1) Lin argued that trying not to try is self-stultifying. The very act of aiming at effortlessness makes the action deliberate and goal-directed, so the target state is excluded by the means.

(2) Rao replied that this proves too much. Many skills start as deliberate techniques and become natural; pianists train fingerings until they stop thinking about them, and athletes drill footwork until it becomes automatic. So deliberate practice can produce spontaneity. Lin answered that the pianist practices a positive skill, whereas the wu-wei practitioner is targeting a negative state—the absence of active managerial effort—and so there is no positive skill for the drilling to build.

(3) Rao said the negative isn't special. People practice not flinching at the plate, not doubting during a serve, and not gripping the club too tightly. Those are all attempts to remove an interfering behavior, and they work. Lin countered that those are still local suppressions of a specific habit, while wu-wei is not suppressing a predicable impulse but a shift in how the whole agent relates to action. A training regimen that keeps checking whether the self has quieted down keeps the self salient. She also said this is why trying to "get out of your own way" often produces the ironic effect of more self-monitoring.

(4) Rao then said that Lin's own classes assign breathing exercises and posture corrections, which are plainly methods aimed at ease. So she was running the same paradox; she had just inserted a teacher and a curriculum into the loop. Lin replied that those forms are scaffolding, and the art lies in dropping them at the right moment, not in executing them perfectly. When Rao asked how one knows when to drop them, she said there is no rule for that, which is exactly why the practice cannot be turned into a protocol.

I left uncertain who had actually conceded what. I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four numbered moves, say whether the speaker's claim is clearly right, defensible but incomplete, question-begging, or clearly mistaken, and justify each rating briefly. Then say what, if anything, both are missing, and whether Lin's final response—that the art lies in dropping the scaffolding, but no rule says when—is a genuine answer to Rao's challenge or just relocates the paradox.
