in my political ethics class we've been talking about dirty hands, the idea that a leader sometimes has to do genuinely wrong stuff (like a strike that kills civilians, or torturing a suspect, or lying to cover a looming disaster) because every alternative is worse, and that they should still carry guilt about it rather than treating it as just another cost-benefit call. I've been trying to design a institution that keeps that guilt in the picture but doesn't let politicians get away with anything. here's the mechanism: a dirty hands registry. An official who thinks they may be about to do something that crosses a moral line can file a confidential, time-stamped statement with an independent commission saying what they're going to do, what alternatives they considered, and why they think the dirty option is the least bad. If the act later becomes public, the commission unseals the statement and reviews it; if it judges the act was genuinely necessary and proportionate, the official gets a substantially reduced penalty and a formal finding of tragic choice rather than ordinary misconduct, and if they never filed, any penalty is automatically harsher. Analyze how this would actually play out. Who files and who doesn't? Does filing function as insurance for bad behavior, a costly signal of good faith, or both? How would politicians game the filing and the commission's judgment, and what would it do to public trust if the state officially certifies someone's action as a dirty hands case? Finally, are the failure modes fixable by changing the rules, or does the whole design undermine the lingering guilt that dirty hands is suppose to protect?
