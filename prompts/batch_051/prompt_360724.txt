A friend of mine, who reads a lot of popular psychology but has never taken a philosophy course, gave me his account of egoism the other day, and I cannot tell whether it is fair or a caricature. This is what he said, as close to his actual words as I can remember:

"Psychological egoism says that everything a person does is selfish at bottom. If you hold a door open for a stranger, you are really doing it because it makes you feel good, makes you look good, or stops you from feeling guilty, and that feeling or reputation or relief is the real point of the action. Ethical egoism says that you ought to be selfish: your own good is the only thing that ultimately matters, and everyone should openly pursue it. What I like about this view is that it is honest. Psychological egoism shows that we all act for our own benefit anyway, so ethical egoism just tells us to stop pretending and admit what we are already doing. Anyone who denies egoism is either fooling themselves or has not thought hard enough about their own motives."

I have turned this over in my mind a fair amount, and something about the jump from "we do act for our own benefit" to "we should" bothers me, but I cannot tell whether that is a genuine difficulty or just my own discomfort. Could you go through his account and tell me which parts a careful psychological egoist or ethical egoist could accept, which parts are exaggerated or confused, and whether his closing conclusion actually follows from what comes before?
