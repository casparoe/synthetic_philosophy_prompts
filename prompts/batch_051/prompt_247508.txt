I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in value theory, and this year the material has become unexpectedly personal. A former student wrote to me after a bereavement to say that what he missed most, in the months that followed, was not the person's presence exactly, but the sense that his own plans and unfinished conversations still had an audience in the future. That letter made me think hard about the badness of human extinction: not the deaths it would directly involve, but what it would do to the value of the lives and projects of people who never live to see it. I would like to set this as the final examination for the course. The centrepiece should be what I will call the afterlife conjecture: roughly, the claim that much of what we now value — our projects, achievements, relationships, and confidence in our values — tacitly presupposes that human life will go on after we are gone, so that a credible prospect of near extinction would not merely prevent future good lives but would retroactively drain much of the value from the lives being lived now.

Please write me: (i) one essay question, to be answered in 90 minutes for 20 marks, that cannot be answered by summarising that conjecture; (ii) a model answer of about 600 words; and (iii) a marking scheme setting out five must-hit moves with the marks for each and a note on how a B-grade answer falls short of an A. The crux of the question should be the contrast between an impersonal-loss explanation — extinction is bad because it prevents the future good lives that would otherwise have existed — and the stronger retroactive-dependence explanation — extinction would undermine the value of our present activities themselves. The question may hand students a short scenario to probe, such as a composer who learns that the musical tradition she works within will end with her, but I leave the exact wording to you. As part of the model answer, please state which argument or consideration would do most to change the verdict you reach, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail. Please work entirely from the conjecture as described here, without quotations or citations, so that the question tests reasoning rather than reading.
