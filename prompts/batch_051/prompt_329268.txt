I am a software engineer building a worship-planning application for Protestant churches. The product must let a congregation record its convictions and then automatically flag which components of a public service are permissible in that tradition: musical instruments, hymnody, choirs, written liturgies, a liturgical calendar, drama, dance, images, incense, and similar items. I have no formal background in theology or philosophy, so I need the underlying debate organized around concrete questions I can turn into application logic rather than around the internal vocabulary of the traditions.

Write the core of a classification instrument, either a decision tree or a nested taxonomy, for positions on the regulative principle of worship: the question whether every component of corporate worship must be positively warranted by Scripture, or whether whatever Scripture does not forbid is permissible. Build it from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of denominations. Each branch node should be a yes/no question about a concrete practice or concrete claim, phrased so that a thoughtful church member could answer it without knowing which named position it leads to. Each leaf should carry the standard label where one exists (for example, strict regulative principle with exclusive psalmody — singing only the biblical Psalms; a moderate regulative principle that permits instruments and uninspired hymns; a Lutheran-style normative principle; an Anglican-style position that treats non-forbidden ceremonies as indifferent) or the word unnamed, followed by exactly one sentence identifying that position's most awkward commitment.

Include branch questions that cover at least the following forks, though you may reorder, split, or replace them if doing so captures the logical space more cleanly: (a) whether a practice is permissible only if Scripture commands it; (b) whether non-commanded practical arrangements such as times, buildings, seating, and sound equipment count as lawful circumstances of otherwise commanded elements; (c) whether musical instruments are themselves an element requiring separate warrant or merely a circumstance of the commanded act of singing; (d) whether uninspired hymns fall under the biblical command to sing or are excluded; (e) whether non-canonical service forms such as a written liturgy, a choir anthem, a liturgical calendar, or a dramatic presentation lie inside or outside the commanded elements; and (f) whether visual and ceremonial additions such as images, incense, and vestments are governed by the same criterion as everything else.

Then flag what the instrument cannot do. Name any positions it cannot separate or that it groups together misleadingly. Note any combinations of answers that lack a historical occupant, and say in one sentence for each whether the gap looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended. Do not flatten distinctions just to produce a tidy tree.

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Framing (roughly 150–200 words): restate the question being classified and its role in the product's allowlisting rules; list the concrete service components the instrument must classify.
2. The Instrument (roughly 600–900 words): the decision tree or nested taxonomy, with every branch node phrased as a concrete yes/no question and every leaf labeled and annotated as specified above.
3. Boundary Notes (roughly 250–400 words): positions the instrument cannot separate, empty answer combinations and the most likely explanation for each, and the point at which the instrument stops being reliable.
4. Worked Examples (roughly 250–350 words): run three sample congregations through the instrument — one strict, one moderate, one on the normative side — showing which branch answers route them to their leaf.
5. Rules-Engine Mapping (roughly 150–250 words): translate the leaves into a short decision table or sketched boolean conditions (plain pseudocode is fine) showing how the classification would gate a single service component in the product, and identify which leaf is hardest to encode and why.

The audience for the instrument is me and later the engineering team, so avoid historical narrative, footnotes, and anything that presumes the reader can identify doctrinal camps by name. The instrument should sort someone by what they affirm about specific practices, not by which label they recognize.
