i've been going round in circles on whether a finite bit string can be properly random, and not just random-looking relative to what you happen to know. I've ended up with a home-made criterion I'm calling the 'no free subsequence' test, and I want it pulled apart honestly.

For a binary string S of length n, consider every subsequence you can pick out by a rule that satisfies two things: (a) the rule is written down before the string is shown to whoever applies it, and (b) it's expressible in a fixed vocabulary in, say, twenty words, without naming the actual bit values or positions of S. Call those admissible selections. S passes the test iff for every admissible selection, the frequency of 1s among the selected positions is within 1/sqrt(n) of the frequency of 1s in S. So 010101... of length 400 fails, because 'take the even positions' is an admissible rule and it gives all 1s, a deviation of 0.5. A random-looking string with about half 1s, where no short natural rule finds a biased subsequence, passes.

The point of the fixed vocabulary and word count, on my account, is that randomness is always relative to a language and a budget anyway, so I'm being explicit about it instead of pretending to absolute randomness. I also think this handles the obvious objection ('just select the positions that contain 1s'), because that rule names the bit values, so it's not admissible.

But it feels like the whole load is being carried by the phrase 'without naming the actual bit values', and that phrase might itself smuggle in the very idea of randomness I'm trying to define. Also the 20 words and 1/sqrt(n) are plainly arbitrary. Is there a defensible finite version of the usual infinite-sequence criterion at all, is mine one of them, and what's the nastiest counterexample or failure mode I've missed? I'd like the answer in British English, please.
