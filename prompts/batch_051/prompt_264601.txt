I teach moral philosophy—mostly applied ethics and a bit of moral epistemology—and after a few too many seminar discussions about motivated reasoning and debunking arguments, I've ended up with a private rule that I'm no longer sure its defensible. The rule goes like this: whenever I notice that one of my own moral convictions would predictably work out in my favor, or that I came to hold it in a setting where expressing it was socially rewarded, I mark it as suspect and refuse to lean on it in teaching, in arguments, or in personal decisions until I can give a justification for it that doesn't cite my interests or the fact that people around me approved. In practice this means I routinely set aside judgments I still emotionally feel are right—including things like my views about what I owe my own family, where self-interest and real moral insight are extremely hard to separate. The rule has probably saved me from some self-serving conclusions, but I worry it's just its own kind of bias. A colleague keeps pushing the point that the whole distinction is broken: biases don't sit on top of otherwise pure values, they partly constitute what we care about, so the idea that I can subtract them and find the real moral judgment underneath may be a mistake I'm building into the rule from the start.

Is this rule rationally defensible as a standing policy for someone in my position? If it is, what exactly makes it better than the biases it's supposed to catch? If it isn't, is there a version of it—maybe with a different trigger, or a less extreme discount—that keeps the protection without quietly throwing out legitimate moral preferences? And if you think the setup itself is confused, or that the question can't be answered in the abstract without concrete examples of the judgments at issue, tell me that plainly rather than humoring the premise. I'd also appreciate the answer in American English.
