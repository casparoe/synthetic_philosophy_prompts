You are a professional philosopher of language working on the semantics of generic sentences — bare-plural statements such as "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria." I have been collecting my own considered, pre-theoretical verdicts on such sentences, and I have found that my verdicts are stable but that I cannot state the rule behind them. Please think carefully, and do not just reach for a slogan. Begin by brainstorming the features that might be doing the work in the cases below — prevalence, causal role in the kind's life, danger, sex-limitation, typicality, "what the kind is like," and so on — before you commit to a principle. Then state the best principle, or a small set of competing principles with their trade-offs, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible.

Here are my verdicts.

1. "Ducks lay eggs" is true, even though only sexually mature females lay eggs, and not all of them do. But "ducks are female" is false. Those two feel importantly different to me: laying eggs is something ducks do, a fact about the species, while being female is a classification of individual ducks, not a fact about what ducks are like. But I cannot say what "a fact about the species" means without begging the question.

2. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true, even though only a tiny minority of mosquitoes ever carry the parasite. "Mosquitoes carry West Nile virus" is also true. But "Canadians are right-handed" is false, even though the large majority of Canadians are right-handed. So prevalence is not the issue: a tiny minority can be enough, and a large majority can fail.

3. "Books are paperbacks" is false, even though most books are paperbacks, while "books have pages" is true. So being true of most members is neither necessary nor sufficient.

4. "Sharks attack bathers" is true, though shark attacks are extremely rare. "Sharks are dangerous to bathers" is also true.

5. "Lions have manes" seems true, though only males have manes; "lions are male" is false. This looks like the duck/female pair again: the sex-limited property yields a true generic, and the sex classification does not.

My half-formed rule is that a generic "Ks are P" is true just when P is characteristic of the kind K — part of what Ks are like — even if P holds of only a minority of Ks, and false when P is merely a widespread or demographic fact about Ks. But "characteristic" is doing an enormous amount of work. It is tempting to cash it out as "part of the stereotype or folk theory of K," but that seems to make the truth of "mosquitoes carry malaria" depend on what people happen to believe, which cannot be right: the sentence was true before anyone knew mosquitoes transmit malaria. It also makes it unclear why a stereotype that is partly false — say, that swans are white, even though black swans exist — should ever make the generic true.

Here is what I want from you. State the best principle, or two or three candidate principles, that captures these verdicts and the hard pairs: "ducks lay eggs" versus "ducks are female"; "mosquitoes carry malaria" versus "Canadians are right-handed"; "books are paperbacks" versus "sharks attack bathers." For each clause of your principle, say which case or pair motivates it. Then apply the principle to cases I have not settled: "peacocks have colorful tails," "humans are right-handed," "swans are white," "Dalmatians are deaf," "pit bulls are aggressive," "bats carry rabies," "ravens are black," and "dogs are purebred." Finally, tell me honestly which of my listed verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should overturn that verdict or complicate the principle.
