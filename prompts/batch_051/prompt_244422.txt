In my environmental philosophy course we have been discussing the tragedy of the commons, and I keep noticing a mismatch in ordinary moral judgment that I would like to see worked through carefully. If a herder adds one more animal to a shared pasture that is already overgrazed, we call him a contributor to the destruction of a common resource and blame him for taking more than his share. But if someone buys a cheap garment whose low price depends on a factory that quietly drains a shared aquifer or discharges waste into a river, we treat that person as an ordinary consumer, not as someone who is helping to deplete a commons. Both knowingly benefit from a practice that overuses a resource no one privately owns, and neither is prepared to pay the full cost of restraint, so the difference in judgment seems to call for justification. Please construct the strongest principle you can that would justify treating the two cases as morally different, test that principle against hard variants of the two cases, and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned, explaining what the more consistent alternative would be if it should be abandoned.
