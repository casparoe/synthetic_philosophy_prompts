I keep finding myself arguing this: the vicious/benign distinction for infinite regresses isn't really about infinity at all. A regress is vicious only when the series was supposed to complete or ground something, so the endlessness genuinely matters; when each step just re-runs a well-defined task one level down (the truth regress, where a proposition leads to the claim that the proposition is true, which leads to...), it's harmless. What should shake me out of this? Give me the best counterexamples — cases that meet my benign criterion but still feel like the regress is doing damage, or finite chains that feel vicious in the same way — ranked by how decisive they'd be. Also say where my criterion misreads the standard regress arguments: rule-application (needing a rule saying to apply modus ponens, then a rule saying to apply that rule, and so on), relation-instantiation (the instantiation tie seeming to need a further tie to connect it to the thing and the property, and so on), or epistemic justification. And tell me whether a single-criterion view like mine could survive only by admitting that viciousness is judged case by case, and whether that concession would definately be a problem for the view.
