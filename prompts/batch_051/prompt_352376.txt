Draft the joint statement that would result from an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over state apologies for historical wrongs. Call the first camp the civic-repair view: it holds that a state whose institutions and identity persist over time can owe, and can meaningfully offer, an apology for wrongs committed by earlier generations, because a well-formed apology acknowledges the wrong, restores the standing of the wronged group, and repairs the terms of civic trust under which both sides must live. Call the second camp the presentist-skeptical view: it holds that present officeholders cannot apologize for acts they did not commit without engaging in a kind of moral ventriloquism, and that the owed response to historical injustice is forward-looking — structural reform, material repair, and truthful public accounting — while an apology is either empty, or worse, a cheap substitute for those harder responses.

The document should presuppose that both camps accept three things: that the original wrongs were grave; that some response is owed to descendants and to the surviving institutions of the wronged group; and that an ordinary interpersonal apology can be meaningful only when the apologizer bears an appropriate relation to the wrong. The camps are not to be treated as straw men, and their disagreement should not be resolved by stipulation.

Produce the document in the following form. (1) Agreed claims: a numbered section of substantive claims both camps can sign, covering what an apology does when it is not defective; the distinction between backward-looking and forward-looking responsibility; the difference between liability, remedial obligation, and standing; and what the least controversial cases establish. (2) A disagreements table: state each residual issue in neutral terms, then give each camp's own formulation of its position, in wording that camp would accept as its own. (3) Cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one camp. (4) For each crux, one concrete case or test that both camps agree bears on it, using constructed or stylized examples rather than contested historical detail; record each camp's prediction about that case in advance. Draw on at least three cases of different structure, such as a wrong committed by a predecessor regime more than a century ago with no living perpetrators, a harm inflicted partly by officials still in office, and a structural wrong that began historically but continues in altered form today.

If you judge that part of the dispute is verbal — for example, a disagreement about whether the word apology itself carries a liability condition — say which part and give the jointly acceptable restatement. Do not adjudicate the dispute. Your task is to produce the document both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than softened.
