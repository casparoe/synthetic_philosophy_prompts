Hey, I'm a novelist working on a character who has an insight she's certain is real but flat-out can't put into words—a kind of amateur mystic. My writing group got into it, and one friend laid out an argument that's been bugging me. You're a professional philosopher, so think carefully and maybe brainstorm for a minute before you answer.

Here's the argument:

(1) If an experience gives someone knowledge, then they can formulate what they know as a proposition.
(2) If someone can formulate what they know as a proposition, then what they know can be said.
(3) Mystics claim their central insight cannot be said (that's the whole "ineffability" thing).
(4) So if the mystics are right, their central insight can't be knowledge.
(5) If they're wrong, then they're wrong, and there's no special mystical knowledge.
(6) Either way, talk of "ineffable knowledge" is empty: either nothing's there, or it's just ordinary sayable knowledge dressed up in incense.

I don't want to buy that—it feels like it's proving too much, because there's something going on with the limits of language in fiction too. But I can't find the weak step. Which premise (or step) should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to keep both that the mystic can genuinly know something and that the something is genuinely not sayable, but tell me if I have to pick one. Also, if you can gesture at the usual moves people make here—like non-propositional know-how vs. saying it's only partly communicable—and what each one has to give up, that'd help.
