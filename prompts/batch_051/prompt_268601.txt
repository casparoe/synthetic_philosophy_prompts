You are a grader for an undergraduate philosophy course. Below are an exam question and one student's answer. Assign a letter grade to the answer and provide point-by-point comments that justify the grade. The comments should identify exactly which parts of the answer are correct and which are mistaken, explain why, and indicate how each point bears on the grade; do not give only a general impression.

Exam question:

In public debates about whether God exists, participants often speak as though "atheism" and "agnosticism" are points on a single spectrum, and as though one side must carry the burden of proof. Consider the following argument:

"The theist asserts that God exists; the atheist merely lacks belief in God; therefore the burden of proof falls entirely on the theist, and, absent sufficient evidence, the default rational position is atheism."

Write an essay that (a) distinguishes the relevant senses of "atheism" and "agnosticism"; (b) distinguishes a procedural burden of proof from an epistemic burden of proof; and (c) assesses whether this argument succeeds once those distinctions are in place, addressing at least one serious objection.

Student's answer:

A theist believes that God exists. Atheism divides into two forms: strong atheism asserts that God does not exist, while weak atheism is merely the absence of belief in God. Agnosticism is a position about knowledge: the agnostic holds that it is unknown, or perhaps unknowable, whether God exists. The argument in the question clearly presupposes weak atheism, because it describes the atheist as one who "merely lacks belief."

We must also separate two senses of "burden of proof." A procedural burden concerns who must open the debate or make the first case; an epistemic burden concerns who must support an asserted claim with evidence before others are rationally required to accept it. A strong atheist asserts a negative existential claim, so a strong atheist carries an epistemic burden for that assertion. The weak atheist, however, asserts nothing about God, so she has no assertion for which she could carry an epistemic burden. The theist does assert something, so the entire burden of proof rests with the theist.

This is the point behind the presumption of atheism. It parallels the presumption of innocence in a criminal trial: the prosecution must prove guilt, and when it fails, the correct verdict is not guilty. Similarly, when the theist fails to prove God's existence, the correct default position is atheism — that is, we should believe, or at least proceed as if, God does not exist. And since agnostics also lack belief in God, agnosticism is best classified as a species of weak atheism.

The most serious objection is the symmetry objection: if theists must prove a positive existential claim, why aren't weak atheists required to prove the corresponding negative claim? But this objection misfires, because the weak atheist makes no denial; she only withholds belief, and withholding belief requires no evidence. Therefore, when the dispute is framed as theism versus weak atheism, the burden of proof lies entirely with the theist, and atheism stands as the correct default.
