Longtime reader of rationalist blogs here (LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias comment threads, some ACX). Something that keeps happening to me is that I'll make a case online for curiosity being underrated—like, we should fund more curiosity-driven work, or we should resist the reflex of asking "who would even want to know that"—and somebody replies with a smug "curiosity killed the cat." Sometimes they add "but satisfaction brought it back," if they're feeling cute. I want to actually dissect this instead of reflexively rolling my eyes.

My suspicion is that the proverb is doing a motte-and-bailey: the defensible motte is "some acts of curiosity are imprudent," and the bailey is "curiosity isn't a good trait, so we shouldn't promote it." But I'd like a clean menu on the table before I accuse anyone of that.

So: as a professional philosopher working in virtue epistemology and the value of knowledge, please think carefully and map out the different precise claims "curiosity killed the cat" could be making, from the trivial to the radical. Start with a brainstorming pass where you list candidate readings, then give me the final menu. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false, and say what it commits someone to. The menu should seperate out at least the following:

- A purely prudential reading (curiosity sometimes has bad consequences for the curious agent). Does that reading have any leverage at all against the claim that knowledge has final value, or that curiosity is an intellectual virtue?

- A stronger reading where the proverb is a verdict on curiosity as a character trait. Courage can get you killed too and is still a virtue; so what extra premise would the proverb need for it to actually count against curiosity being a virtue, and is that extra premise defensible?

- A reading where the real concern is costs to other people (nosiness, privacy) or information hazards (dangerous knowledge). Is that the same claim as the cat one, or a different proverb wearing the same coat?

- What "satisfaction brought it back" is doing. Is the satisfaction an epistemic good (getting the truth), or just relief from an itchy uncertainty? Why does the difference matter for the value-of-knowledge side of things?

- The worthless-truths problem: if some truths just aren't worth knowing (the grass-blade-counting cases), does that mean curiosity isn't always good after all, and if you deny that, are you stuck saying every idle question is worth pursuing?

Finally, tell me which reading the person on the forum most likely meant, and which reading would actually be doing the argumentative work in that exchange. No citations, no reading list—I want the philosophy, not a bibliography. Answer in American English.
