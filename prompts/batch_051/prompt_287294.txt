I am a retired physicist rather than a professional philosopher, though I have spent the last few years working through the philosophy of measurement and value theory, and I keep returning to the way the sciences and the popular press rank civilizations. On the scheme Nikolai Kardashev introduced, a civilization is graded by the rate at which it puts energy to use: Type I commands roughly the power arriving at its home planet from its star, Type II the full output of its star, and Type III the output of its galaxy. The cosmologist John Barrow later proposed the natural complement: grade a civilization by the smallest scale at which it can deliberately manipulate matter — engineering at the scale of tools, then cells, molecules, atoms, nuclei, and below. Both schemes are presented, even by careful writers, as if they measured a single monotone quantity called "advancement." Yet they are very strange instruments. A civilization that doubles the energy efficiency of its economy would fall on Kardashev's scale rather than rise; a civilization that perfects atomic-scale lithography only to manufacture commemorative dust advances on Barrow's scale exactly as far as one that uses the same capability for medicine; and the logic of Kardashev promotion points toward dissipating ever larger fluxes of energy, waste heat included, whatever the energy is for.

I would like this to be a piece of satire rather than an essay arguing the point. Please write a parody of an ISO-style compliance self-assessment form, roughly 700 words, deadpan, in the voice of a standards body administering a joint Kardashev–Barrow certification scheme for civilizations. Call it something like "Form KD-B: Application for Certification as an Advanced Civilization." The target should be the practice of turning the multi-dimensional, value-laden notion of advancement into a single capability metric — not any individual scientist. The form should take the ranking logic with total bureaucratic seriousness: items that award points for gross energy throughput and deduct them for efficiency; a section on small-scale mastery that asks only how small a structure the applicant can pattern, never what the patterning is for; a schedule of progressive levels; and an appeals clause in which a civilization may claim to be advanced on other grounds — welfare, knowledge, justice, durability — and the form ensures the appeal fails by its own internal procedure. The objections must never be stated; they should be felt through the form's requirements. The humor has to come from the real physics and the real logic of the scales — energy balances, entropy and waste heat, the difference between capability and welfare, the oddity of treating a vector of goods as a scalar ranking — so that a specialist finds it fair and a reader who has never heard of Kardashev or Barrow can still read it as plausibly absurd.
