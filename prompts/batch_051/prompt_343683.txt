I'm taking a philosophy of language course and we've been doing slurs, and I'm realizing my gut reactions don't fit either of the clean positions we covered (the derogatory force is part of the word's literal meaning vs. it's all about how its used). I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures my verdicts. Here they are, roughly sorted from clearest to murkiest:

- A straight news anchor says "queer community" in a segment about Pride. Fine. It reads as neutral/descriptive to me now, not as a slur.
- A different anchor, reporting on a racist attack, reads out the attacker's words and says the full n-word once. Not fine. She should say "a racial slur" or "the n-word."
- My roommate, who is Black, texts the n-word (with the -a ending) to his brother. Totally fine, and I'd never dream of objecting.
- A white friend sings along to a song whose lyric contains the n-word at karaoke. Not fine, even though he's "just singing along."
- A white actor in a period drama plays a racist cop and says the full word in character. I think that's fine. The film isn't endorsing the character, and I'd defend the filmmaker.
- A linguistics lecturer writes the full n-word on the board as an example while explaining slurs. Not fine to me. She should use a euphemism, and maybe a asterisk-y spelling at most.
- Someone says, speaking of no actual person, "if the new hire turns out to be," and then uses the full slur, "I'll resign." Still not fine. The fact that it's inside an "if" doesn't sanitize it at all.
- On "Karen": I genuinely can't decide. Using it for a specific woman who demanded to see the manager neither shocks me nor sits right with me. Is it a slur or just an insult?

I started with the simple principle that a slur is a word that literally encodes a derogatory claim about a group, so any use of it is bad. My own cases seem to kill that (queer community, in-group use). Then I tried the principle that whether a use is okay comes down to the speaker's standing to use the word. But then the actor, lecturer, and karaoke cases become a mess: they're all out-group people "just repeating" the word in some ritual, and I judge them differently.

So here's what I'm after. What principle, or small set of candidate principles, gets as many of my verdicts as possible? Please state it precisely and say which case or cases motivate each clause. Then tell me what it predicts about a couple cases I didn't list — say, a non-Black kid reading aloud a passage from a novel that contains the full word in English class, or a dictionary that prints the full word as a headword. And be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, if any — I'd rather know which one I should give up than have a theory that just gerrymanders around all eight.
