In a seminar on Kant's theoretical philosophy, the professor kept returning to a sentence from the Critique of Pure Reason whose gist I have seen translated in several different ways, but which always says roughly that thoughts without content are empty, while intuitions without concepts are blind. She used it first to explain why neither sensory input alone nor conceptual activity alone amounts to cognition, and later to dismiss a question about whether non-human animals might have some kind of conceptual understanding without language. Her claim was that the second half of Kant's remark already settles the matter, since intuitions without concepts are blind, and animal perception therefore cannot count as cognition.

I am not convinced the sentence settles anything, because it seems to compress several different claims whose meanings shift depending on how one reads them. Lay out the distinct precise theses this dictum might be taken to express: for example, claims about the mutual dependence of sensory receptivity and spontaneous thought, about whether either faculty can operate without the other, about whether their contributions are separable in principle or only in analysis, and about what kind of blindness or emptiness is actually at issue. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial point about the structure of cognition, a substantive Kantian commitment, or something that would be false on standard contemporary views of the mind. Then tell me which reading, if any, would support the professor's use of the remark against animal conceptual understanding, and whether that reading survives scrutiny once the ambiguous terms are pinned down.
