Consider how one and the same inductive move is treated differently depending on which famous catastrophe it invokes. When someone says a rising or revisionist power must be confronted now rather than appeased, the Munich analogy is received as historical wisdom: just as Chamberlain and the other Western leaders failed to stop Hitler over the Sudetenland in 1938 and got a larger war, so a failure to stand firm today will only make the eventual war worse. But when someone argues against escalation by invoking Vietnam — escalation in a country whose politics outsiders understand poorly, with no clear front line and no clear victory condition, tends to become a quagmire — many of the same people dismiss it as a tired cliché, as if invoking Vietnam is not an argument but an admission that one has run out of ideas. Alongside both sits the so-called Thucydides trap: the claim that when a rising power meets an established one, war is the historical norm. That idea is treated as deep structural wisdom in talk about the United States and China, even though it is an induction from a small and contested set of past power transitions, exactly the kind of base-rate-from-a-few-cases reasoning people would reject in almost any other domain.

These three moves share the same form: this case resembles one famous past case in a few salient respects, so expect the famous outcome. Yet Munich sounds like learning, Vietnam sounds like a cliché, and the Thucydides trap sounds like a law of history. Is that asymmetry justified? The strongest candidate justification would be that the underlying mechanisms differ: perhaps the cost of underreacting to a revisionist power is systematically worse than the cost of overreacting, or perhaps appeasement and escalation traps have different dynamics that make one analogy a tool and the other a bias.
