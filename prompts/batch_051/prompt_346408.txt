So I had a long, going-nowhere argument last night with a friend about whether a word like bald has an exact boundary. He said it obviously doesnt and that's the end of it; I said if every sentence has to be either true or false, there'd have to be some single last hair that tips a guy into baldness, even if nobody could ever know which one—otherwise sentences calling someone bald would be neither true nor false and ordinary logic takes a hit. We never got anywhere, and the whole time I was thinking that the real version of this fight isn't about piles of sand, it's baked into how the legal system actually operates.

Here's the thing. Courts constantly apply vague statutory language—reasonable doubt, obscenity, unfit parent, reasonable time—and they hand down flat yes-or-no rulings as though those words have determinate content. When an appellate court interprets a vague term, lower courts then cite its gloss as binding, as if the statute always had that exact edge. The same legal system also has a void for vagueness doctrine that strikes down laws when they fail to give ordinary people fair notice of what is prohibited, which suggests vagueness can make a law invalid, not merely hard to know. And right beside all that, the law routinely invents bright lines—age 18 for voting, 0.08 blood alcohol, ten-day filing deadlines—and everybody treats those as arbitrary stipulations, not discoveries.

So what theory of vagueness does this mixed practice presuppose? Is the system secretly epistemicist—vague legal terms already have sharp boundaries we can't access, and courts are doing their best to find them? Is it supervaluationist—borderline applications are genuinely neither true nor false, and an appellate gloss works as an authorized precisification that later courts treat as law? Or is the practice just inconsistent, treating standards as discovered when they are really stipulated? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories), and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say what would actually have to change in how courts write opinions and apply precedent if the system openly adopted a consistent supervaluationist view of legal vagueness, and whether the void for vagueness doctrine would still make sense under that view.
