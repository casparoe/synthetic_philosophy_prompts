I'm putting together the first week of a undergrad seminar called "Cultures of Capitalism," and I want to pair Robinson Crusoe with Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. I've taught the novel before, but I've never had a good way into the question of whether Defoe's whole book is basically a Weberian document. In chapter 2, Weber quotes Benjamin Franklin as an almost classical expression of the spirit of capitalism, and then he quotes this bit of advice from Franklin:

"Beware of thinking all your own that you possess, and of living accordingly. It is a mistake that many people who have credit fall into. To prevent this, keep an exact account for some time both of your expenses and your income. If you take the pains at first to mention particulars, it will have this good effect: you will discover how wonderfully small, trifling expenses mount up to large sums, and will discern what might have been, and may for the future be saved, without occasioning any great inconvenience."

Then Weber says something like the real point isn't mere greed, but a kind of ethical duty to increase one's capital, an ethos. This is where I get stuck. Robinson Crusoe is full of money, account books, inventories, and rational use of time. It seems like he acts like Franklin's ideal tradesman even when there's no market, no credit, no one to impress. So is Defoe staging a secular, Protestant-economic ethic before Weber names it? But Robinson hardly has a this-worldly asceticism — he enjoys his comforts once he has them. If the novel already shows that this economic rationality can exist without the religious motivation, doesn't that cause trouble for Weber's claim that Protestantism was a cause? Or does the problem go the other way: maybe Crusoe's bookkeeping is just how a certain kind of commercial culture narrates itself, and Defoe doesn't give us any evidence that values really did any causal work?
