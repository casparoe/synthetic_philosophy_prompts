I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I mangle the terms. I'm writing a book set in a city that's been shrinking for years. One faction wants to lure in new residents almost at any cost to the quality of life of the people already there; the other says the city would be better with fewer people if the ones who stay are better off. Underneath their fight there's clearly a distinction between judging a population by its average wellbeing and judging it by its total wellbeing, but I dont trust my grasp of it, and I especially don't know how to map it onto how ordinary people actually argue about whether a bigger family, a bigger city, or a bigger species is a good thing.

Can you explain how the average and total views actually come apart when population size changes? I want the test cases, not the intro survey: adding people whose lives are genuinely worth living but below the current average, so the average falls while total wellbeing rises; the small, very well-off population versus the big, middling one that beats it on total but loses on average; and the notorious puzzle where total value seems to prefer an enormous population living lives barely worth living over a smaller, much happier one. Is that last one a bug, a feature, or just a sign the view is maximising the wrong thing? And the part I care most about for the book: when real people fight about whether a city or a species should be larger, is there a way to tell whether their intuitions are tracking totals, averages, or something else entirely — some person-affecting view, maybe, where it matters whether you're making existing people happier or just making more people? What would the telltale patterns be? I don't need citations or a survey; I need the conceptual guts, because I want the disagreement between my characters to be a genuine conceptual split rather than two people talking past each other.
