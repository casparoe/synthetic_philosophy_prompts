I work as a software engineer on a documentation tooling team. For the past year we have run a prose linter on every merge request that touches user-facing documentation. The linter is configured with a hand-maintained ruleset: it fails the build whenever it encounters a sentence in the passive voice, a split infinitive, the pronoun they used with a singular antecedent, or a count noun modified by less instead of fewer. Those rules were pulled years ago from a widely used style manual, and ever since they have been treated in our pipeline as non-negotiable definitions of correct grammar.

In parallel, the editors at the company use a commercial grammar checker and autocomplete layer whose machine-learning model was trained on a very large corpus of published English. That model routinely leaves unchanged, and sometimes itself suggests, exactly the constructions the linter treats as build-breaking errors. A linguist I know tells me the linter's rules are prestige conventions from one register of English, not facts about the structure of the language as it is actually used.

I am trying to understand what our tooling actually commits us to. The build gate behaves as though one register's conventions were facts about the language itself; the editor tool behaves as though grammaticality is mostly a matter of what speakers and writers in fact do. What view of grammar does a tool like our linter presuppose? Could it be coherent under the view that grammar is simply a summary of widespread usage, or does it strictly require the idea that some external standard stands above common use? Reconstruct the implicit theory of grammar behind this kind of linter, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit with, and say where the design is underdetermined. In particular, what would actually have to change if we rebuilt the tooling around the usage-based view, and what could stay the same?
