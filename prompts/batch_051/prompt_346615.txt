I have been persuaded — or nearly persuaded — that for most wild animals, life contains more suffering than enjoyment. Disease, parasitism, predation, and starvation kill the vast majority before maturity, and even the survivors face chronic hunger, injury, and stress. From this premise, I have come to regard the view that habitat destruction is an uncomplicated moral catastrophe as a lazy default: destroying species-rich habitat may be welfare-positive, or at least much less bad than conservationists assume, because it prevents vast amounts of future animal suffering. I want an honest map of what would count against this position. If it is wrong, what exactly has to be true? Tell me which observations and arguments would outright refute it, which would merely make it awkward, and which are only logically possible escape routes rather than live ones. Say what a defender of the view should themselves concede as counting against it — not just what critics say. And assess whether the position has been insulated from falsification by hedging (about our uncertainty in measuring net welfare, or about the other goods intact ecosystems provide); if it has, is that insulation a problem, or an appropriate response to genuine uncertainty? For what it is worth, I would be most moved by a direct, non-question-begging argument that representative wild animal lives are on balance worth living, not by appeals to the beauty or intrinsic value of intact nature. Answer in British English. End with a single line of the form \answer{...}, where the enclosed label is one of: highly exposed, moderately exposed, well insulated.
