Question: Is self-deception possible? In particular, does self-deception require a person to hold contradictory beliefs, and can the project of deceiving oneself get off the ground? Discuss at least one account on which self-deception appears paradoxical and at least one account on which it does not.

Student answer:

Self-deception faces two distinct problems. The static problem is that the self-deceiver would have to believe both p and not-p at once: she must believe not-p in order to know what she is hiding from herself, yet believe p if the deception succeeds. The dynamic problem is that if she deliberately sets out to make herself believe p, she must already know p is false, and that very knowledge would undermine the attempt from the start.

Sartre's discussion of bad faith in Being and Nothingness captures both problems. On his view the person in bad faith is, like a liar, in possession of the truth she hides; the only difference is that the person deceived is herself. She therefore plays the roles of deceiver and deceived simultaneously and holds the truth and its denial at once, which is why bad faith is a precarious, unstable state.

Alfred Mele's deflationary account is meant to dissolve these problems. Mele denies that self-deception requires an intention to deceive oneself, and he denies that it requires believing not-p. Instead, a person is self-deceived when she ends up with a false belief as a result of motivationally biased treatment of evidence: she discounts or avoids unfavorable evidence, seeks out and overweights favorable evidence, or interprets ambiguous evidence in a convenient direction. Since no contradictory belief is required, the static problem does not arise; since the process is not intentionally aimed at producing the false belief, the dynamic problem does not arise either.

It follows that on Mele's view self-deception just is wishful thinking—a false belief caused by desire. And because wishful thinking is plainly possible, self-deception is plainly possible, with nothing left for the paradoxes to grip.

The only remaining worry is that such a person looks merely biased rather than self-deceived, so the deflationary account succeeds only by changing the subject. But that worry presupposes the very requirement—simultaneous contradictory beliefs—that the deflationary account has already shown can be dropped.
