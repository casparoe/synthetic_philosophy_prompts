I've been teaching myself some neo-Confucian philosophy on the side (mostly to annoy a friend who thinks the only real philosophy happens in journals with 'analytic' in the title), and the other day we got into an argument about Wang Yangming's doctrine that knowledge and action are one. My friend reconstructed what he's pleased to call a little 'master argument' against it, and I half-suspect there's a trick in it, but I cant put my finger on the exact step. The argument runs like this:

(1) If knowledge and action are genuinely one, then whoever genuinely knows the good is already acting on it. (2) But there are perfectly ordinary cases where someone acknowledges that a course of action is right and yet doesn't do it - the person who says they know they should visit their parents more, but never does. (3) If knowing just were acting, such a gap couldn't exist. (4) So a defender of the unity view has to say the person in (2) didn't genuinely know the good. (5) But by (1), 'didn't genuinely know' can only be cashhed out as 'didn't act', since knowing is being defined in terms of acting. (6) So the doctrine either denies the plain phenomenon of weakness of will or empties 'knowing the good' of any independent content, leaving it just a re-description of acting well.

He says the argument is valid and the only way out is to reject (1), which he thinks is just giving the doctrine up. I'm not so sure - it feels like the word 'know' shifts between two senses somewhere, maybe between mouthing the words and actually knowing in the way Wang meant. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what exactly is the equivocation? Also, how should (1) be read so that the unity of knowledge and action stays a substantive claim rather than collapsing into a definition? Please write the answer in British English.
