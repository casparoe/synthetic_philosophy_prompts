The Editorial Rules of the Meridian Mathematical Society, adopted in 1961, contain this clause:

"Rule 7(c). A paper that announces the proof of a theorem may be accepted for publication only if one of the referees appointed by the Editors has followed the proof in its entirety, has examined each step of the reasoning, and certifies that each step is valid."

The editors now receive a submission that claims to prove the four-colour theorem. The reasoning has two parts. The first is an ordinary paper-and-pencil argument showing that the theorem is proved if every member of a specified family of roughly two thousand maps is "reducible." The second part reports that an algorithm for testing reducibility was written, that it was run on an electronic computer over several months, and that it returned the needed verdict for every member of the family. The execution itself is not printed and would take longer than a human lifetime to check by hand; the paper supplies the algorithm, the source code, and a machine-generated log of the final outputs. One referee has followed the paper-and-pencil argument in its entirety. A second referee has checked the algorithm's design, and a programmer has inspected the code line by line. But no human being has followed, or could feasibly follow, the computation itself step by step.

Does Rule 7(c) require the editors to reject the paper, or does it permit them to accept it? Argue the question from (1) the plain wording of the rule, (2) the purpose the rule exists to serve, (3) what the 1961 drafters would most likely have said had this case been put to them, and (4) what a reasonable mathematician would today take the rule to mean. Then tell me which of these approaches ought to control here, and why. Would your answer differ if the case-checking had been done by a modern proof assistant such as Lean, whose small proof-checking kernel had itself been manually inspected and formally proved correct?
