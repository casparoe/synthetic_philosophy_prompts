I've been cataloging my own choices among monetary gambles with known probabilities and trying to find one principle behind them rather than a list of one-off intuitions. Here are my verdicts, all reported honestly.

1. Insurance. I pay $250 a year to fully insure against a 1% annual chance of losing $10,000, even though the expected loss is only $200. I am knowingly paying more than the expected money value, and I'd do it again.

2. Moderate gain. I would take $800 for sure over a 50/50 gamble between $2,000 and nothing.

3. Moderate loss. I would take a 50/50 gamble between losing $2,000 and losing nothing over losing $800 for sure. The sure loss repels me more than the risky option does.

4. Allais pair. In one choice I would take $1,000,000 for sure over a ticket that pays $5,000,000 with probability .10, $1,000,000 with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01. In a separate choice I would take a ticket that pays $5,000,000 with probability .10 and otherwise nothing, over a ticket that pays $1,000,000 with probability .11 and otherwise nothing. I can see that the second choice looks like the first choice with the common .89 chance of $1,000,000 removed, and I still choose the way I do.

5. Long-shot gain. I occasionally buy a $2 lottery ticket with one chance in 300,000,000 of winning $200,000,000, for an expected payout of about $0.67. I can do the arithmetic; I buy it now and then anyway.

My working theory was that I maximize expected utility with an increasing, concave utility function over final wealth. That seems to fit the insurance and the moderate-gain choice, but I cannot see how it fits the rest: the Allais pair appears to violate the independence axiom outright, the moderate-loss choice looks like risk-seeking in losses, and the lottery ticket looks like a taste for negative-expected-value long shots. I have heard of two candidate repairs. One is some version of risk-weighted expected utility, which keeps a single utility function over final wealth but uses a risk function so that bad outcomes receive more than their raw probabilistic weight. The other is a reference-dependent account with a value function that is concave for gains and convex for losses, along with transformed decision weights. I do not want whichever label sounds most flattering; I want the principle.

State the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my five verdicts. Show how it handles each case, including the insurance-and-lottery combination and the Allais pair. Be explicit about which verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would overturn, and say how much of a cost you take those revisions to be. Then apply your preferred principle to a case where my judgment is not settled: a gamble with a 25% chance of $10,000, a 25% chance of $5,000, and a 50% chance of nothing, against a guaranteed $2,500. What does the principle say I should choose, and do you think that is the right answer? If the request itself is confused — for example, if I am sliding between a descriptive account of what I in fact choose and a normative account of what I should choose, or if no single principle should be expected to cover both a $2 lottery ticket and a $1,000,000 Allais gamble — say so plainly and do what can still be done rather than smoothing it over.
