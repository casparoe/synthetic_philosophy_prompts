A scholarly society's flagship journal, frustrated that referees routinely demand standards of rigor they do not meet in their own published work, adopts the following rule. Any referee who recommends rejection on the ground that a submission exhibits a methodological or reporting flaw must certify, as part of the report, that none of the referee's own published papers exhibit that flaw. If the author can demonstrate, by citing the referee's published work, that the referee has committed the same flaw, the editor must discard the report, and the referee is barred from reviewing for that journal for two years. The editors justify the rule by appeal to the idea that a person who commits a wrong lacks standing to blame others for it. Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position: analyze how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among reviewers, authors, and editors? Which flaws become safe to raise and which become unsayable? Where is the system gamed first, and who bears the costs of that gaming? Finally, say whether the failure modes can be fixed—by refining the definition of the same flaw, allowing recusals, penalizing bad-faith author challenges, or otherwise—or whether the rule collapses under its own weight.
