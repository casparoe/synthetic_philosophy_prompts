I am a PhD student in public law, and I have been trying to make precise an argument I have encountered in discussions of sovereign immunity and state responsibility. The argument, as I reconstruct it, runs as follows:

(1) To do a wrong is to perform an action for which some agent may justifiably be held answerable.
(2) The state is not an agent in that demanding sense: it has no unified practical standpoint of its own, and any action attributed to it decomposes without remainder into the actions of individual officeholders.
(3) Therefore no action can be attributed to the state in a way that would make the state itself answerable for it.
(4) Thus the state cannot do wrong, and the phrase “the state wronged them” is at best compressed talk about wrongdoing by officials.

I suspect the argument is valid only if “answerable” is being used equivocally, but I cannot locate the equivocation, nor can I tell whether the conclusion follows from (1) and (2). Please lay out the argument’s logical form, evaluate each step on its strongest interpretation, and identify the exact step, if any, that fails. If the argument is valid, say whether the conclusion is merely a legal point or has moral force as well. American English, please.
