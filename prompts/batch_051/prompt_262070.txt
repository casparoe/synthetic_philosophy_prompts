Until recently I would have called this a tangent, not a work problem. I am a software engineer; most of my professional life is in Haskell, and this quarter I rewrote our group's exact-arithmetic library so that a real number is represented as a computable Cauchy sequence of rationals, with a modulus of convergence and with equality carried separately as a setoid relation, because the language has no quotient types. In the design meetings, two factions formed. One faction says our Cauchy sequences and the rival Dedekind-cut encoding are both just imperfect shadow-castings of something already there: the real line, a completed continuum whose properties hold whether or not anyone encodes them. The other faction says there is no such thing over and above the constructions; to speak of a real number apart from some chosen definition is, as they put it, a category error. The argument has started to feel like it matters: if the first faction is right, our library is an instrument for approximate perception; if the second is right, it is the thing itself under an interface.

I want you to restate this "discovered or defined?" dispute about the real numbers and the continuum in the vocabulary I actually work in: types and terms, quotient types and the setoid encoding of quotients, total versus partial functions, computability, decidable versus undecidable equality, inductive versus coinductive data (streams, infinite expansions), canonical forms, and extensional equivalence between representations. Work through the main positions one by one. For each, say what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then give the residue report: what, if anything, is left over once the translation is done, and is that leftover a sign that my vocabulary is missing something, or a sign that the original position was leaning on a picture rather than a claim?

I suspect the whole dispute is just "the reals are a quotient type over Cauchy sequences, and the rest is interface design." Treat that as a claim to be tested by the translation, not as a conclusion: does my formulation capture the discovered/defined debate, or does it quietly settle it by assuming that construction is all there is?
