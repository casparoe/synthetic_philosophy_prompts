I've been going back and forth with a friend in phil of physics, and I've mostly come around to taking many-worlds seriously, so now I keep stubbing my toe on quantum immortality. The argument, as he puts it: if decoherence keeps producing branches, then in any situation where I might die there's at least one branch where the quantum coin keeps landing the right way and I pull through. Since I can only ever register the branches where a me exists to do the registering, I should expect my own experience to never end — the quantum suicide version just makes it vivid. It has that awful smell of something that cant be argued with, which is exactly why I want to know what would argue with it.

I guess I'm asking: what would have to be true for quantum immortality to be wrong, assuming many-worlds itself holds? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to distinguish the ones that only bite if you reject Everettian QM from the ones that bite even inside it. I already know the obvious candidate — the Born rule says I should care about branch weights, not the bare existence of a survivor branch — but I don't know how much work that actually does. I also keep wondering whether 'alive' and 'dead' even pick out a stable branch, since decoherence selects a pointer basis that might not line up with my medical status, and whether the survivor in that branch is really me or just a branching-successor with my memories. I'd also like a straight answer on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability (every apparent death is just someone else's problem), and whether that's a problem for a claim that's ultimately about first-person experience rather than a third-person prediction. And please write the response in American English.
