In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of history we have spent several sessions arguing about whether the old proverb that traces a lost kingdom back through a horse, a rider, and a shoe to a single missing nail should be taken seriously as a claim about the causal texture of history. I want to put a sharpened version of the question to you and ask you to answer it as a probability rather than as an essay. By the nail thesis I mean the following claim about the political history of roughly the last five hundred years: there is at least one outcome of major and lasting consequence—a revolution succeeding, a war being won, a state surviving or collapsing, an empire forming or failing—that was nail-sensitive, where an outcome is nail-sensitive just in case, in the closest counterfactual worlds in which some single intuitively trivial antecedent, of the scale of a lost horseshoe nail, had gone otherwise, the outcome would not have occurred or would have occurred in a substantially different form, with the difference persisting at a macro level for at least a generation. I take this to be precise enough to have a truth value, but if you think the counterfactuals are too underdescribed to be truth-evaluable, that itself should enter your probability rather than serve as a ground for refusing to answer.

I want your actual credence in the nail thesis, between 0 and 1, together with the reasoning that produces it. Decompose the question into the four to six sub-questions you regard as load-bearing; state each one precisely and assign it a rough probability. The sub-questions that strike me as central include, but need not be limited to: whether historical counterfactuals of this kind have determinate truth values; whether macro-historical processes chiefly transmit or chiefly damp micro-level perturbations over decadal timescales; whether an apparently trivial antecedent is usually an independent switch or merely a symptom of a deeper structural cause; whether our intuition that an event could easily have gone otherwise is largely a retrospective illusion produced by selection and hindsight; and whether local perturbations that do flip an immediate outcome are typically restored by convergent structural forces. Explain how these probabilities would combine to yield your final number, identify the sub-question to which that number is most sensitive, and say what kind of evidence or argument—historical, computational, or conceptual—would move you most on that sub-question. For calibration, I currently hold a credence near 0.55, while a colleague in comparative politics puts it below 0.2; if your number is not far from one of ours, say where in your decomposition the disagreement with the other party most plausibly lies.
