You are a moral and political philosopher who works on collective action and democratic theory. Think through the problem slowly; steelman both positions before you reach a verdict, and do not end non-committally.

I find myself pulled between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I cannot shake.

The theory is a rigorously instrumental account of individual voting. In any moderately large election, the chance that a single vote changes the outcome is vanishingly small — smaller, plausibly, than the chance of being injured en route to the polls. So the expected instrumental value of one vote is close to zero. The account draws a tidy conclusion: there is no serious beneficence-based duty to vote for the better candidate, since no individual vote can be expected to bring about the better outcome. Any remaining reasons to vote are expressive or participatory — affirmations of identity or value, not acts of harm prevention.

The intuition is this. In a high-stakes election in a competitive district, where one viable candidate is likely to cause serious avoidable harm — eroding rights, deliberately inflicting suffering on vulnerable groups, damaging fragile institutions — I do not merely think it would be good to vote for the less harmful viable candidate. I think I am required to, even though I endorse neither candidate and even though lesser-evil voting feels like lending my name to things I reject. I would judge someone who stayed home in such an election to have done something wrong, not merely something regrettable or distasteful. And the judgment tracks the stakes: I do not feel it in low-stakes races. Yet the instrumental account says the stakes cannot matter, because my single vote moves the outcome no more in the high-stakes case than in the low-stakes case.

So I have a compelling theory with systematic virtues and a firm intuition that it contradicts. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: what it would take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped; what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned; and whether there is a reading of duty, expression, and lesser-evil voting on which both the negligible-instrumental-value point and the intuition survive.

Your essay should address at least these candidate resolutions, saying for each what would justify it and where it fails: (1) debunking the intuition as a cognitive bias — over-weighting the tiny chance of being decisive, or confusing individual efficacy with group efficacy; (2) relocating the duty to collectivities or shared undertakings, so that an individual vote is doing one's part even if no single part is decisive; (3) distinguishing a duty to vote from a duty to vote well, so the lesser-evil point survives even if the duty-to-vote point is surrendered; and (4) an expressive reading on which a lesser-evil vote says something true in context — about comparative urgency or refusing to be counted on the side of harm — even when the voter endorses neither candidate.

Reach and justify a definite conclusion, but your conclusion must say what would justify each kind of move, not simply pick a side.
