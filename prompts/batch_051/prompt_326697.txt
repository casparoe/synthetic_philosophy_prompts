I keep running into an asymmetry in how people talk about what its ok to be loved for, and I cant tell if it tracks anything real. If a friend says he loves his girlfriend for her sense of humor or her kindness, everyone approves — those get called deep, good reasons. If another friend says he loves his girlfriend for her looks or her family money, people call it shallow, or gold-digging, or not real love at all. And it goes for being loved too: most of us would hate to hear that our partner loves us for our face or our salary, but we'd be happy to hear they love us for our wit or our patience. The thing is, I don't see a clean difference on any axis I try. Looks and wit are both largely luck; money and patience both depend a lot on circumstances; a partner depressed after an accident can lose their kindness as fast as their looks; and if you're loved for your wit, someone wittier should be able to replace you just as much as a prettier person could if you were loved for your face. So what is actually doing the work — some distinction between character and accident, between qualities that bear on the relationship and qualities that are just properties of the person, between being valued as a person and being valued as a bearer of traits? Or is the whole deep/shallow contrast just snobbery, where 'deep' means 'the qualities I and my friends happen to admire'? Spell out the best candidate justification you can find, test it against a few nearby cases, and say whether the ordinary asymmetry survives or whether we should give it up.
