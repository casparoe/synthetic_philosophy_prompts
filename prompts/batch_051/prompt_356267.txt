I've been poking at Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life after my reading group got through it, and I keep wanting to bolt a fourth category onto the usual three. As I understand them: monumental history hands you models of greatness to emulate; antiquarian history preserves and cherishes the inherited past as the thing that's yours; critical history puts the past on trial and condemns it so you can act without its weight. They all serve life, but they're stances you can take toward the past, with different needs behind them.

I've been calling my addition "runbook history" (sometimes "incident-response history"). It doesn't want great individuals, a heritage to preserve, or a past to break with. It treats history as a queriable database of roughly repeatable situations — the way an SRE reads old postmortems, not to admire the engineers involved but to extract templates for how systems behave under stress. A debt crisis, a movement that ate itself, an institution that survived a succession crisis, a technology that looked ridiculous until it wasn't. The unit is the recurring situation, not the exceptional man, and the payoff is calibration about rare events rather than inspiration or identity. I caught myself doing it with the Paris Commune recently: I didn't want heroes, I wanted a wiring diagram. It feels distinct from monumental history (the hero is swapped out for a pattern) and from critical history (the point is to reuse the past, not escape it). But I can also see the argument that it's just monumental history with the object generalized, or that Nietzsche would say it's not history "for life" at all, just instrumental problem-solving with a fancier name.

So: is "runbook history" actually a fourth mode, or a rebranding of one of the three? Has someone worked this out under a better name? And where does it break — what would it get wrong about how history can or can't serve life?
