I spent thirty years in geochemistry and only started reading philosophy of science after I retired, so be patient if I am reinventing the wheel. The thing that keeps nagging me is the Bayesian problem of old evidence: the idea that a fact you already knew to probability one cannot confirm a new theory, because the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E|not-H) just comes out one against one. But real science is full of old facts coming alive as evidence. The precession of Mercury's perihelion was in the books for decades before general relativity; it did not become evidence only because nobody had noticed it. It became evidence because a new theory suddenly gave it a good run for its money.

So here is my homemade idea, which I have been calling the reopened-case principle. An old datum E confirms a new theory H when H handles E better than the best rival available before H did, where better means: H gives E a higher likelihood, and H does not spend more adjustable parts than the rival had to spend. Confirmation from old evidence is measured against the runner-up, not against the history of the scientist's own state of information. I think this escapes the old-evidence problem without faking ignorance, because you never have to pretend you don't know E. You only have to ask who, before H came along, did best with E, and whether H beats that score without paying extra for it. I have a nagging feeling this is just something already in the literature under a better name, which is fine, I just want to know.

Can you give me an honest assessment? Reconstruct my principle in its strongest form, then tell me whether it is actually distinct from standard moves like lowering or counterfactually erasing your knowledge of E, and whether my insistence on counting adjustable parts is doing real work or is just a side preference. Then give me the three strongest objections to it, and one clear case where it gives the wrong answer. I am not asking for a bibliography, just whether I have talked myself into something false or something boring.
