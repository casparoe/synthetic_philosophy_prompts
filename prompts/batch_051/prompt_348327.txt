I've been going back and forth with a friend about what should count as a translation of a poem, and I'd like you to tell me whether my criteria survive a hard case. Here's what I've been insisting on. Something counts as a translation, as opposed to an adaptation or an imitation, only if it does four things: (a) it carries over the content of the original, the images and claims and the order in which they come; (b) it reproduces the original's formal features where the target language can manage it, so rhyme scheme, meter, stanza shape; (c) it matches the original unit for unit, line for line, with nothing wholesale added or cut; and (d) it stands on its own as a poem in the target language, so a reader who didn't know it was translated could still be moved by it rather then just pulling information out of it. Now the hard case. A translator takes a short, tightly rhymed French poem addressed to a sick girl — she is described as pale, then listless, then slowly getting her color and appetite back — and he produces an English version with exactly the same rhyme scheme, the same syllable count per line, and the same stanza shape, and it reads so well in English that you'd never suspect it was a translation. But he has a strict policy: when the rhyme and meter pull one way and the literal sense pulls the other, sound wins. In this poem they pull against each other constantly. So nearly every image gets nudged a little, and some get swapped outright, to satisfy the form: where the original says her cheeks are white as paper, the English says her cheeks are pale as bone; where the original says she eats a peach at the end, the English says she drinks the light, because that's what the line wanted. Each individual change keeps some thread of the original nearby, but by the end the whole field of imagery has drifted. Criteria (b), (c), and (d) look satisfied; (a) is the problem. Go through all four honestly and say whether you'd call this a translation of the original or a new poem wearing the original's form. Where the criteria conflict, which one should give? Does the case show that (a) is too flat-footed, maybe because for this kind of poem the form is part of the content instead of a detachable shell? And if it isn't a translation, then what did he produce, and would it matter to a reader who knows no French? Please write your answer as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just email-style paragraphs.
