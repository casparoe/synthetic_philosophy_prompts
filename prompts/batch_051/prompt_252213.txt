I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature on why some things are funny. One section of the piece will compare two explanatory traditions in the study of humour. For the reader, I'll gloss them briefly: incongruity accounts hold that laughter is a response to a violated expectation that gets resolved or fitted into an alternative pattern, while benign violation accounts hold that laughter occurs when something is appraised as a norm violation and simultaneously as benign — a mock attack rather than a real one. Rather than repeating textbook contrasts, I want to press both accounts on a single puzzle. Much of what we find odd is incongruous and benign but not funny: a non sequitur, a stranger wearing a lampshade on the bus, a typo that turns a sentence into nonsense. Compare what each account must say to explain why these cases fail to amuse, and judge which account better draws the line between the merely strange and the genuinely funny. Give me exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
