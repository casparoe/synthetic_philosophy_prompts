I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm only reading Spinoza because the book I'm writing has a character who needs to make peace with the fact that her worst mistake was, in some sense, already in her from the start. The dinner-party version of Spinoza I keep hearing runs on an analogy: the world is like a novel that's already been written, God is its author, and everything that happens follows from God's nature the way every later scene follows from the first page. So nothing — not even my character's betrayal in chapter three — could have been otherwise. And the amor Dei intellectualis, the intellectual love of God, gets explained as something like the author's (or the ideal reader's) love of the whole book: not loving it despite the necessity, but loving it because you see why each part had to be exactly what it is. That seeing supposedly is the love.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds, because I'm about to write the scene where she forgives her ex, and I keep reaching for the analogy to justify the ending.

So lay it out for me. Give me the correspondence table: what in Spinoza plays the role of the characters, the plot, the author, the act of writing, the draft that got thrown away, the reader. Then tell me where it breaks. I already suspect a few places: a novelist can revise, can imagine the book going otherwise, and stands outside the story in time, wheras Spinoza's God doesn't deliberate, doesn't have alternatives, and isn't exactly outside the whole thing in the same way. But I want you to separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion — that understanding necessity and loving it is the right response to your own life — from the ones that are just decorative. Does the intellectual-love conclusion survive once the novel analogy is stripped back, or was the analogy doing all the work? And if I shouldn't be using the novel analogy here, what would be a better one for a novelist to think with?
