I spent thirty years in physical chemistry, mostly writing simulation code, and now that I'm retired I've been reading philosophy—lately about identity and change—so this has been nagging at me. I have a homemade theory about why mutation is hard to think about, and I'd like an honest assessment of it.

The idea is that a mutable variable is really two things sharing one name: a cell (a place, something that persists and has an identity of its own) and a value (the contents of the cell right now). I think most of the difficulty with mutation comes from what I'll call "cell-value collapse": we collapse the cell and its current contents into one thing, because in ordinary language and in the math we learn, they mostly are one thing. In an equation, x is not a container that gets refilled; it's a number, full stop. So when I first met x = x + 1, my training made me read it as "the number is one more than itself," which is false, rather than "the cell now holds the successor of its previous contents," which is true. The cell survives the assignment, the value does not. Mutation forces us to keep two kinds of sameness seperate—the cell is the same thing across time while its contents are not—but we only have the one name, x, doing both jobs.

I suspect a lot of familiar bugs are like this. In a loop like x = x + y, you have to track the cell through time while the contents change; beginners freeze and treat the contents as fixed. Aliasing (a = [1,2]; b = a; b.append(3)) is the mirror image: two names open onto the same contents and we mistake sharing for copying. In a pure functional language names are bound once and cells are not reused, so the cell and the value never come apart this way—and I suspect that is the real reason pure code feels clearer, not just the absence of side effects.

So: is "cell-value collapse" a coherent distinction, or have I just reinvented the usual reference-vs-value, == versus is stuff and dressed it up? Does the explanatory claim do any real work—that we inherit the cell's persistence and smear it onto the value—or is it just a nice story? And where does the idea stop being useful? Please start by reconstructing the view in its strongest form, then say what it's actually called if it already exists, then tell me where it breaks.
