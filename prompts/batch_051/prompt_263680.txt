I serve on the ethics committee of a geriatric care hospital. Last night I spent an hour arguing with a friend who teaches philosophy; he maintained that human dignity is a vacuous concept that never adds anything to autonomy and ordinary beneficence, while I kept resisting, wanting to say it marks something more: a constraint on how a person may be treated regardless of what she currently understands or controls. I left unsettled, and this morning the agenda for Thursday's meeting arrived with a case that forces exactly that question. I mention the argument because I do not want my side of it to quietly rig the memo I now have to write.

Mrs. H., 81, has had advanced dementia for seven years and lives in our memory unit. Eight years before her decline she executed an advance directive stating that she did not want artificially provided food and fluids if she ever reached an end-stage condition with no realistic prospect of recovering meaningful awareness. She is now bed-bound, no longer recognizes her family, speaks only rarely and without clear referent, and requires total care; but she tracks faces, quiets at a familiar voice, and sometimes swallows spoon-fed purée. Her attending physician says she retains only minimal, fluctuating awareness and has no prospect of cognitive recovery, and he estimates that without a feeding tube she will likely die within three to six weeks, while a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube could keep her alive for six to twelve months. The unit's palliative consultant is less certain: she notes that in advanced dementia the evidence does not clearly show that tube feeding prolongs life or prevents aspiration, and that careful hand-feeding with comfort measures is a defensible plan. The committee is split.

Her son, who is her healthcare proxy, argues that the directive should govern and that inserting a tube into a woman who cannot comprehend it would be an assault on the person his mother was. Her daughter argues that the directive's awareness condition is not clearly met, that her mother remains minimally aware, and that allowing her to die of inadequate intake would be a failure to protect her dignity as she is now. A senior nurse on the unit has asked that staff's position be considered as well: she does not want care workers to be placed in the role of watching a slow death when a routine tube might prevent it, and she says the unit's ethos is to safeguard resident dignity rather than facilitate dying.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should recommend clearly whether the committee should authorize placement of the feeding tube or adopt a plan of careful hand-feeding, mouth care, and comfort measures only. The recommendation must be actionable, and the reasoning must engage the losing side's strongest dignity-based argument before explaining why it is not decisive. The memo should also take up directly the question of whether dignity is doing genuine normative work in this case, or whether the matter can be settled entirely by autonomy, the advance directive, and an honest weighing of benefit and burden. Where the recommendation depends on a particular reading of the advance directive's language, say so plainly. End with one sentence the committee could adopt as its working account of dignity for future cases, or, if you think no such sentence is possible, explain briefly why the committee should refuse to adopt one.
