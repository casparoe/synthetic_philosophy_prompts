I've spent the last few months in the weeds of a real life version of the killing/letting die problem — my dad went into hospice and I'm the one who signs the forms. What got me is how differently I feel about the ventilator versus the morphine, and I ended up building a little homemade theory to sort out those feelings. I need someone to tell me if its any good, whether it already exists under a respectable name, and where it breaks.

I call it the Counterforce Test. Before you act, look at the causal landscape around the dying person. If you add a new death-directed force to that landscape — a lethal injection, a pillow over the face — that's killing. If you remove a counterforce that is already there and actively holding death off — turning off a ventilator, pulling a feeding tube, stopping dialysis — that's also killing, because you took away the thing that was standing between the person and the death that was already coming at them. If you merely decline to add a counterforce that wasn't there in the first place — you don't jump in the river, you never enroll the patient in the trial, you don't donate a kidney — that's letting die, not killing.

So: add a force, killing. Remove a force, killing. Withhold a new force, letting die.

Under this, turning off my dad's vent was a killing, though a justified one, and I'm okay saying that. But here's where I get uneasy. A doctor prescribes a patient a new drug and forgets to warn them that it interacts badly with a drug they already take. On my own test that looks like merely letting die, because the missing warning is a counterforce that never got added. But I can't shake the feeling that a negligent doctor who introduces a new problem and then fails to warn is worse than a bystander who never got involved at all. If my test can't say that, maybe something's wrong with it.

So three questions: (1) Is the Counterforce Test already a named position in the literature, or am I reenventing a wheel badly? (2) Give me two or three of the strongest counterexamples you can think of and say whether they're fatal or repairable. (3) The negligent doctor case — does my theory really commit me to calling that letting die, and if it does, should I accept that result or amend the test? I'd rather be told the test is broken than keep a flattering mistake.
