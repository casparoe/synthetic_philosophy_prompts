I keep noticing a parallel between two things I learned in different classes and I can't tell whether it's deep or just a shape match.

The first is the money-pump argument for transitivity in decision theory. If you strictly prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone can start you with A, trade you to B for a tiny fee, then to C for a tiny fee, then back to A for a tiny fee. At each step you take the trade because it's strictly preferred; at the end you're back where you started but poorer. So cyclic preferences seem rationally costly in a very concrete way.

The second is the Condorcet paradox behind Arrow's theorem. Each of three voters has a perfectly transitive individual ranking, but pairwise majority vote can give A beats B, B beats C, C beats A. The group's "preference" cycles even though no individual's does.

It feels like the exact same shape: a bunch of local pairwise facts that are each fine on their own, and a global cycle that breaks the thing they were supposed to add up to. And in both cases the same move is available: pin the blame on whatever aggregator you were using. In the decision theory case the aggregator is "the self over time" (or across contexts); in the social choice case it's the voting rule. So is a money pump just Arrow's theorem with the time-slices of one person playing the role of voters? If I model future-me and tired-me as separate voters, is dynamic consistency just a set of social choice axioms in disguise—and would an Arrow-style impossibility result then say that no agent can aggregate its own local preferences without either a dictator time-slice or violating something like IIA? Or does the analogy break becasue a person's time-slices share a memory and can bind each other with commitments in a way that separate voters can't? Spell out the mapping precisely, then tell me where it fails or what it would predict that's clearly false.
