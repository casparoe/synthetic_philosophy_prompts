I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been poking at voting theory for a while now. I'm putting together a workshop that should make people *feel* the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result instead of just hearing it explained. The rough idea—that once you've got three or more options, just about any voting rule that isn't a dictatorship can be gamed by someone misrepresenting their actual ranking—sounds like a technicality until you've been the person who realizes you can get a better outcome by lying on the ballot, and after that you can't unsee it. I want to get a room full of people to that point.

The setting: a 75-minute session at a local science festival, about 18–24 adults, mostly non-experts who are curious about math and democracy but haven't seen any of this before. I can bring printed ballots, tokens, colored cards, a whiteboard, whatever. The one thing I'm fixed on is that participants should have real skin in the game, not hypothetical preferences—so I'm planning to tie outcomes to a small real payoff, like raffle tickets toward one decent prize, or points they can trade for snacks. Budget is tiny.

What I'm after is a runnable design for an activity where the group votes repeatedly under a couple of different rules while their underlying preferences stay the same, and the rules are chosen so the incentive to misreport shows up naturally—somebody tries it, it works, and other people notice. No lecture up front, and nobody hears the name Gibbard–Satterthwaite until the debrief. The point should come out of what happens in the game, not from what I say afterwards.

Can you lay this out concretely? I need:

- how to assign preferences and stakes so the payoffs are real but easy to run, and whether the facilitator should know everyone's true preferences or stay blind;
- the exact voting rules to use and the order to run them in;
- the rules around discussion, secret ballots, and whether people can talk or coordinate between rounds—this feels like the crux, since too much secrecy means nobody learns anything and too much coordination means one loud person turns it into a coalition-building exercise;
- a timeline for the 75 minutes;
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the moving parts of the theorem—the three-or-more-alternatives condition, non-dictatorship, strategy-proofness—and shows where each move toward honest voting could be blocked and what it costs;
- and an honest note on what the game cannot reproduce: the theorem is a proof about all possible voting rules, not a demo of the three rules you happened to run, so tell me what the facilitator has to say to keep people from overgeneralizing or undergeneralizing.

Also warn me what will probably go wrong when this gets run—ties, people getting confused by ranked ballots, one dominant personality bulldozing everyone into a coalition, that sort of thing—and how to handle it without spoiling the reveal.
