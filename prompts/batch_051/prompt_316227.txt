I have been defending a principle I call "baseline-relative gratitude," and I want an honest assessment of it. The claim is that how much gratitude one owes a benefactor is not proportional to the benefit actually received, nor to the effort or sacrifice the action required in absolute terms, but to how large a departure the action represents from the benefactor's own settled pattern of choice. On this view, a habitually selfish person who strains to do you a small kindness deserves more gratitude than a habitually generous person who does you a large one, because the first act consumed more of the agent's prior self. Gratitude, I keep saying, tracks how much of the person was spent, not how much good was transferred. I can see the obvious objection: it seems to imply that a reliably loving parent's daily care, being entirely in character, merits less gratitude than a neglectful parent's single dramatic sacrifice, which strikes me as perverse. Is the principle salvageable under another formulation, is it just a poorly worked-out cousin of existing views about moral worth, or does it break at exactly that point? I would rather have a candid diagnosis than an encouraging one.
