I keep running into a move in arguments about civil disobedience that I can't quite parse. A friend justified blocking a highway by saying, roughly, that when a law is unjust you're not merely allowed to break it—you're morally required to. Someone else 'corrected' her to the more careful claim that an unjust law imposes no moral obligation in the first place, and everybody treated this as though it settled the matter. I'm not asking about the legal side, i.e. weather you should expect to be arrested or whether courts should punish you. I'm asking about the moral claim.

I want you to take the slogan in its two loose forms—an unjust law imposes no obligation, and you're morally required to disobey unjust laws—and pull apart the distinct precise theses either of them could be expressing. Go from the nearly trivial to the radical: for example, 'this particular law fails to generate a moral reason in this particular case' at one end, versus 'every citizen is required to disobey every law they personally judge unjust, whatever the cost to others' at the other. For each reading, state it precisely enough that it has truth conditions (whose obligation, conditional on what, against which laws, with what costs permitted), say whether it's trivially true, plausible but contestable, or just false, and say what it would imply for someone deciding whether to block a highway to protest a law criminalizing sleeping outside.

Then tell me which reading or readings actually do the justificatory work when this slogan gets used to defend disobedience, and whether the two formulations pull apart—that is, whether 'no obligation' and 'required to disobey' really amount to different claims here, or one does all the work while the other just sounds stronger.

Put the readings in a table, one row per reading. Then on the very last line, write \answer{...} with a short label naming the reading that does the actual justificatory work in the highway case—or \answer{none of them} if you think no single reading suffices.
