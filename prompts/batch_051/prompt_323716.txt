I recently joined the grants committee of a private foundation, and the experience has forced me to confront how unsystematic my judgments are about funding knowledge with no foreseeable application. Here are my actual verdicts. I would fund a proposed telescope that would map the cosmic microwave background in finer detail to constrain inflation, but not because it would train people or produce spinoffs. I would not fund a proposal to compute the quadrillionth digit of pi, even though that too would be knowledge without application, and I am convinced the difference between these two matters. I would fund a pure-mathematics proposal that connects two mature fields and promises no engineering payoff, and I feel roughly the same about a historical study of medieval grain accounting in one small region, despite having no use to name for either. Yet I hesitate before a cosmology proposal whose proponents admit it is not presently testable, even though it might tighten a mathematically specified theory. And I would fund battery research aimed at lowering storage costs within a decade without any hesitation, while being unable to say whether this is the same principle as the telescope or a different one, which bothers me. Is there one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to a case I may soon face: a proposal to search for indirect statistical evidence of a multiverse in the same kind of data the telescope would produce. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
