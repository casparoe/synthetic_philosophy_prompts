I'm a novelist, and I'd rather my books not depend on an idea I can't see the bottom of. At my writers' group last week, two friends got into it over the central fantasy of my work-in-progress, and I sat there taking notes. Here's the premise they were arguing about: my protagonist is under a curse—her daughter dies unless, before the year is out, she genuinely admires the king, not just bows and flatters. The king is, as I've written him, vain, unremarkable, and cruel in the way that systems are cruel, though he's not personally about to kill anyone.

Nadia said the protagonist obviously has an overwhelming reason to admire the king: admiring him is the only way to save her daughter, and a reason just is the sort of thing that makes an attitude sensible from the agent's own point of view. If that threat can't count, nothing counts.

Ezra said no: the curse gives her a reason to bring it about that she admires the king—prayer, hypnosis, self-deception, a year of deliberate effort—but not a reason to admire him. Admiration is the kind of attitude that should fit its object, and the king has no admirable qualities. The threat is a reason of the wrong kind.

Nadia shot back that calling a reason "wrong-kind" is still conceding it's a reason. It may not make the admiration correct the way good qualities would, but it makes trying to admire him rational, and that's all the premise needs—she has a reason to do something.

Ezra said that if she succeeds, what she has isn't admiration; it's a forced imitation, maybe a payoff she makes inside her own head. The curse can justify faking or inducing a state, but not holding it, because holding it means the state has become a response to the king's merits, and his merits never did any of the work.

Nadia came back that people do not only admire from standing starts; we train our attention, and in doing so we often discover real virtues that were invisible before. The curse could be the trigger for a process whose endpoint is genuine fitting admiration. If she ends up admiring him because she sees something finally worth admiring, the threat was just the cause of the process.

Ezra answered that this gives everything away: the reason at the end is the fittingness, not the threat. A cause of a reason is not the same thing as a reason, and besides, a person ordered to reshape her own character to fit a man she has no reason to admire is not choosing freely.

I've gone back and forth and I can't referee them. Go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right about what, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall—I need the scorecard, not just a winner.
