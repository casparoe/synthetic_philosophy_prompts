I keep returning to the problem of where experience begins in the animal kingdom, and the usual landmarks — nociception, mirror self-recognition, cortical complexity — all seem either to beg the question or to be proxies for something they don't quite name. After a good deal of circling, I've settled on a provisional criterion I've been calling 'self-indexed valence', though the name is negotiable. The thought is this: plenty of living and non-living systems are modified by outcomes without there being anything it is like to be them. A plant bends towards light, a thermostat switches state, a bacterium adjusts its swimming in a chemical gradient. What such systems lack, I want to say, is that the outcome is never represented as good or bad for the organism itself; they are merely calibrated. My criterion, then, is that an organism has conscious experience just in case at least some of its internal states carry the content that a situation is better or worse for this very organism, and those states are used to revise the organism's behaviour when the mapping between a situation and its welfare changes. The point is to distinguish a system that is being tuned from a subject with a point of view. On this criterion, a spinal reflex that withdraws a limb from a hot surface does not count, because although the reflex serves the organism's interest, the reflex arc does not itself represent that interest as its own; a honeybee that revises which floral odours it treats as rewarding when the nectar behind those odours changes does count, which I regard as a feature, though I suspect others will regard it as a bug.

I would like this stress-tested before I build anything further on it. Is 'self-indexed valence' a genuine distinction, or does it come apart under pressure? Please be charitable but candid: reconstruct the criterion in its strongest form, say whether it collapses into something already standard in discussions of animal feeling (and, if so, under what broad heading rather than via a reading list), and then identify exactly where it draws the line in the wrong place or silently smuggles in too much with phrases like 'represented as good or bad for the organism itself'. Could a well-designed robot satisfy the criterion while being obviously unconscious, or does the criterion come closer to sufficiency than my examples admit? I am after the three strongest objections and a verdict on whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
