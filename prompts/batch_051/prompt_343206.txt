I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of fiction seminar, and we have just finished two weeks on imaginative resistance. The students can all reproduce the familiar Gendler-style example in which a story invites us to accept some morally appalling act as the right thing to do, and they can name a few standard responses involving authorial authority, fictional truth, and the alleged analogy with impossibility. But I have found that this is mostly label-memorization: when pressed, many cannot say why the phenomenon is genuinely puzzling, or what would count as a good explanation of it as opposed to a redescription of the examples.

For the take-home final, I would like one essay question, with students writing about 1,500 words in response, that cannot be answered by reciting the standard cases. The question should force them to think about the unity and scope of the phenomenon: does imaginative resistance form a single kind, or is the label a placeholder for several distinct phenomena? In particular, the question should make them take a stand on the difference between being genuinely unable to imagine a deviant proposition and being unwilling to imagine it, and to consider whether moral, evaluative, aesthetic, and epistemic cases of resistance call for one mechanism or several. It should also require engagement with at least one test case that does not sit comfortably under the usual moral explanation, such as a case where the resistant element is epistemic or affective rather than moral.

Please provide (a) the essay question exactly as students would see it, (b) a model answer of roughly 600 to 800 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 that says which specific moves an A answer must contain, which mistakes would cap an answer at a B or a C, and how the marks are distributed across those moves. Begin with the essay question itself, then give the model answer, then the marking scheme, with no preamble, no restating of the assignment, and no commentary.
