I have been developing a distinction I call "neural paratext," and I want an honest assessment of it. In a brain–computer interface, I call the decoded output the system is built to deliver — an intended word, a selected command, a cursor movement — the "neural content." Everything the decoder necessarily picks up around that target I call the "neural paratext" (borrowing the literary-studies term for the material surrounding a text that shapes how it is read): hesitation and delay before selection, error-monitoring and correction signals, confidence levels, emotional valence, and the competing alternatives suppressed on the way to the chosen output. My claim is that the mental-privacy debate is protecting the wrong thing. Content is the least revealing and most voluntary part of a BCI's output: a user can choose, withhold, or shape it. Paratext is largely outside voluntary control and far more diagnostic of indecision, ambivalence, fatigue, self-deception, and stress. Consent to "read my thoughts" therefore cannot meaningfully extend to paratext, and any privacy regime built around protecting inner speech or explicit intentions will systematically underprotect people. I propose inverted defaults: a BCI may decode only the minimal content required for its function, while paratext is sensitive by default and may be processed only under strict, function-specific purpose limitations — regardless of what the user consented to.

Tell me candidly: is this a real distinction, or just the familiar distinction between raw neural data and derived information wearing new clothes? Which existing positions in the cognitive-liberty and mental-privacy literature does it most resemble, and where does it break?
