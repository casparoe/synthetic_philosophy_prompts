I'm teaching a philosophy of family law seminar this term, and there's a knot I can't quit untangle myself, so I want to see what you make of it. Look at the actual legal institution of marriage in the jurisdictions most of us know. There is no fertility requirement: a ninety-year-old, a woman past menopause, a man who has had a vasectomy, and a couple who announce they never want children can all marry, and childlessness is never grounds for annulment or divorce. Yet the law keeps making a fuss about sex. A marriage can be annulled for non-consummation, and consummation was historically understood as vaginal intercourse; adultery is a fault ground for divorce in many places; and same-sex marriage had to be bolted onto a doctrine that was plainly built around a heterosexual act. So here is my question: what view of marriage does this whole apparatus actually presuppose? It looks like parts of it presuppose that marriage is for procreation, parts presuppose that it is a sexual union of one man and one woman, and parts presuppose that it is just a chosen loving relationship between adults, and I want to know whether those parts can be made to hang together or whether the law is quietly carrying several incompatible theories at once. Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories, be precise about what the law strictly presupposes versus what it merely historically fit with, and then tell me this: if we really replaced the presupposed view with the now-common philosophical line that marriage is a chosen relationship of mutual care and commitment, what would actually have to change in the law of annulment, adultery, and divorce — or could it all survive under a new description? And since I want to drop this into my teaching notes, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just continuous prose, the way you would write an email.
