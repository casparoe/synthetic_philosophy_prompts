I've been trying to get my head around the Korean Four–Seven debate, and I keep having the same reaction: these two people are answering different questions and neither notices. Quick version: the Four Beginnings (commiseration, shame and dislike, deference, and the sense of right and wrong) look like a list of purely good moral sprouts, while the Seven Emotions (joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, desire) look like a morally mixed list of ordinary affects. The debate is over whether the Four and the Seven are really two different things with two different sources, or one underlying thing with the Four just being the good slice of it. Yi Hwang ended up with the formula that the Four issue from principle (li) with material force (ki) following them, while the Seven issue from ki with li riding along. Ki Taesung kept pushing back that li is a pattern or norm, not the kind of thing that can actually issue or move anything, so all issuance is ki and the Four are just the good subset of the Seven. They wrote letters back and forth for years refining this and never landed on the same picture, and later Yi I jumped in and the thing still didn't get settled.

My working theory is that the whole dispute turns on the word "issuance": one side reads it as a causal claim about where an emotion comes from, the other reads it as a claim about whether the emotion is good or what makes it intelligible. If that's right its not a metaphysics problem at all — more like a confused question that smuggles a category mistake into a verb. But I can also see a real substantive question underneath it about whether principles, norms, or patterns can be causes at all, which is a live problem in completely different dress.

So: diagnose the stall. What kind of disagreement is this really — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one word, a real metaphysics question masquerading as a taxonomy question, or something else? And say what, if anything, would have unstuck it. Give me exaclty five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
