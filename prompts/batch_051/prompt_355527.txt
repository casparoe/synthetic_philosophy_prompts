I've been thinking about automation in a less abstract way than I used to. My employer has started replacing large parts of our planning work with software that does in an afternoon what our team used to need a week to do, and I keep going back and forth about whether the real issue is the technology itself or the way its productivity gets owned and directed. A colleague who studied political economy pointed me to a passage in Marx's Grundrisse, in the part of Notebook VII that people often call the "Fragment on Machines." What struck me is a tension between two adjacent sentences. Marx first says, in effect, that the value relation rests on direct labour-time as the determinant factor in the production of wealth, and then, in the next breath, appears to take it back:

"Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose ‘powerful effectiveness’ is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

I can see that Marx is not merely describing machinery but making a claim about what "wealth" fundamentally is, and I suspect the word "real" in "real wealth" is doing a lot of conceptual work. But I do not understand how the second sentence fails to contradict the first, or why he treats this decoupling of wealth from labour-time as a crisis of the value form rather than as evidence that his earlier value theory was wrong. Could you explain the conceptual machinery behind this passage, especially the distinction between wealth in the sense of use-values and value as socially necessary labour-time, and why Marx presents the growing dependence of production on science and technology as an internal limit of capitalism rather than a simple refutation of the labour theory of value? If it helps, you can test the reading against a contemporary case such as software or machine-learning systems, where the "agencies set in motion" no longer look much like a machine on a shop floor.
