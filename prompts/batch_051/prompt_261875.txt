As a journalist who covers moral philosophy and its collisions with everyday argument, I've noticed that the question "what if everyone did that?" leads a curious double life. If a commuter jumps a turnstile, we treat it as a serious objection that if everyone evaded fares the transit system would collapse. If a hiker cuts a switchback across an alpine meadow, we say that if everyone did so the meadow would be ruined, and we count that as a reason for the individual hiker to stay on the trail. But when a couple decides not to have children, the observation that if everyone made the same choice the human population would dwindle towards extinction is not treated as a moral objection — even by people who find the turnstile argument persuasive. And when a young person decides to become a poet, or someone resolves to give away most of her savings, the same conditional is brushed aside as a fallacy. The projected aggregate outcome is at least as dire in the second set of cases, yet it does no moral work there.

That looks like an inconsistency in need of justification. I can see two philosophical resources that might supply one. Kant's universal-law test, taken strictly, seems to say the conditional should always matter: I may act only on a maxim I can will as a universal law. The later debate then splits. Marcus Singer's generalization argument treats the undesirable consequences of everyone's doing X as itself a reason not to do X, while R. M. Hare insisted that universalizability is a consistency constraint on my own judgment — I must be willing to prescribe the same action for anyone relevantly similar — not a forecast of aggregate effects. But neither distinction, as usually stated, tells me why the fare-dodger's maxim fails while the childless couple's or the poet's maxim passes, without covertly appealing to the very intuitions at issue.

So my question is whether there is a principled difference between the cases where "what if everyone did that?" carries moral force and the cases where it does not, or whether ordinary moral thought is applying a bad or imperfectly understood argument selectively. Please test whatever principle you propose against a hard case: a commuter who takes a short cut down a quiet residential street each morning, where no single car makes a perceptible difference to congestion or danger but where the result of every commuter's doing it would be intolerable. I want a reasoned verdict rather than a survey of the literature, and I would ask you to state how confident you are in the central claims and to single out the point you are least sure of.
