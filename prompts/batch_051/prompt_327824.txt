I’m a PhD student working on courage and whether the martial virtues have any real grip in civilian life. I keep getting tangled on two passages in Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* that look to me like they pull in opposite directions.

In Book III, chapter 6, he says:

“The courageous man, therefore, in the proper sense of the term, will be he who fearlessly confronts a noble death, or some sudden peril that threatens death; and the perils of war answer this description most fully.”

Then in Book III, chapter 7, he says:

“But the courageous man is proof against fear so far as man may be. Hence although he will sometimes fear even terrors not beyond man's endurance, he will do so in the right way, and he will endure them as principle dictates, for the sake of what is noble; for that is the end at which virtue aims.”

The first passage seems to make fearlessness before death in battle the paradigm, but the second says the courageous man will sometimes fear even terrors within human endurance. Are these actually consistent? I can see possible readings: maybe “fearlessly” in the first passage means something like “without flinching” rather than “without feeling fear”; maybe the first is about the external act and the second about the inner feeling; maybe Aristotle just changed his mind, or he’s relying on a distinction he never bothers to draw. Which of those readings is honest to the text, and which ones are special pleading? And does the best reconciliation still effectively tie courage to martial death, or does it leave room for courage in peaceful settings—say, facing serious illness, professional ruin, or other civilian dangers?

Please give the candidate reconciliations, what each one costs, and then state which one you think is the honest reading.
