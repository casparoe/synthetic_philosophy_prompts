On the boundary problem—who should get a say in a collective decision—I have a friend, Maya, who teaches democratic theory, and I've been keeping a running list of her calls on who should actually be included in particular votes. So far: in a city referendum on a zoning change that would allow a six-story building on a quiet block, she said the long-term renters and owner-occupiers on the block should vote, but a tourist staying in an Airbnb on the same block for three days shouldn't. In a national election, she said a non-citizen permanent resident who'd lived and worked in the country for a decade should vote, but a citizen who left at age five and has no ties back home shouldn't. In a regional transit-authority fare increase, she said a non-resident who commutes in every day on its trains should have a say, but a resident of a faraway town with no connection to the system shoudn't. And in a school-district budget vote, she said an out-of-state landlord who owns a rental building in the district should vote, while a parent in a neighboring district who has no kids in the district's schools and no property there shouldn't.

Now she's visiting, and I want to pin down what principle she's actually running. The downstream residents wouldn't be governed by the referendum's law—it imposes no obligations on them and nobody would claim they can be fined under it—but the effect on their livelihoods would be enormous. I want to know whether Maya would include the downstream residents in the vote.
