I'm TAing a second-year undergrad class that mixes decision theory with philosophy of action — about 18 students, one 75-minute session where I get to run the whole thing. I want an activity that makes them feel, not just hear, why assigning probabilities to your own choices while you're still deliberating is so strange. I don't want to say that up front. The rules, incentives, or roles should be set up so they walk into the problem themselves, and only in the debrief do we put names on it.

The target idea, roughly (you can sharpen or reframe it): when you forecast someone else's choice you can stand outside it, but when you forecast your own choice while deciding, the forecast isn't a neutral messurement of an independent fact. It either commits you, or changes your incentives, or just reports a plan you've already settled. Yet probabilities come in degrees and choices seem to land on one act, so there's a weird tension between the question what will I decide and the question what do I decide. I'd like them to leave seeing why those two questions can't be cleanly seperated.

Can you design me a runnable game or sequence of short tasks, with exact rules and a points/scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, plus a materials list and a 75-minute timeline? I'm imagining something with a predict-then-choose loop and a proper scoring rule like a Brier score, because I want them to notice on their own that scoring their own forecast gives them an incentive to conform to it — but I'm open to whatever structure you think works better. I'd especially like at least one round where the choice is genuinely live for people and one round where the answer is already settled, so they can feel the contrast instead of just being told it exists.

The debrief should walk through specific moments in the activity and connect them to the distinctions that matter here (predictive stance vs deliberative stance, self-prediction as commitment vs as evidence, randomization as the one case where prediction and choice line up without friction). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it in the room, and be frank about which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce — where I'll need to supply something in the debrief rather than let the mechanics speak.
