I am a novelist working on a story about demographic prediction, ecological pressure, and whether a theory can be right but early. I want the story to circle around a passage from Thomas Robert Malthus’s An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798). In the preface he writes:

“It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer that the Author recollects has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society.”

Please write a short story of about two thousand words, in British English, in which this passage matters. The protagonist should be a young agricultural adviser on a colonised planet or in a remote upland commune that has prospered for three generations by treating Malthusian limits as falsified: population has risen, yields have risen faster, and the old prediction is invoked only as a joke. Over the course of the story, the protagonist should begin to suspect that the question is not whether Malthus was wrong, but whether the check has been displaced, postponed, or transformed into something unrecognisable, such as soil toxicity, atmospheric heat, reliance on a single engineered crop, or an unspoken system of labour that exports the cost elsewhere. The story should not simply vindicate Malthus, and it should not simply mock him. It should dramatise the difficulty of judging a long-run prediction that fails in its original form but may still be tracking something real. Include a scene in which the protagonist returns to the quoted sentence and argues with someone about what “the means by which this level is effected” would look like now. Leave the ending unresolved enough that a reader could honestly disagree about whether the prediction has been rescued or merely metabolised into a new ideology.
