Exam question: Explain Langdon Winner's claim that artifacts can have politics, distinguishing his two senses of the claim. Then discuss whether Robert Moses's low overpasses on the Long Island parkways are a good illustration. Should we accept the stronger version of Winner's thesis, that some technologies are inherently political?

Student's answer: Winner has two ways in which technical objects can be political. In the first, an artefact or system settles a community's affairs by the way it is arranged: the design has built-in social effects. The Moses case is this kind. By building the bridges too low for buses, Moses's parkway system effectively excluded bus users, who were poorer and mostly black, from Jones Beach. The racism was written into the concrete, not the rulebook. Winner calls this an inherently political artifact because, he says, a technology of this kind requires a strong centralised authority to build and run it. From this he concludes that even if no one intended to discriminate, the bridge's politics are a feature of the machine itself, not of its use. So the stronger thesis holds: some technologies are political by their very nature, and the Moses bridges are proof of it.
