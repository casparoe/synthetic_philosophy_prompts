Two Kant scholars meet for an adversarial collaboration on Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795), First Supplement, 'On the guarantee of a perpetual peace'. One holds that Kant is there committed to a theoretical–teleological prediction: the mechanical workings of history will bring about a lawful international order even among morally unreformed human beings. The other holds that the guarantee is a practical postulate, a way of representing history for agents who have a duty to work towards such an order, not an assertion about what will in fact occur.

The focal passage is Kant's statement, closely paraphrased: nature's purposiveness supplies a guarantee that perpetual peace will eventually issue from human inclinations themselves, because war-weariness, self-interest, the spirit of commerce, and the separation of peoples work mechanically against war; and this guarantee does not abolish freedom in matters of right, because it is not intended as a prediction that imposes necessity on the will but as a ground of practical confidence in pursuing the end.

Write the joint document such a collaboration should have produced. It should include: a numbered section of claims both can sign, stated in neutral wording; a table giving the precise residual disagreement in wording each side accepts for its own view; and a list of the genuine cruxes, including at least these three: whether Kant's teleological language is assertoric or heuristic, whether the guarantee's independence from moral conversion is essential to its function, and whether persistent failure of international institutions would embarrass one reading more than the other. For each crux, give a concrete case or body of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prior prediction recorded. If the two readings cannot be separated by any possible evidence, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.
