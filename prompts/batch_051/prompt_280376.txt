I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been chewing on an asymmetry in how people talk about minds that I cant get rid of. We treat perception as something that happens to you and action as something you do. If a patient sees something that isnt there, we say the experience befell him; if he then speaks back to it, we treat the speaking as his act and hold it open to praise or blame. The law and ordinary moral judgment both run something like this line, blaming people for what they do and not for what they see. But on the predictive processing / free energy picture (active inference and all that), perceiving and acting are two directions of the same operation: the brain minimizes prediction error, and it can do that either by updating its model to fit the incoming signal or by altering the world to fit the model. From that standpoint the old receptive/agentive split looks like a folk distinction rather than a real boundary, and yet our judgments lean on it constantly. My question is whether anything actually justifies the asymmetry. If you had to defend the commonsense idea that seeing is something we undergo while doing is something we perform, what's the strongest principle you can offer, and does it survive the predictive processing picture? Try it against hard nearby cases like willful ignorance or choosing not to look, where we do partly hold someone responsible for what they failed to see. I want an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned, not just a survey of ways the two descriptions differ. And please write it in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email.
