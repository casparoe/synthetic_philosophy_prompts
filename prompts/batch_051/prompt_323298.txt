I recently began attending a Vedānta study circle after years in analytic philosophy, and I have been asked to lead next week's meeting on the Advaita view that ātman is non-different from Brahman and that the world of distinct things is māyā. The audience is a philosophy reading group, no Sanskrit, and I expect most of them to arrive thinking that māyā means the world is a hallucination and that the identity claim is either a metaphor or a mystical slogan. Don't explain the position to me; plan how I should teach it in one fifty-minute session. Set out what has to be in place first for the identity claim even to be intelligible to them—for instance, the jīva/ātman distinction or the three states of waking, dream, and deep sleep—and why; pick one carrying example, such as the rope that appears as a snake or the clay and its pots, and say what conditions it must satisfy so it does not reinforce the illusion reading; list the misconceptions this audience will form, in the order they are likely to arise, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; say what you would openly refuse to cover at this level; and give one final question or short check that would show they have grasped the identity claim rather than merely repeated it. Throughout, separate what is settled among scholars of Advaita from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
