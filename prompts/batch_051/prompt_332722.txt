I am a high-school senior and the head adjudicator for my school's interscholastic debate society. I judge two or three rounds a week, about half of them novice rounds, and I write the ballots that teach younger debaters what counted and why. I have a recurring problem I want to solve before next season: debaters use the claim that an argument leads to an infinite regress as if it were a universal stop button, and my fellow adjudicators have no consistent way to tell when that objection is a real refutation and when it is a rhetorical device for making any position look as though it never bottoms out.

Please write a decision procedure we can actually run. It should be a set of questions to ask in order, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient yes or no, and with explicit exits: where the procedure rules that the regress objection is sustained, where it rules that the objection fails, and where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is about burdens, debate norms, or values rather than about the logic of the regress. It has to be usable in the two minutes between the end of a round and the delivery of a ballot, by a judge with no formal philosophical training, from a single page. Flag every step where judgment cannot be eliminated by the procedure.

Ground the procedure in the best philosophical account you can give of when a regress is vicious and when it is benign, translated into plain language without dropping the distinctions that do the actual work. At minimum, the procedure should be able to separate: a regress that defeats a claim, one that merely shows an explanation is incomplete or a chain is costly, one that the objector manufactures by refusing to accept a stopping point already built into the theory, and one that is generated on demand rather than requiring an actually completed infinite chain. If those distinctions are too crude, correct them from within the theory; do not simply list them.

Then run the procedure, showing each step, on the three objections below, all of which I heard in real rounds. For each one, say where the procedure exits and what ballot instruction follows. One of the three will probably come out opposite to my initial read; identify which one it is and do not soften it.

First, in a round on lowering the voting age for student government, the negative argued as follows. Every empirical claim the affirmative uses needs an evidentiary standard, but any stated standard is itself a claim and therefore needs a further standard, and this continues forever, so a burden of proof can never be met and the judge should disregard the affirmative's evidence. My initial read is that this is sophistry, because it would make the objection's own evidence unassessable too. The procedure may disagree; if so, say so.

Second, in a round about whether a district policy is binding on students, the negative argued: a policy is binding only if it was made under an authorized procedure, but the procedure is binding only under a higher authorization, and so on upward, so the policy was never actually authorized. My initial read is that this works against an affirmative that says authorization must always come from higher authorization, but dissolves if the affirmative locates legitimacy in something accepted or practiced rather than authorized from above. I am prepared to learn that this distinction is not as clean as I think.

Third, in a round about what the honor code requires, one side argued: a written rule cannot by itself determine what counts as following it; someone must interpret it; but the interpretation is itself a kind of rule and requires a further interpretation, so the code never actually fixes any particular application. I do not have a stable initial read on this one.

Finally, in a short paragraph or two, tell me what the procedure cannot settle even when it is applied correctly, and what a judge would have to supply from somewhere else.
