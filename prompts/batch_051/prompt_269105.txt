Consider the following situation in mathematical practice. Since Appel and Haken’s proof of the four-colour theorem, mathematicians routinely cite the theorem as established, textbooks present it as proved, and almost no one expects to check the case analysis or the computer code by hand. In his essay The Four-Color Problem and Its Philosophical Significance, Thomas Tymoczko describes the situation roughly as follows: the proof eliminates thousands of cases through brute computation, so the chain of reasoning is too long for any human mathematician to survey, and accepting the theorem means accepting a result whose proof is not fully inspectable by a single mind. Assume his description is basically accurate. As a philosopher of mathematics, think carefully about what the practice presupposes: does the ordinary acceptance of such proofs commit mathematicians to the view that computation itself can transmit mathematical justification, or only to something weaker about the reliability of the machine? Is that presupposition in tension with the older idea that a proof must be surveyable by a competent human? Reconstruct the implicit theory of proof, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if mathematicians instead demanded that every proof be surveyable. Respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
