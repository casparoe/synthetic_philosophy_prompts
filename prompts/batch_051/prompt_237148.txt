I am one of five unrelated adults, aged 64 to 78, who recently sold our separate homes and pooled the proceeds to buy a large old house in a neighborhood zoned R-1 (single-family residential). We did this deliberately as an alternative to assisted living: we share meals, keep a joint account for household expenses, and have given one another powers of attorney so that we can care for each other as we age. The city has now sent us a notice stating that our household may violate the zoning code. The provision it quotes reads:

"Family means (a) one or more persons related by blood, marriage, adoption, or legal guardianship, or (b) not more than three unrelated persons, provided that in either case the occupants live together in a single dwelling unit as a single housekeeping unit."

We are unrelated by blood, marriage, adoption, or guardianship, and we are five. We eat, budget, and keep house as one unit. The planning official who wrote the notice concedes that the drafters, in the late 1970s, were responding to rooming houses and student group houses, and never imagined five older adults pooling resources for mutual care.

Please act as a legal scholar who works on the philosophy of land-use regulation and on the question of who has a right to the city. Think through the problem step by step before reaching any overall conclusion. Begin by brainstorming the plausible approaches to interpreting the provision—the plain words, the drafters' purpose, what the drafters would likely have said had they been asked about our arrangement, and what a reasonable member of the community would take "family" to mean. Then work through each approach and explain whether it would classify our household as a permitted family, a prohibited group, or something about which the provision is simply silent. I am less interested in predicting what a court would actually do than in the structure of the interpretive arguments: which reading is most faithful to the text, which is most faithful to the purpose, and whether there is any coherent reading under which our household is at once plainly outside the words and plainly within the provision's underlying concern. Finally, address the broader point that a city's definition of "family" is one way of deciding who may live in its neighborhoods, and say what it would mean to treat our exclusion as—or as not—a denial of a right to the city.
