In Part I, near the proposition where God is said to be the immanent cause of all things, Spinoza argues that nothing could have been produced by God/Nature in any manner or order other than the manner and order in which it was produced. So every action of every person follows necessarily from the same causal necessity. Then in Part V he still writes about becoming more free through understanding our place in God/Nature, culminating in the intellectual love of God. I can't tell whether this is a real kind of freedom or just a shift in how we experience the very same necessity.

Here's the modern issue I want you to extend him to. What would Spinoza say to that reform argument? Would he say "yes, finally you're catching up to Part I"? Or would he think it misdescribes the state of the person who lives badly, because it collapses everything into passivity and misses the difference between being determined by external affects versus understanding yourself as a necessary part of the whole? And how exactly would the intellectual love of God bear on whether we punish, reproach, or treat someone?
