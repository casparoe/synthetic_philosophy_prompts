My friend Dana used to run operations at a medical group and now does consulting; she's always quoting Herbert Simon at me and says she's a satisficer, not a maximizer. Over the last year I've been writing down her verdicts on other people's "when to stop searching" decisions, because I cannot figure out what the actual rule is.

So far I have five:
1. A coffee shop hiring baristas: the manager hired the first applicant who met the written requirements. Dana said that was exactly right; once the aspiration was met, more interviews were pure cost.
2. A boutique hotel choosing a booking platform: they bought the first one that had the required integrations, despite mixed reviews. Dana said bad call; demos are cheap and you're stuck with the system for years, so the bar was set to low.
3. A family buying a generator with a hurricane two days out: they took the first in-stock model that met the wattage and budget, even though the reviews were meh. Dana said right, the deadline meant the core need was the whole bar.
4. A couple hiring a nanny: after one interview they offered the job to a person who met the license, schedule, and safety criteria. Dana said way too quick; with stakes that high you keep interviewing until you have evidence of judgment and warmth, not just credentials, because the cost of more interviews is small.
5. A healthy friend picking a primary care doctor from a new HMO's directory: she took the first in-network doctor who was accepting patients and didn't even look at the quality scores. Dana said fine; the requirements were met and comparing further had low value for someone with no pressing issues.

Now we're arguing about the new case. A friend needs a physical therapist after a knee replacement, and she booked the first clinic that is licensed, in-network, specializes in post-op rehab, has an opening within a week, and has no board complaints; the reviews are sparse but the couple she found are positive. She didn't call any of the dozens of other in-network PTs nearby, even though checking a few more would cost an afternoon of hold music. What does Dana say, and which of the five precedents is the closest analog? I keep going back and forth between reading her as a genuine satisficer (stop once a reasonable aspiration is met) and as someone who is just doing optimal stopping in disguise (keep searching while the expected gain beats the cost). If her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say what rule she'd be adopting either way, and what further case would settle which one she's actually running.
