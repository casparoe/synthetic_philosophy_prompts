I am preparing a lecture on the philosophy of perception in which I defend a disjunctivist account of seeing, and I need a working definition of "sees" in its epistemically relevant sense—the sense in which seeing that the cup is on the desk can put one in a position to know that the cup is on the desk. I do not mean the loose everyday use, which lets us say that a person "sees" things in dreams, in photographs, and in her mind's eye. I mean the use that makes the verb factive: if a subject sees an object or a scene, the object or scene must actually be there.

Here is my working view, which the definition should respect unless the cases force a revision. I take genuine seeing to be an achievement in which a mind-independent object figures in the episode itself; when no such object figures, the subject is not seeing but is in a different kind of state—a mere seeming—which may be subjectively indistinguishable from seeing. I also distinguish illusions from hallucinations: in an illusion (the straight stick half-submerged in water that looks bent), the subject is still seeing, because it is the stick itself that looks some way to her, even if it looks a way the stick is not; in a total hallucination of a rose, she sees nothing at all. The argument from illusion, as I reconstruct it, runs: (1) illusions and hallucinations can be subjectively indistinguishable from corresponding veridical perceptions; (2) the best explanation of that indistinguishability is that all three share a fundamental kind of conscious state; therefore (3) veridical perception is not fundamentally relational. I want to block the move from (1) to (2): indistinguishability is not sameness of kind, any more than a forged note that is indistinguishable from legal tender is the same kind of thing as legal tender. But I have not found a formulation of "sees" that draws the line where I want it once the cases stop being clean.

Give me the definition, run it through all nine cases below, say for each whether the subject sees and what she sees, flag any case where your definition gives an answer I should find surprising, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Also tell me which of your nine verdicts is least secure.

Here are the cases, with my inclinations where I have them:

1. A person in good light looks at a coffee cup on her desk and reports its color and location; the cup is present and is, in the ordinary way, the cause of her experience. This is seeing if anything is.
2. A person looks at a white wall under red illumination and reports a pale pink surface. I think she sees the wall but misperceives its color.
3. A person looks at a straight stick half-submerged in water and reports it as bent. I think she sees the stick and misperceives its shape.
4. A person in a blindsight study reports no conscious visual experience in the blind field, but guesses the direction of a moving dot correctly at 80%. I do not think she sees the dot.
5. A person under the influence of a drug hallucinates a rose in the middle of an empty table; the experience is subjectively just like a veridical perception of a rose. I think she does not see a rose.
6. The same subject sits at a table on which, by coincidence, a real rose stands exactly where her drug-induced experience places one; the rose is not the cause of her experience. I have no settled view about whether she sees the rose.
7. A person looks at her friend's face reflected in a flat mirror and reports the friend's expression. I think she sees the friend, not merely a reflection, but I cannot yet say what in the definition secures this.
8. A person views a Müller-Lyer display and reports two equal lines as unequal. I think she sees the lines and misperceives their relative length.
9. A person sits with her eyes closed, producing a vivid voluntary image of the same coffee cup from case 1; the cup is still before her. I take this to be clearly not seeing, and I want the definition to say which feature rules it out.
