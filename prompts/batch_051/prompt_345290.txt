I am a retired physical chemist who has lately come to philosophy, and I find myself in an advisory situation that exposes a question I do not know how to answer. I chair the scientific advisory board of a private foundation. This year the board must decide whether to fund a strikingly wide-ranging proposal: a global monitoring and early-warning system for zoonotic disease, spanning wildlife ecology, clinical surveillance, logistics, and health economics. No single board member, including me, can responsibly appraise the proposal as a whole.

A young analyst at the foundation has proposed a procedure for reaching the decision. The board would first decompose the funding decision into a handful of broad questions: whether the scientific approach is sound, whether the proposed intervention points are real, whether the projected costs are credible, and whether the governance plan can work. Each question would be assigned to a panel of outside experts. Each panel would then decompose its own question into smaller subquestions and convene subpanels to answer those, and so on downward, until every subquestion is narrow enough that a single suitably qualified person can answer it from existing knowledge alone. No new research would be commissioned. The answers would then be passed back upward: each subpanel would synthesize the answers it received into a recommendation for the panel above it, and the board at the top would simply ratify the final recommendations.

The analyst's rationale is that no single mind can hold all the relevant considerations at once, but a tree of specialists can, provided each specialist is asked only the fragment of the problem they are competent to judge and the fragments are recombined level by level. I cannot decide whether to regard this as a genuine improvement in deliberation or as an elaborate repackaging of judgments the participants already held.

My difficulty is this. Every participant in the procedure applies only their existing knowledge; no new evidence is generated at any point, so it is hard to see how the final synthesis could contain anything not already present somewhere in the minds of the participants. Yet the analyst maintains that the procedure itself is a form of reasoning no individual could perform, because the division and recombination of partial judgments changes what the group can see even though the raw material is unchanged. I can feel the force of both thoughts without being able to settle the matter.

Is there a principled reason to expect such recursively decomposed deliberation to produce a better final judgment than simply putting the ablest generalists in a room to argue the whole question together? If the hierarchy genuinely adds something, what exactly does it add, and where is it most likely to fail?

I am less interested in parliamentary procedure than in the underlying epistemic structure of what the analyst is proposing.
