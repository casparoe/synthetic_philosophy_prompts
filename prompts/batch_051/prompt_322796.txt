Taking a seminar on population ethics and we spent last week on the nonidentity problem, and I ended up in a late-night argument with my roommate who works in a gene-editing lab, which has made me wonder whether the problem survives something the original formulations didn't really anticipate. The setup we learned: a choice that determines which particular future person comes to exist. A woman can conceive now, in which case the child will be born with some condition that makes life harder but still worth living, or later, in which case a different child is born without it. The child born now can't say they were harmed, because the alternative for them was never to have existed at all; yet it still seems like the woman does something wrong by not waiting. That's the puzzle. My roommate's point: if safe germline editing lets us intervene on the same embryo before transfer—same future child either way, just with or without the condition—then many of these choices stop being identity-affecting. Choosing not to edit doesn't substitute a different possible person; it makes one and the same child worse off than they could have been, and the nonidentity argument doesn't even get of the ground.

So I want to know: does the nonidentity problem survive this development, or does it retreat into a shrinking box of cases where you genuinely can't touch the same individual? Be specific about which premise of Parfit's argument the editing case puts pressure on, whether it can be repaired into a weaker claim, and whether the original cases still do the work they used to. And tell me what would change your verdict—which argument or consideration would flip your answer—and where your reasoning is most likely to break.
