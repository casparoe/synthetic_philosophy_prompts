Consider a country that has just disestablished its national church and is designing a replacement for the old church–state settlement. Instead of adopting the German church tax (a surcharge collected by the state from declared members), the English model of a single established church, the French laïcité model, or the American disestablishment model, the government proposes the following mechanism.

Every adult citizen may nominate one registered religious or secular association to receive an annual per-capita payment from a fixed state fund. A citizen who files no nomination has their payment directed by default: it follows the nomination of their parents, or is split between the parents' associations if those differ. Nominations are private, may be changed online at any time, and take effect immediately. To be registered, an association must provide a standard package of civic services — marriage, funeral, and bereavement counselling — open to non-members as well as members. The total size of the fund is set by statute and does not grow with the number of registrants.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a question about whether the state should support religion. Walk through how it would actually behave: what it incentivises among citizens, among the associations, and among the officials who audit registration. Where is it likely to be gamed first, and what second-order effects follow from the inherited-default rule, the open-services requirement, and the fixed fund? Which of the failure modes you identify could be fixed by adjusting the mechanism, and which seem intrinsic to channelling state money through private membership at all? Please answer in British English.
