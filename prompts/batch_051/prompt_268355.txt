I ended up in a row with a mate about whether populism is just democracy done properly. His line was that populists are the only ones who actually respect 'the will of the people' and that anyone going on about the rule of law, separation of powers, or independent courts is just dressing up elite vetoes as principle. When I asked what this will of the people actually is — where it lives, how you would measure it, whether it changes on a Tuesday — he just said the phrase again but louder, and eventually told me I must not believe in self-government.

I suspect the phrase is quietly doing several different jobs at once, and my inability to prise them apart at the time is what lost me the argument (if you can call it an argument). Could you lay out the distinct precise claims someone might mean by 'the will of the people' in this kind of debate — say, a factual claim about what most citizens currently want, an output of some fair procedure like an election or referendum, a pre-existing collective will that isnt reducible to individual opinions, a normative principle that the majority should get its way, a claim that ordinary people collectively have better judgement than the experts, or a way of defining 'the people' so that whoever disagrees with you stops being part of it? For each reading, say whether it's plausible, trivially true, or dubious, and whether it actually gets you to the conclusion that respecting the will of the people is the core of democratic legitimacy. And could you say which readings my friends argument actually needs to work, and which ones are cheap enough that he could win with one and then quietly switch to another? Please write the answer in British English.
