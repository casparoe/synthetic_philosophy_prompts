I am trying to consolidate my views on personal identity and would like a consistency audit. My commitments are:

(1) A person is a forensic concept: a person is the subject to whom past actions can be imputed as his own, and who is answerable for them in praise, blame, punishment, and ownership.

(2) Sameness of person over time is constituted by a chain of memory links: each adjacent stretch must include at least some authentic, first-personal action-memories, not merely incidental psychological continuity.

(3) Reid's brave-officer case shows that a single direct memory link is too strong as a criterion. The old general is the same person as the young officer, and the young officer is the same person as the schoolboy, even though the old general no longer remembers being flogged at school.

(4) Identity is transitive and one-to-one. A relation that can branch or be intransitive cannot be identity.

(5) In a fission case where my two cerebral hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies, each with my full psychological profile, neither resulting person is me, yet my survival is as good as survival ever is.

(6) What matters in survival is not identity but overlapping chains of psychological connectedness and continuity, including memory, intention, and dispositional continuities.

(7) Ordinary prudence is rationally grounded: I have a special reason to care about the person who will wake up in my bed thirty years from now, and I cannot treat that person as merely a future stage rather than me.

Which of these actually conflict, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity and be specific about what would have to give.
