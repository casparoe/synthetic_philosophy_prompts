I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science course, and I am drafting the 90-minute final essay. Please write one question on natural kinds that a student cannot answer by reciting Kripke–Putnam on "water = H2O" or Locke's distinction between real and nominal essences. The question should turn on the tension between two realist commitments: that natural kinds are mind-independent joints of nature, and that the sciences are our best guide to where the joints lie. It should force students to confront the fact that the sciences do not converge on a single taxonomy — rival species concepts in systematics cut the same organisms differently, the kind planet was redefined when Pluto was demoted, and one discipline's well-behaved kind may be no kind at all for another. The question should require a precise statement of the dilemma and then a direct assessment of the best realist reply, including what that reply gives up; it should not let students conclude too quickly that classifications are "just made up," and it should not let them hide behind the claim that "science decides" without saying whether science decides uniquely.

Provide three components. First, the question as it would appear on the exam, written for students. Second, a model answer of roughly 750 words that a strong student could realistically produce in the time; it should distinguish monistic realism, pluralist or promiscuous realism, and conventionalism, and it should say what a weakened joint-carving realism can still claim about the world. Third, a marking scheme for 20 marks built around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong; the scheme should reward the moves themselves rather than the mention of particular authors or labels.

Begin your response with the exam question itself, with no preface or commentary, and continue directly into the model answer and the marking scheme. Do not restate my instructions.
