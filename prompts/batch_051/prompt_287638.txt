Solomonoff's idea was to assign a prior probability to every possible explanation of the data, where an explanation is simpler—in the sense of having a shorter description, or lower Kolmogorov complexity—just in case it receives a higher prior. Formally, you enumerate all programs for a universal Turing machine and weight each program by 2 raised to the negative of its length in bits, so shorter programs get exponentially more weight. After seeing the data, you should believe whichever surviving program is shortest, since that is the explanation the prior declares most probable, and you should make your next predictions from it. Solomonoff proved that this procedure eventually converges to the true regularities in any sequence of observations whatsoever, because the true program is guaranteed to be somewhere in the enumeration and Bayesian updating gradually suppresses all its rivals. The post concludes that Occam's razor is therefore not a mere heuristic but a theorem, and that the only reason we do not use Solomonoff induction in practice is that running infinitely many programs is computationally infeasible—a practical limitation rather than a matter of principle.
