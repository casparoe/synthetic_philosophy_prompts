I keep flip-flopping on this and I'd rather be walked through the actual tradeoffs than given the usual "they're compatible" line. My training is in syntax, so my default is the distributional view: a category like "noun" or "dative" is just a bundle of slots and morphology — a noun is whatever can sit after a determiner, pluralize, head a subject, and so on — and meaning is irrelevant to what the category is. That view has real virtues. It's precise, it's internal to the language rather than borrowed from metaphysics, and it doesnt embarrass itself the moment you notice that "destruction" is a noun naming an event or that a dative can mark someone who is merely affected or judged, not a recipient. It also gives you a tidy decision procedure: run the tests, forget the meaning.

But the semantic intuition keeps coming back. Across languages, the items that land in noun slots are overwhelmingly objects and substances, predicate-slot items are overwhelmingly events and actions, and spatial cases or prepositions start out marking spatial relations before they get stretched into abstract grammatical functions. If the categories were nothing but distributional facts, that alignment between slots and semantic types would be an enormous coincidence. And children seem to bootstrap into categories by meaning — treating "the blick" as a thing and "is blicking" as an action — before they've had enough distributional evidence to run any real tests. That pushes toward the idea that noun, verb, dative, locative and the rest are natural kinds, something like species: each has a semantic/conceptual core, and the distributional signature is just the surface reflex of it.

I know the standard move is to say one is a formal definition and the other a notional one, and I can own both. That feels like changing the subject. My actual question is which is the better account of what a grammatical category is. Should the semantic intuition get debunked as prototype effects plus the grammaticalization of banal semantic tendencies — so the distribution is real and the kind is not? Should pure distributionalism get given up in favor of categories as semantic kinds with contingent, partial distributional signatures? Or is there a non-hand-wavy reading on which the distribution and the semantics are the same cut in the world viewed from two sides? And what would tell us apart here: what sort of language data would actually distinguish these hypotheses rather than just re-describe them?
