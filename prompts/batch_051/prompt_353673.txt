Two people in my epistemology group got into it after the meeting, and I've been chewing on it since. The topic was epistemic akrasia: believing something while also believing your evidence doesn't support it. Sam said that's just incoherent, not merely irrational. If you genuinely take your evidence not to support P, then you don't believe P; if you do believe P, then you must take your evidence to support it. You can't occupy both states at once, so there's nothing to rationalize.

Mira said he was dodging the interesting cases. Suppose you work through a weird logic problem and land on Q. Then you remember you're running on four hours of sleep, and in the past, logic done on four hours of sleep has gone wrong for you about a third of the time. You can't locate any error in the reasoning. Surely the rational response is to become less confident in Q, maybe to suspend judgment — even while the object-level argument still looks right to you. If Sam forbids that, he's telling you to ignore evidence about your own reliability.

Sam answered: if the sleep corrupted the reasoning, then there's a mistake in it, and the rational move is to find the mistake; if it didn't corrupt it, the sleep evidence is irrelevant. Either way, "I probably went wrong but I can't see where" isn't a reason against Q. It's just the standing possibility of error, which accompanies every belief, and if that counted as a reason we'd never believe anything.

Mira said he was conflating the standing possibility of error with specific evidence about this performance. Higher-order evidence isn't the thought that I might be wrong; it's the fact that this token reasoning was produced under conditions that independently predict error. And it can come from outside too: if a friend whose logical sense you respect reads the same proof and walks away unconvinced, that should move you even if neither of you can point to the bad step. Refusing to budge there doesn't look principled, it looks dogmatic.

Sam replied: the friend case is exactly the akratic one, and I don't see why her inarticulate unease is evidence against Q unless it can be cashed out as an objection to some premise or rule of inference. Worse, the whole maneuver self-destructs: your judgment that the higher-order evidence supports lowering your confidence in Q is itself a piece of sleep-deprived reasoning, maybe one of the unreliable third. If you take the evidence seriously at the object level, you should also take it seriously at the meta level — and then at the meta-meta level, and so on. So you either stop arbitrarily or slide into skepticism.

Mira said the regress is too theatrical. You update on the evidence you have, once, and then you're done; you don't need each level to be maximally reliable. The initial state — full confidence in Q while knowing the faculty that produced it is error-prone right now — is what fails to respect evidence. A perfectly transparent reasoner might never rationally be akratic, but we aren't that reasoner, so rational akrasia may just be the price of bounded cognition.

I couldn't tell who was winning. Sam seemed right that an inarticulate hunch shouldn't be a reason, but Mira seemed right that ignoring evidence about your own reliability is dogmatic. Can you adjudicate this move by move? I want to know which claims are right, which are overstated, and whether there's a stable position between "never budge without an identified error" and "the regress."
