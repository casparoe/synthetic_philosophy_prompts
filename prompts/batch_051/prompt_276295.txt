I'm working through Aristotle's De Anima alongside the short psychological treatises collected in the Parva Naturalia, and I want a complete inventory of the distinct jobs that phantasia (conventionally translated as 'imagination' or 'appearance') does in his account of the soul. My working list has five entries:

1. Retention and re-presentation: phantasia is a movement generated by actual perception, and it explains how a phantasma can remain present to the animal after the perceived object is gone.
2. Dreaming and illusion: residual motions in the sense organs, surfacing in sleep, produce dream images; phantasia is also the source of cases in which something appears other than it is.
3. Thinking: Aristotle holds that the soul never thinks without a phantasma; images serve thought in a way analogous to the way percepts serve perception.
4. Desire and locomotion: an animal moves only when desire is combined with phantasia, and in humans deliberative phantasia combines and compares images so that one course of action can be selected.
5. Memory: remembering is directed at a phantasma taken as a likeness of something perceived earlier and accompanied by awareness that the thing belongs to the past.

Give me one more reasonably distinct function of phantasia in Aristotle's texts. It should not be a modern philosophical reconstruction, and it must not be a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on this list. Say precisely what distinguishes it from the nearest item or items above, indicate where in Aristotle it is grounded (a paraphrase of the relevant line of thought is enough), and give an honest assessment of whether it earns a separate entry in the inventory. Please also separate what the texts plainly warrant from what is contested or a minority reading among scholars of Aristotle, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus view.
