I teach epistemology, and after sitting in on a couple of machine-learning reading groups I keep tripping over the same parallel. Philosophers have a long-running debate about whether curiosity is an intellectual virtue and whether knowledge is worth having for it's own sake—not merely because it gets you a job or lets you predict the weather, but because understanding is a final good, the kind of thing that makes a life go better even if it never cashes out. Meanwhile, the RL people have a literature on "intrinsic motivation": when an agent's environment gives only sparse rewards, you hand it a small bonus for reducing its prediction error, so it seeks out surprising states and explores. The odd thing is that they call this bonus "intrinsic curiosity," but in the implementation it's just another scalar added to the objective, tuned so that, on average, the exploration helps the agent eventually maximize the real, external reward. That doesn't sound intrinsic in the philosopher's sense at all—it sounds like instrumentally useful ignorance-seeking with better branding.

So here's what I want to know: is this a genuine structural parallel, or am I just pattern-matching on the word "intrinsic"? One reading is that philosophy's intrinsic/instrumental value distinction maps neatly onto the ML distinction between auxiliary rewards and the true reward signal—and if so, the ML case might suggest that "knowledge for its own sake" is really deferred instrumental value with a fancier normative story. But maybe the parallel breaks because in philosophy "intrinsic value" is a normative notion about what ought to be pursued, while the reward function in RL is just whatever the designer writes down; no matter how we dress it up, an agent isn't valuing learning for itself, it's being paid to look confused. What would need to be true on each side for the analogy to run deep, and can you name something the analogy predicts that turns out false?
