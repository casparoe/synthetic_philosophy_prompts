In a seminar on whether large language models understand language, a colleague shut down a disagreement by saying that the whole question is confused, because these systems are just "stochastic parrots." The phrase was asked to do two pieces of work in his argument. First, it was meant to establish that a model's outputs are not about anything — that when a model produces a sentence that looks like an answer or a claim, there is no meaning, reference, or assertion behind it. Second, it was meant to support a practical conclusion: we should not treat anything a model says as a report about the world, and it is a category mistake to ask whether the model understood the question.

I find the phrase suspiciously elastic. Sometimes it seems to be nothing more than a description of the training setup: the model is trained to predict upcoming tokens, and its outputs are sampled from a probability distribution over continuations. Sometimes it seems to be a much stronger thesis about the absence of intentionality, reference, or grounding. The slide from the first sort of claim to the second is doing real work in his argument, and I cannot tell which reading is supposed to license it.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "LLMs are stochastic parrots" might be used to express? For each, say whether it is a near-platitude about the training objective, a substantive and defensible thesis about semantics or intentionality, or something stronger that is probably false — and then say which reading or readings, if any, would actually support the two conclusions my colleague drew. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single preferred interpretation, so I can bring it back to the group and see whether we are even arguing about the same claim.
