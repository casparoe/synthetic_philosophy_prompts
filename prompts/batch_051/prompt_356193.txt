One answer to the boundary problem—who should get a say in a political decision—is that the demos should be everyone whose interests the decision substantially affects, rather than everyone inside a territory. Imagine a city that tries to implement this literally. It replaces its territorial voter roll for municipal referendums with a rolling stake registry. For each ballot measure, any person aged sixteen or older, resident or not, may register a claim of affectedness: a short form stating how the measure would affect them, backed by whatever evidence they can attach (leases, deeds, employer letters, medical records, travel records, utility bills, and the like). A five-member civil-service Panel of Stakes assigns each claimant a weight between 0 and 1 for that measure, based on the magnitude, directness, and verifiability of the claimed interest; weights are published with a one-line reason before voting opens. Claimants then vote yes or no, and the measure passes if the weighted yes vote exceeds the weighted no vote. Ballot measures themselves are also initiated through the registry: a proposal qualifies if the panel certifies that its sponsors' aggregate registered stake passes a threshold.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. Analyze how it would actually behave: the incentives it creates for residents, one-time claimants, organized groups, and the Panel; how each of those actors would game it; which interests it would systematically overweight and underweight; and whether the failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the aim that the demos track affectedness rather than geography. End by identifying what existing political institution, if any, this mechanism most comes to resemble under a different name.
