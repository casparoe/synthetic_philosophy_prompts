At our department happy hour yesterday, two friends of mine — Maya, who does statistical mechanics, and Dan, who does quantum information — got into a long argument about Maxwell's demon, and since I'm the philosophy-of-physics person in the group they both now want me to referee. I honestly couldn't settle it on the spot. Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it.

1. Maya: The demon only has to open a tiny massless, frictionless trapdoor whenever a faser-than-average molecule comes at it from the left or a slower-than-average one from the right. Opening and closing that door requires no work, so an initially uniform gas ends up with a hot side and a cold side. That's a temperature difference from a single heat bath with no work input — exactly the thing the second law says can't happen.

2. Dan: But the demon has to know when to open the door, which means it has to measure molecule speeds and store the results. Information processing isn't free: the Landauer bound says erasing one bit costs at least kT ln 2 of dissipated heat, and a demon that keeps working has to erase its memory. That dissipation is enough to heat the cold side back up, so the second law survives.

3. Maya: You've put the cost in the wrong place. Measurement itself can be done reversibly — you can couple a memory bit prepared in a known state to the molecule so that the bit just ends up correlated with it, with no minimum work. And the erasure bill only comes due if the demon reuses the same cells. A demon can, in principle, write each result to a fresh cell and never erase at all, so no kT ln 2 ever gets paid.

4. Dan: Fresh cells aren't free. Before you use a cell, it has to be in a standard blank state, and resetting an occupied cell to blank is erasure even if you do it on the tape before the run. A finite, cyclic demon has to return to the same state each cycle, so it has to dump the information it acquired, and that's precisely where it pays Landauer's price. So a cyclic demon still can't violate the second law.

5. Maya: Then you've only shown that a demon that has to close its cycle can't cheat. Give it an effectively unbounded blank tape and it can keep producing hotter and colder gas while the tape just fills up. If you still want to say the second law holds, you are forced to count the blank tape itself as a thermodynamic recource. That's a much stronger, and weirder, claim than just saying the demon loses.

They asked me to tell them who's right about what. Please adjudicate the whole thing move by move: for each of the five moves, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right and a short justification, then say what each of them is right about, who is more right overall (or if it's a draw), and what they're both missing.

Give me the verdict as a JSON object with this schema:

- moves: an array of objects, each with fields speaker (Maya or Dan), move_number (integer), claim (a one-sentence paraphrase of the move), rating (one of clearly mistaken, mostly mistaken, mixed, mostly right, clearly right), and justification (one to three sentences).
- what_each_is_right_about: a string spelling out the specific contributions of each side.
- who_is_more_right_overall: a string giving the overall winner or draw.
- what_both_missed: a string describing the considerations neither of them fully addressed.
