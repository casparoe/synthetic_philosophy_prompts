I teach an undergrad seminar called "Who's in the Archive?" — mostly philosophy of history with some archival-studies reading mixed in, 18 students, juniors and seniors, one 80-minute block. I want a single-session small-group activity (not a lecture, not a discussion of a reading) that makes them feel the force of the idea that archives are shaped by power and that silences get built into sources. The point I want them to arrive at themselves is roughly this: the archive isn't a passive storehouse that happens to underrepresent the poor or the marginal; silences get produced by a run of small, boring administrative choices — what gets collected at all, what gets kept under a quota, what gets a subject heading, what gets "weeded" under a retention schedule. By the time a researcher sits down with a box, those choices have been naturalized into the look of an obvious record.

Design the activity with rules, materials (I can print slips and index cards, but there's no budget beyond that), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the concepts. I want the design to include at least these moving parts: (1) a triage moment where they can't keep everything and have to say what's "evidentially valuable"; (2) a fixed subject-heading or controlled vocabuarly card that lacks categories for some of the people or events in the material, so they either stretch a heading, dump something under "miscellaneous," or drop the item; (3) a second round where an outside directive — a funder, a government office, a new director — changes the retention rules, so power enters not as censorship from on high but as a change in what must be kept; and (4) some items that were never in the packet at all, recorded nowhere in the first place, so the pre-archival silence only becomes visible in the debrief. The rounds should run in small groups of 4-5 with roles whose actual job constrains them — someone with final say, someone bound by the vocabuarly, someone with a small conservation or preservation budget.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to steer back, and be honest about which feature of the real phenomenon the activity can't reproduce and will have to be said by me in the debrief. My suspicion is that one such feature is that the simulation shows the kept and discarded items side by side, whereas the real archive hides the discard pile, so the naturalness of the surviving record is nearly impossible to fake in a classroom. Work through that or whatever else you notice.

Keep it runnable: printed slips, index cards, maybe a whiteboard. And write the whole thing in American English.
