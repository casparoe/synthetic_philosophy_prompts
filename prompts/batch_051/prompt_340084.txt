Been rereading some old decision-theory sequences and I tried to write out the coherence argument for expected utility maximization as carefully as I could, and now the careful version looks like it proves too much. Here's what I have. (1) If you prefer A to B, then, other things equal, you'll pay some small sum to switch from B to A. (2) Take someone with intransitive preferences: A over B, B over C, C over A. (3) A trader can start him with C, charge a penny to switch to B, a penny to switch to A, then a penny to switch back to C; he accepts each trade at the time and ends right where he started, minus three cents. (4) No acceptable theory of rationality can call that pattern rational. (5) So intransitive preferences are irrational. (6) And the standard line is that every violation of the expected-utility axioms—independence, completeness, continuity—can be turned into some pump of this general shape, which is supposed to establish that rationality just is expected-utility maximization. But here's the part that now looks too strong: the pump doesn't actually check whether the victim's preferences satisfy the axioms, it only needs each local trade to look good in the moment. So a framing-sensitive person, or someone whose tastes shifts between Tuesday and Wednesday, or someone less clever than the trader who can't see how the trades combine, is equally exploitable by the same machinery. That would make every actual human irrational, which can't be what the argument was trying to show. I'd like to keep (4) and (6) if I can, since giving either up feels like abandoning the whole coherence program. Where does this go wrong, and what do the main ways of fixing it each have to give up?
