I have been working through Gödel, Escher, Bach on my own, without any formal background in logic, and I keep trying to turn the book's analogy between self-referential formulas and self-aware minds into an argument I can inspect step by step. I have ended up with the following reconstruction, and I would like a precise verdict on it: is the argument valid, and if not, which step fails exactly?

(1) Gödel showed that any consistent formal system with enough arithmetic can construct a sentence that says of itself that it is not provable in that system.
(2) If the system is consistent, that sentence cannot be proved within the system, and since it says exactly that it cannot be proved, what it says is true.
(3) A human mind that understands the system's rules can see that the system is consistent, and can therefore see that its Gödel sentence is true.
(4) So the human mind reaches a truth that the system's own rules cannot generate.
(5) Now suppose that some particular human mind simply is a consistent formal system of the same kind; call it M.
(6) Then, by (4), the mind cannot reach M's Gödel sentence: that sentence is unprovable in M.
(7) But, by (3), the mind can see that M is consistent, and can therefore see that M's Gödel sentence is true.
(8) So the mind both reaches and fails to reach M's Gödel sentence. Contradiction; therefore the mind is not any consistent formal system.
(9) A computer running a program is a formal system at work, so the mind is not merely a computer.
(10) The extra ingredient is the loop by which a system refers to itself, and this same loop — a self looking at itself — is what we experience as self-awareness; so a purely mechanical system cannot be self-aware.

I suspect the trouble is around (5)–(7), but the part of the book I most want to pin down is (10), and I cannot tell whether (10) inherits its plausibility from the earlier steps or falls apart on its own. Please give the exact diagnosis. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the argument is valid, or which step fails — and then give your reasoning step by step, with no preamble and no restating of the question. If a step fails, give the most defensible reading of that step and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading.
