I have been teaching the statue-and-the-clay puzzle and notice that students and textbooks keep defaulting to the same stock structure: a pre-existing lump of clay is shaped into a statue, so the lump predates the statue and can outlive it by surviving a squashing. I want a case that reverses the direction of seniority. Please construct a thought experiment in which a statue made from unfired clay in an ordinary art-conservation studio is gradually rebuilt by a conservator, section by section, using a newly purchased batch of clay, until none of the original material remains in the exhibited figure; the original clay must still exist, kept in a crate and cohesive enough to be handled as a distinct lump. The scenario must be realistic and no more than a paragraph, with no exotic physics, and it must not stipulate from the outset whether the statue survives the rebuilding; that verdict has to fall out of each theory's commitments about sortals, persistence conditions, coincidence, and the transitivity of identity.

The case is meant to test whether the one-object or identity solution to the puzzle is quietly dependent on the usual lump-predates-the-statue arrangement. On the constitution-is-not-identity view the outcome should be straightforward: two coincident objects at the start, two at the end, with the statue surviving a change of constituent matter while the successive clay parcels remain non-identical. The one-object view should be forced into a dilemma: either deny that the statue survives the complete replacement of its clay, or admit that the statue at the end cannot be identical with the new clay, since the old and the new clay are distinct parcels and identity is transitive. Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) the constitution-is-not-identity verdict and reasoning; (3) the one-object verdict and reasoning, including the dilemma; (4) the feature of the case doing the work, and what the case would collapse back into if the original lump instead predated the statue; (5) the strongest reply available to a defender of the one-object view, and whether it changes the verdict.
