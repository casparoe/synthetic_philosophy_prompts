I've been trying to work out something about Moloch-type traps and I want a candid check before I start using it in writing. I call it 'sincerity inflation'. Standard Moloch, as I understand it, is a multipolar trap: each player would prefer the cooperative outcome, but no one can choose it unilaterally without being eaten, so everyone competes their way to a worse outcome. I think there's a subclass of these where what's being competed over isn't material payoff but the audience's belief that you are sincere, spontaneous, vulnerable, the real thing. And the difference is that the trap is maintained by the audience's expectations rather than the players' incentives.

The mechanism goes like this. You have a bunch of people competing for something scarce — attention, jobs, dates, votes — and the thing is partly allocated according to judgements of sincerity. One person works out that faking a bit of it gives them an edge: a slightly rehearsed anecdote that lands as off-the-cuff, a bit of manufactured vulnerability, a polished messiness. Others copy, because the edge is real. Now the audience adapts: the fake becomes the new baseline, so it stops reading as sincere, and the bar for what counts as genuine rises. So competitors invest more in the performance. The audience gets more cynical and more sophisticated. The fakes get better because the stakes have gone up. Real sincerity — clumsy, unrehearsed, inconsistent — stops being legible, and sincere people either learn to perform or get filtered out. The kicker: everyone ends up worse of, and no individual can unilateraly stop performing, because the audience now punishes anything below the inflated standard. So it's a trap, but it isn't a prisoner's dilemma between the competitors; it's a race to the bottom where the bottom is a moving standard held by people outside the race.

I think this explains three things at once: why 'just be yourself' is useless advice in a market where everyone is being themselves; why political authenticity keeps spiralling, each confession raising the demand for the next while the public trusts it less the more practised it looks; and why online confessional writing and performative radical honesty have become genres with formulas and conventions.

Three questions. Is this a real concept or just Moloch plus cynicism with a new name? Has someone already worked out the same mechanism under another label — I keep thinking Goodhart's law, signalling theory, or some sociology of 'authenticity work' — and if so, what does my version add or get wrong? And where does it break: are there cases where competing for perceived sincerity makes sincerity better or cheaper instead of inflating it, and what would that tell me about the limits of the idea?

Be as harsh as you like; I'd rather kill it now than build on it. I don't need a reading list or citations — just tell me the names if they exist and I'll chase them up myself.
