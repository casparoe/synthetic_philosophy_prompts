I've been hanging around rationalist-adjacent forums long enough to have watched several karma systems eat themselves alive, and I've ended up with a fairly settled view: scalar reputation systems (karma, upvotes, points) do more harm than good once a community gets past a few hundred or so people. My reasons are roughly these. First, Goodhart: once the score is a proxy for "good contributor," a meaningful number of people optimize the score directly, and you can't cleanly separate that from legitimate participation. Second, context collapse: one scalar blends "gave a life-saving correction," "made a genuinely great joke," and "flattered the ingroup in a charming way" into the same currency, so the system can't represent locally valid reputation that doesn't transfer across contexts. Third, status-market dynamics: karma becomes a positional good, old accounts become the landed gentry, and newcomers read the leaderboard as a social hierarchy rather than as a guide to who is worth listening to. Fourth, engagement selection: the cheapest stable way to earn points is fast, agreeable, or outrage-flavored content, so the system slowly selects for the exact behavior it was meant to supress. Fifth, attack surface: distributed identity makes the currency forgeable at scale (sykbils, brigades, coordinated voting), and the standard fixes — minimum karma to post, vouching, karma-weighted votes — reintroduce centralization and cliquishness. I don't think these are finger problems; I think they're in the deep structure of attaching a scalar, transferable, accruing score to heterogeneous social judgment.
