I'm a PhD student in political theory, and working on institutional change has left me with an asymmetry I can't decide whether to keep or get rid of. When a city council proposes abolishing an old bylaw whose rationale nobody can remember — say, a rule about how wide awnings may overhang the pavement, or wich side of the street a cart may stand on — even people who think of themselves as rigorous and modern about policy will say something like 'someone put it there for a reason, leave it alone unless you can explain why it was put there.' The same people do not say anything like this about old computer systems. A developer who finds a strange condition in legacy code and can't say what it does is supposed to treat that very ignorance as a reason to flag or remove it, not keep it. Same for an obsolete medical practice or a leftover piece of a building whose function nobody knows. So we treat unexplained persistence as evidence of hidden wisdom in social rules, but as evidence of rot in technical artifacts. Is there a principle that actually justifies this difference, or is it just status-quo bias wearing a Burkean costume? I want the strongest justification you can give for the asymmetry, tested against two hard cases: a legacy subroutine that has caused production failures before for reasons nobody can reconstruct, and an old social rule that inflicts small but measurable, ongoing harm on an identifiable group. Then give me an honest verdict on whether to keep the asymmetry, ditch it, or reverse it. And tell me what specific argument or case would change your mind from whichever verdict you land on, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
