I've been thinking for a while about whether it's ever wrong to hope too much, and I've noticed that people treat hope very differently: some think hope should track the odds, some think it's good only when it keeps them acting, and some think keeping hope alive is itself right even when success is nearly impossible. I'd like a 'which position on hope do you actually hold?' quiz for a general audience, eight to ten questions long. Each question should present a concrete case (a terminal diagnosis, a struggling political campaign, a town deciding whether to rebuild after a flood, someone returning to a relationship that has failed before, a lottery-ticket habit, and similar) and offer three answer options that quietly separate three commitments: whether hope's value depends on how likely the outcome is, on what hope makes the person do, or on what hope expresses about what the person cares about. The options should be concrete enough that people answer from their gut, and the questions should never invoke the labels. Then give a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto at least five positions, such as accuracy-first realism, usefulness-based realism, tragic idealism, hope-as-fidelity, and a view that hope is wrong when it substitutes for action. If some combinations of answers match no defensible position, say so and briefly indicate whether that combination looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; also flag any two of the five positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate. Please say how confident you are in the scoring key and identify the question-to-position mapping you are least sure of.
