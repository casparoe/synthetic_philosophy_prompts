I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and my study group has been working through modal epistemic logic. The other night two members got into a long argument about the problem of logical omniscience, and I couldn't referee it at the time — I'm still not sure who's right. We'd been using the standard possible-worlds semantics for knowledge: Kφ holds at a world w iff φ holds at every world accessible from w. On that semantics knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if you know φ, and φ entails ψ, then you know ψ. One of them — call him Dan — said this alone shows the semantics is false as an account of knowledge. The other — call her Priya — said he was confusing an idealization with a refutation. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

Dan: The formalism doesn't just simplify, it gets the central fact wrong. I know some axioms of Peano arithmetic, but I don't know their hard consequences. The semantics says I know all of them, immediately and for free. An idealization that's false of every finite agent in just that way isn't an idealization, it's a different subject.

Priya: That's like rejecting frictionless planes because no actual surface is frictionless. The box isn't supposed to track what's occurrent in your head; it tracks what your information rules out. If your information rules out every not-φ world, and φ entails ψ, then it already rules out every not-ψ world. As a point about information, that's just correct.

Dan: Fine, but then you're not modeling knowledge. The semantics was introduced to model what agents know. If you reinterpret K as "the agent's information entails," you've relabeled the target to get away from the objection.

Priya: I'm not relabeling — I'm distinguishing occurrent knowledge, the stuff before your mind, from implicit knowledge, the stuff your information already settles. People do say things like "he knew that all along, in a sense." The box models the second. Read as occurrent knowledge, of course it's absurd; I was never defending that.

Dan: But I'm not even asking for occurrent knowledge. Closure under known implication is plausible: if I know φ and I know that φ entails ψ, then I'm in a position to know ψ. But the semantics gives me closure under implication simpliciter — I never have to recognize the entailment at all, and the conclusion is mine with no reasoning whatsoever. That's the false part, and calling it "implicit" doesn't fix it, because implicit knowledge should at least be something a finite mind could in principle derive, not every logical consequence there is.

Priya: Why not? Being in a position to know is exactly what "implicit" means here — the conclusion is already contained in your information, whether or not you've done the derivation. The semantics abstracts from the cost of reasoning. That's what makes it a model rather than a description, and idealizations don't have to be nearly true to be useful; they have to isolate the structure you care about.

Dan: But for a model of a finite agent, the cost of reasoning is the structure I care about. If you abstract away all reasoning costs, the model can't distinguish me from a perfect reasoner with the same information. It's not like ignoring air resistance; the gap between what the model attributes and what any agent actually has is infinite, and you can't just add it back as a small correction.

Priya: Actually, you can add it back — that's what awareness semantics and impossible-worlds models do. Add an awareness function or allow non-normal worlds where closure fails, and you get a bounded reasoner while keeping the core possible-worlds machinery. So closure isn't a false prediction built into the framework; it's a modeling choice, and the framework is flexible enough to turn it off.

Dan: I grant the framework can be modified. But you've now fixed the problem twice by moving to a different model. The unadorned box, taken as a semantics for knowledge, was false — which was my original claim. The fact that a different model avoids the problem is a concession, not a defense of the original one.

Priya: I already conceded in my first reply that, read as occurrent knowledge, it's absurd. What I'm defending is that the semantics is a good-enough idealization of knowledge for what epistemic logic actually gets used for — not a literal theory of the mind. You still haven't said which purpose it actually fails at.

After that the two of them went in circles over whether "read as occurrent knowledge, of course it's absurd" was her already granting his point, and no one else in the group would take sides — including me, partly because I wasn't sure there was a clean answer and partly because the whole thing had gotten to deep into the semantics.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication. For each substantive move — I count maybe five or six — say whether it's clearly mistaken, weak but defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Then say who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing or conflating. If I've set the dispute up badly, or if the task as I've framed it rests on a confused premise, tell me that plainly instead of forcing a winner. Answer in American English.
