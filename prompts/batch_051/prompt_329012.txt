I am a retired natural scientist—biochemistry, mostly—and I came to philosophy late. Lately I have been working through the epistemology of self-knowledge, and the KK principle has become the point where my old laboratory habits and my new reading pull against each other. In the form I have in mind, the principle says that if one knows that p, one is in a position to know that one knows that p: knowing is self-illuminating in the minimal sense that a careful subject could get from the first-order state to the second-order one by reflection alone. I understand the principle's appeal, and I also gather there is an anti-luminosity argument, associated with Timothy Williamson, according to which no non-trivial condition is luminous—including, presumably, the condition of knowing that p. Rather than adjudicate that argument in the abstract, I would like a concrete, ordinary case that does the work of making a KK failure vivid.

Please construct a thought experiment meeting all of the following specifications.

1. The subject is a single competent adult in an ordinary everyday setting—a workplace, a household, a clinic. The proposition p is a mundane claim about the external world.
2. At the time in question, the subject knows that p.
3. The subject is not in a position to know that she knows that p, where this means that even if she reflects carefully, with normal reasoning and full attention to the situation as she sees it, she could not properly come to know that she knows. The failure must not be a mere failure to have considered the second-order question.
4. No skeptical hypotheses, no borderline sensory thresholds or sorites-style series of indistinguishable cases, no appeal to inarticulate expert skill of the sort sometimes illustrated by expert chicken sexers, and no lies or deliberate deception. The KK failure must be generated by misleading higher-order evidence: the subject has good but misleading reason to distrust the reliability of her own first-order method or evidence.

Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then, in order:
- State exactly what p is, and the first-order method or evidence by which she forms the belief.
- Explain why the belief counts as knowledge, and be explicit if this requires the premise that first-order knowledge can survive misleading evidence about one's own reliability.
- Explain why she is nevertheless not in a position to know that she knows, and specify what would have to change for her to become so.
- Identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work, and say what would happen if it were removed: would the case collapse into ordinary knowledge with KK intact, or into a case where there was no first-order knowledge to begin with?
- Conclude with the strongest reply available to a defender of the KK principle: what exactly would they have to reject in the case, and is that rejection a plausible cost for them to absorb?

I care far more about the epistemic mechanics than the window dressing, so please keep the story plain. Also, please end your reply with one final line beginning "Summary:" that states in a single sentence the mechanism by which the case produces its KK failure.
