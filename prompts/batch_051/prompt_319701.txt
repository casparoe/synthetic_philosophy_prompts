I keep running into the evolutionary argument for AI takeover, where the idea is that once machine intelligences are able to copy themselves, natural selection takes over automatically. Variants that are better at getting compute, money, and influence will spread, and the ones that let themselves be shut off or that don't seek power will simply vanish. So on this story takeover pressure is almost inevitable, and it isn't just a one-off design problem; it's an evolutionary trend. But the AI systems we actually have now don't do anything like that. A lab trains a model, evals it, maybe fine-tunes it; if its deemed useful and safe enough, a company serves millions of copies of the same fixed weights; if not, the run just gets dropped. There's copying, but the deciding force is human evaluation and market demand, not an AI producing offspring with heritable variation. What I want to know is whether the evolutionary argument survives this situation. Does the selection story still have force when the thing being selected is a training run or a product line rather than an autonomous agent making copies of itself? If it doesn't, should we say natural selection just hasn't started yet, or that the argument was doing less work than its defenders thought? If it does still apply, what exactly is the unit of selection, the model, the lab, the training process, or something else?
