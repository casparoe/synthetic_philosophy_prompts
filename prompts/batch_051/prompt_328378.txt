You are a senior decision theorist, and I want you to act as a methodical referee auditing the internal consistency of a set of views before I present them. I am drafting a paper defending the thesis that ambiguity-averse choice of the sort displayed in the Ellsberg paradox is rationally permissible, and a colleague has suggested that the package of views I rely on may be subtly inconsistent. I cannot see where, and I do not want to signal in advance which commitment I am prepared to abandon.

My commitments are the following. (a) For decisions under risk, where the objective probabilities are given and known, expected utility theory is the correct normative model. (b) For decisions under ambiguity, where the relevant probabilities are not given, the standard Ellsberg choices are rationally permissible: an agent may prefer a bet on red to a bet on black in an urn with 30 red balls and 60 balls that are an unknown mix of black and yellow, while simultaneously preferring a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow. (c) The best formal representation of this permissible pattern is the smooth ambiguity model: the agent has a second-order probability distribution over possible first-order priors and evaluates each act by taking the expectation, under that second-order distribution, of a concave transformation of the act's first-order expected utility. (d) Part of the model's appeal to me is that it makes ambiguity aversion intelligible as Bayesianism one level up—ordinary risk aversion applied to expected utilities rather than to monetary payoffs. (e) I still accept the reduction of compound lotteries as a rationality requirement for objective risk: a rational agent must be indifferent between a compound objective lottery and its single-stage reduced equivalent. (f) But I deny that my view collapses into subjective expected utility theory; I take the ambiguity aversion captured by (c) to be a genuine departure from expected utility maximization with respect to one prior, not a harmless relabeling.

Please work through this slowly. First brainstorm the pairwise implications among the six commitments, then sort the pairs into genuinely incompatible, only apparently in tension, and fully compatible. Order the genuine tensions from most to least severe, and for each one say what the cheapest revision would be, what that revision costs elsewhere in the set, and whether the revised set can still vindicate the rationality of the Ellsberg choices.
