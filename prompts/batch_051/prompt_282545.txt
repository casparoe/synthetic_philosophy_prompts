I'm taking a seminar this term on philosophical methodology and the question of weather we should trust our intuitions about tidy principles—transitivity, simplicity, that sort of thing—more than our intuitions about particular cases. Our running example is transitivity: the principle that if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A has to be better than C. The principle-first people treat that as a background constraint on rational evaluation, so if a person's pairwise judgments cycle, the problem has to be a performance error or a bad description of the options, not a discovery about value. The case-first people say each concrete comparison is a data point, and a genuine cycle would suggest our values don't form a single scale at all—maybe they're plural, or only partially ordered, or keyed to context.

I'd like a thought experiment built to order for this disagreement. Construct a realistic, low-stakes, ordinary-life trio of options A, B, and C where a thoughtful person could defensibly judge A better than B, B better than C, and C better than A, with each pairwise judgment driven by a different local consideration, and not by framing effects, memory lapses, regret, temporal discounting, or uncertainty. The local considerations should all bear on a single sort of all-things-considered betterness rather than three unrelated scales, so the case can't be defused by saying the three comparisons merely use better in different senses. The cycle should also be hard to fix by re-describing the options, and transitivity should end up looking genuinely costly to keep rather than obviously wrong or obviously untouched. After the scenario, walk me through: (a) exactly what the principle-first person says about each pairwise comparison and what they have to reinterpret or talk themselves out of to keep transitivity; (b) what the case-first person says and why they take the case to be evidence for plural values rather than evidence that one of the three comparisons was just a mistake; (c) which single feature of the scenario is doing the most work to pull the two camps apart, and what would happen to the disagreement if that feature were removed; and (d) the strongest counterattack the principle-first person could make against using one ordinary-life cycle as evidence against transitivity. The point of the case is to test whether a case-based objection can ever rationally dislodge a principle like transitivity, so dont just stipulate that the cycle exists—the pairwise judgments have to earn it.
