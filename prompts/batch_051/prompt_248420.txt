After a long argument with a friend about hell, I wrote down the position I ended up defending, and I want a careful audit of whether it hangs together before we pick the argument back up. The commitments I left defending are these:

(1) A punishment is just only if it is proportionate to the wrong done; no one may be punished beyond what they deserve.
(2) The gravity of a wrong can depend on the standing of the one wronged: a wrong against an infinitely good being can in principle have infinite gravity, and that is what makes the idea of infinite punishment coherent at all.
(3) Between human beings, however, justice is impartial: the same act is not worse because its victim is a king rather than a peasant.
(4) I think eternal conscious torment would be just only if something like (2) were true; otherwise it is simply disproportionate.
(5) But I also hold that God's love constrains punishment: no perfectly good being could will unending, irredeemable, conscious suffering as a punishment, since that would be cruelty by every standard we accept elsewhere.
(6) Yet I cannot accept universal salvation, because it seems to dissolve the seriousness of how we live — if every wrong is eventually repaired and every person restored, what was ultimately at stake?
(7) And I cannot accept annihilationism either: simply ceasing to exist seems like the wrong kind of response to wrongdoing, because it leaves the moral order with no final, explicit answering for what was done.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe is each genuine tension? Rank them rather than treating them all as equally bad, say what each tension would require giving up, and identify the single least costly revision. Also tell me how confident you are in your analysis and single out the point you are least sure of.
