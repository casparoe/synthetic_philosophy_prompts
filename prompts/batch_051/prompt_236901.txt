At a moral psychology workshop last term, two of my fellow graduate students—a philosopher and a social psychologist—had an extended argument about whether the situationist critique undermines virtue ethics. I took notes and have reconstructed their exchange below. I would like a careful, step-by-step adjudication of what each of them said.

(1) The psychologist claimed that the classic situationist evidence—Milgram's obedience studies, Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan experiment, the finding that unexpectedly finding a dime in a phone booth sharply increased helping in one well-known study—shows that conduct tracks minor features of the immediate situation far more reliably than it tracks stable dispositions. Since virtue ethics explains action by appeal to virtues such as honesty, courage, and compassion, and since these studies suggest that people largely lack such stable traits, virtue ethics rests on a false empirical psychology.

(2) The philosopher replied that this was too quick. A virtue need not be a global trait that manifests with equal regularity in every context; it can be a disposition to act well within a domain or across relevantly similar situations. Moreover, these studies often test what happens when a virtue faces strong situational pressure, which shows only that dispositions can be overridden, not that they are absent. And even if the studies show that most people fall short of full virtue, virtue ethics is in part a normative account of the kind of character we should cultivate, not merely a description of the character we already have.

(3) The psychologist answered that the rarity reply only pushes the problem further back. If virtues are rare or local in the way now being proposed, they can no longer do the explanatory and predictive work the tradition assigned to them—for example, accounting for what a good person will do across the varied circumstances that make up a life. And if the revisionary proposal is to replace global traits with highly localized dispositions, that buys consistency with the evidence only by changing the subject: a disposition to act well in a narrow range of cases is not what we are invoking when we judge someone courageous or kind.

(4) The philosopher countered that the evidence does not force that conclusion. For one thing, these studies are mostly cases in which virtue is under pressure; they tell us little about cross-situational consistency in ordinary contexts, and longitudinal research on personality finds substantial rank-order stability in relevant behavior. For another, the real lesson may not be that character is unreal but that we are more dependent on our situations and environments than we previously thought. That lesson actually favors a virtue ethics that emphasizes the cultivation of supportive communities and environments, rather than one that leaves it to isolated individuals to exhibit heroic virtue.

(5) The psychologist replied that this sounded like a concession presented as a development. The position began as an account on which individual virtues explain action; it ends up as a claim that we should engineer environments so that our local dispositions happen to produce good outcomes. At that point the distinctively explanatory commitments of traditional virtue ethics have been given up, and what remains is a theory about moral luck, situation management, and institutional design. That may be true, but it is no longer a vindication of virtue ethics as traditionally understood.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, state whether it is clearly correct, defensible but incomplete, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify the assessment. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing that a correct resolution of their dispute would need to include.
