ok so i'm about a month out from presenting a paper at a department seminar, and i've been losing sleep over the Q&A. the paper is basically my attempt to make Hayek's dispersed knowledge point do more work than the usual simple reading of the socialist calculation debate. my thesis is something like: even if we grant Lange everything about trial-and-error pricing, the real Hayekian objection isn't just about whether a central planning board can compute prices—it's that the knowledge that matters for economic coordination includes judgments about what's worth noticing, what counts as a scarce resource, what counts as a relevant substitute, which constraints are hard vs. soft, and those judgments aren't generated by the same process that responds to price gaps after the fact. so the price system isn't just a calculator for pre-specified problems; it's part of the process by which the problems get specified in the first place. i know that sounds a little grand, but i've got a decent argument for it.

the audience is a mostly mainstream economics department, not an Austrian or libertarian crowd. there are two people who do mechanism design and actually kind of like Lange as a theoretical exercise—they're the ones i'm most worried about. there's one older guy who's basically a Hayekian but thinks my reading over-philosophizes and that the original 1945 point was mostly about informational efficiency, not problem-construction. and there are three or four applied micro people who will probably think the whole thing is too abstract and will want to know what empirical claim i'm actually making. the passage i'm hanging a lot of the paper on is from Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society," where he says:

The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.

my worry is that the mechanism design people will say something like: fine, but an iterative Groves-style mechanism or a Lange auctioneer can in principle elicit whatever private tastes people have, so the real disagreement is just about computational complexity, not some deep epistemic point. and the Hayekian guy will probably say i'm smuggling in a concept of "judgment" that does no real work. and someone is going to bring up modern Amazon/Walmart logistics and ask whether centralized coordination with dispersed local data doesn't just refute me empirically. i need a prep sheet, not a defense. give me the eight or so objections i should actually expect from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each audience member would actually put them in a seminar, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. and please include the question i'm hoping nobody asks.
