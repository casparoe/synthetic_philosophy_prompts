I've been reading LessWrong since long enough that my first instinct in any argument is to stop people when they use a slogan as if it were a step in a proof. A friend who translates novels for a living and I have been fighting about whether Google Translate 'understands' anything, and at a certain point she said 'traduttore, traditore' — translator, traitor — like that ended the matter. I think the work it was doing was roughly: statistical translation is necessarily shallow, because real translation requires a kind of creative disloyalty that a stats engine can't preform. But the proverb seems to smear together several different claims: sometimes it sounds trivially true ('any translation changes something'), sometimes it sounds false ('no translation is ever faithful'), sometimes it sounds like a defense of literary translators personally ('translators lie on purpose and that is good'), and sometimes it's doing real anti-machine-translation work. Please lay the readings out as a menu: what are the distinct precise claims 'traduttore, traditore' could be making, from the boringly true to the radical; which ones are actually defensible and which are not; and which exact reading does my friend need for her move about Google Translate being shallow to go through. Also separate which readings are compatible with a statistical system producing lots of good, useful translations without understanding any of it. I don't want the history of the proverb or a survey of translation apps; I want the disambiguation with verdicts, aimed at someone who has already read the Hofstadter-style argument that machine translation is shallow and wants it handled charitably without letting a pun do more work than it earned.
