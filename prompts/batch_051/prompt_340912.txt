Consider the U.S. Supreme Court's 2005 decision in Roper v. Simmons, which abolished the juvenile death penalty, and analyze it through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The Court's stated rationale was broadly that offenders under eighteen are less culpable than adults because their characters, impulse control, and sense of responsibility are still developing and therefore not yet fixed. Strawson's Basic Argument, in rough paraphrase, runs as follows: a person acts from the way she is, and could be ultimately morally responsible for what she does only if she were ultimately responsible for the way she is; but that would require an infinite regress of self-creation, since any act of self-creation would have to issue from a self already in place. Do the mapping in both directions. By the lights of this argument, what should the Justices have made of a culpability distinction that turns on how fixed a defendant's character is—and does the opinion implicitly treat adults as possessing the very kind of ultimate responsibility the argument denies to everyone? Then run the analysis the other way: what does the case, so read, show about the Basic Argument—for instance, that the law tracks something the metaphysics misses, that the argument is too coarse to guide legal grading, or something else? If this framing conflates ordinary responsibility with ultimate responsibility, or otherwise rests on a confused premise, say so plainly and redo the analysis on the corrected framing.
