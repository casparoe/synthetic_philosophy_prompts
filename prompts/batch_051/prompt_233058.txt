A friend cornered me with a version of Frege's puzzle and now I can't find the exit. He says the whole thing can be set out as an argument that proves too much, and one of the steps has to be wrong, but rejecting any of them feels like it costs something I don't want to pay. Here it is:

(1) A true identity statement "a = b" is true just in case the thing named by "a" is the very same thing as the thing named by "b".
(2) So "Hesperus = Phosphorus" and "Hesperus = Hesperus" both say, of the same planet, that it is identical to itself.
(3) If two sentences say the same thing, then anyone who fully understands both should find them equally informative; one can't be a empirical discovery while the other is a triviality.
(4) But "Hesperus = Phosphorus" was a nontrivial astronomical discovery, while "Hesperus = Hesperus" is trivially true.
(5) Therefore, given (1)–(3), there cannot be any genuinely informative identity statements like the Hesperus/Phosphorus case.
(6) But there are such statements — that's just what ancient astronomers found out.

He says (6) is non-negotiable, so one of (1), (2), or (3) has to go, and the conclusion that all true identity statements are trivial is the "too much" bit. I want to keep both (1) and (4) if I can: names as labels for things feels right, and it really does seem like "it's the same object" can be news. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a way to keep (1) and (4) without buying a whole theory of "senses"?
