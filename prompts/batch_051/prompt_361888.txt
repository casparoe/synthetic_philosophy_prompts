I'm a novelist, not a historian, and I'm researching a novel set in Florence in the 1490s. One of my characters is a printer who genuinely believes he's living through a rebirth of antiquity; another is a Jewish doctor trained in Arabic medicine who thinks that's provincial nonsense, since in his view the interesting work has been continuous since the twelfth century. Reading around them, I've stumbled into a historiographical position I can't shake: that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are mostly later inventions — labels imposed by nineteenth-century historians and textbook writers on a much messier continuity, not real joints in the past. The more I read, the more the boundaries look like they tell you about the people who drew them than about 1350 or 1550.

I'd like you to play the critic of this conviction. What would count against the view that these periods are inventions rather than discoveries? I want the strongest evidence and arguments that should move me away from it, sorted by how decisive they'd be — wich bits of the historical record would actually discriminate between the invention view and the discovery view, and what the discovery view predicts that the invention view doesn't. Bring in self-conscious periodization by people at the time: did medievals already talk about a middle age, did Petrarch and Vasari coin the dark ages and the rebirth as ways of seeing their own moment, and does that cut for or against me?

Then bring in the discontinuities: institutions, technology, legal culture, the content of natural philosophy, anything that would show up even if no one had ever drawn a line. And I want an honest answer to a meta-question: has this view been rigged so it can't lose, where every bit of continuity counts as evidence for it and every bit of rupture gets dismissed as the period label doing its work? If so, is that a problem for the view, or just how periodization always goes?
