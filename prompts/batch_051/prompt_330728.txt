You are an expert on the history and theory of nationalism as well as on modern European intellectual history. I am writing a paper on constructivist accounts of the nation, and I keep running into two canonical claims that seem to point in opposite directions. I would like your help working out whether they genuinely conflict, and what your answer implies for the question of how old nations are.

The first claim comes from Eric Hobsbawm’s introduction to The Invention of Tradition (edited by Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, 1983). Hobsbawm argues that many traditions which present themselves as ancient and continuous are in fact recent, deliberately assembled, and often invented to serve new forms of political authority and social cohesion; he treats the nation and its supposedly immemorial customs as central examples, and a stock illustration from the volume is the nineteenth-century fabrication of what later passed as age-old Highland dress. The second claim comes from Benedict Anderson’s Imagined Communities (1983). There the nation is treated as an imagined community, finite in extent and sovereign, yet Anderson also stresses that it is imagined as a bond of deep comradeship reaching horizontally across society, that its members experience it as moving through time like something ancient, and that this fraternal feeling is what has led so many people over the past two centuries to be ready to lay down their lives for their nation. On the first view the nation can look like a recent and somewhat contrived artifact; on the second it can look like a profoundly real, even age-old horizon of belonging.

Are these two positions in tension, or only apparently so? Pin down the exact site of any disagreement: is it about the actual age of nations, about what invention and imagination mean, about whether the relevant question concerns elite strategy or popular experience, or about whether a constructed identity can be authentic? Begin by brainstorming the main candidate reconciliations—different senses of a term, different levels of explanation, different questions being asked, a distinction the authors never draw explicitly, or a genuine difference of emphasis—and then give a verdict on which reconciliation is most faithful to both texts and which would amount to special pleading. To test your answer, consider a present-day separatist movement that revives a medieval kingdom and claims its rituals are continuous with the deep past: would Hobsbawm and Anderson analyze it differently, and would that difference matter?
