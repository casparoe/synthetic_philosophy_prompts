I work in process safety at a chemical plant, and a few times a month I have to decide whether a material gets classed as 'inherently hazardous' for storage and transport, rather than hazardous only under the particular way we happen to run it. Getting it wrong either leaves a real risk unplanned-for or costs us a fortune over-classifying something harmless. What keeps tripping me up is stuff with a nasty disposition that never actually manifests because some safeguard is in place: a pyrophoric powder kept under nitrogen, a monomer that would polymerize violently but is kept inhibited, a heating loop that would burst if overpressured except for the relief valve. Some of these feel like the hazard is still there and we're just masking it; others feel like the safeguard actually changes the system the moment the trigger occurs, so the disposition is gone before it can act. My manager says this is all just engineering judgment, but I think its a real distinction, and it matters for what happens if conditions change downstream. Could you write me a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or flow—for deciding when a material still has a hazardous disposition even if it can't manifest under current conditions, and when the safeguard means the disposition is genuinely absent? I'd like it grounded in the philosophical stuff on dispositions (finks, masks, antidotes, whatever distinction actually does the work), but written for engineers who'll never read it. It should be runnable in half an hour, and it should flag every step where we're making a judgment call rather than applying a concept. Then run it on two or three cases, including one where it says a material we currently treat as safe is actually hazardous, and one where we've been over-classifying. Also say what the procedure can't settle.
