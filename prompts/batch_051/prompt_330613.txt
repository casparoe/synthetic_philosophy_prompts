I have been turning over a structural resemblance between forecasting and demography, and I cannot tell how deep it goes. Forecasters who take the outside view set aside the internal details of a case and ask how often comparable cases have turned out a given way in the past; the trouble of choosing which past cases count as comparable is the reference class problem. Applied to states and empires, the same move appears: set aside this power's peculiarities and consult the base rate for powers of its general kind. That procedure now looks formally like actuarial survival analysis: define a population of units, record entry and exit events, censor the units still alive at the end of the observation window, and estimate how the hazard of termination changes with age. If the mapping is genuine, the actuarial toolbox transfers in non-obvious ways — the reference class problem becomes the choice of the underlying population; still-existing powers are a right-censoring problem; powers that did not survive long enough to enter the historical record are a left-truncation problem; and the shape of the hazard function, whether collapse grows more or less likely with age, becomes the central empirical question. If the mapping is only a loose analogy, importing those tools is a category mistake. Is the outside view of empires best understood as survival analysis with an under-specified population, or does the analogy fail at some particular joint? What would have to be true on both sides for the actuarial results to transfer, and what is one claim the analogy seems to license that turns out false?
