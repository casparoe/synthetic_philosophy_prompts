We often treat the refusal to begin honoring someone as morally unremarkable, while treating the removal of an honor already given as a serious act that must clear a high bar—even when the person and the evidence are the same. I want to know whether any principle justifies that asymmetry, or whether it is status quo bias projected onto public memory.

Consider a controlled case. One city declines to name a new school after a long-dead local figure whose record is genuinely mixed: he founded the city's public hospital and library, and he also spent a decade administering a forced-labor scheme in a colony—a fact that has been in the public record from the beginning. The refusal attracts little opposition; no one prominent calls it erasing history, judging the past by present standards, or ingratitude.

A second city has had a school named after a figure with an effectively identical record for three generations, and it votes to remove the name. The curriculum will continue to teach the figure's role and the period, and the archives remain untouched. Yet many of the people who found the first refusal unremarkable now condemn the renaming as erasing history and imposing today's standards on the past.

Assume the two cases are identical except that in the first the name was never bestowed, and in the second it has stood for generations. Is there a defensible principle under which declining to start an honor is different in kind from bringing an established honor to an end?

If you defend the asymmetry, state your principle and test it against the following objection: a name on a school is not only a record of a past decision but a continuing act of public endorsement, so keeping a name we would refuse to bestow today looks like performing the very honor we would decline, renewed daily. If you reject the asymmetry, say what follows: should renaming be treated as the exact mirror of refusing to name, and what should be said to residents for whom the old name has become, through long use, an address and a site of non-honorific memory rather than an endorsement?
