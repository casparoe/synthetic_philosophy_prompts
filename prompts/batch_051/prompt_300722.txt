I'm a student in a philosophy course on death, immortality, and the shape of a good life. We have been discussing Bernard Williams's treatment of the Makropulos case, and it has made me realize that I don't yet have a clear working definition of boredom—which matters, because the word is doing real argumentative work in the debate over whether an endless life would necessarily be tedious. I'd like you to help me construct and test one.

Propose a definition of boredom precise enough to yield a verdict in each of the following cases. Then apply it case by case. Where the definition gives a verdict that strikes you as wrong, or gives no verdict at all, say so, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising result.

The cases:

(1) A ten-year-old on a six-hour car ride, with no screens, no toys, and nothing to look at but an empty highway. I take this to be a paradigm case of boredom.

(2) A factory worker repeating a single motor task for hours, mentally checked out and watching the clock. Also a clear case in my view.

(3) A musician practicing an absorbing passage for three hours, losing track of time completely. Clearly not boredom, I think—even though the activity is repetitive.

(4) A blessed soul in heaven, held in the beatific vision (a direct, unmediated enjoyment of God): no felt lack, no need for novelty, no change. I take this to be a clear case of not-boredom, and I want a definition that does not count it as boredom merely because nothing changes.

(5) Elina Makropulos after more than three centuries of life: she can still do the things that once absorbed her, but everything now seems hollow and repetitious, and when she has the chance to prolong her life further she chooses not to. Is this really boredom, or is it a distinct state that English is tempted to confuse with it—tedium, weariness, meaninglessness? Williams uses her case to argue that an endless life would inevitably become tedious, so I need to know whether my definition counts it and why.

(6) A person whose memory is erased each night and who relives the same pleasant, fully absorbing day over and over, never realizing that it is a repetition. Subjectively they are never bored; externally, the pattern is as repetitious as the assembly line. Should the definition treat them as bored?

(7) A person with severe depression, who feels flattened and empty, experiences time as dragging, and has no desire to do anything. Is that boredom, or does the absence of any 'wanting something to do' rule it out?

I'm especially interested in the contrast between (4) and (5): both are indefinitely long stretches in which novelty is absent and nothing is sought, yet one is supposed to be supreme fulfillment and the other is supposed to be unbearable. A good definition should not simply equate boredom with monotony, low stimulation, or lack of novelty—or if it does, it should explain why the beatific vision comes out differently.

End your response with a single line beginning 'Summary:' that states your final definition in one sentence.
