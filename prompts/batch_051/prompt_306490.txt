I have adopted a rule about how I handle arguments for humanitarian intervention, and I would like to know whether it is rationally defensible or simply a bias dressed up as a principle. When people argue that outside powers should intervene to stop a government from killing civilians—for example, in debates about Kosovo, Iraq, or Libya—they often lean on a vivid story about one named victim, one razed village, or one family's escape. I have started treating that kind of material as a consideration against the argument until I am shown something broader: credible estimates of how many people are dying, how the numbers have changed over time, how the crisis compares with other crises, and whether the proposed intervention would plausibly make things better rather than worse. My reasoning is that particular stories are chosen for their salience, and salience does not reliably track importance. A single documented atrocity can be true and still be unrepresentative, and I suspect advocates on all sides reach for the story precisely because it makes intervention feel clean and obviously right while the aggregate case may be far messier.

I accept that the rule has a cost. It can make me indifferent to exactly the kind of testimony that expresses why intervention matters, and it may let me use a healthy suspicion of propaganda as an excuse not to confront real suffering. So I have two questions. First, is the rule as I have stated it defensible, or does it discard relevant moral evidence? Second, if it is too crude, is there a revised version that keeps the protection against manipulation while still allowing a survivor's account to move me when it should?
