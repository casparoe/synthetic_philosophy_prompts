I'm drafting the opening chapter of a project on the structure of moral reasons, and I want to begin with a clean specification of what a theory of supererogation must accomplish before I commit to any particular account of acts that are morally good or praiseworthy but not required. Please write out the desiderata: the requirements an adequate theory has to satisfy, the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the phenomena it must either explain or explain away. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial commitments of particular camps (for example, that supererogatory acts must involve significant self-sacrifice, or must be rare, or must have a distinctive gift-like intentional structure). I expect the list to cover the standard puzzles — how a genuinely good act can fail to be required without making duty arbitrary, how to keep supererogation distinct from the merely permissible, the ideally virtuous, and ordinary decency, and how to accommodate both heroic rescue and small everyday goings-beyond — but I also want the less obvious requirements surfaced.

Two structural problems should be treated as potential joint-satisfiability hazards rather than settled constraints. First, the baseline problem: if what counts as beyond duty is fixed relative to an agent's social or professional baseline, the account will perversely classify a worker in a corrupt industry who refuses to poison a river as doing something supererogatory, when the real issue is that the baseline itself is wrong; an adequate theory must avoid ratifying degraded baselines while still tracking what is actually required of this agent here and now. Second, the ratchet problem: acts that start out supererogatory often migrate into expectation or requirement as enough people perform them, and a theory should say whether an act's deontic status can change in that way even though its intrinsic features do not. For each structural problem, I want a requirement motivated by the case, not merely a restatement of the problem. Finally, tell me which single requirement would most efficiently expose a candidate theory as inadequate — the one that popular accounts tend to fail first — and briefly justify that ranking. A table mapping requirements to their motivating cases is welcome, but the argumentative weight should sit in the justification of the list itself.
