I've been going back and forth with a physicist friend about the Wigner puzzle — whether it's actually surprising that mathematics, which can look like it was invented for its own sake, keeps turning out to be exactly what physics needs. He keeps tossing out different explanations, and I've realized I don't know how to weigh them against each other: some seem to genuinely dissolve the mystery and others just rename it. I'd like you to do the ranking for me.

Here are the candidates as I understand them (if I'm botching one, just rank the charitible version):

- It was built in. Mathematical concepts start as idealizations of physical operations — counting objects, measuring land, noticing symmetry from turning things around — and later math mostly got forced into shape by physics (calculus for mechanics, functional analysis for quantum mechanics). So the fit is no accident.
- Scoreboard effect. The enormous amount of math that never applied anywhere is invisible; we only retell the success stories. Maybe effectiveness is partly a selection effect in what we remember.
- The bar is lower than it sounds. Being 'effective' in physics often just means giving local, approximate, corrigible models. If that's the bar, math's success is less miraculous than advertised.
- Structure all the way down. Physical reality just is mathematical structure, so a science of structure is bound to fit; the surprising thing would be if it didn't.
- The question is circular. What counts as a physical explanation or even a measurable physical magnitude is partly defined by mathematical tractability, so math fitting physics is partly true by definition.
- Same world, same minds. Our brains evolved in the same physical world that physics describes, so the categories and reasoning we find natural should track physical regularities.
- It's just compression. Mathematics is a family of tools for compressing and predicting patterns. 'Effectiveness' just means the compression is good, and we selected for good compression; it doesn't point to anything metaphysical.

Please sort these into three tiers — 'carries the real explanatory weight,' 'does some real work,' and 'mostly explains the question away or misses the point' — and give me your answer as a table with these columns: Candidate, Tier, One-sentence reason, Confidence (0–10). Ties are allowed, and every candidate goes exactly once.
