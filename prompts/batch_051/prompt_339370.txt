At a dinner party my cousin, who translates poetry, was defending a version he'd done of a French poem that came out sounding kind of off in English. His argument was that translating a poem is like covering a song. A cover can change the genre, the tempo, the key, the arrangement, even some of the harmony, but if the melody's gone you're not covering the song anymore, you're just reciting the lyrics over new chords. For a poem, he says, the form is the melody: meter, rhyme, line length, stanza shape, all of it. Keep those and you've translated the poem; drop them to get the word meanings right and you've made a crib sheet, not a translation. He went so far as to say he'd rather have a version that twists the literal sense hard, the way a jazz cover bends a tune, than one that keeps every word and looses the rhyme. He used all this to conclude that a formal translation is generally a better translation than a free one, and that if, say, a villanelle's refrains don't repeat in English, it's not really the same poem. I'm suspicious, and I want to know how far the song-cover analogy actually goes before I argue with him.

Could you audit it? Lay out the mapping — what corresponds to what in the two domains (a table is fine). Then say where the analogy breaks down, which of those disanalogies matter for his conclusion and which are just surface, and what features of song covers the analogy is quietly importing into translation that don't belong. Then give an overall verdict on whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy, say what it is. End with a one-sentence summary as the last line.
