I'm a political theory PhD student and I've been moonlighting for a development finance institution that screens charter city and seasteading proposals. We see maybe a dozen a year and the current process is basically vibes plus "does the developer have money." One of the partners wants a proper decision procedure for the legitimacy question: when can a private jurisdiction actually claim authority over the people who end up living and working there, instead of just coercing them de facto? They want it grounded in the philosophical literature on consent and exit rights—the idea that ongoing actual consent, proxied by a real right of exit, is what could legitimate private government. Write me a reusable checklist/decision procedure my non-philosopher colleagues could run on a proposal in under two hours: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to punt to a person because its a values call rather than a factual one. It should handle the hard cases: exit that exists on paper but is ruinously expensive, people who never meaningfully consented (children, workers who followed the only job), a seastead where exit means a dangerous open-ocean crossing or being handed back to a state that wants to imprison you, and whether procedural voice can substitute for exit. Then run it on three proposals: a charter city with a guaranteed exit corridor and departure compensation; a seastead that is the sole employer on-site and recovers recruitment costs through an exit fee; and a well-run private city that flunks the exit test but whose residents report high satisfaction. Make sure one of them produces an answer the partners won't like, and flag where the procedure will systematically misfire no matter how well we write it.
