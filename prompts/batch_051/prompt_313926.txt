You are a professional philosopher with expertise in formal epistemology and the theory of self-locating belief. Two researchers have retained you as the neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration: one defends the Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) and the other defends the Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) as the correct principle for reasoning about anthropic situations, where which observer you are is part of the evidence. Both researchers treat the other as a serious interlocutor and have committed in advance to producing a document each could sign in good faith.

Work from the following provisional characterizations; part of the collaboration's job is to refine them into formulations each side accepts.

- SSA: all else equal, one should reason as if one's observation were randomly selected from the set of all actual observers in one's chosen reference class under the hypothesis in question. In Doomsday-style applications, this tends to favor hypotheses on which the reference class is smaller.
- SIA: the fact that one exists at all is itself evidence; all else equal, one should favor hypotheses that predict more observers, with the strength of the update sensitive to the number of observers predicted.

The disputed territory includes at least the Doomsday argument (the inference from one's birth rank to a higher probability of a shorter future for the human population), the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment (two empirically equivalent theories differ only in how many observers they predict), and the reference class problem (how to select the reference class under SSA, and whether the need for such a choice shows that SSA is incomplete rather than merely unfinished).

Draft the final document of this collaboration. Before writing it, think carefully and brainstorm: list candidate points of agreement and candidate cruxes, then discard anything one side could fairly describe as slanted, vacuous, or question-begging. Prefer a small number of sharp, non-vacuous claims over an exhaustive list.

The document should have two main parts.

Part One: Agreed claims, numbered. Cover at least the following: the shared formal machinery (e.g., Bayesian updating and centered or self-locating evidence); the non-anthropic empirical facts in each disputed case that both sides accept; precise statements of SSA and SIA, each worded so its proponent would sign it and the opponent would accept it as an accurate statement of the other view; and whatever the two sides agree would count as settling the dispute if it could be settled.

Part Two: A disagreements table. For each live disagreement, include (a) the exact claim in dispute, phrased so each side accepts the wording of both its own position and the other's; (b) the crux, understood as the sub-question that would have to be answered to resolve that disagreement; (c) a concrete case, argument, or formal result both sides agree bears on the crux; and (d) each side's prediction about what that case, once fully specified, would yield.

Ensure the table addresses at least each of the following: whether the fact of one's own existence favors hypotheses that predict many observers; whether the Presumptuous Philosopher's conclusion is a reductio of SIA or a bullet its defender should bite; whether the Doomsday shift is an insight or an embarrassment for SSA; whether the reference class problem can be solved in a principled way; whether SIA overcounts merely possible observers and, if so, whether some modified version of SIA avoids that; and which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal rather than substantive.

Close with a brief residual-disagreement note: identify the remaining substantive sub-questions, any disagreements that dissolved under precise formulation, and which crux each side names as the one most likely to move them.

Format the output as a short preface that both parties could sign, followed by the numbered agreed claims, followed by the disagreements table. Do not include a bibliography, citations, or suggestions for further reading; the document should stand on its own argumentative structure. Do not write a verdict—your task is to produce the exact residual disagreement, not to settle the debate.
