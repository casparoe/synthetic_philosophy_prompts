My church's council professes sola scriptura—the principle that Scripture alone is the final authority for doctrine and practice—and it has to keep deciding where an individual's reading of Scripture ends and public teaching that the council may regulate begins. Over the past decade the council has made these rulings:

- It did not discipline a member for using a thought-for-thought modern translation in a home Bible study, on the ground that the Bible's authority attaches to what the text means, not to the wording of any one translation.
- It removed a Sunday-school teacher for teaching that the resurrection was not an event but a symbol, even though he said this was simply his own reading of the Bible, on the ground that some points are so clear and so central that an individual's differing reading is no defense.
- It did not discipline a member who privately held and quietly discussed an old-earth creation view, since the church's statement of faith does not settle every secondary question.
- It barred a home-group leader from distributing his own unpublished commentary on Revelation until the council had reviewed it, on the ground that teaching materials distributed in the church's name fall under the council's oversight of teaching.
- It removed from membership a member who had written public blog posts denying the Trinity, treating a public blog as a form of public teaching rather than private discussion.

The council must now rule on a member who has written a public blog post arguing for amillennialism (the view that the millennium of Revelation 20 is symbolic rather than a future literal reign), while the church's statement of faith is premillennial (the view that Christ returns before a literal millennium). She says she is not a teacher, that the post is only her own attempt to read Revelation faithfully, and that her readership is small.

I am less interested in the verdict alone than in the rule the council seems to be running, the other rules that fit the earlier decisions just as well, and which of those rules yield a different verdict here. Please give four sections in this order: (1) Candidate rules, one or two sentences each, setting out the plausible principles that fit the five rulings; (2) Predictions, what each candidate rule implies for the blog case; (3) Best guess, your prediction of the council's ruling, the principle it would be committing itself to (and what that principle implies about who, in practice, decides what the text means), and the closest earlier decision it would rely on; (4) Deciding case, one further scenario that would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules, with what each would say about it. Keep the whole answer between 400 and 500 words.
