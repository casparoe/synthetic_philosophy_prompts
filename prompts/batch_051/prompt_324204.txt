So I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and a draft I'm working on keeps forcing me into a question I'd normally skip: when is it actually true that two beings are in the same mental state? I've definately read some stuff about multiple realizability over the years, but I don't have a definition I can use under deadline. In the book, one character's neurons get replaced one at a time by synthetic parts that preserve all the input–output patterns and internal causal roles, and afterward she's supposed to be in the same state of fear she was in before the surgery. Another character is an AI whose designers arranged its internal transitions to match a grieving human's, whatever that really comes to. My editor keeps asking whether these count as the same state or just a similar one, and "it depends on what you mean" is not going to save the scene.

I want a working definition of "the same mental state" in the functionalist/multiple realizability sense — the same kind of state, not the same token occurrence. It should get these cases right: (a) me and a friend both grieving over the same death, in slightly different ways; (b) a dog and a human both afraid of the same snake; (c) the neuron-by-neuron replaced person, comparing her fear before and after the replacement, with all the relevant patterns and causal roles held fixed; (d) an AI built to have the same causal role as a grieving human but with no biological stress response; (e) someone who can locate a pinprick, says it would be painful for other people, but doesn't actually dislike the sensation; (f) a chatbot that says it is grieving and acts sad in conversation but nothing in its design does the internal work grief is supposed to do. I have leanings — (a) clearly the same, (f) clearly not, and (b) through (e) genuinely hard — but leanings aren't a definition. Give me your best definition, run it through all six, say where it gives a counterintuitive answer or no answer at all, and tell me where I should revise the definition or just bite the bullet.
