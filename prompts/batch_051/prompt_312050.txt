We routinely treat the same pattern of explanation as a fallacy at one scale and as wisdom at another, and I want to know whether that split can be defended. When an individual explains a past setback by the outcome it later made possible — the rejection that had to happen so that a better opportunity could arrive, the failed venture that had to fail in order to teach a lesson — most of us treat the explanation with suspicion. The outcome is not doing any causal work, we think; the story runs backward from a consoling ending and cannot be tested. Yet when the same explanatory form is applied to history — a movement's early defeats are said to have been necessary for its later victory, an institution is said to have arisen in order to meet a need, the arc of history is said to bend toward justice — it is widely treated as serious and often as profound rather than as the same error enlarged. What, if anything, justifies holding the historical version of teleological explanation to a lower standard than the personal one? Is there a principle that makes this kind of reasoning a bias in a biography but a legitimate form of understanding in a history, or is the collective case just the personal case made respectable by scale and stakes? Give me the strongest candidate principle you can construct, test it against the hard case where the historical claim is meant literally as an explanation of why events unfolded as they did rather than as a hope or a moral commitment, and then say honestly whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
