I'm in a metaphysics course and we're just wrapping up modal realism. My tutor summed up Lewis's position like this: "For Lewis, to say that there could have been blue swans is just to say there's a concrete possible world, as real as ours, where swans are blue. These worlds aren't seperate places in one big space; none of them is spatiotemporally related to any other. What makes our world actual is just that it's the one we inhabit, so 'actual' is an indexical like 'here'. Likewise, 'you could have been a doctor' is true because there's a counterpart of you in another world who is a doctor. And since there's a world for each way things could be, every scenario you can coherently imagine is realized somewhere. So on Lewis's picture, all the ways your life might have gone are equally real and fully populated, which is why people often feel the view makes our choices not really matter. Lewis's reply to people who find this crazy is that it's the price you pay for a clean reductive analysis of possibility, and that rival accounts have costs just as bad."

I'm not sure how fair this is. I get the bit about actuality being indexical, and I think I get why the worlds aren't just distant planets in some bigger space. But the step from "a world for each way things could be" to "every scenario you can coherently imagine is realized somewhere" seems too quick to me, and I don't see why any of it would make our choices pointless, since there's still a fact about which world is the one we're in. Could you go through the summary and tell me what's right, what's subtly off, whether a defender of Lewis would accept it as stated, and which of the conclusions at the end actually follow from the accurate parts?
