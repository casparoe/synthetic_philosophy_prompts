I'm outlining a novel about a woman in the 1520s who starts translating the Bible for herself, and I've been trying to get the theology right rather than slapping modern individualism onto it. A history podcast I listened to summarized sola scriptura like this: the reformers held that the Bible is clear about everything necessary for salvation, that scripture is its own best interpreter, and therefore no institutional church is needed to settle what the text means; any sincere, ordinary reader will be led by the text toward the true faith, since the unclear passages can be read in light of the clear ones. The host then said this just pushes the problem back a step—someone still has to decide which passages are the clear ones and which readings are the 'plain' ones—so the real authority lands on the individual's private judgement, which is why Protestantism shattered into a thousand sects. This sounds mostly right to me, and I'm espically unsure about the move from 'the text is clear' to 'any reader ends up in the same place,' and about whether 'each believer decides for herself' is being quietly conflated with 'nobody else can decide for her.' Which parts of the summary would a careful defender of sola scriptura accept, which parts would they call a distortion, and does the conclusion about endless splintering actually follow from anything accurate in the summary?
