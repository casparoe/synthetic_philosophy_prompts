I keep noticing a weird inconsistency in how people around me (and, honestly, me) think about authority, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle that saves it or whether one of my instincts has to go. In ordinary life, we treat performance as a source of legitimate authority without thinking twice: the surgeon runs the operating room because she is the one who saves lives and keeps the ward functional; nobody elects the CEO, but if the company thrives under her, her authority over the org chart feels fine; a football manager picks the lineup because the team wins. In these settings, competence and results just are the justification. But when somebody applies the same formula to a state, a lot of those same people change the rules. Say that Singapore produced decades of growth, housing, low crime and clean administration under a party that hasn't faced a serious threat of losing power, or that China lifted hundreds of millions of people out of poverty under an unelected party, and they'll insist performance can't make unelected rule legitimate: you need elections, contestation, consent. So we have one criterion for the hospital, the firm, and the dugout, and another for the state.

I'm trying to figure out whether anything actually justifies that split. The obvious candidates look shaky to me. Consent? A low-level employee never meaningfully consented to the CEO, and a patient under anesthesia isn't taking votes on intraoperative decisions. Exit? You can quit a job, but most people can't meaningfully leave their country — though Singaporeans with a passport and savings can, and the unease about performance legitimacy doesnt obviously disappear once exit becomes easier. Monopoly on force? The state can lock you up, not just fire you, but that sounds like a reason to hold it to a higher standard, not to switch the criterion of legitimacy. Maybe political equality just matters in a way that equality in a workplace doesn't, but then I want to know why. I want the strongest version of the distinction you can articulate, tested against the nearby cases that threaten it — a company town you can't leave, a professional body that licenses you, a long-ruling party that holds elections but tilts the field. And if no distinction holds up, tell me which move I should make: accept that a competent, orderly authoritarian state is legitimate after all, or stop treating the surgeon's skill as a source of authority. One of them has to go.
