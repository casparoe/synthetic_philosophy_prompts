I work in long-horizon population and civilisational-risk forecasting, and I have recently had to set priors over how many human beings will ever exist. This has made the presumptuous philosopher directly relevant to my work, and I would be grateful for a careful analysis of a variation I have not seen set out elsewhere.

Take the canonical case first. Hypothesis A says that the total number of humans who will ever exist is 100 billion. Hypothesis B says that the total is 100 trillion. Both fit all ordinary demographic and astrophysical evidence equally well, and I take the prior probabilities of the two hypotheses to be equal. Under the self-sampling assumption, my bare existence is uninformative between A and B; under the self-indication assumption, it is claimed to favour B strongly, roughly in proportion to the ratio of the two totals. Let us accept that as the standard baseline.

Now vary the case. Let H_low state that the total number of humans who will ever exist is drawn from a uniform distribution over the integers from 100 billion to 400 billion inclusive. Let H_high state that the total number is drawn from a uniform distribution over the integers from 100 billion to 400 trillion inclusive. As before, I know only that I exist, and I do not condition on my birth rank.

1. State the likelihoods and posterior odds between H_low and H_high under SSA and under SIA, taking the prior odds to be even.

2. Now reduce H_high’s upper bound in stages: first to 1 trillion, then to 500 billion, then to 410 billion, and finally to 400 billion. Report how the posterior odds change at each stage, and indicate where the SSA and SIA answers become practically indistinguishable.

3. Does this variation show that the presumptuous philosopher is largely an artefact of comparing two point values separated by three orders of magnitude? Or does the SIA update remain principled and significant even at modest ratios, such that one should simply accept it as ordinary Bayesian reasoning rather than treat it as a reductio?
