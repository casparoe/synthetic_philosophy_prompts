I'm taking a philosophy of economics course and got into it with a friend about Malthus. His line is the standard one: Malthusian predictions are unfalsifiable because whenever the catastrophe fails to arrive on schedule, the story just gets adjusted — the check hasn't kicked in yet, technology bought us a temporary reprieve, or the model was right about the mechanism and only wrong about the timing. So on his view, being right but early is a rhetorical escape hatch rather than a scientific claim. But it seems to me that the newer planetary-boundaries work changes what a defender of Malthus can actually say. Instead of pointing to an indefinite future check, you get specific budgets: how much nitrogen and phosphorus can be loaded before aquatic systems flip, how much freshwater can be drawn down, topsoil loss rates, ocean acidification thresholds, and near-term markers for when those budgets are being overshot. A neo-Malthusian can now say there are testable intermediate steps between now and the disaster, not just a postponed end-state. So here's what I actually want help with: does the old unfalsifiability objection survive this development? Which premise is doing the work — the vagueness of the timetable, the lack of a mechanism, the absence of intermediate predictions — and is there a repaired version of the objection that still bites the newer forms? Or do the newer forms turn being right but early from an excuse into a checkable empirical claim that can fail in principle?
