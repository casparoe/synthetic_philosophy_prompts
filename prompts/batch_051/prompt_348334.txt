A friend and I got into it after I admitted I teared up at a movie, and he backed me into an argument I can't get out of. His conclusion sounds way too strong, but each step looks okay, so I need help finding where it breaks.

(1) For an emotion to be genuine, it has to be directed at something you take to exist.
(2) When I cry at a film, I don't believe the character exists.
(3) But the sadness is genuine — my chest hurts, my eyes are wet, I'm not preforming it.
(4) So (1) is false: a genuine emotion doesn't require belief that its object exists.
(5) And if that's false, then whether the object exists can't matter to whether the emotion is genuine — which, he says, means it also can't matter to whether the emotion is fitting or appropriate.
(6) So being devastated by a fictional death and being devastated by a real death are the same kind of response, with the same claim to being reasonable; they just have different causes, like a burn from a stove and a burn from the sun.
(7) But that proves too much. If a neighbor mourned a sitcom character's death as long and as hard as someone mourns a parent's death, I'd say something had gone wrong with him, not merely that his sadness had a different cause.

Where does this go off the rails, if it does? Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I'd most like to keep (3) — crying at a movie doesn't feel fake to me. Also, when you answer, please separate what's actually settled about this from what's still genuinely contested among people who study it, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
