I'm a journalist who covers food, population and environment stories, and a friend of mine — an economist who now works in policy — keeps cornering me with an argument I can't shake. I can't tell if its devestating or just slick.

Here's the argument as he lays it out:

(1) Take any Malthusian-style prediction: population or consumption is on track to outrun food, soil, water, or energy, so a crisis is coming — maybe not this decade, but the trajectory is unsustainable.
(2) When the predicted crisis doesn't arrive on schedule, the defender never counts it as a failure. Instead they say the underlying mechanism was real but was masked or postponed by some factor the model didn't include: a new crop variety, a recession, a demographic transition, a cheap substitute nobody saw coming.
(3) There is always some such factor available after the fact, and each one is individually plausible — which is exactly why it never looks like cheating.
(4) But if a prediction can be saved, plausibly, no matter what happens, then the original claim didn't actually stick its neck out.
(5) A claim that doesn't stick its neck out is unfalsifiable, and an unfalsifiable claim isn't doing scientific work.
(6) Therefore Malthusian long-run predictions are unfalsifiable, and dressing them up as science is a category mistake.

He then says the same applies to every long-run prediction in the 'we're running out of X' genre, and maybe to long-run predictions generally.

I want to resist this. I've covered cases where a specific forecast failed on its timetable but the mechanism really did seem to be operating — a resource squeeze was real, and a technology bought a few decades rather than solving it. That seems like 'right but early,' not pseudoscience. But I can't point to the exact step where his argument goes wrong, and I worry I'm just special-pleading for predictions I happen to find alarming.

Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why? If it is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what does that mean for how a science journalist should treat any long-run prediction?
