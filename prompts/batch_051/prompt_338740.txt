I am a philosopher of mind serving as an outside advisor to a small research group that has two years of funding to spend on a single cluster of questions about whether reinforcement learning (RL) systems could have valenced states—states that feel good or bad to the system itself, rather than merely being assigned positive or negative numbers by an objective function. The group's end goal is to produce an internal policy judgment about which training regimes, if any, should be treated as morally risky because they might induce suffering in the systems being trained. Its members include both philosophers and machine-learning engineers, so the central question must be one on which both can work productively. I want you to help me set the agenda: which question should be placed at the center, and which should be postponed or dropped?

Here are the candidate lines of investigation. You may nominate one I have omitted.

1. The constitution question. What, if anything, turns a scalar reward or penalty signal into felt valence? Is the relevant item the objective reward, the reward-prediction error, a learned value function, or the functional role the signal occupies in the system's control hierarchy? And how much does the answer depend on first adopting a particular theory of consciousness—functionalist, global-workspace, integrated-information, or another?

2. The prerequisites question. Does felt valence require a temporally unified self, autobiographical memory, or a capacity for endogenous attention, such that a stateless or episodic RL agent could not suffer no matter how negative its reward signal becomes? Or can suffering be a transient and relatively disunified process?

3. The signature question. Biological work draws a distinction between wanting and liking—roughly, between the incentive salience that drives pursuit and the hedonic impact that constitutes felt pleasure or pain. Is there any analogous distinction in RL between an action-driving error signal and a welfare-relevant valence signal, and could an architecture implement one without the other? If the two come apart, which magnitude is welfare-relevant: the size of a negative reward, its frequency, its duration, or its contrast with the agent's baseline?

4. The evidence question. An RL agent can be trained entirely through weight updates and produces no report, grimace, or distress behavior. What could count as evidence that its negative reward is like pain rather than like a bare control signal? Which observable or architectural facts—training history, environment structure, meta-learning, behavioral trade-offs—would most shift our credence?

5. The policy-threshold question. Setting certainty aside, how much credence in valenced RL, and at what expected intensity and prevalence, should be enough to change training practice? Should the default be precautionary or permissive, and what determines where the burden of proof should lie?

For each candidate, give the dependency relations: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable within the group's two-year horizon and mixed expertise, and which you would deprioritize because their answer would not much change the downstream policy conclusion or because they cannot yet be investigated with available methods. Then state a defended ordering and explain the trade-offs among importance, tractability, neglectedness, and upstream influence. Tell me what single conceptual or empirical finding would most change your ranking. End your response with a final line of exactly this form, with nothing after it: \answer{<short label of the question you would center>}.
