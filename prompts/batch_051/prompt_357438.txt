Suppose a common-law country of moderate size adopted, effective next year, a single new doctrine of imputed knowledge. For any civil or regulatory standard that turns on what a person knew, courts must treat the person as knowing every deductive consequence of the facts already in their possession, together with ordinary background facts of the kind widely known in their trade or community. It does not matter whether anyone actually drew the inference, or whether it would have been easy or hard to draw; possession of the premises is treated as possession of the conclusions. This is, in effect, a legal fiction of the kind of logical omniscience that epistemologists usually reserve for ideal reasoners; human cognition itself is unchanged. The doctrine applies to fraud and misrepresentation, negligence, insider trading, disclosure obligations, and insurance warranties, and binds plaintiffs, defendants, and regulators. Predict, with reasoning, how this jurisdiction's legal and commercial life looks fifty years on. Address at least: how contracting and disclosure practices adapt; which industries and professions grow or shrink; whether people respond by knowing less, by refusing to receive information, or by delegating inference to software that actually computes consequences; how courts can operationalize a fiction they cannot literally enforce; and whether commercial life becomes, on net, more or less honest. For each link in your prediction, mark it as robust or conjectural, and identify which further facts about litigation costs, automated reasoning, or the structure of the economy would most change your conclusion.
