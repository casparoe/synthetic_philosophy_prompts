I am trying to get clear about what David Hume actually argued about money and trade, because the question has recently become personal rather than academic. I serve on the economic development committee of a small city in the industrial Midwest, and after months of fights about tariffs, offshoring, and whether our region's factory losses are anybody's fault, a friend who studied economics sent me what he called the settled Humean position. Before I repeat any of it at a public meeting, I want to know how much of it I can trust.

His summary runs like this:

Hume proved that a country's balance of trade is self-correcting. When gold flows out, domestic prices fall, exports become cheaper and imports dearer, and the gold flows back; when gold flows in, the mechanism runs the other way. It follows, on Hume's view, that trade imbalances are essentially harmless — a deficit is just the monetary system doing its adjusting. Hume also held that money is a veil: adding more of it only raises prices, so hoarding gold cannot enrich a nation and governments that worry about the trade balance are making a mercantilist mistake. Jobs lost to foreign competition are part of the same benign process of adjustment.

I have the nagging sense that this is too tidy, and that it quietly converts an eighteenth-century argument about bullion into a modern claim that trade deficits and deindustrialization don't matter. Go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which claims Hume did and did not hold, where the summary overreaches or collapses distinctions he kept apart, and what I would need to correct before repeating it. Lead with your overall verdict on how fair the summary is in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question.
