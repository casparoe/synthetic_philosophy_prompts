I'm a novelist, and I've run into an asymmetry in how my beta readers handle two things in my current draft, and I want to know whether it's actually justified or just reader psychology I should resist or lean into.

In the book there's a poison ring that never actually poisons anybody: each time it would prick someone, the victim happens to be wearing the antidote charm, or the ring jams, or someone knocks the hand away. Nobody complains; they all keep calling it "the lethal ring." But there's also Margit, a character the narration keeps saying is generous, and in every scene where she could do something generous, something blocks it — someone else gives the money first, the person refuses her help, the phone rings. The same readers say Margit isn't really generous, she's just asserted to be generous.

So the asymmetry: for an object, a disposition survives any number of blocked manifestations. The ring is still lethal, and a match thats only ever been struck while wet is still called flammable. For a person, a trait that never shows up in behavior gets withdrawn, no matter how much external blocking you show. I know there's a distinction between a mask (something outside stops the display) and a fink (the trigger itself removes the disposition), and I can't tell whether it solves this or just gives the double standard a fancier name. I'd like the strongest justification you can build, tested against nearby cases — a dog, a car that never starts, someone whose generosity is blocked by something inside her rather than outside, a finkish version of the ring if that matters. Honest verdict: should I keep the asymetry in the book, reverse it, or drop it?
