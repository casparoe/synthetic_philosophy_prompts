We are often ready to say, in the same breath, that an employee who is made to work sixty-hour weeks by an employer has been wronged, and that a founder, a doctoral student, or an artist who chooses sixty-hour weeks is showing drive or devotion. The first judgment is embedded in practice: legal limits on working time, from France's 35-hour week to long-hours protections elsewhere, and the Japanese term karoshi for death from overwork, treat imposed overwork as a distinctive and nameable harm. The second judgment is no less real: we admire the person who owns their time and voluntarily spends it on work. Yet the hours look physically and socially identical when we abstract from the choice: the same exhaustion, the same neglected health and family. That seems to mean the entire moral difference rests on whose will fixed the schedule.

Can that difference carry the weight we put on it? I want the strongest principle you can find that justifies judging the same amount of overwork differently depending on whether it is chosen or imposed. Then pressure-test it against the hard cases: the employee signed a contract and could in some formal sense resign; the founder's choices were themselves shaped by markets, investors, and an inherited ethic that treats unceasing labour as virtue; and the body's injuries do not ask who chose them. Give me a committed verdict, not a survey of options. If the asymmetry can be defended, defend it and say exactly what makes the schedule genuinely chosen rather than merely uncoerced in the narrow legal sense. If it cannot, say which side we should give up — the admiration of the voluntary overworker, or the condemnation of the required one — and state what that implies for the ideal of a shorter working week.
