A friend who does open-source licensing work floated a scheme for the copyleft derivative-works problem, and I want to understand how it would actually behave before I bother arguing about it. The proposal: every copyleft release ships with a taint ledger assigning every public function and data type a number from 1 to 10; anything left unpriced defaults to 10. At release time, at least a fifth of a project's public entries have to be priced at 1, and no more than a quarter can be priced at 10. After release, prices are locked but can only be lowered. A downstream program counts as a derivative work automatically if the sum of the prices of the entries it calls exceed 50, or if it embeds any whole source file from the original. There's a small foundation that hears challenges to particular prices: if you successfully challenge one, you get a two-year safe harbor against infringement claims for code built against that entry. The selling point is that this replaces all the where-does-one-program-end-and-another-begin litigation with a ledger and an arithmetic threshold.

Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it incentivize maintainers to do with pricing and refactoring, what would downstream developers do to route around expensive entries and stay under 50, and where does it get gamed first? What does the ledger do to how libraries get designed — do projects start hiding the useful stuff behind a few cheap choke points, and what happens to those choke points? Also say whether the scheme ends up producing more or less freely licensed code than the current mess, and whether the failure modes are fixable without turning it back into court cases.
