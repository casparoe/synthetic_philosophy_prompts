I write software in which "random" seems to do several different jobs at once, and I'm trying to figure out what notion of randomness each job actually presupposes. In one codebase we seed a Mersenne Twister from a few bytes of `/dev/urandom` and then treat the resulting bitstream as random for a Monte Carlo simulation, even though the stream is a deterministic function of the seed and anyone who knows the seed can reproduce every bit. In another system we use a cryptographically secure PRNG whose contract is that no feasible adversary, given any finite prefix, can predict the next bit with non-negligible advantage. And when we validate either of these, we feed finite files to statistical test batteries — Diehard, TestU01, the NIST suite — and the tool prints a p-value and effectively calls a particular finite sequence "random" or "not random."

These practices seem to pull in different directions. The statistical battery treats a finite sequence as something that can be random full stop; the cryptographic contract doesn't care about the sequence in itself, only about unpredictability relative to bounded adversaries; the Monte Carlo code treats deterministic output as random because it's patternless enough for the sampler. Meanwhile, the algorithmic-information-theory accounts I've run into — Kolmogorov complexity for finite strings, Martin-Löf-style tests for infinite sequences — suggest that randomness in the strict sense is a property of infinite sequences or of sequences relative to a reference measure, while a finite string can at best be more or less incompressible. What theory of randomness does each of my three practices actually commit me to? Is there a single coherent notion underneath all of them, or are they just borrowing one word for several distinct properties — incompressibility, unpredictability, typicality under a distribution — and would anything about how we build and certify these systems have to change if we committed to one notion? I'm not after a bibliography or a survey, and I don't need a final metaphysical verdict. Give me your best reconstruction, flag how confident you are in each part, and tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined. If the honest answer is that my question is badly formed — that there is no single notion of randomness these practices collectively presuppose and the task as posed can't be done well — say so plainly instead of forcing a unified theory.
