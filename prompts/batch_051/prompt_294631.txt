I keep noticing a resemblance and I want a second opinion on whether it's real or whether I'm just pattern-matching. The pessimistic meta-induction says: past theories (caloric, phlogiston, the ether) were empirically successful in their day and still turned out badly wrong about the unobservable stuff; so by the same induction, our current best theories are probably wrong about their unobservables too. The selective realist response is that this throws away the baby: the parts of past theories that were actually doing the predictive work—the mathematical structure, the stable causal mechanisms, the equations that carried the successes—tended to be retained in successor theories, while what got dropped was the idle or speculative overhang. So you can be a realist about the parts that survive theory change and a pessimist about the rest.

Here's the thing: that reads to me like the difference between training error and test error, with selective realism playing the role of regularization. A big enough model can fit its training set perfectly and still be garbage on held-out data; regularization and cross-validation force you to keep only the features that generalize and zero out the ones that fit noise. Past theories are models fit on the data of their time; theory change is the held-out test set arriving late. The pessimistic meta-induction is just the observation that flexible models overfit. Selective realism is an L1 penalty: shrink to zero the posits that don't pull predictive weight across the historical record.

Where I get stuck is that in ML the train/test split is set up in advance and you can measure generalization on the test set, wheras in science the "test set" is just whatever nature does next, and "the parts that survived" gets read off after the fact. So the analogy might be smuggling in the exact thing the realist needs: a way of telling, at the time, which parts of a theory are doing the work and which parts are noise. In ML you can tell because you have the test labels; in science you don't get those until you've already committed.

I want a committed answer, not a survey. Is this a genuinely deep structural parallel, a loose analogy, or a confusion—and what would have to be true of theory change for the transfer from statistical learning theory to the realism debate to actually go through? Give me the mapping, tell me where it breaks, and name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict about scientific theories that turns out false. American English, please.
