My cousin works on a palliative care ward, and her hospital is trying out a new pain policy that I can't stop thinking about. They're going to stop using the patient's self-reported 0–10 pain score to decide how much opioid (or anything else) someone gets, and instead dose off a "suffering score" that nurses record from observed pain behaviour: grimacing, guarding, breath-holding, calling out, how often the patient is actually trying to escape the pain. The patient's own number is still written in the chart but it doesn't drive dosing anymore. The rationale they gave in training was roughly that pain asymbolia shows pain and the badness of pain can pull apart — those patients will say they're in pain and point to where it is, but it doesn't bother them and they don't try to stop it — so the thing the ward actually wants to treat is the suffering, not the raw pain signal itself.

I want to know how this actually plays out, not whether it sounds humane on paper. What does it incentivize patients and nurses to do? Who ends up getting more or less relief than they would under the old system, and where does it get gamed first? I'm especially interested in stoic or high-masking patients, depressed patients, chronic pain patients who've learned not to visibly react, people who can't produce normal pain behaviour (intubated, paralyzed, dementia, very young kids), and nurses who simply aren't in the room enough to observe much. And can any of those failure modes be fixed without basically reverting to self-report?

Give me exactly five ways this scheme goes wrong or gets gamed, ranked from "happens first" to "most serious long-run damage," each a sentence or two saying what happens and whether it can be fixed without just going back to self-report. No intro or conclusion.
