Below is a passage from the Preface to John Henry Newman’s The Idea of a University.

“The view taken of a University in these Discourses is the following:—That it is a place of teaching universal knowledge. This implies that its object is, on the one hand, intellectual, not moral; and, on the other, that it is the diffusion and extension of knowledge rather than the advancement. If its object were scientific and philosophical discovery, I do not see why a University should have students; if religious training, I do not see how it can be the seat of literature and science.”

I am trying to work out whether Newman’s picture of a university leaves principled room for what are now often dismissed as “useless” fields—palaeography, pure mathematics, historical linguistics, the study of obscure institutions. When Newman distinguishes the diffusion and extension of knowledge from its advancement, is he saying that a university’s core business is transmission rather than discovery? And if so, does that transmission-centered account protect such fields by making the maintenance of broad knowledge itself the aim, or does it leave their status accidental and vulnerable? I am interested less in whether Newman was right than in what a careful reading of the quoted sentences commits him to.
