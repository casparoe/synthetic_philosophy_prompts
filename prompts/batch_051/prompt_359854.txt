I'm a PhD student in bioethics working on trial design, and I keep going back and forth on something I'd love a careful take on. The standard line is Freedman's clinical equipoise: it's ethical to randomize patients only while there's honest, professional disagreement in the expert community about which arm is better, and once the evidence tips far enough that the community would no longer be genuinely uncertain, the trial has to stop randomizing. That's always made sense to me. But response-adaptive randomization seems to sit uneasily with it in a way I can't quite articulate. The trial literally updates it's allocation ratio as data accumulates, assigning more future patients to whichever arm is performing better so far, sometimes pretty aggressively. So the trial itself is acting on interim evidence long before the expert community has reached anything like consensus, and a patient who enrolls late has different odds of getting the worse-performing arm than someone who enrolled on day one. Is that a violation of clinical equipoise? Or is equipoise just a property of the expert community's state of mind, so it's fully compatible with an algorithm that's tilting allocation on the strength of a posterior nobody in the community is committed to? I'm trying to work out which premise actually breaks, if any, and whether the requirement can be repaired in a weaker form — maybe something like randomization is only fine so long as no enrolled patient has a substantial chance of being assigned a treatment the current evidence makes look inferior, or equipoise constrains stopping but not allocation ratios. I care about what happens to the argument, not whether RAR is good policy. Does clinical equipoise survive response-adaptive designs, or does some part of the original argument have to give?
