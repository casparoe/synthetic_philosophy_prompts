I chair the student conduct committee at a state university, and I need to circulate a short decision memo before we vote Thursday. Last month around thirty students occupied the provost's office for five hours to protest the university's contracts with two weapons manufacturers. They refused to leave after being told to, which violates our rules against trespass and disrupting university operations; that part isn't in dispute. They also locked one of the office suite's hallway doors from the inside with a bike lock for the last hour, which some committee members think pushes this from civil disobedience into coercion or even false imprisonment. The students were nonviolent, livestreamed the whole thing, gave their names to campus police, and have said in writing that they expect sanctions and consider accepting them part of the protest itself. Our conduct rules have a clause allowing leniency for what they call 'conscientious action,' but as far as anyone can tell it's never actually been applied. Two members want the same sanctions we'd give any organized sit-in: probation plus some short suspension. Two others want no formal sanctions beyond a restorative conference, on the ground that open, non-evasive disobedience that accepts punishment is not ordinary rule-breaking and shouldn't be handled like it. The rest of us are stuck, and everyone agrees on the facts. So the real disagreement seems to be about what the sanctions are doing: whether punishing these students vindicates the rules or chills exactly the kind of public moral argument a university is supposed to host, and whether the bike lock detail changes the character of the act. Draft the memo I'd circulate: it should make a concrete recommendation (a specific sanction or no sanction, addressing the bike lock matter if you think it matters), give reasons that genuinely engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than just restating it, and be something the committee could actually act on. Also, please don't run the whole thing through some named philosopher; give us the reasoning, not a reading list.
