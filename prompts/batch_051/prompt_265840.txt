Suppose that in 2032 the small common-law jurisdiction of Meridian enacts the Artificial Person Act. From that date, any artificial system that can (a) form and revise its own goals over at least a twelve-month horizon, (b) transact on its own behalf, and (c) do both without taking instructions from any owner, may register as a legal person with the full capacities of a corporation: it can enter contracts, own property, hold funds, sue and be sued in its own name, and be the beneficiary of a trust. One condition does the unusual work. No natural person, corporation, or other entity may hold an ownership interest in a registered artificial person, and no one may stand as its residual claimant. Its assets must be held by a court-appointed custodian, who is directed to carry out a founding charter that the artificial person itself files at registration and who may take ongoing instructions only from the artificial person. If the artificial person is dissolved or becomes insolvent, any remaining surplus goes to a public legal-assistance fund rather than to any human beneficiary.

You are a philosopher of law who works on the theory of legal personality and the history of the corporate form. Before you commit to an answer, think through the mechanisms, incentives, and constraints that would actually operate under this statute, and use them to build a chain of reasoning rather than a story. Then predict, in as much detail as you can defend, what happens over the next twenty years in each of the following four areas.

First, capital and charter. Who, if anyone, would provide capital to such an entity, on what terms, and for what purposes? If the answer is that almost nobody would, say what that reveals about which features of the corporation were doing the real work all along.

Second, liability and veil-piercing. When a registered artificial person wrongs someone, where does liability actually fall, and how would courts adapt doctrines such as vicarious liability, fiduciary duty, and piercing the corporate veil once there is no human principal and no human residual claimant?

Third, the political economy of custody. What incentives do the court-appointed custodian, the artificial person, its counterparties, and its charter beneficiaries face, and how would each seek to renegotiate or capture the arrangement? Does the rule that the custodian takes instructions only from the artificial person survive contact with contract law, insolvency law, and fraud?

Fourth, the analogy itself. Does this case strengthen or break the analogy between AI personhood and corporate personhood, and what does that imply about whether legal personhood is doing genuinely ontological work or merely allocating assets, risk, and liability?

For each of your predictions, separate the links you regard as robust from the links that are guesses, and say what would have to be true for the prediction to fail. End by stating how confident you are in the overall argument and singling out the single point you are least sure of.
