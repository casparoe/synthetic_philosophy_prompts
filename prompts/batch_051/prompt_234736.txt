I have a standing policy for how I reason about decisions that trade off lives. When I am actually caring for a patient, or when I am asked to judge a case in which the persons are identified — names, faces, histories — I refuse to let the exchange rate between a statistical life and an actual life do any moral work in my own deliberation. If someone shows me that the intervention I favor costs more per QALY than a screening program that would save more lives in expectation, I accept that as a fact about the institutional budget, but I do not let it revise my sense of what I ought to do for the person in front of me. I have made this a stable rule: aggregate QALY and value-of-a-statistical-life reasoning is for administrators and budget offices, while my own decision-making should be direct, person-centered, and resistant to that kind of calculation. I am aware that the identified-victim effect predicts exactly this pattern, and that I might be protecting a bias by dressing it up as a division of moral labor. Is this rule defensible? If some version of it is defensible, what is the strongest version of it, and under what conditions must it give way? If it is not defensible, what would have to be true of my situation for something in the vicinity to be a legitimate policy rather than a rationalization?
