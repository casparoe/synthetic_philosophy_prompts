Before a workshop on the moral psychology of inequality I'm trying to get my own commitments straight, and I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Here's the set:

(1) Envy is a vice — something to be managed and, ideally, eliminated, not a way of finding out what's true.
(2) Resentment is different: it can be a fitting response to being wronged, and the fact that I feel it is sometimes a reliable sign that an injustice has actually occurred.
(3) What separates them is their object: resentment responds to (percieved) wrongdoing, while envy responds to someone else's having more or doing better, regardless of how the difference came about. So envy, unlike resentment, can't tell us whether an arrangement is just.
(4) But in a radically unequal society, I also want to say that the envy of the worst-off toward the best-off isn't really a moral failing on their part — it's excusable, even reasonable, and it seems to register something genuinely objectionable about the basic structure, even if it doesn't name a particular wrongdoer.
(5) And I agree with Rawls that envy shouldn't itself be used as an independent ground in justifying principles of justice; one mark of a well-ordered society would be that the comparisons that make people envious don't arise much in the first place.

Go through these and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way. For each real tension, rank it by severity, tell me how confident you are in that call, and say what would have to go and what it would cost. And if you think I've built the envy/resentment distinction in (3) so that it's already doing all the argumentative work and the rest is window dressing, say that plainly instead of fixing it politely.
