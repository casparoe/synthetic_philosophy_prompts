I serve on the Data Custodianship Committee at a large research hospital. We are being asked to adjudicate a dispute that has been simmering since the hospital’s precision medicine initiative began combining patient-generated data from wearable devices with electronic health records to train a commercial risk-prediction model. A patient whose heart-rate variability, sleep, and activity data were used without direct consent, though under an umbrella research authorization she signed years ago, has formally requested that the derived risk score for her cardiac event profile be returned to her and that she be allowed to license access to that score to a third-party insurer. Her argument is that the information is about her body and her life, and therefore belongs to her. The hospital’s legal office counters that the raw data were de-identified and that the model’s output is a new analytical product created by the hospital’s labour, not a record of the patient. The committee must deliver a written opinion by Thursday recommending whether the patient has a property-like claim to the derived score, and if so under what conditions it should be released or licensed.

To focus that opinion, I would like you to engage with an argument from John Locke’s Second Treatise of Government, Chapter V. Locke holds that in the original common state nobody owns the earth’s spontaneous fruits, but a person acquires a right in them by mixing their labour with them, provided there remains enough and as good left for others to use. In our case, the patient contributed the raw biometric facts by living her life and wearing the device; the hospital contributed the scientific and computational labour that transformed those facts into a predictive score. Does Locke’s labour-mixing argument, and especially his enough-and-as-good proviso, imply that the patient has a claim on the derived data, that the hospital does, or that neither has a full property right because the object is not a rivalrous thing? Draft the opinion: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should treat seriously the strongest consideration in favor of the losing side rather than merely restating the hospital’s legal position.
