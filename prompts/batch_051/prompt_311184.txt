I am taking a high school philosophy elective, and we have been discussing Aristotle's account of virtue alongside some findings from social psychology. I keep coming back to an argument that seems to show the whole virtue-ethical approach rests on a false picture of character, but I cannot tell whether it is actually a good argument or whether one of the steps is doing something illicit. I would be grateful for a careful verdict.

The argument runs as follows. (1) A virtue is a stable character trait: a settled disposition to notice, feel, and act in the ways a situation calls for. (2) If a person has a virtue such as compassion or honesty, that trait should manifest itself reliably across a wide range of situations that call for it; a genuinely compassionate person should not be helpful only in convenient circumstances. (3) Social psychology experiments suggest that ordinary people's moral behavior is strongly influenced by seemingly minor situational features — whether they are in a hurry, whether other people are present, whether an authority figure is nearby — so that the same person often acts very differently in situations that seem morally alike. (4) Therefore, ordinary people generally lack the stable, cross-situational dispositions that virtue ethics describes. (5) Virtue ethics holds that right action is, roughly, what a virtuous person would do, and that we become good by cultivating the virtues through practice and habituation. (6) If almost no one has such dispositions, then virtue ethics is built on an empirically false account of character and recommends an ideal that cannot realistically be attained. (7) Therefore, the situationist evidence seriously undermines virtue ethics.

I want to know whether the argument is valid as stated, and, if it is not, exactly which step fails and why. If the argument is valid but some premise is false, which premise is the weakest, and what would the strongest version of it be? I would also like to know whether there is a form of virtue ethics that can accept the psychological findings without giving up the idea that virtues are stable character traits, or whether accepting those findings really does force a major revision of the view.
