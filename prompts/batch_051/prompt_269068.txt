I'm a PhD student working on political philosophy, and I keep coming back to a weird asymmetry in how liberal states think about forced labour. A state will criminalise a private employer who makes someone work under threat of imprisonment, yet the same state will conscript its own citizens into the military under the same threat, often in peacetime. People who would call the first case slavery are usually fine with the second — and would even say that a place like Switzerland can afford its armed neutrality only because it conscripts its citizens into a militia. So the neutrality itself seems to rest on an institution we would otherwise call forced labour. I want to know wether there is a principle that genuinely generates both judgements, or whether it is only a psychological habit. What is the strongest candidate justification — democratic authorisation? collective self-defence as a special public good? some distinct civic duty? — and does it survive nearby cases, like conscription for pandemic nursing, private security firms, or a state that drafts people for wars of choice? Please give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up, and please write in British English.
