I keep noticing a funny asymmetry in how people talk about the past, and I'd like to know whether it's defensible. On the one hand, no one objects if you say 'if Franz Ferdinand's driver hadn't taken that wrong turn, the Archduke wouldn't have been shot that day' — we treat that kind of near-miss counterfactual as perfectly meaningful. On the other hand, if a historian says 'once long-distance railways and the telegraph were built, a national market was pretty much inevitable,' the same people start muttering about hindsight bias and teleology. But isn't an inevitability claim just a counterfactual with a bigger antecedent: given the big structural causes, no nearby possible world comes out differently? I want the strongest principle you can find that treats the small counterfactual and the inevitability claim differently, and an honest verdict on wether it really works — or whether we should either get more comfortable with inevitability talk or a lot more suspicious of small counterfactuals. Please write in British English.
