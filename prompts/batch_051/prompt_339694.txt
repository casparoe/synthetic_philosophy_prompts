In a seminar on the philosophy of measurement and dating, I keep hearing a comparison that I suspect is doing more work than people acknowledge. The comparison goes like this: choosing a calendar epoch — the point from which years are numbered — is like choosing the origin of a coordinate system or the zero point on a temperature scale. Since an origin is not a property of what is measured but a convention adopted for convenience, we should no more ask which epoch is "correct" than ask where zero on a ruler "really" is; and by extension, disputes over the epoch of the Common Era, or over replacing it with some astronomical or secular epoch, are disputes about convenience and politics rather than about correctness. I would like you to audit this analogy rather than merely accept it. First, lay out the correspondence table in detail: in the calendar case, what plays the role of the coordinate axis, the units, the direction, the origin, and the operations that are or are not invariant when the origin is moved — such as interval arithmetic, ordering, and the alignment of recurring cycles. Then identify the disanalogies that might matter for the intended conclusion, among them: a coordinate axis is a uniform continuum on which the origin can be moved without changing anything structural, whereas a calendar bundles its epoch with rules for year length and leap years, with cyclical recurrence, and with the absence of a year zero in the usual reckoning; and the Common Era epoch carries historical and cultural freight that a coordinate origin does not. For each disanalogy, say whether it threatens the conclusion that the choice of epoch is arbitrary, or whether it is beside the point for that conclusion. Finally, tell me whether the analogy was carrying the argument, whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better analogy exists. Please separate what is settled — for example, the arithmetic consequences of inserting a year zero and how calendrical intervals are computed — from what is genuinely contested among historians, astronomers, and philosophers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
