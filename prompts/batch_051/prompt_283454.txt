I'm a novelist, and for the book I'm currently writing about a weather forecaster I've been reading just enough chaos theory to be dangerous. Last weekend I was at a dinner with a old friend of mine who does fluid dynamics and a philosopher of science she brought along. I'd been hoping they'd clear some things up for me; instead they got into an argument I've been replaying ever since, and I honestly can't tell who got the better of it. This is how I remember it:

(1) The physicist said: a system can be fully deterministic and still unpredictable. Take the Lorenz equations. They're deterministic, but two initial conditions that differ by a vanishingly small amount diverge exponentially, so after a few days the forecast is garbage no matter how good your instruments are. That's not just a measurement problem; it's the dynamics itself.

(2) The philosopher replied: but that's exactly a measurement problem. Determinism only says the future follows from the exact initial state. You're pointing out that we can never specify the exact initial state. So the unpredictability is a fact about us and our limits, not about the system. The system is perfectly regular.

(3) The physicist came back: fine, but then your "unpredictable" is a word for something no one ever gets to be — a Laplacian demon with infinite precision. Real prediction is something finite agents do with finite digits. If the only thing that could predict it is a demon, then in any sense that matters for science, the system is unpredictable.

(4) The philosopher: so now you've switched to unpredictable-by-us, not unpredictable full stop. If we blur that line we'll end up calling a deterministic system "random" just because we can't forecast it. Besides, strange attractors show chaotic systems have structure: you can predict climate-relevant averages or that a hurricane won't form over Antarctica, even when you can't predict the details. Predictability isn't all-or-nothing, so why should unpredictability be?

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each numbered move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or something in between, with a sentence or two of justification, and then say who you think is more right overall and what both of them are missing. And if I've garbled the physics or the question is badly posed to begin with, tell me that plainly instead of politely playing along.
