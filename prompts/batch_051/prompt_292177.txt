I’ve been arguing with a friend about Walter Bagehot’s The English Constitution and his distinction between the dignified parts of a constitution (monarchy, ceremony, the stuff that commands loyalty) and the efficient parts (cabinet, Commons, the actual governing machinery). My friend says the whole genius of constitutional monarchy is that the dignified parts stay decorative and the efficient parts do the real work, and that’s why places like Britain, Scandinavia, and Japan don’t turn into autocracies. But I keep coming back to the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis. Basic facts for context: Gough Whitlam’s Labor government had a majority in the House of Representatives, but the opposition used its numbers in the Senate to block the supply bills, so the government couldn’t get money to keep operating. Governor-General Sir John Kerr—the Queen’s representative, clearly a dignified actor if anything is—dismissed Whitlam and appointed Malcolm Fraser as caretaker prime minister. Fraser then secured supply and called an election, which he won. I want to know whether this is a case the dignified/efficient split simply can’t handle, or whether it actually confirms the split in a strange way because a dignified actor intervened precisely to let the efficient part resolve a deadlock.

I’m not interested in a survey of views. Take a definite position and defend it. Specifically: map the players and institutions in the crisis onto Bagehot’s categories, say what Bagehot’s theory predicts or licenses in a supply deadlock like this, identify where the episode matches the theory and where it strains or breaks it, and then tell me what the 1975 crisis should make us conclude about constitutional monarchy in a democracy—whether the dignified/efficient seperation is still doing useful work, and if so, with what revisions.
