I cover AI for a general-audience magazine, and there's an argument from the early interpretability crowd that I keep hearing repeated long after the world changed. The argument, as it was put to me, went roughly: these systems are artifacts, we can in principle inspect every weight and see exactly what they compute, and that transparency is what lets us keep them from doing dangerous things as they scale. That was plausible when the model was small enough that a person could look at a feature map and point to a neuron that lights up for faces. But the models that matter now are trained end to end on most of the internet and nobody reads them by hand. The workaround is to point another AI at the first one and have it produce an explanation, which has it's own confidences and blind spots and can fail in ways nobody is checking. So the transparency argument has gone recursive: we're using the same kind of opaque system we don't understand to generate our understanding of it. I want to know whether the original argument survives that move. Which premise was it actually leaning on, and does recursive interpretability break that premise or just make it more expensive? Is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful, like we don't need certainty, just better audits? Or is the recursive case different in kind, because each layer of explanation adds opacity rather than removing it? I'm asking what happens to the argument, not whether we should pause labs or regulate anything.
