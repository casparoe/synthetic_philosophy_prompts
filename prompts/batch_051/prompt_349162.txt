Skolem's paradox is usually taught as a theorem students learn to prove and a puzzle they learn to resolve verbally, but the resolution rarely lands with any force: the fact that a countable model of first-order set theory can satisfy "there is an uncountable set" keeps sounding like a contradiction even after it is explained. I want to change that. Design a low-tech, structured activity for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy-of-mathematics seminar — fifteen students, seventy-five minutes — that makes students occupy both sides of the inside/outside distinction before the distinction is named. Assume they have already seen the statement of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and Cantor's theorem, but have not been walked through the standard resolution. The activity should have students do something: take on roles, operate under explicit restrictions on which functions or "bijections" they may appeal to, make classification decisions, or run a small enacted formal system. The discovery that the relevant set is uncountable from within while countable from without should emerge from the mechanics of the activity, not from a closing explanation. Provide the roles or rules, the physical materials (no specialized software, no real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the distinction between the model's resources and the metatheoretic standpoint. Also anticipate the most likely way a first run will go wrong and how to recover from it, and state honestly which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the instructor.
