A friend who works in disability advocacy has an argument about advance directives that's been sitting in my head for a week and I can't find the exit.

(1) A patient's right to refuse treatment is grounded in what they autonomously want right now.
(2) An advance directive is a decision made by an earlier time-slice of the person. It's evidence about what they'll probably want later, not a little law they pass to bind their future self.
(3) So when someone with advanced dementia pulls out the IV, turns away from the nurse, or just eats with obvious pleasure when they're allowed to eat, their present preference is real autonomy, and the signed directive is a stale artifact.
(4) A live present preference always beats a recorded past one. The past self has no jurisdiction over the present self.
(5) But then it's hard to see how promises or contracts ever bind, because those are also just recorded preferences of an earlier time-slice, and the person right now who doesnt want to perform is a present preference like any other.
(6) But promises and contracts do bind, and not just when you still feel like keeping them.
(7) So (4) can't be right as stated. But I can't tell whether the mistake is already in (2), or in my move from (4) to (5), and whichever I give up I worry I lose either the possibility of commitment or the intuition that the demented person in front of us still gets a say. Which step should go, and what does rejecting it actually cost?
