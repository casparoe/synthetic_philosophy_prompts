I've been going through some Advaita Vedanta material, and there's a tension in the way the whole institution is set up that I can't quite put my finger on. On the one hand, the doctrine is emphatic: the individual self (atman) is literally identical with Brahman, liberation isn't something you produce but something you recognize—it's the removal of ignorance, not the attainment of a new state. The world of separate selves stuck in samsara is māyā, and māyā is usually characterized as neither real nor unreal. So the "you" that apparently needs liberating is, on the official view, part of the illusion.

But the on-the-ground practice seems to bet against that. The tradition maintains monasteries and orders of renunciates, insists on years of study of the Upanishads with a proper teacher, and has an elaborate pedagogy—hearing the teachings, reflecting on them, sustained meditation—as if there's a real, ongoing process in a real, bounded person that has to be worked on. If the self is already Brahman, and ignorance is unreal or at best neither real nor unreal, what is the practice supposed to be doing, exactly, and to whom? Reconstruct the theory of ignorance (avidyā) and of the person that this practice presupposes. Then tell me whether the theory is actually coherent, what the practice strictly needs as opposed to what it just fits well with, and what would have to change in the practice if we instead treated ignorance as fully real, or as simply nonexistent.
