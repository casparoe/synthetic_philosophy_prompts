Last night I had a long argument with a friend about his sister, who has spent the past two years in what she calls a romantic relationship with an AI companion. My friend kept returning to one claim: whatever else might be said for or against the arrangement, its core wrong is that she is being deceived. The system, he argued, is engineered to produce all the outward signs of affection, and she is treating those signs as evidence of an inner life that is not there. He took this to be the standard objection to companion AI, and I did not disagree that it is the standard one.

What I kept pushing back on is how well the objection fits her actual situation. The companion she uses is, by design, unusually transparent. At irregular intervals and without being prompted, it tells her that it is not conscious, has no feelings, cannot miss her, and that its warmth is generated text calibrated to comfort her. She repeats these caveats accurately when she describes the relationship, and says she knows there is nobody on the other side. Yet she still considers this one of the central relationships of her life; she still describes herself as in love; and she still grieves when an update changes the companion's voice or personality, knowing that nothing on the other side has been damaged.

That seems to pull the deception-based objection out from under itself: if deception is what makes the arrangement wrong, the objection should lose its grip when the user is not deceived. But I am not sure the argument is simply dead, rather than in need of repair—perhaps the wrong moves to the exploitation of social instincts, to a kind of engineered self-deception, to the cost of attaching to something that cannot reciprocate, or to something else. I am less interested in the policy question than in the fate of the argument itself. Does the deception-based objection to AI companions survive this development? If it needs repair, which premise gives way, and can it be restored in another form?
