I'm a philosophy professor, and the chair of our university's research ethics comittee — a clinical pharmacologist — has asked me to turn the idea of clinical equipoise into something her committee can actually use. She reviews around fifty trial protocols a year, mostly Phase II and III drug and device studies, with a committee of clinicians and lay members who mostly haven't read any of the philosophy. The committee meets monthly, so a given protocol gets perhaps twenty minutes of discussion, and a decision has to be signed off by the full committee and the institutional sponsor. The stakes run both ways: approve a trial where the relevant expert community already regards one arm as worse, and patients get inferior treatment for the sake of the protocol; reject or stall a trial, and clinical knowledge loses evidence it arguably needs. She keeps saying the current guidance, a vague demand for genuine uncertainty, is too loose to run on, which strikes me as exactly right.

Your job: act as an expert in the ethics of clinical research and in translating philosophical principles into operational decision procedures. Before you commit to a procedure, brainstorm what clinical equipoise is really meant to protect and where a naive just-be-uncertain rule breaks down. Then write a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — that a busy committee member could run on a protocol in about fifteen minutes. It should cover how to identify the relevant community of experts whose views count; how to distinguish community equipoise from an individual member's hunch or from a patient's preference; whether equipoise has to hold only at the start or also as the data accrue; and what to do when a trial uses a placebo or waitlist control even though an effective treatment exists. Mark each step where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and a person has to decide. Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical protocols: (a) a trial comparing a new anticoagulant after minor stroke with standard aspirin, where pilot data look excellent and a vocal minority of stroke neurologists already say the new drug is clearly better; and (b) a three-arm trial of a novel psychotherapy for adolescent depression against both CBT and a waitlist control, given that CBT already has a strong evidence base. Include at least one worked application where the procedure forces the committee into a decision its members will not like, and finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot settle. Write the entire answer in British English, please.
