Having retired from a career in the physical sciences, I have lately been reading my way into the philosophy of history and collective memory, and I have been asked by a reading group to circulate a short sketch of my position on national myths so that the other members can press on it before our next meeting. I would be grateful for a rigorous consistency audit of the position below. My scientific training disposed me to think that truth is not something one may adjust for the sake of morale; the more I read about how nations actually remember their defeats and lost causes, the less sure I am that this principle transfers cleanly from the laboratory to public memory. I am not asking which of these seven commitments is true, and I am not looking for scholarship or a reading list; I want to know whether they can all be held at once, and if not, where the strain lies. I suspect the trouble is somewhere near the concepts of truth and social need, but I have not been able to locate it precisely, and I would rather not discover it mid-discussion.

Here is the sketch.

1. National myths — Dunkirk, the siege of Masada, the Battle of Kosovo narrative in Serbian memory, the Lost Cause of the Confederacy — are typically unreliable in their particulars, but the cultural work they do is largely independent of that unreliability. They make vivid abstract qualities such as endurance, sacrifice, or the meaning of defeat, and a scrupulously qualified historical account often cannot do this as effectively.

2. Public history nevertheless owes citizens strict accuracy. What states fund, teach, and commemorate should not assert as fact anything known to be false, or anything misleading by omission or arrangement.

3. No large political community can sustain itself on a fully disenchanted and rigorously corrected past. A measure of selectively narrated, identity-forming usable past is a practical precondition of the solidarity that makes collective sacrifice and redistribution possible.

4. The line between such myths and respectable historiography is not sharp. All historical writing selects, arranges, and assigns significance; national myths differ from professional history in degree of simplification and emotional loading, not in kind.

5. What makes a national myth pernicious is the moral character of the lesson it most naturally encodes. The Dunkirk myth of unassuming endurance seems to me acceptable; the Lost Cause, which ennobles a war fought to preserve slavery, is objectionable because of what its omissions and accents teach its inheritors to believe.

6. A myth's moral valence, however, is carried entirely by its present uses. A dormant or repurposed myth can become benign, and the same inaccuracies that once underwrote injustice can later serve an inclusive narrative; considered apart from their employment, the inaccuracies themselves have no moral color.

7. Professional historians nonetheless have a standing duty to correct national myths wherever they appear in public life, regardless of the myth's present political uses, because the historian's primary allegiance is to truth rather than to collective identity.

Please go through these commitments and identify which pairs pull against each other in a serious way and which only appear to do so. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say which commitment would most plausibly have to give, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision would cost me. For at least one apparent tension, explain why it dissolves on closer inspection. I need the audit to discriminate rather than to summarize; I can summarize the position myself.
