I hold a version of the is-ought gap. My position is really two claims. First, no argument whose premises are purely descriptive can validly yield a genuinely normative conclusion, where "genuinely normative" excludes mixed truth-functional compounds in which a normative clause occurs only inertly. Second, moral terms such as "good," "ought," and "right" cannot be given wholly non-normative definitions; the pattern standardly traced to Hume's remarks on is and ought and to Moore's naturalistic fallacy is, as I understand it, a real constraint. I want to know what would count against this view, at a level of precision beyond the usual textbook treatment. Please address these candidate defeaters. (1) Disjunction-style derivations: from a descriptive premise P one may infer "P or Q," where Q is normative; add a further descriptive premise "not-P," and disjunctive syllogism appears to yield Q from purely descriptive premises. Does this show the gap is empty? Or does it only force qualifications about consistency, relevance, or what counts as a genuinely normative conclusion? I would take such a derivation seriously only if it could be run from consistent, relevantly connected descriptive premises; otherwise I am inclined to treat it as a logical curiosity rather than a counterexample. (2) The open-question test for definitions: does it prove too much by ruling out true identity claims of the water-is-H2O kind, and can the gap proponent weaken it without giving up the anti-naturalist conclusion? (3) The boundary problem: can the descriptive/normative distinction itself be drawn in a principled way that does not beg the question against the naturalist? (4) The triviality problem: perhaps the whole doctrine is only a logical conservativeness fact — an "ought" cannot be extracted from premises in which no "ought" occurs — in which case does the view collapse into a triviality, or is that exactly the right way to state it? For each candidate, say whether it is fatal, serious but repairable, or merely awkward, and say which of these a defender of the gap should themselves concede counts against them rather than dismiss. Finally, assess honestly whether the position has been, or could be, insulated against all possible counterexamples — and if so, whether that insulation is a reason to reject the view or is legitimate for a claim about what follows from what.
