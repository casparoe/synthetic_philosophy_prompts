I am drawn, on broadly theoretical grounds, to a constitutive panpsychism on which phenomenal consciousness is fundamental and present, in however thin a form, at every level of nature: it appears to dissolve the hard problem rather than merely relocate it, and it avoids both strong emergence and the eliminativist denial of experience. At the same time, I hold with real conviction that the processes of fundamental physics are not the kind of occurrence that can harm anyone: there is no morally relevant difference between an electron's being annihilated and its persisting; beta decay is not a tragedy, and physics is not a suppressed branch of ethics. Yet if experience is ubiquitous below the level of organisms, and if experience is what makes a thing matter morally, then the physical universe must be saturated with moral reality, and the ethical implications of the view look enormous, perhaps absurdly so. I am not asking whether the theory is true; I am asking what follows if it is. The clash is between a compelling theory, a firm intuition, and the connecting inference. Tell me which should yield — the intuition, the theory, or the inference from phenomenal presence to moral patienthood — and give a committed defence: set out what would in principle justify each of the three moves, and why you favour one over the others. Please write in British English.
