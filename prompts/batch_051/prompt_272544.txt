Courts that present themselves as bound by the text of a statute nevertheless depart from that text in two familiar situations. They will correct an obvious scrivener's error — say, a filing deadline printed as 300 days when the rest of the provision makes clear that 30 was meant — even though the corrected reading contradicts the words on the page. And under the absurdity doctrine they will sometimes decline to apply a provision whose literal application would produce a result so irrational or self-defeating that the legislature could not plausibly have intended it, even when the only way to avoid that result is to rewrite the provision. Yet the same courts will not rewrite a provision whose literal application is merely unwise, harsh, or bad policy; there they say the text controls and the legislature must amend it. The practice therefore treats a garbled expression of a legislative decision differently from a misguided legislative decision, and an absurd result differently from a merely bad one. Is there a principle that draws these lines and survives hard cases? Construct the strongest principle you can, test it against a provision whose literal reading is perfectly intelligible but yields an outcome the enacting legislature would almost certainly have rejected, and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or abandoned.
