Here's something I keep getting stuck on. In Germany, France, Italy, and Spain surrogacy is banned outright — the contracts are void, and in some of them arranging one is a crime — and even in the UK it can't be commercial: the carrier may only be reimbursed reasonable expenses. Egg donation is supposed to be a gift on that side of the Atlantic too. Meanwhile, in much of the United States there's a full market: matching agencies, attorneys, escrow, paid egg donors, and paid surrogates who charge for their time and risk. I want you to treat the European rules — and the American ones as their mirror image — as practices that presuppose some theory of value, exchange, and parenthood, and then reconstruct it.

The obvious reading is that gestation is the kind of thing that is degraded or misdescribed if you put a price on it, or that paying the woman makes her consent invalid because it exploits the poor. But I don't think that reading survives contact with the details. If the worry is exploitation, or medical risk, why is altruistic surrogacy allowed at all — the unpaid carrier takes the same risks? If the worry is commodification, why do the same systems let the clinic charge real money for the IVF and the retrieval, and in the U.S. let the agency, the lawyers, and the egg donor all profit while the gestation itself must be a gift? Notice that the "reasonable expenses" line is doing a lot of philosophical work: the medical care costs what it costs, so what is actually forbidden is paying the carrier for her time, pain, risk, and lost wages. The rules seam to say those have to be donated, while everyone else's contribution gets a price. And there's a matching puzzle on the American side: in adoption you can't legally buy a baby from a birth mother, yet in a commercial surrogacy the intended parents pay someone to carry a pregnancy and walk away with a baby, and we insist it's not a sale.

So reconstruct the implicit theory each set of rules presupposes about what exactly is being exchanged — a service, a child, a relationship — about whether some things are corrupted by pricing, and about who counts as the mother. Tell me where each system strictly presupposes that theory and where it merely fits with several candidate theories, and whether the pieces are consistent. Then tell me what the law would have to look like if we replaced the presupposition with the view that gestational care is just a bodily service like any other.
