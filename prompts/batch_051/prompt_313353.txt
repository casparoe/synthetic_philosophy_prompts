I run an external-communications review group at a consumer financial services firm; my team clears roughly a dozen customer letters, product brochures, and regulator responses a week, and the legal team separately screens them for false or deceptive claims. I want a reusable ethical procedure, separate from the legal screen and usable by staff with no philosophy background in under twenty minutes per communication, that turns the philosophical distinction between lying and merely misleading into a working decision tool. Assume the working definitions that a lie asserts something the speaker believes false, and that a merely misleading communication asserts or presents something true but is worded or arranged so that a reasonable reader will take away something false. Please write the procedure so it specifies, in order: the questions that determine whether a proposed communication, or a particular claim within it, counts as a lie, as misleading-but-not-a-lie, or as neither; what counts as an adequate answer to each question; what follows for our recommendation to approve, revise, or escalate; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it must hand the item to a person because the next step is a value judgment that the distinction cannot settle. It should also make explicit its position on the underlying moral question, whether a liar is worse than a misleader because the liar directly asserts a known falsehood, or the two are on a par because both knowingly produce the same false belief, and it should flag the steps where that position does the real work. Then run the procedure on three cases and show the output for each: (1) a landing page whose large type says "No monthly account fees" while the body notes the fee is waived only for balances over $500; (2) a customer email saying "We have credited $40 to your account to correct the error," where the $40 is promotional credit that cannot be withdrawn and lapses after 90 days; and (3) an answer to a regulator saying the firm "does not charge a fee for standard electronic withdrawals," which is true but omits that telephone-assisted withdrawals cost $15. At least one of the outcomes should be one our legal team would not reach, and the final section should state plainly what the procedure cannot do.
