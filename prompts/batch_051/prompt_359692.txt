Locke’s memory criterion in “Of Identity and Diversity” says that a person at one time is identical with a person at an earlier time only insofar as the later self can remember the earlier self’s thoughts and actions from the inside. In his Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man, Reid presses the standard brave officer objection: the old general remembers capturing the enemy standard as a young officer but does not remember being flogged as a boy for robbing an orchard, so Locke seems forced to say the general is the officer but not the boy, even though the officer was the boy. Now vary the case. Suppose the general has no spontaneous episodic recall of the flogging, but he shows a degraded recognition response to the orchard and to the man who punished him, and under a mild retrieval cue he half-endorses a reconstructed image of the flogging as his own memory, assigning it only sixty percent confidence. Does Reid’s objection still have the same force, or does it trade on treating memory as all-or-nothing? What should a defender of Locke do here—introduce a threshold, replace identity with a graded relation, or require a chain of memory links—and what does each option do to the transitivity problem the brave officer is meant to expose?
