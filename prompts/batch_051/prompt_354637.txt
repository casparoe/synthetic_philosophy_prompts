I am a PhD student in political economy, and last week our graduate seminar on the history of economic thought discussed Oskar Lange's model of market socialism. Afterward, two of my classmates, Alex and Sam, were still arguing about whether Lange had actually answered the Austrian challenge, and their exchange was careful enough that I wrote it down; I would like a point-by-point adjudication of it, because I genuinely cannot referee it myself.

(1) Alex began by framing Mises's original objection as a computational one: without markets in producer goods there are no prices, so a planner cannot compare alternative techniques. Lange, Alex argued, answered exactly this with his trial-and-error procedure — the Central Planning Board posts provisional prices, managers are instructed to minimize average cost and produce where marginal cost equals price, and the Board raises prices wherever there is excess demand and lowers them wherever there is excess supply, much as the Walrasian auctioneer does in general equilibrium theory. So calculation is possible in principle under socialism, and everything left over is an implementation problem.

(2) Sam replied that this changes the subject. The decisive Austrian point, he said, belongs to Hayek, not Mises: the information the equations require is never given to any one mind. Prices transmit dispersed practical knowledge — a regional bottleneck, a foreman's inarticulate sense of which equipment is about to fail, a farmer's judgment about the weather — that no central board could collect in reports. Lange's auctioneer can begin its tâtonnement only after someone has already decided which goods are worth pricing and what a shortage or surplus indicates, and that identification is itself the discovery function real markets perform.

(3) Alex answered that this runs two distinct objections together. Mises said calculation is impossible, and Lange produced a procedure that would in principle find the right prices. If Sam's actual worry is that the Board would not have the data, or that managers would hide or falsify information, then those are reporting and incentive problems, in principle addressable through audits and bonuses; they do not refute Lange's claim about logical possibility. A possibility proof cannot be defeated by implementation difficulties.

(4) Sam said the two objections cannot be separated as cleanly as Alex thinks, because the relevant data do not exist in a reportable form. A manager's knowledge is often embedded in practices and hunches rather than figures she could transmit. Prices do more than solve equations, he added: they let people act appropriately on others' knowledge without anyone needing to know the reasons, and a Board adjusting a few administered prices has no counterpart to the entrepreneurial process that discovers which goods should be priced at all — the process that keeps introducing new products and techniques.

(5) Alex granted the force of this but said Sam was now holding socialism to an ideal standard that capitalism itself does not meet. Trial-and-error pricing does use real market feedback, because it observes whether warehouses clear; it is therefore a discovery procedure, if a slower one. Actual capitalist markets are rife with monopoly, asymmetric information, and coordination failures, so the right comparison is not socialism versus a perfect market but socialism versus the market we actually get — and Lange only needs approximate allocative efficiency.

(6) Sam's last reply was that the difference is not one of degree but of system. In a competitive market, an entrepreneur who misreads an unmet demand loses her own capital and is replaced, so error is disciplined by loss. In Lange's model the Board is a monopolist price-setter with no residual claim on its success and no rival to expose its mistakes, so the trial-and-error mechanism has no force driving it toward the equilibrium it is supposed to approximate, and nothing generating the novelties that render yesterday's prices obsolete.

I can see that each of them lands real blows, but I cannot tell which specific claims survive — for instance, whether Alex's charge in (3) that Sam conflates Hayek's argument with Mises's is fair, whether (5) legitimately lowers the standard of comparison, and whether (6) is really a systematic point rather than the incentive objection Sam earlier seemed not to rest his case on. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: for each numbered turn, say which claims are correct, which are overstated or mistaken, and why; then say who has the better overall position, and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
