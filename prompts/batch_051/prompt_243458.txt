Here's the checklist I was taught for deciding whether something is humour: superiority (we get a little jolt from someone else's failing or come-down), incongruity (two scripts or patterns collide in a way that still makes a strange kind of sense), relief (pent-up nervous energy gets discharged once the threat turns out to be harmless), and benign violation (something wrong, taboo, or threatening is happening, but it's appraised as okay in the moment). I don't use them as all-or-nothing necessary conditions; I use them as a working checklist.

The case I can't settle: being tickled. A parent tickles their toddler, who shrieks with laughter, squirms, can't catch thier breath, and asks both "stop" and "again" in the same second. No joke has been told, no one is the butt of anything, and there's no conceptual pattern being violated—yet the laughter and pleasure look an awful lot like the ones we get from a good joke. The one criterion that seems to reach the case is benign violation: the touch is too much and safe at the same time. But there's no content to appraise, which incongruity says there has to be.

So: is tickling humour? Go criterion by criterion and say which ones apply, which ones fail, and where the criteria just go silent. Then take a real stand. Either tickling counts, in which case tell me which criterion needs widening or demoting and why that won't wreck the checklist for jokes; or tickling doesn't count, in which case explain what principled difference separates tickle-laughter from humour-laughter, beyond just saying one is a reflex. Don't retreat into "it depends on your definition."
