I'm a political science PhD student working on how much individual leaders actually matter in cases of mass violence, and my department is split between the great-man people and the structuralists. My advisor is a hard structuralist, and every time someone brings up Hitler he reaches for the match-and-kindling analogy: Hitler was the match, but the kindling—Versailles, the depression, the weakness of Weimar, a century of antisemitism—was bone dry, so if it hadn't been Hitler it would have been somebody else, and the interesting causes are all structural. He uses this to license the strong conclusion that a no-Hitler counterfactual wouldn't change much: Germany still goes authoritarian, still starts a war, and something like the genocide still happens.

I want an honest audit of how far that analogy holds. Lay out the correspondence table first: what's the match, the kindling, the oxygen, the dryness, the hand that strikes it, the surrounding houses. Then hit the disanalogies. I've got my own candidate list (dry wood doesn't respond to the match or get recruited by it; matches are interchangeable in a way charismatic leaders maybe aren't; ignition is quick but Hitler spent a decade preparing the fuel; a fire's path is mostly set by its fuel once lit, whereas a regime's path isn't), but I want you to seperate the disanalogies that actually threaten my advisor's argument from the ones that are just cosmetic. The crux: does the structuralist conclusion survive once those are on the table, or has the analogy been doing the heavy lifting? And if it breaks, give me a better analogy or say what the fairest version of the 'Hitler dies in 1932' counterfactual would have to look like to actually test his claim.

Also flag what the analogy quietly imports—my guess is it sneaks in the idea that the outcome was a fixed event waiting for a trigger, that the fuel is inert, and that the match and the kindling are independent—but I want you to tell me which of those imports are doing real argumentative work.
