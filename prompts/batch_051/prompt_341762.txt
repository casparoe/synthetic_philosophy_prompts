I'm putting together a two-page pitch for a small internal philosophy grant, and I want it to be about the Pasadena paradox but not just another paper defending one more way of summing the series. The game, for context, is the one where you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if the first heads is on flip n you get paid (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n; the expected value is then the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 under its natural ordering but only conditionally, so reordering the terms lets you get any finite value, or none at all. Everyone I've read treats that as a problem about how to sum expected values when the sum isn't absolutely convergent, and the standard fixes are either to ban such games outright or to impose some privileged ordering convention. My half-baked thought is that this is aimed at the wrong stage: before you can sum anything, you have to represent the gamble, and a finite reasoner never represents the whole infinite game in one piece — it represents a truncated version plus some vague catch-all for the tail. So the question I'd want funded is somthing like: is the Pasadena paradox an objection to expected utility maximization as such, or is it an artifact of pretending a bounded agent has already completed the infinite sum, and would a decision theory that explicitly models truncation and residual risk dissolve the paradox without needing a summation convention?

I need the proposal to state the question precisely, explain why the existing "ban it or reorder it" debate leaves it open, lay out what I'd actually do month by month, say why the truncation approach is better than defending yet another ordering, and give an honest account of what would count as failure for the project.
