I'm a philosophy professor, formal epistemology mostly, and a colleague who runs a small public-health early-warning unit has roped me into a project. Her analysts see dozens of field reports every shift, under real time pressure, and a wrong call either way is expensive — a missed outbreak or a needless scare. The reports are uncertain in a particular way: not a case of we-know-X-happened, but more like the clinic nurse thinks she's seen half a dozen cases of the same odd rash, or the lab flagged a sample but warns it could be contamination, or a local GP says there's maybe a cluster but won't stand behind it. Right now the senior person on shift eyeballs the report and nudges there overall confidence up or down, and the duty director signs off on whatever gets escalated. My colleague wants something more defensible, and she has latched onto Jeffrey conditionalization — the rule for updating your probabilities when the evidence doesn't arrive as a proposition you've learned for sure, but as new probabilities over a partition of possibilities, with your old conditional probabilities given each cell of the partition held fixed. She wants a decision procedure her analysts can actually run, not a talk.

Please write me that procedure. It should be usable by someone who has never taken a probability course: a checklist or ordered set of questions, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops because it can't be applied, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining call is judgment rather than computation. Be blunt about the places where the theory is doing no work and the analyst is just supplying numbers — priors, the choice of partition, whether those conditional probabilities really should stay fixed. Then run it on two cases: first, a nurse reports she is around 70 percent confident that three patients in the last week share a distinctive rash; second, a lab report says a dangerous-pathogen PCR came back positive but the lab itself puts the chance of contamination at one in four. I want to see exactly what the procedure asks for, how to proceed when the numbers aren't available, and at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the team won't like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle — for instance, whether a shift in probabilities counts as really having evidence versus just becoming more of a believer in something you already half-suspected.
