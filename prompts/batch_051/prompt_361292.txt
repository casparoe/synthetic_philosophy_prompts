After thirty-five years in oncology I retired last spring and, mostly to keep the philosophical part of my brain from turning to mush, started reading Hume. Two months later my old parish talked me onto the medical board that reviews alleged miracle cures for the archdiocese — the panel that has to decide whether a reported healing is "medically inexplicable" before the theology committee can even look at it. I did not expect the new hobby and the old job to collide this fast, but here we are.

The case in front of us: a woman in her early fifties with a biopsy-proven stage IV pancreatic adenocarcinoma, liver metastases on two separate scans, who declined chemo and radiation and instead prayed a novena to a deceased nun who's a candidate for beatification. Her three-month scan was clean — primary gone, liver gone — and she's disease-free going on two years. The pathology slides were re-read three times. Half the board says this is just a rare spontaneous regression, or an error we can't locate, so the honest vote is no. The other half says that move makes "inexplicable" an empty category, because there's always some imaginable natural story if you're willing to make it improbable enough.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — issue the certificate, refuse it, or issue it with conditions — and it should engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than caricaturing it. What I most want help with is where Hume's argument about testimony and miracles actually bites: his point that testimony for a miracle has to overcome the whole accumulated weight of prior experience, not just look better than the local alternatives. Are we allowed to apply that at the "medically inexplicable" stage, or does it smuggle in the conclusion the whole canonization apparatus is set up to resist? And be precise about the difference between "we found no natural explanation" and "there is none," becuase half the board keeps sliding between them. I have to be able to defend this to oncologists and to theologians, so make the reasoning travel.
