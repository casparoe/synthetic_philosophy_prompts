Lately I've been using constructive mathematics as a rule for everyday reasoning, and I can't tell if it's making me more rigorous or just more stubborn. My policy is: I don't accept that something exists unless someone can produce the thing or at least describe a procedure that would turn it up. Showing that we'd be in a bad position if it didn't exist—that all the alternatives fail, that the problem would be intolerable, that it would be crazy for there not to be one—doesn't count as evidence for me, and I quietly discount any argument that leans on that move. I apply this from 'some explanation must exist for why the results came out like this' to 'there has to be a policy that would fix this.' Is this a defensible form of epistemic hygiene outside math, or am I applying a proof-theoretic scruple where it doesn't belong? Where would it lead me badly astray, and is there a version of the rule that keeps whatever is good about it without throwing away legitimate existence claims?
