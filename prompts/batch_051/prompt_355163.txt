I'm leading a small undergrad discussion on W. E. B. Du Bois's essay "Criteria of Negro Art" (The Crisis, October 1926), and this passage keeps tripping people up:

> The thing we are talking about tonight is part of the great fight we are carrying on and it represents a forward and an upward look -- a pushing onward. You and I have been breasting hills; we have been climbing upward; there has been progress and we can see it day by day looking back along blood-filled paths. But as you go through the valleys and over the foothills, so long as you are climbing, the direction -- north, south, east or west -- is of less importance. But when gradually the vista widens and you begin to see the world at your feet and the far horizon, then it is time to know more precisely whither you are going and what you really want.

I think the climbing metaphor is doing more work than people first notice, but I'm not sure how much. On one hand it looks like Du Bois is saying that while you're in the thick of a struggle, the precise direction matters less than continuing to move; once the view widens, you need a clearer picture of where you're going. On the other hand I read it as him saying art becomes practically useful exactly at that point where you need to know what you want, which would make art not a break from the struggle but a kind of steering. That seems to be doing a lot of work for his later view that all art is propaganda, but the passage itself never says "propaganda."
