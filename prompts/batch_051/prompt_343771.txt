I chair the five-member review committee of a research institute. The committee meets three times a year to decide a round of appointments, and our standing practice is that each member votes yes or no on three criteria — scholarly strength, teaching and mentoring, and fit with our program — and then votes on the appointment itself. We record all four votes but have never specified what to do when the criterion votes and the direct appointment vote pull apart; last month they did. A candidate passed each of the three criteria by majority vote, yet the same members then rejected the appointment by majority vote, even though all of us had previously accepted, as a matter of policy, that any candidate who meets all three criteria should be appointed. A colleague tells me this is known as the discursive dilemma, but the name has not given us a working rule. The episode happened on my watch, and the full committee has to approve whatever I propose before the next round. I do not need a survey of the literature; I need an ordered checklist a non-specialist can use in under half an hour, for decisions whose stakes are people's careers and the institute's direction. The checklist should mark, at every step, where the committee is making a value judgment rather than applying a rule — for example, whether the appointment is to be built out of the criterion votes or settled by a direct vote, how the three criteria are weighted, and how abstentions are handled — and it should state plainly what no aggregation procedure can guarantee. Then run the procedure through three cases: the case above; a routine case in which every vote is unanimous; and a case in which the procedure, correctly followed, forces the committee to accept an outcome a majority of its members will dislike. In each case, note what discretion remains.
