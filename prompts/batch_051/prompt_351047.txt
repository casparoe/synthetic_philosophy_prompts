Treat the following two positions as parties to an adversarial collaboration, and write the document such a collaboration would produce.

Position A — the open-future position. Consider the sea-battle case: an observer asserts on Monday, of a genuinely undetermined engagement, "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," and the battle does occur on Tuesday. According to A, the Monday assertion is neither true nor false at the time it is made. A future-tensed claim is truth-apt only when the relevant outcome is already settled by Monday's facts together with the laws; open alternatives are properly expressed with "might," not "will." Truth values are assigned only to what is determinate as of the time of evaluation.

Position B — the settled-truth position. The same Monday assertion is already true or false on Monday: true if and only if the battle occurs on Tuesday. Future tense no more deprives a sentence of a present truth value than past tense does. On B's view, its being true on Monday does not make the battle necessary and does not imply fatalism; inferring the necessity of the battle from the prior truth of the assertion commits a modal fallacy whose precise form the collaboration should make explicit.

Both positions reject fatalism; both accept bivalence for non-future-tensed claims and for claims about what is already settled; both are to be treated as sincere and technically competent, not caricatured.

Draft the joint statement both camps could put their names to, in the following format. Part I: numbered claims both sides can sign, worded so neither side would object to the wording; these should be specific enough to pin down what is actually shared and to constrain the table that follows. Part II: a disagreements table with exactly these five columns: Issue; Position A's wording; Position B's wording; Crux (the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side); Test, case, or argument both agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded. The table must contain at least these rows: (i) the semantics of "will" and "might"; (ii) the Tuesday status of the retrospective claim "it was already true on Monday that there would be a sea battle"; (iii) whether a bet placed on Monday on the battle's occurrence was already, on Monday, a bet on something true, or only became true with Tuesday's outcome, and what the odds express in each case; (iv) whether B is committed to the view that Monday's deliberation was pointless or merely apparent. Add further rows only if they mark a genuine, non-verbal difference; if a candidate row collapses into a dispute about words, fold the substantive part into Part I and say what was terminological. End with a brief section in which each side states, honestly, the strongest case for the other side's predictions and the condition under which it would change.

Make definite choices throughout: commit to a specific wording in each cell rather than offering alternatives, and below the table give a short note defending the two or three hardest wording decisions. A response that only surveys the options without writing the document itself does not answer this prompt.
