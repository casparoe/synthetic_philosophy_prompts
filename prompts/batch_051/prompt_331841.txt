Could you design a classroom activity or game that makes them feel the force of the paradox? I'm imagining something where they write membership rules on index cards and then have to sort every card into "is a member of itself" or "is not a member of itself," with a point system that pressures them to make a call on each card—no skipping and no amending a rule once its written. Please include rules, materials, a timeline for 90 minutes, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, and a debrief that maps what happened onto naive set theory and unrestricted comprehension. The takeaway I'm going for is that the natural rule of set formation (any clear membership condition gives you a set) is the thing that breaks, not just this one odd example.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it. I suspect the kids will try to wriggle out by saying the paradoxical card "doesn't count" or by changing its rule mid-game, and I want the setup to make that move feel costly rather than like a clever escape. One constraint: the activity has to work while the students don't yet know the formal distinction between a set and a proper class, and I'd rather not use that vocabulary until the debrief. Also be honest about which part of the real paradox the activity can't reproduce—whatever the facilitator has to supply afterwards. And write the answer in American English, please.
