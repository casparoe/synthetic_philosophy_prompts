I am a third-year undergraduate enrolled in a philosophy of language course, and as part of a formal assessment I must design a single forty-five-minute introductory lesson on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. The audience is a study group of second-year students who have completed only introductory logic and one survey course in metaphysics. They can apply modus ponens mechanically and understand that a predicate may be true of an object, but they have never worked through a relation regress, and they are disposed to treat the claim that a proposition just is its constituents arranged in a certain way as a complete explanation rather than as something that itself needs scrutiny. I would like a teaching plan rather than a written-out explanation.

The plan should specify:

(1) the order in which the components must be introduced, with an explicit account of what depends on what;
(2) a single carrying example, which should involve a dyadic relation whose relata could easily be misread as one's being a property of the other; the example should avoid the stock 'a is F'-style cases and should be chosen so that none of its incidental features distract from the regress;
(3) the misconceptions students are likely to form and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off—especially the idea that positing an instantiation relation stops the regress, the premature slide from the regress to the conclusion that relational thought is incoherent, and the conflation of Bradley's regress with the Tortoise regress about the force of modus ponens;
(4) a check for understanding that would distinguish genuine comprehension of the difference between treating a relation as one more constituent of a fact and treating it as what relates the constituents from mere ability to reproduce a diagram of the regress;
(5) what should be deliberately left out at this level and how to flag it.

Please write the plan in a formal register suitable for submission.
