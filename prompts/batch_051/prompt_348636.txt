I keep noticing a pattern in how people treat probabilities, and I want to know whether it has an established name and precise boundaries. The pattern is this: people will let a probability settle a matter when it is used one way, and refuse to let the same probability settle the matter when it is used another way. A delivery firm runs four of every five vans on a stretch of road; a van hits a parked car and leaves; nobody saw the firm's markings; the owner of the car sues. Colleagues who are otherwise ready to apply the civil standard of more likely than not say the owner should lose, even though the claim is 80 percent probable. Or consider a ticketed concert where slightly more than half the audience got in through an unguarded gate without paying: a randomly selected attendee is more likely than not to have gatecrashed, but almost nobody thinks the promoter may recover the ticket price from that attendee. Yet many of those same people will wager on exactly those propositions at the odds the frequencies imply, and they will let the same frequencies set an insurance premium or a municipal budget. A doctor I put this to drew a similar line: a tissue finding that gives an 80 percent chance moves her to treat, while an 80 percent chance computed only from the frequencies for a patient's age and sex often does not, though the number on the table is the same.

This looks at first like the criminal standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt, but that cannot be the whole story, because in the van and gatecrasher cases the applicable standard is the civil one. I am not after a bare label; I need the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, which nearby concepts it is easily mistaken for, and where its edges fall. If no established term fits precisely, say so and propose one, with a definition tight enough to mark the boundary.

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five items should, in order: give the best-fitting established term and define what it covers; name the nearest concept likely to be confused with it and state the precise difference; name a second nearby concept and where it parts ways; explain how much of the phenomenon the standard of proof genuinely explains in my cases; and give your verdict on whether the legal, medical, and betting cases are one phenomenon or several, including a coined term only if nothing established fits the whole.
