Write a dialogue between two proponents of opposing views on whether novelty and variety are components of well-being. Character A defends the claim that novelty is a genuine constituent of a flourishing life: a life rich in other goods but wholly routinised would on that account be worse, and a rational fear of a boring life is not merely fear of lost opportunities for pleasure. Character B defends the claim that novelty’s value is at most instrumental, and that the deeper requirement is stable, wholehearted absorption in a small number of self-chosen ends; on this view, the pursuit of variety for its own sake is often a symptom of restlessness. Both should be equally competent and neither should be a strawman. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each responding to the other’s strongest formulation rather than talking past it. In particular, B should concede that hedonic adaptation makes some novelty instrumentally necessary, and A should concede that novelty-seeking can become a serial abandonment of commitments.

The dialogue should be built around this verbatim passage from Seneca’s On the Shortness of Life (De Brevitate Vitae), in John W. Basore’s translation:

“many, following no fixed aim, shifting and inconstant and dissatisfied, are plunged by their fickleness into plans that are ever new”

The passage should be quoted at least once in the dialogue and should be the object of genuine interpretive dispute. The characters should consider, among other things: whether Seneca’s indictment applies only to novelty without a fixed aim, or to variety as such; whether a fixed aim could itself require variety; and what the passage implies about a life that is stable but predictable. Each should be willing to say what the other’s reading gets right before rejecting it.

After the dialogue, add a brief note separating what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on well-being, and indicate where the characters have relied on a contested reading of Seneca or an empirical claim that a defender might resist. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
