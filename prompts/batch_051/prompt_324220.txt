I have been developing a taxonomy of the ways in which concern for the far future might be thought to justify political control. I distinguish four routes by which the worry is supposed to work. First, the arithmetic route: future people vastly outnumber present people, so present costs look negligible. Second, the emergency route: existential risk turns every generation into a standing emergency, and emergencies suspend ordinary limits on state action. Third, the vanguard route: because long-run outcomes are opaque, only those who have studied them are competent to steer, and everyone else should defer to them. Fourth, the ratchet route: present choices can lock in irreversible futures, so coercive action now may be needed to prevent irreversible bad lock-ins. My hypothesis is that only the fourth route survives scrutiny, and that the totalitarian charge against longtermism leans mostly on the first three. I would like an honest assessment. Is the taxonomy exhaustive? Are the four routes genuinely separable, or does one collapse into another? Does something close to this already exist under other names? And is my claim about where the danger lies defensible, or does the ratchet route quietly smuggle in the other three? Please answer in British English.
