I am a novelist, and my working method has always leaned on something I never had reason to question: when I draft a scene I partly see it — a street, a face, the weight of a door — and this seeing is not a metaphor for quickly believing a pile of propositions. A friend in cognitive science recently tried to persuade me otherwise, and now I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) The vehicle of an occurrent mental image must be one of two kinds: depictive or descriptive. A depictive vehicle is picture-like: it represents by spatial isomorphism or resemblance, and it is inspected. A descriptive vehicle is sentence-like: it has constituent structure, represents by satisfying a description, and enters into inference.

(2) If the vehicle is depictive, then it is a kind of inner picture. But a picture does not fix its own content; it means something only relative to an interpreter. If that interpreter is another inner picture-reader, we must then ask what fixes the interpreter's content, and a regress opens. So a depictive vehicle either has no determinate content or requires a homunculus.

(3) If the vehicle is descriptive, then the image is just a structured set of represented facts, and the felt visual character of imagining — that one is inspecting a scene rather than merely judging that such-and-such is the case — becomes an illusion or an inert side-effect. The description does all the cognitive work; the visual experience contributes nothing.

(4) Therefore a mental image cannot be both genuinely visual in the way it does its work and contentful without an endless chain of interpreters. It is either an unexplained inner picture awaiting a homunculus, or an epiphenomenal gloss on propositional thought.

(5) So the ordinary idea of a mental image — a picture-like state that is actually used in thinking — is incoherent, and my conviction that I solve narrative problems by inspecting imagined scenes is either false or self-deceived.

(6) That conclusion is more than I can accept: it says that one of the most reliable instruments of my craft does not exist in the way it presents itself.

I am prepared to hear that my introspective resistance in (6) is doing no rational work. But I want a precise diagnosis: which step in (1)–(5) should be rejected, and why did that step look like common sense? What must each standard position — the depictive view, the descriptive view, and any view that refuses the dichotomy in (1) — pay in order to escape the conclusion? I would rather keep the reality of imagery than keep the dichotomy, but I want to know exactly what that loyalty costs me.
