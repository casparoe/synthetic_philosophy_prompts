I'm teaching Charlotte Perkins Gilman's The Yellow Wallpaper in a unit on the concept of disease, and I want a careful reading of the story through the debate in philosophy of medicine between naturalism and normativism. Naturalism, roughly, holds that whether someone is diseased is a factual question about the failure of biological functioning; normativism holds that the judgment is ineliminably value-laden, because it depends on social judgments about which capacities matter and what a person is supposed to be like. The story gives us a narrator who is confined by her physician-husband for a nervous condition, forbidden to write or work, put on a rest cure, and who seems to descend into psychosis while imprisoned in the room with the yellow wallpaper. Does the story treat her condition as a real dysfunction, as a society's way of pathologizing a woman's rebellion against enforced idleness, or as something that cuts across that distinction? Walk me through the specific scenes where each philosophical position illuminates the story and where each does violence to it. I want a reading that neither reduces the story to an anti-psychiatry tract nor flattens the naturalism/normativism debate into a slogan; in particular, tell me what the story shows about the concept of disease that the philosophical debate, conducted in the abstract, tends to miss.
