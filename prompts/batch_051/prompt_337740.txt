In a seminar on corporate sovereignty, a speaker pressed the following analogy. Early modern chartered companies — the East India Company, the Virginia Company, the Hudson’s Bay Company — began as creatures of royal charters, yet several of them came, in their different settings, to coin money, maintain armed forces, make treaties, hold courts, and govern territory. The modern state, he claimed, is a chartered corporation of the same kind: the constitution is its charter, the citizens are its members or shareholders, officeholders are its directors, taxes are a kind of dues, and sovereignty is a bundle of corporate privileges granted by the surrounding legal order. From this he concluded that the state holds no distinctive kind of authority that a corporation could not in principle hold, so deference to the state needs no different justification than deference to any large chartered body. I want to know how far this analogy actually carries. Lay out the correspondence explicitly; show where the mapping breaks; identify which features of the chartered-company source are being imported into the target without argument; separate the disanalogies that matter for the normative conclusion from those that are merely superficial; and say whether the conclusion survives, needs qualification, or collapses.
