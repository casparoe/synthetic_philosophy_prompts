I'm a PhD student in philosophy and political science, and in two weeks I'm giving a workshop talk at my department's PPE center. The paper is about the unilateralist's curse: a group of N equally competent, well-intentioned agents each get a noisy private signal about whether some action would be net-positive, and any one of them can act unilaterally in a way that imposes costs on everyone else—deorbiting somebody else's satellite, leaking a dataset that might do more harm than good, releasing a pathogen from a lab for study. From any individual's point of view acting sometimes looks justified; but with enough independent decision-makers, it's extremely likely that at least one of them gets a misleadingly strong positive signal, so the group ends up bearing the costs of an act that the group itself would reject if it pooled information and voted. The formal model is simple (common prior, conditionally independent signals, risk-neutral agents, false positives much worse than false negatives), and I show the curse is worst when the prior favors inaction and the signals are only moderately informative. My normative claim is that for this class of "unilateral harms" the group should adopt a shared ex ante norm of inaction unless some public threshold—supermajority, prior review, something like that—is met, even thought that will sometimes block genuinely good unilateral actions. I try to handle the obvious whistleblowing/civil-disobedience objection by restricting the norm to cases where the harm is diffuse and shared rather than aimed at a wrongdoer.

The part I'm unsure about is the Q&A, because the audience isn't the existential-risk/EA crowd who'd push on the maths. The PPE faculty in the room are mostly democratic theorists who care about legitimacy and public reason, two microeconomists who work on mechanism design, and one person who works on Condorcet jury theorems. They're going to treat my model as a toy and care less about whether I've solved weird asteroids-and-datasets cases than about whether a norm like this is stable, legitimate, and not just a status-quo bias dressed up as caution. I can guess the rationalist-flavored objections, but I want a cheat-sheet for the questions these particular people will actually ask: the seven or eight objections I should expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased in the kind of vocabulary a democratic theorist or mechanism designer would use, with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where that reply is thin. And include the one question I should be praying nobody asks—the one that would genuinely embarrass the paper.
