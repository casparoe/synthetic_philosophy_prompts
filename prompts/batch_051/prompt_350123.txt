I'm a high school student writing a paper on what it means to say that one thing caused another, and every definition I try breaks down on at least one example. Could you help me build a working definition of "cause"?

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right:

1. A lit match is dropped onto a patch of dry grass and a fire starts. The match caused the fire — but so, in some sense, did the oxygen in the air, the dryness of the grass, and the fact that no rain was falling. A good definition should explain why we single out the match, and whether we are right to.

2. A rooster crows every morning just before sunrise. The crowing does not cause the sunrise. Why is this different from case 1, even though the crowing reliably precedes the sunrise?

3. Our school's data shows that students who attend the peer tutoring center tend to earn higher GPAs than students who do not. This might mean tutoring raises grades, but it might not. What, specifically, is missing before we can say that attending tutoring causes higher grades?

4. Two separate power surges hit a lab computer at the same instant, and each surge alone would have been enough to destroy the hard drive. Did each surge cause the destruction, did both, or did neither? Whatever definition you give has to handle this.

5. A basketball player wears his lucky socks during every game this season, and his free-throw percentage improves. He insists the socks caused the improvement. He is almost certainly wrong — but why, exactly? What does this tell us about what a cause requires?

6. A teacher switches her quizzes from multiple-choice to short-answer, and the class average rises. But the students also read a new textbook that same year. Did the change in quiz format cause the rise in scores? If we cannot tell, what would settle it?

7. A doctor gives a patient an antibiotic, and the patient recovers. It turns out the infection would have cleared up on its own anyway. Did the antibiotic cause the recovery? Notice that "if the drug had not been given, the patient still would have recovered" pushes us to say no, while "the drug actually killed the bacteria in this patient's body" pushes us to say yes.

8. A family goes on vacation and no one waters the houseplant; the plant dies. Did the omission — the not-watering — cause the death? Or can only events count as causes?

9. The school later runs an experiment in which students are randomly assigned either to attend tutoring or to a study-hall control group, with teachers and staff kept unaware of which students are in which group. The tutored group's grades rise more than the control group's. Here we feel much more confident saying tutoring caused the improvement. What, in the logic of the situation, changed between case 3 and case 9?

Running through all of these, I keep noticing that people mix up three different questions: what tends to occur together (prediction), what happens when we deliberately change one thing (intervention), and what would have happened in this very case if one thing had been different (the alternative). I would like the definition to say which of these questions a causal claim is really answering, and when ordinary language quietly slides between them. My hunch is that "cause" does more than one job, and that the cases above pull those jobs apart.

Please propose a definition, run it through all nine cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.
