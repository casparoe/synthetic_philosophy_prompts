I am drafting a policy statement for a civic-accountability organization on when government secrecy — especially non-disclosure agreements, confidentiality clauses in settlements and contracts, and similar withholding practices — is legitimate and when it violates the public’s right to know. The phrase “the public’s right to know” is used with confidence in public debate, but the principle behind it is not well articulated, and I suspect it covers several different values that can pull apart. I would like a working definition of the contested notion “a matter in which the public has a right to know” — that is, a criterion for when the public at large, not merely an individual complainant, a court, or a specialist oversight body, is entitled to have information disclosed by a government despite a countervailing commitment to confidentiality. The definition should let me classify the following cases and should express a principle rather than simply redescribe the most salient examples.

1. A city pays $2.3 million from its general fund to settle a lawsuit alleging excessive force by a named police officer, and the settlement contains a provision forbidding disclosure of the amount and the underlying allegations. I take it the public plainly has a right to know the amount and the general character of the alleged misconduct.
2. A city publishes a searchable database giving the name, current salary, and pension entitlement of every employee, including senior officials. I take it this falls squarely within the right.
3. The identity of a confidential informant in an ongoing organized-crime investigation, where disclosure would create a credible threat to the informant’s life. I take it this falls squarely outside the right.
4. A mayor’s notes from private psychotherapy, held by the city only because the city administers the municipal health plan. I take it these fall squarely outside the right.
5. A police department licenses a proprietary predictive-policing algorithm from a private vendor; the contract designates the source code and training data as confidential trade secrets, and neither can be inspected by city councillors or journalists.
6. Closed-session minutes of a city council’s deliberation about the maximum price it is willing to offer for a parcel of land, kept confidential while negotiations are ongoing.
7. An internal memorandum from the city solicitor advising that a proposed zoning change is probably vulnerable to legal challenge, withheld on the ground of attorney-client privilege.
8. A senior official privately settles a harassment complaint from a subordinate, paying with the official’s own money; the complainant signs a non-disclosure agreement, and no public funds or public disciplinary process are involved.
9. An employee of a defense ministry leaks to a journalist an unredacted classified internal budget that reveals wasteful procurement but no sources, methods, or operations.
10. A private contractor’s detailed cost breakdown submitted in a tender for municipal garbage collection, marked “commercial-in-confidence”; a former council member alleges that the secrecy concealed that the service could have been obtained much more cheaply.

Please propose a definition, run it against each case, and say explicitly where it delivers the wrong verdict or no verdict at all. If a case forces a revision of the definition, show the revision; if the definition yields a surprising verdict, defend it rather than softening the criterion. Then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and address whether “the public’s right to know” does one job or several — for example, an epistemic job concerning what citizens need to form accurate beliefs about their institutions, a participatory or accountability job concerning what they need to hold officials responsible, and an expressive or dignitary job concerning a refusal to be governed as dependants — and say how those jobs come apart in the hard cases above.
