I've been arguing with a friend about which newly released recordings count as "the same song" as some earlier recording, and I find I can't state the rule I'm relying on. I'd like a working definition of the ordinary listener's concept of being the same song—as distinct from whatever copyright law says, which I want left to one side—tested against a series of cases from contemporary production practice.

The cases are these. (1) A singer records a new performance of a well-known song, keeping the melody, chord changes, and lyrics unchanged. (2) A producer takes only the isolated lead-vocal recording from a hit, shifts its pitch, and places it over an entirely new instrumental; listeners and the producer alike call the result a remix of the original. (3) A hip-hop producer loops a two-bar drum solo lifted from a 1960s soul record and builds an original rap song around it; the new track shares no melody, harmony, or words with the old one, only that looped audio. (4) A band writes an original song that copies another hit's chord progression, tempo, and guitar tone but not its melody or words; listeners say it feels like the hit without being able to point to any quoted phrase. (5) An AI system trained on a singer's catalog generates a wholly new song with a novel melody and novel words but with the singer's unmistakable timbre; on first hearing, people ask which human singer it is. (6) A mashup artist layers the complete vocal from one song over the complete instrumental from a second song, producing something fans treat as a third thing. (7) A songwriter later discovers that a melody they believed they had invented is nearly identical to an obscure record they heard once years before and have no conscious memory of.

I'm confident (1) is the same song and (4) is not; I lean toward counting (2) as the same song; on (3), (5), (6), and (7) I have no stable view. Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and say where it surprises you, where it gives no answer, and where you would revise the definition rather than defend the verdict. I suspect the ordinary concept slips between at least two tasks—tracking the identity of a musical work and tracking the copying of particular recorded sounds—and I'd like to know whether the cases force those tasks apart or whether a single definition handles both.
