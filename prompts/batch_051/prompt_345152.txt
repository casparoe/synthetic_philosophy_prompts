I keep going in circles in my philosophy of history class about the slogan that history is written by the victors. I can see the weak version: whoever wins a war or a sucession struggle has an oversized hand in which documents survive, which monuments stay up, and which caricatures harden into what everyone knows (our textbook uses Octavian's side blackening Mark Antony after Actium, so even our image of him is filtered through his enemy's propaganda machine). But I keep hearing people go from that to something much stronger: that there's no neutral fact of the matter about the past at all, just narratives ordered by power, and that objectivity is itself just the self-presentation of a dominant perspective.

What confuses me is that the further step can feel almost inevitable. If the archive is itself a product of power, and any corrective narrative we now write is also produced under some configuration of power, then how isn't every supposedly better history just another winner's story, with the current historians playing the victors over their predecessors? The standard answer I get is just that I'm conflating historiography with history, but no one spells out what that distinction can still do once we grant that our only access to the past goes through the very record that is tainted.

I'd like a precise diagnosis of where the relativist conclusion stops being supported by the premises—not just the fact that it has bad consequences or sounds like a cop-out, but the specific step that breaks.
