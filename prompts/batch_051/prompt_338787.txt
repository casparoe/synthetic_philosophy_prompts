You're a philosopher of social science who also knows how to write operational guidance for civil servants; think carefully before you answer. I run the legacy-systems review desk at a state infrastructure authority. Every few weeks another department sends us a proposal that leans on a path-dependence-flavoured claim: either 'this standard/route/system is locked in, so stop trying to change it' or 'this thing is only here by historical accident, so we should switch.' My analysts get about two days per case before a committee decides whether to pay for a full switching-cost study or drop the idea. I need a reusable decision procedure the analysts can run on each incoming proposal, not a one-off judgement.

Write it as a set of questions asked in order. For each question, say what counts as a good answer, where the procedure stops with 'no, not actually a path-dependence claim,' where it concludes 'weak path dependence' versus 'strong path dependence' (the outcome is inferior and still persisted), and where it says 'stop: this is now a values decision, not a factual one.' Ground it in the actual theory—increasing returns, network effects, switching costs, critical junctures, contingency versus inevitability, and the idea that a process can be non-ergodic (no single long-run attractor to return to after a shock)—but translate the jargon so a policy analyst without that background can use it. Make it seperate four claims people blur: (a) the past matters causally; (b) the outcome is sensitive to early events; (c) an inferior outcome got locked in; (d) a decent-but-contingent outcome is expensive to leave. Also make it catch the move where someone uses 'path dependence' to dress up 'we dont want to pay the switching costs' as if it were a discovery about history.

Then run the procedure on three made-up cases; fill in plausible details where you need them. Case 1: a proposal to replace a widely adopted but technically mediocre document format the agency has used since the 1990s. Case 2: a claim that a legacy rail alignment through a city is locked in because the surrounding land use has grown up around it. Case 3: a proposal to reverse a small, unplanned pilot program that happened to work, on the grounds that leaving it in place now would be path-dependent. Make at least one of the three come out in a way the original proposer would hate, so we can see the procedure actually bite. At the end, tell me what the procedure cannot establish—especially whether the counterfactual 'the same outcome would have arrived anyway' is directly knowable, and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as method. Before you commit to the final procedure, brainstorm the distinctions that matter and the places a well-meaning analyst is most likely to misapply it; then give the clean final version.
