People invoke the no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning in discussions of induction to support two nearly opposite conclusions: that no learning method can succeed without substantive prior assumptions, and that all learning methods are equally good in expectation, so inductive preferences are arbitrary. I'd like a careful conceptual explanation of what the theorem actually shows for the problem of induction, aimed at a reader who can follow a nontechnical argument. In particular, (1) give the core symmetry or counting intuition behind the result; (2) explain why uniform averaging over all possible target functions is doing most of the work and how a nonuniform prior changes the conclusion; (3) assess whether the theorem is best read as a formalization of Humean skepticism, as a proof that induction is impossible only in the absence of assumptions, or as something more limited; and (4) state precisely in what sense it is true, and in what sense it is misleading, to say that no induction method is better than any other.
