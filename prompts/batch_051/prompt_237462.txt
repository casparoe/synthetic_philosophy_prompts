Construct a thought experiment in which a decision-maker who has formed a competent outside view — an appropriate reference class and accurately estimated base rates — would nevertheless do better to set that view aside and act on the inside view, that is, on the specific causal story of the case. The case must be realistic (for example, a workplace decision, a clinical decision, or a project), no more than one paragraph, and it must not make the outside view statistically incompetent or misapplied. The base rates should be accurate for the reference class, and the reference class should be one a sensible observer would choose before seeing the case-specific details. The case must not cheat by hiding facts from one perspective: both views should evaluate the same situation, and the disagreement should be about how much weight to give distributional evidence as against mechanistic evidence, not about access to evidence. The case should generate its verdicts from the two views rather than stipulating them: the outside view's recommendation must be what a competent user of base rates would actually infer, not a strawman. The purpose of the case is to test whether epistemic modesty — the standing presumption against letting a case-specific story override base rates — can be rationally overridden without impugning the outside view itself. After presenting the scenario, walk through what the outside view and the inside view each recommend, and explain why the inside view is correct here without making the outside view foolish. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work, say what the outside view would correctly conclude if that feature were removed, and explain how a committed defender of the outside view would attack the case, for example by redescribing the reference class or by arguing that the causal story is a rationalization.
