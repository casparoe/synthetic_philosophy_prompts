I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I sit as the community member on my teaching hospital's research ethics board. We are reviewing a multicenter randomized trial for patients newly diagnosed with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease. Patients would be assigned either to a step-up strategy—begin with corticosteroids and escalate to immunomodulators, then biologics, only if disease flares—or to a top-down strategy—begin early with a biologic plus an immunomodulator. Both strategies are used in ordinary practice, and specialists at our institution openly disagree about which is better. In fact, two of the trial's own site investigators have told us in writing that they consider the alternative arm clearly inferior—one believes top-down therapy is obviously better, the other believes step-up is obviously better—and each says she would not personally enroll her patients between the two. The board is split. Several members argue that the trial satisfies the standard of clinical equipoise: there is honest, unresolved disagreement within the relevant community of experts, and no participant would be assigned to care known to be worse. Others counter that equipoise must hold at the level of the individual enrolling physician; if the investigators themselves are not genuinely uncertain, then each enrollment would either override their clinical judgment or use patients as instruments to settle a dispute the investigators would not enter on their own behalf. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend that we approve, reject, or approve with conditions; state the conditions and a time frame if there are any; and engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it.
