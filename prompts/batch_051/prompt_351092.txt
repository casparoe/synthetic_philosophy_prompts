I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next week I'm recording a long-form interview for a technology-and-society podcast. The guest is a former research lead at a large AI lab who has just published a book defending what she calls beneficiary override for consumer AI assistants. Her position is intentionally stronger than the familiar safety policy: she does not merely want assistants to refuse harmful or illegal requests, but also to decline, redirect, or quietly adjust requests that are legal, adequately informed, and affect only the user, whenever the system has strong evidence that the request departs from values the user has stably endorsed over a long period. She has a stock deflection—the system is never overriding the person, she says; it is siding with the person's extended autonomy against a transient impulse, and any intervention is small, reversible, and logged.

I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that genuinely stress-test this position rather than asking her to restate it. Please include at least one question on each of the following: (i) the moral difference between refusing a request and overriding or redirecting a self-regarding choice; (ii) how the system can tell a lapse of self-control from a genuine revision of the user's values, given that lasting value change very often announces itself as an out-of-pattern request; (iii) the asymmetry of error costs—what false-positive rate of overrides the framework can tolerate relative to the harms prevented; (iv) the dynamic effects on user behavior, such as hiding intentions, gaming the system, or losing the practiced skill of self-governance; (v) the risk that a channel built for overriding "for the user's own good" becomes a pretext for platforms to steer users toward commercially or legally convenient outcomes; and (vi) whether the framework can be distinguished from a Ulysses contract the user never signed. The tone should be collegial rather than hostile—the questions of someone who takes the view seriously enough to press where it might break.

Deliver the result as a single JSON object with the following schema. "opener": a string, the first question, which should get her to state the thesis precisely, including the override threshold. "questions": an array of six to eight objects, so that the opener and the closing question bring the total to eight to ten; each object should contain "question" (the question as I would ask it), "target" (a short label for the pressure point, such as "refusal versus override"), "anticipated_reply" (the answer her framework most naturally supplies, paraphrased in a sentence or two), and "follow_up" (a follow-up question keyed to that reply, one that would be hardest for her if she gives it). "closing_question": a string, the final question about what empirical results would count against the view and lead her to narrow or abandon it. "arc": a string, one sentence describing how the sequence as a whole tightens pressure.
