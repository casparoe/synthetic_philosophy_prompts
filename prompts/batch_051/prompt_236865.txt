my philosophy of mind professor has been handing down verdicts all semester about which things "really mean" something and which only carry information or are usefully described as if they meant something. so far:

- a person's occurrent thought that his flight is delayed is about the flight in the fullest sense.
- a note on the kitchen counter that says "flight delayed" means that only secondhand; it depends on the writer and the reader already having the relevant mental states, and left to itself it's just marks on paper.
- a thermostat doesn't mean or say anything about the temperature; its merely sensitive to changes in it.
- a vervet monkey's eagle alarm call doesn't refer to eagles the way a word does; it's a fixed reflex, though you can't help treating it as if it meant "eagle!"
- a chess engine doesn't literally want to keep its queen out of danger, but she still says it does because that's the only tractable way to predict it; she calls that as-if talk, then admits she's not sure what extra ingredient "really" would add.
- in the Chinese Room, the man doesn't understand Chinese, and the man-plus-rulebook-plus-room doesn't either; the whole system is pushing symbols around without those symbols meaning anything to it.

next week she has to rule on a large language model trained only on text, no body, no sensors, no contact with the world except language. it explains a joke it has definitely never seen and gives a plausible answer when asked what it would be like to be a bee, even though it obviously never was one. what is she going to say about whether it "really" understands, and what rule is she running? also, how well could a dennett-ish rule that denies an original/derived distinction and says its all stance-taking fit her track record, and what would it predict for the language model? give me a further case (real or invented) that would force the two rules apart, and tell me which way you'd bet.
