I am a retired chemist who has, in the last few years, started reading philosophy of mind and philosophy of science seriously. I have developed a standing epistemic policy in how I approach this literature, and I would like you to evaluate it as a policy, not merely as a philosophical thesis. Please respond as an expert in philosophy of science and epistemology; take your time, and begin by brainstorming two or three different ways of reconstructing my policy before you assess the strongest version of it.

My policy is this. When I assess a philosophical argument about what exists or about the mind, I count something as physical only if it belongs to the ontology of our current best fundamental physics, or if it is straightforwardly composed of, or wholly realized by, things that do. More importantly for my daily reading: when an argument asks me to take something seriously as physical on the strength of what a future physics might say, or when it relies on a notion that cannot be paraphrased even in principle in the language of current physics, I discount that argument almost entirely and decline to use it as a premise. The rule is not merely verbal. Last month I set aside a panpsychist paper because its central move was that a completed physics might count experiential properties as fundamental in roughly the way it now counts charge. In a discussion group, I also rejected the claim that natural selection is a physical cause operating on populations, on the grounds that the claim stretched the phrase physical cause beyond anything current physics licenses.

As I have gone deeper into the literature, I have begun to suspect that my policy simply chooses one horn of Hempel's dilemma. On my understanding: if physical means whatever current physics says there is, then physicalism is probably false, since physics has repeatedly replaced its own ontology; but if physical means whatever a suitably completed future physics would say there is, then physicalism threatens to become empty or trivially true, since it is no longer clear what it rules out. My policy appears to embrace the first horn and then use the resulting narrow definition as a filter for what I will even engage with.

My question is not whether physicalism is true. I want to know whether this policy is defensible for someone in my position: a retired scientist with limited time, no laboratory of my own, and no independent way to settle these disputes. Is the policy a reasonable division of cognitive labor, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? When is a filter like this reliable, and when does it fail? Can it be restated, perhaps as a principle about burden of proof or about what counts as an empty promissory note, so that it keeps the practical discipline without smuggling in a definition of physical that all but guarantees my conclusions? Finally, how would I tell from the inside whether I am following the repaired policy honestly rather than using it to protect beliefs I already hold?
