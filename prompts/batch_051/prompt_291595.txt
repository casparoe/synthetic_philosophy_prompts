You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in consciousness, and you are comfortable drawing sharp contrasts between theories of what makes a mental state conscious. I am a neurology resident. Recently I sat in on a family meeting about a patient who was emerging from sedation and intermittently followed simple commands and squeezed a hand; the senior clinician and I disagreed about whether the patient was conscious of anything during those moments, and the disagreement turned out to track a controversy among higher-order theories. I want to use this in a teaching session for other residents, and for that I need a case built to order rather than one drawn from a textbook. Please think carefully and construct it to the following specifications. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios, then explain why you reject all but one; do not simply assert the verdicts.

Set-up. Use two versions of the higher-order approach, defined as follows so that we share vocabulary. Actualist HOT theory: a first-order mental state (a perception, a bodily sensation, and so on) is conscious at a time t only if, at t, the person also tokens an actual higher-order state — roughly, a non-inferential awareness or thought that they are in that first-order state. Dispositionalist HOT theory: a first-order state is conscious at t just in case, at t, it is available to (or disposed to produce) the right kind of higher-order representation; no higher-order state need actually be tokened at t. Assume throughout that both theories use 'conscious' for phenomenal consciousness — there is something it is like for the subject — not merely outward responsiveness.

Specifications. Construct a single ordinary-life scenario of at most one paragraph, set in a hospital or at home, involving an adult whose attention is realistically fluctuating, for example while emerging from anesthesia, after extreme sleep deprivation, or during a brief absence episode. The scenario should contain a first-order state X, a perception or bodily sensation, that at a decisive moment guides the person's behavior or would be available for report, but for which no actual higher-order state is tokened; the absence of the higher-order state should fall out of the situation rather than being stipulated in the theory's own vocabulary. No exotic neurosurgery, no science-fictional devices, and no perfect introspective access.

Then deliver the following in order. (1) State what X is and what its contents are. (2) Show that actualist HOT gives the verdict 'not conscious of X at that moment,' identifying the missing item. (3) Show that dispositionalist HOT gives the verdict 'conscious of X at that moment,' identifying what the availability consists in and what behavioral or report-based evidence it points to. (4) Add one control case from ordinary waking perception in which the two theories agree, to rule out the worry that they are merely using 'conscious' differently. (5) Name the single structural feature that is doing the work, and say briefly what the case becomes if that feature is removed. (6) Finally, explain how a committed actualist would most credibly resist the case, for instance by denying that the case exhibits phenomenal consciousness rather than mere access, or by insisting that a fleeting higher-order state must have occurred below the subject's notice, and state the minimal change to the scenario that would make the two theories converge again.

Two framing constraints. First, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists; do not present the actualist or the dispositionalist verdict, or the assumption that these are the only live higher-order options, as the field's consensus. Second, since I will show this to other residents, flag any place where the case leans on a contested reading of 'available to higher-order representation' rather than on a neutral description of the episode.
