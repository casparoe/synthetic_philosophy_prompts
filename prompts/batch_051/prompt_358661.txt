I'm in a reading group on Malthus and the history of population panics, and we've stalled in a way that's starting to feel structural. Everyone agrees on the record: there have been famines and local collapses, but the global catastrophe Malthus pointed toward hasn't happened on anything like the schedule he seemed to have in mind. The Malthusians in the group say the theory wasn't wrong, just early—population can grow geometrically while the food supply at best grows arithmetically, and the check has simply been postponed by fossil fuels, synthetic fertilizer, and a couple centuries of drawing down resources. The critics say that's exactly the move you'd make if the theory were never going to be allowed to die: each failure gets a fresh ceteris paribus clause or a longer timeline, and the prediction ends up unfalsifiable. My suspicion is that we're not disagreeing about facts at all but about what a long-run prediction has to do to count as falsified: one side is defending a tendency that holds under ideal or unchanged conditions, and the other is attacking a concrete forecast about actual history. Diagnose the disagreement. Is 'right but early' ever a legitimate epistemic status for a theory, or is it just face-saving when nobody has specified in advance what would kill it? And what would actually count as progress here—a pre-registered deadline, a formal result, a distinction we're missing, or something else?
