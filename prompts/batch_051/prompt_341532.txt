I've been turning over this Lucas–Penrose style argument and I can't figure out if its airtight. I half-suspect there's a trick somewhere but I can't pin down where. Here it is, numbered:

(1) Suppose for reductio that my mind is a mechanism: there is some finitely specifiable formal system F such that exactly the mathematical truths I can establish are the theorems of F.

(2) F is consistent and strong enough to prove basic arithmetic, since my own mathematical reasoning is consistent and covers arithmetic.

(3) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic has a sentence G_F — one that says of itself that it is not provable in F — and this sentence is true but unprovable in F.

(4) I can reflect on F, identify G_F as F's Gödel sentence, and see that G_F must be true: I only need to verify that F is consistent, which I can do by the same kind of mathematical reflection I use when I prove the consistency of other systems from outside.

(5) So there is a mathematical truth I can see that F cannot prove.

(6) But by (1), every mathematical truth I can see is a theorem of F. Contradiction.

(7) So my mind is not a mechanism.

I lean toward thinking the conclusion follows, but I can't shake the feeling that something's off. Am I right that it's valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, on what reading of that step, and whether there's a repaired version of the argument that would still refute mechanism.
