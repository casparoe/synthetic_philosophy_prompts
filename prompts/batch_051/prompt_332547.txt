After a long argument with a friend about Maxwell's demon, I kept coming back to an asymmetry in how physicists describe two processes that can be physically identical on a given run. I want you to work out whether that asymmetry has a defensible physical basis.

Consider a box of volume V at temperature T containing a single molecule treated as a one-particle ideal gas. Contrast two protocols.

Blind. A mechanism with no information about the molecule inserts a partition at the midpoint, then moves the partition a small fixed distance in a direction selected in advance and independently of the molecule. When the direction happens to let the molecule's side expand, the gas does work on the partition and the mechanism gains; when it happens to compress the molecule's side, the mechanism pays. Over many trials the gains and losses cancel in expectation, and the successful trials are treated as statistical fluctuations rather than as threats to the second law.

Measured. The same mechanism first determines which half of the box the molecule occupies and stores the answer as one bit, then moves the partition in whichever direction makes that side expand on every trial. Every trial gains work. In the standard discussion this is treated as a second-law problem until the information-processing overhead is counted: erasing the bit to reset the memory must dump heat into the bath, at least kT ln 2 per bit at the canonical limit, raising the bath's entropy by k ln 2 and compensating.

Now choose a successful blind run and a measured run whose action on the gas is identical: same insertion, same displacement, same work gained by the mechanism. The only difference is that the measured run was preceded by a bit correlated with the molecule's location and will be followed by that bit's erasure, while the blind run merely happened to move the right way. One run is called an innocent fluctuation; the other is called a would-be violation of the second law that must be paid for in information. Is there a real physical difference between these two runs—not merely a difference in how we average over trials or where we draw the system boundary—that justifies the asymmetry?

Give me the strongest principle you can find that grounds the asymmetry. Then test it: let the blind mechanism be followed by an observer who inspects each trial and records only the successful ones, so that the compiled record shows consecutive successes indistinguishable from the measured protocol's record. Does your principle still separate the two protocols, and if so, what precise physical feature is doing the work? End with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
