I teach philosophy of mind and I'm embarassed to say illusionism has crept up on me. The case for it isn't one argument, it's the whole shape of the view: it keeps everything physical, it doesn't need consciousness to do any causal work beyond what the brain already does, it relieves me of explaining how a private, qualitative "something extra" fits into nature, and—this is the part that's hard to shake—it actually predicts that I would swear there's a private, qualitative something extra. Of course the system reports qualia; that's exactly what an introspective illusion would produce. The moment a theory predicts the very evidence that seems to refute it, I start wondering whose side the evidence is on.

But then I clipped my finger in a car door yesterday, and the hurt was not a report, not a disposition, not a functional role I was misdescribing. It was a loud, specific, miningless there-that-hurts. I don't know how to tell you how certain I am of it except that I'm more certain of that throb than I am of physicalism, more certain than I am of the argument from parsimony. If illusionism asks me to treat that as a trick I play on myself, I want to know what kind of trick has the trick doing the hurting.

So don't tell me which side wins. Walk me through what it would actually take to either (a) debunk the throb—to show my certainty is the kind of certainty the illusionist has already predicted and thereby undermined—or (b) amend or abandon the view—what would a version of illusionism that "keeps the pain but drops only the philosophical gloss" look like, and does it still deserve the name illusionism, or is that just physicalism with better marketing. If there's a third reading where both survive, spell out exactly what the surviving intuition asserts, and what it costs the theory. American English please, and no citations—I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
