You are a historian of philosophy with a specialty in Kierkegaard's social criticism and a working knowledge of contemporary media theory. I have been arguing with a colleague about whether Kierkegaard's critique of the modern press and "the public" still has force. As I understand it, the original argument ran roughly like this: the daily newspapers of his time made possible a new kind of anonymous collective — the public — in which individuals comment on everything and commit to nothing; because no identifiable person acts or is answerable, the public flattens all qualitative distinctions (the "leveling" he describes) and dissolves the passionate, responsible "single individual" that ethical and religious existence requires. The newspaper was the enabling mechanism: a small number of anonymous writers addressing an anonymous mass. My colleague says the argument is obsolete, because the mass-circulation newspaper it targeted no longer structures public life: today we have social media, where speakers are often identifiable, audiences are fragmented into algorithmically curated feeds, and there is no single "public" at all. I suspect the diagnosis has migrated rather than died, but I cannot pin down exactly what survives.

Where does the original argument actually get its force — which premise does the technology of the daily newspaper do the real work in, and which premises are independent of that technology? Walk through what changes when the enabling mechanism becomes an algorithmic feed rather than a mass newspaper: partial anonymity, personal branding, viral outrage, engagement-maximizing curation, fragmented micro-publics. Does Kierkegaard's argument still go through, does it need repair (and in that case, give the repaired version precisely), or is it dead? Be explicit about which parts of the repair are still recognizably Kierkegaardian and which parts we would have to abandon. Where you think his critique actually predicts the new media environment better than it predicted his own, say so.
