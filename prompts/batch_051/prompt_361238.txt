I am attracted to a verificationist account of what it is to understand a proof: to understand a proof is simply to be able to check that each line follows validly from the preceding ones and that the conclusion is the theorem. Its appeal is that it makes understanding objective, teachable, and assessable, without positing a private feeling of insight that no one can inspect. But I have a firm intuition that this leaves something out. I have watched a student verify every step of an induction proof that the sum of the first n positive integers is n(n+1)/2, and reproduce it correctly, while being unable to say why the formula must hold, unable to adapt the reasoning to a nearby problem, and unable to see which hypotheses do any work. I want to say the student has certified the proof without understanding it. Should I treat that intuition as a real datum about the nature of mathematical understanding—evidence of something like a grasp of reasons or dependence relations—or as a fluency-and-confidence effect that the verificationist account correctly tells me to discount? Present each side at its strongest, then give me a negotiated verdict: what should be retained, amended, or abandoned, and what would justify each move.
