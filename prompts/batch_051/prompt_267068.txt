I am a programmer, not a student of philosophy, but I keep returning to a parallel between a distinction I use every day and a distinction I have only read about. In the languages I know, there are two ways two things can be the same: they can literally be one object reached through two references, or they can be two separate objects whose contents happen to match right now. In Python, for instance, two lists can satisfy `a == b` while `a is b` is false; append to one of them and the equality disappears. From what I have read, philosophers draw a similar-looking line between being one and the very same thing and being exactly alike in all respects, sometimes called numerical versus qualitative identity.

I would normally dismiss this as two fields sharing the word "same," but the structures seem to line up a little too well. A mutable object is like a particular that remains itself while its properties change; its fields are its current properties; mutation is how one thing gains and loses qualities over time; and a second, equal object shows that two things can answer the same description at a moment without being one thing. Is this a real identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching?

If it is real, results should transfer in both directions. The test case I keep getting stuck on is aliasing: two names bound to one object, so that a change made through one name is automatically visible through the other. What is the philosophical analogue of aliasing — one entity under two designations — and if there is none, does that indicate the analogy breaks? There is also what looks like a mismatch from the other side. I have read that some philosophers hold that if two things share absolutely every property, they cannot genuinely be two. Yet code makes it routine to create two distinct objects with identical data and no difference except where they live in memory. Is that a real divergence, or does it only show that "every property" must be spelled out to include location and history, in which case a memory address is just another property and the comparison says less than I hoped?
