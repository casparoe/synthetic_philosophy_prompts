I'm drafting a short essay about constitutional monarchy in parliamentary democracies such as the United Kingdom, Denmark, Sweden, and Japan, and I would welcome comments on the argument before I expand it. The working thesis is that the standard defence of constitutional monarchy, which says a hereditary monarch is a good neutral head of state because he or she stands above politics, gets the causal story backwards. My claim is that what makes a monarch a safer holder of reserve powers than an elected president is precisely the monarch's democratic illegitimacy. Because a monarch has no personal mandate and no party behind him or her, any attempt to use reserve powers such as refusing assent, dismissing a government, or dissolving a legislature for political ends would immediately look like unaccountable overreach and would put the monarchy itself at risk. That threat of self-destruction is what keeps the powers dormant, and their dormancy is what makes them credible as a neutral backstop in a genuine constitutional crisis. An elected president holds similar reserve powers but also has a popular mandate and political allies, which supplies both an incentive and a perceived authority to use those powers in a partisan direction. The monarchy's weakness is therefore the source of its usefulness. I then want to extend this into a broader claim about constitutional design: the safest home for a rarely used but decisive power is an actor who would pay an intolerable price for deploying it outside its intended trigger conditions. I am aware of some obvious objections, for instance that constitutions and conventions rather than formal powers do the real constraining work, and that several elected presidencies are far more constrained than my argument assumes. I would be grateful for comments on which parts of the argument are least convincing, which comparisons or counterexamples would most strengthen or weaken it, and whether the final generalization adds anything beyond restating the thesis in more abstract language. Please write your comments as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email, and avoid headings, bullet points, bold text, and tables.
