I sit on the unusual-recovery review committee at a teaching hospital. A patient with biopsy-confirmed metastatic pancreatic cancer, who had declined oncologic treatment and been discharged to hospice, returned ten months later with no detectable disease. Her oncologist, two radiologists, and the hospice nurse have each signed affidavits that the before-and-after scans are genuine and from the same patient, and none has a detectable stake in the matter. The family asks the hospital for a letter stating that the recovery is "medically inexplicable and, in our view, miraculous." Committee members are divided. One side holds that this is precisely the sort of uniform, multiple, disinterested testimony that ought to move a reasonable person, and that refusing the requested letter would simply presuppose that miracles cannot happen. The other side argues that no testimony, however credible, can rationally establish a miraculous recovery unless it would be more improbable for the testimony itself to be false or mistaken than for such a recovery to occur; on that standard, the hospital should issue only a neutral statement of "spontaneous remission of unknown cause." Draft the committee's decision memo—either issue the requested letter, issue the neutral statement, or approve some intermediate wording—with a clear recommendation and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side.
