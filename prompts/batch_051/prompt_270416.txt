I've been spending a lot of time with Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and the two definitions of cause in section seven, part two, are really giving me trouble. If I have this right, the first definition says roughly that a cause is an object followed by another, such that all objects resembling the first are followed by objects resembling the second. Then he gives a second definition, something like: a cause is an object followed by another, where the appearance of the first immediately conveys the thought to the second. Taken literally, these look like they're about two different things. The first is about regularities among objects in the world, while the second makes the causal relation depend on what happens in the mind of an observer. So one seems to locate causation in the world and the other in the head, which on the face of it is a genuine conflict, not just a difference of emphasis.

The thing that makes it worse is that in section seven part one he also says we never discover any power or necessary connexion in the objects themselves; we only find that one event actually follows another. That sounds like the anti-realist, regularity-only reading. But people in my seminar keep bringing up the so-called New Hume reading, where Hume isn't denying real necessary connexions in nature at all; he's just saying we cant know them, and all those references to secret powers and ultimate springs in objects are supposed to show he's a realist about hidden powers. I dont quite see how to square that with the second definition, because if causation is partly defined through the mind's transition, why would we posit unknown powers in objects at all? Are these two definitions genuinely compatible, and if so, what is the second one doing? Or is one of them doing the real philosophical work while the other is something else? I'd rather you take a definite position on the best reading, rather than just laying out the options, and tell me what the position costs.
