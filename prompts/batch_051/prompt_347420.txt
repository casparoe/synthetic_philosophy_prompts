I have a policy I follow when I argue about moral and political questions: when a general principle conflicts with my intuition about a concrete case, I treat the case intuition as having default authority. I require the principle to give me a positive explanation of why the case intuition is misguided; I do not require the case intuition to do the same to the principle. My rationale is that I can hold a concrete case in view all at once, while a principle always brings along tacit commitments I have not yet inspected, and clever people can build a principle to reach almost any conclusion anyway. Recently I did exactly this: a friend defended a broadly attractive principle that clashed with my judgment about a concrete family case, and I kept the case judgment and dropped the principle. But I have noticed the cost: my policy makes me systematically hostile to simple, parsimonious theories, and to theories that impose transitivity on judgments I would otherwise make case by case, because those theories earn their elegance precisely by demanding that I revise some clear-seeming case intuition somewhere. Is the policy defensible, or is it just a bias toward the vivid and familiar dressed up as methodology? Is there a revised version that keeps the advantages without the built-in hostility to simplicity? Give a committed verdict: say whether you would keep, drop, or replace the policy, and justify that choice.
