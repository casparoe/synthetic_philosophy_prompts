I'm working on a column about why people who start out with very different beliefs sometimes move toward each other after seeing the same evidence, and other times don't budge, and I want the formal story straight before I wave my hands at politics. The standard upbeat result, as I understand it, is that two Bayesians watching flips of a coin, both with a prior over every possible bias in [0,1] (different shapes, one centered near 0.3 and the other near 0.7), will end up with posteriors piled around the true bias after a long shared sequence of flips; their next-flip forecasts converge and the priors wash out. But a friend of mine think that's unrealistically clean, because actual reasoners only ever entertain a coarse menu of hypotheses, not a continuum. So here's the variation I want to stress-test the theorem with. Alice and Bob each work with only three possible biases. Alice's hypohteses are {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}; Bob's are {0.4, 0.6, 0.8}. Each puts positive prior weight on all three of their own hypotheses, though the weights differ. The actual coin has bias 0.55, which is on neither menu. They both observe the same 10,000 flips, treating them as i.i.d. and updating by Bayes in the usual way. What should I expect to happen to each person's posterior weights and to their next-flip predictions as the data pile up? Does the original washing-out story survive in any form—for instance, the prior inside a given menu becomes irrelevant even though Alice and Bob never come to agree with each other or with the truth? Work through what each of them converges to (a KL-divergence argument between the true bias and each menu hypothesis seems like the right tool here), and then isolate the exact feature of the continuum setup that was doing the work—truth in the support, full support, or something else. Finally, tell me what happens if I gradually refine the menus toward a fine grid, say all multiples of 0.01: do the two forecasters then converge to each other and to the truth, or do the same issues just become smaller?
