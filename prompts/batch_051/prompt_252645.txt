I'm putting together a set of arguments for the claim that genuine moral dilemmas are possible. By a genuine moral dilemma I mean a situation in which an agent is under two moral requirements that cannot both be satisfied, where the unchosen requirement is not canceled, overridden, or reduced to something merely apparent: no matter what she does, she does something wrong.

The arguments I already have are these. First, the argument from moral remainder: an agent who chooses correctly between two terrible options is still appropriately subject to regret, guilt, or a felt need to make amends, and the best explanation of that residue is that a real requirement went unsatisfied. Second, the argument from role-grounded obligations: a person can occupy two roles, each with its own source of moral requirement, and nothing guarantees that the requirements will resolve into a single ranking; both remain binding. Third, the argument from incommensurable values: if honesty and compassion, or loyalty and justice, cannot be measured on a common scale, then neither requirement simply outweighs the other, and the conflict stays alive. Fourth, the argument from tragic cases: situations such as Agamemnon's at Aulis seem to leave the agent with no option that is not a wrong, and the persistent sense that a wrong remains even after choosing the least-bad option suggests that both acts were genuinely required.

I am looking for a fifth argument for the possibility of genuine dilemmas that is genuinely different from these four — not a rewording, not a special case, and not a combination of items already on the list. In particular, it should not rest on our retrospective feelings of regret, on the mere fact that we occupy multiple roles, on value incommensurability, or on the bare intuition that tragic choices leave a stain.
