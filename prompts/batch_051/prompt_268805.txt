I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I've spent the last year trying to keep a pair of researchers from yelling past each other about whether religion makes people more moral. One of them, call her the religious-social-capital position, thinks participation in a religious community has a real causal effect on everyday moral behavior, mostly through felt supernatural monitoring, dense moral community, and habit, and that secular replacements only work when they reproduce those features. The other, call him the secular-institutions position, thinks what looks like a religion effect is mostly selection, self-report inflation, and giving aimed at the in-group, and that once you control for those, secular societies build trust and cooperation fine through laws, institutions, and non-religious norms.

I've finally talked them into doing an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they could both sign. Make sure it does the following, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept, including what the evidence shows before anyone starts arguing about causes (survey correlations, lab-game results, cross-national patterns, whatever they can actually agree on). Also force them to fix early what "more moral" means: anonymous fairness toward strangers, everyday honesty, volunteer hours, charitable giving, third-party punishment, or something else, and whether in-group-directed giving counts.

2. A disagreements table with the exact remaining disputes, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own cell. No strawmen.

3. The cruxes, the sub-questions that would actually move at least one side.

4. For each crux, a concrete test, data set, or natural experiment both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written in advance.

End by saying which part of their fight, if any, was only about words. Keep the whole thing under 1,400 words, tight enough that they'd actually put their names to it.
