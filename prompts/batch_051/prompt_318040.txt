I have been reading about presupposition in a philosophy of language course, and a linguist friend of mine treats one part of the standard story as settled that I keep thinking is less empirical than it looks. The standard observation is easy to state. A sentence about the present king of France being bald carries a presupposition, and the sentence is assertible only against a common ground that already contains the corresponding proposition; otherwise the utterance is defective in something like the way a move in a game is defective when its precondition is not met. Yet ordinary conversation is full of informative presuppositions: a speaker says she has to leave early to collect her sister, the hearer had no idea she had a sister, and the conversation normally proceeds instead of crashing. The theoretical description is that the hearer quietly updates the conversational score, adding the sister to the common ground retroactively, provided the new material is uncontroversial. On this picture the score has a rule of accommodation: if an utterance requires the score to have a certain value and the score lacks it, the score adjusts to make the utterance acceptable, unless someone objects.

My suspicion is that this could just be a graceful redescription of the fact that people often let presupposition failures pass, dressed in the vocabulary of rules and scoreboards, and that the word accommodation names the redescription rather than a mechanism. I want to know whether the claim that hearers actually update the common ground is an empirical claim, and what would be the strongest evidence for or against it. Please design the best concrete test you can: specify the experimental setup in enough detail that it could in principle be run, say exactly what pattern of results would count as evidence that hearers perform something recognizable as accommodation, and what pattern would count against it. Then be honest about the residue. After the best conceivable positive result, which parts of the philosophical claim would still be unsettled — for example, whether the updated representation deserves to be called common ground, whether the rule is genuinely retrospective, or whether the scoreboard is anything more than a convenient summary of individual beliefs and their revision?
