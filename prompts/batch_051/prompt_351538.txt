An article in a popular philosophy magazine summarized the situationist critique of virtue ethics as follows. Virtue ethics, it said, rests on an empirical assumption: that people possess robust character traits—honesty, courage, compassion—which produce consistent behavior across different kinds of situations. A series of classic experiments in social psychology supposedly undermines that assumption. Seminary students who were in a hurry failed to stop and help a man slumped in a doorway; ordinary people administering what they believed to be severe shocks continued to obey an experimenter's instructions. The article concludes that behavior is controlled overwhelmingly by situational pressures—haste, authority, even trivial mood-lifting coincidences—rather than by stable traits, that the evidence for stable character traits is negligible, and that virtue ethics therefore prescribes the cultivation of something psychologically unreal. The real lesson, it ends, is that we should stop appealing to character and instead design situations and institutions that make decent behavior easier. I suspect this overstates things, but I am not sure where the misstep is, and I do not want to dismiss an empirical challenge simply because I like virtue ethics. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate from what is simplified or overstated, say what a careful virtue ethicist would and would not need to concede, and then assess whether the final practical conclusion about institutional design follows from the accurate core of the critique.
