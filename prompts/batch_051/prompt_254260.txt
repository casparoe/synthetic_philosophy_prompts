In my Gov class we’ve been doing this unit on the EU and Brexit, and something keeps bugging me. When the UK left the EU, one of the big arguments was about the customs union: staying in it meant you couldn’t strike your own trade deals, because there’s a common external tariff and the EU negotiates as a bloc. People on the Leave side said this is basically giving up soverignty. But a week later we covered how the UK was signing its own free trade agreements, and those also come with rules your country has to follow — like agreeing to treat certain foreign goods a certain way, or limiting subsidies, or using a dispute process where a panel can find against you. Nobody in class called that giving up sovereignty. They said signing a trade deal is just what a normal sovereign country does — it's even exercising sovereignty, because you're choosing it.

Both look to me like “we voluntarily accepted rules that restrict what we can do later.” So why is one counted as losing sovereignty and the other counted as using it? I can sort of see possible lines: in a customs union the rules are set partly by other countries through a body you get one vote in, whereas in a trade deal the rules are more reciprocal and fixed at signing. But some trade deals are super deep and hard to exit too. I want the strongest actual distinction you can find, tested against the case where a country signs a very deep trade agreement that it can only leave at huge cost. Is the asymmetry justified, or is it mostly just that one is called a union and the other is called an agreement?
