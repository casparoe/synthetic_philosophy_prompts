So I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene I can't get morally right. A woman has just buried her father and inherited his farm; a neighbor she's known for years offers to buy it. She's barely sleeping, still in a fog, and he's been 'helping' by showing her the accounts and telling her the place is a money pit, that she should sell before the bank takes it—and when she hesitates, he reminds her he's the only one who's stepped up since the funeral. Later we learn he intercepted a letter from the bank offering to restructure the loan so she could keep it. He mostly thinks he's saving her from a burden—he's not a cartoon villain—but he hid the letter. She says yes and signs. My question is about the consent itself: did she actually consent? She said the words and signed her name; what more would a reader be right to want before treating it as consensual, and what would be enough to undo it? I go back and forth on whether the grief, the sleeplessness, the one-sided 'help,' the guilt-trip, or the hidden letter is the thing that does or doesnt break it. I don't want to teach readers that a woman in a rough patch can't make her own choices, but I also don't want to write the whole thing off as a harmless nudge. Where's the line, and which details should carry the moral weight?
