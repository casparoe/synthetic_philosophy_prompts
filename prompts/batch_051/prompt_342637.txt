After my sister lost her husband last year I got into reading Seneca's Moral Letters to Lucilius, and I keep noticing that its not in line with what her bereavement counsellor tells her. In Letter 63, "On Grief for Lost Friends", Seneca writes: "Let not the eyes be dry when we have lost a friend, nor let them overflow. We may weep, but we must not wail." A bit later he even says the sensible thing is to look for someone new to love rather than go on weeping. The counsellor keeps telling my sister not to rush: grief is not an illness you recover from, trying to force yourself back to normal too fast only stores up trouble for later, and staying connected to the person you lost can be healthy. What I want to know is what Seneca would have made of modern grief counselling if he could sit in on one of those sessions. Would he write it off as the self-seeking mourning he complains about earlier in the letter, or would he see it as another route to the same steady state he was after, just a longer one? And underneath that: is freedom from disturbance always the goal, or does the counsellor have a point that making your mind go quiet too soon has a cost Seneca doesnt count? Please answer in British English. I've never studied philosophy, so plain language would be appreciated.
