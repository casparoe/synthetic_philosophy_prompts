In my seminar on rational choice, cooperation, and institutional design, we have spent several weeks working through two bodies of work that I have not yet managed to fit together: accounts of multipolar traps and races to the bottom, on the one hand, and formal results on program equilibrium, on the other. I am trying to write down my own position before drafting the term paper, and I would welcome a consistency audit rather than an exposition of either view. My current commitments are these.

(1) In an anonymous one-shot prisoner's dilemma with no reputation or enforcement mechanism, each player has most reason to defect, and a player who defects is not morally blameworthy; whatever is wrong lies in the structure of the game. (2) In the program-equilibrium version of the same payoff matrix, where each player submits a conditional strategy visible to the other before the moves are executed, I think cooperation is rational: each player can submit a program that cooperates just in case the opponent's program is exactly this program, and defects otherwise. I do not think this requires promises, reputation, or nonstandard preferences; the transparency of the conditional strategies does the work. (3) I accept the core of the Moloch diagnosis of races to the bottom. There are real multipolar traps in which each move is a locally rational best response to the others' moves, yet the resulting equilibrium leaves everyone worse off. I want to say that such an equilibrium can constitute a genuine moral failure, not merely a regrettable outcome. (4) I also hold a form of methodological individualism about moral evaluation: social outcomes must be assessed through the decisions of individuals, and if no individual decision is wrong, then I do not understand what it could mean to call the aggregate outcome wrong. (5) I take it that in a typical race to the bottom we cannot pick out any particular actor's move as the wrong one. The first mover is not worse than the second, since someone would have entered anyway, and the later entrants are only matching what earlier moves made necessary. (6) Finally, I think agents who can see a multipolar trap forming have a defeasible obligation to support credible commitment devices—treaties, standards, charters, unions, transparency infrastructure—precisely because those devices make something like the program-equilibrium strategy available in real life.

Please audit this set of commitments. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which pairs only seem to conflict once the relevant distinctions are made? Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each, say what would have to be revised and what the least costly revision is, and then tell me whether there is a single revision that would do the most to make the whole set hang together, and what that revision would cost.
