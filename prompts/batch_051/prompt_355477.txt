I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general audience, and I keep running into a puzzle about Noether's theorem. The familiar gloss says that symmetries explain conservation laws: energy is conserved because the laws do not care what time it is; linear momentum is conserved because the laws do not care where the experiment happens. Call this the explanation-by-symmetry argument. Two later developments seem to pull against it from different directions, and I want to know whether the argument can survive them.

The first development is the modern treatment of gauge symmetry. The local gauge symmetries at work in the Standard Model are routinely described as redundancies in our mathematical description, not as physical features of the world. If they are bookkeeping rather than physics, it is strange to say they explain charge conservation in the way that indifference to clock-setting explains energy conservation.

The second development is the anomaly. In quantum field theory, a symmetry of the classical action need not survive quantization, and the conservation law associated with it can fail. The move from the dynamics having a symmetry to some quantity being conserved therefore stops being unconditional once the theory is quantized.

There is also a quieter problem. On the standard variational presentation, the mathematics is often described as a two-way map: symmetries of the action produce conserved quantities, and conserved quantities can be paired with symmetries. If either side licenses the other, it becomes unclear that symmetry is doing the explanatory work rather than merely restating the conservation law.

Please reconstruct the explanation-by-symmetry argument in the strongest form you can, with explicit premises and conclusion. Then say what each of the following does to it: (a) gauge symmetries understood as descriptive redundancy, (b) anomalies, and (c) the two-way character of the mathematics. The central question is whether the original argument survives, survives only in a repaired form, or is dead, and whether after any repair it remains correct to say that symmetry explains conservation, rather than conservation explaining symmetry or the two being no more than equivalent.
