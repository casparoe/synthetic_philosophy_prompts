ive been trying to pin down what actually makes a political order legalist and came up with a concept I want checked before I use it in an essay. I call it *incentive closure*. A state has incentive closure when every job the ruler needs done can be enforced just through the rewards and punishments attached to the office itself, so that a purely self-interested official will do the job and will also not do the version of it that enriches himself at the state's expense. My claim is that Han Feizi is best read as demanding incentive closure, not as making a dark claim about human nature. He isn't saying people are greedy in a way Confucians denied; he's saying virtue should never be load-bearing in the design of a state, because a well-built state is one that would work even if everyone in it were selfish. The two handles (punishment and reward) and his insistence that an official's claimed results match actual results look to me like technical machinery for getting closure, and his distrust of ministers follows from it rather than from cynicism.

Honest assessment: is incentive closure a real concept or just Han Feizi restated under a new name? Give me the strongest version of it, the nearest idea I might be reinventing (in Legalism or modern governance talk), and the hardest case for it — an important job where the incentives can't be made right, or a place where even Han Feizi quietly has to lean on virtue or discretion after all. Also tell me if my reading of the two handles as closure-machinery is too cute.
