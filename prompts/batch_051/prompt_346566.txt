Next week I lead a fifty-minute discussion section in an introductory philosophy course on practical rationality. The students are first-years, mostly humanities and science majors, with no economics, game theory, or decision theory behind them. I want to teach them Warren Quinn's self-torturer as a way into the idea that rational preferences might fail transitivity. The case I will put in front of them is this: a person is fitted with a device whose dial runs through a long series of discrete settings; turning it up one notch pays a fixed, substantial sum and adds a pain increment too small to notice on its own; at any given setting the person will advance one notch for the money, but he would refuse to jump directly from the first setting to one of the far settings, where the accumulated pain is severe. The understanding I want them to reach is not that the person is obviously irrational, but that the case forces a considered choice among rejecting transitivity as a requirement of practical rationality, denying that each one-step advance is rational, or accepting that he should take the all-at-once leap, with each option carrying a real cost. Plan how I should teach this, rather than explaining it to me. What has to be in place before the puzzle can bite—an everyday grasp of better-than relations, pairwise choice versus whole-sequence choice, transitivity as an idealization—and in what order should these be introduced? What should the single carrying example be, and what properties must it have and lack so that the usual escapes (a hidden pain threshold, diminishing marginal utility of money, 'just stop at a moderate setting', confusing sequential with one-off choice) can be named and shown not to dissolve the puzzle? Which misconceptions should I expect, in what sequence will they surface, and at which point in the session should each be headed off? What should I openly leave out at this level? Finally, give me one end-of-session question whose answer would tell me whether a student sees the structural problem—that a series of individually rational-looking steps can jointly commit the agent to a state he rejects—rather than merely describing the story.
