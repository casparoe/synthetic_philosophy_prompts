I am a high school student writing a paper for a policy competition on whether my country should adopt Nordic-style fiscal and welfare arrangements: high taxes, broad universal benefits, and heavy reliance on citizens paying and claiming in good faith. The strongest objection I encounter is that the Nordic model cannot be exported because it presupposes a stock of social trust that other countries lack. On this view, people accept high taxes and resist abusing benefits because they trust that most others will do the same; in a low-trust country, importing the same institutions would produce tax evasion, benefit fraud, resentment, and a political backlash long before any trust could accumulate. Now suppose I become convinced that the causal arrow mostly points the other way: social trust is itself generated by universal, rules-based welfare institutions, because being treated impartially by the state and seeing others contribute reliably teaches people to trust strangers. My question is about the argument, not about which policy my country should pick. Does the no-export objection survive this reversal of causation? Does it collapse into a bootstrapping problem that could be managed through sequencing, or does it still carry force through some other premise? Please say which premise the original argument needs, what the reversal does to it, and under what conditions the argument might be repaired.
