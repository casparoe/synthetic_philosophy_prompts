I'm a research assistant on a multi-year study of adolescent mental health, and I've hit something about the design that I can't get out of my head. The study screens teenagers who score just below threshold for a newly proposed diagnosis — the working label is "emotional instability" — and the ones who qualify are told they're "at-risk" and tracked for two years to see whether they convert to the full diagnosis. The trouble is what happens once the label lands. Kids start describing ordinary bad weeks as "episodes." One boy turned down a restaurant job because, in his words, someone with his condition shouldn't be under that kind of stress. One girl said the label finally gave her language for what was already wrong with her, but another participant has so fully reorganized her life around being at-risk that I can't tell anymore whether we're measuring a condition or making one. My supervisor says none of this matters: the category just picks out real traits the kids already had, and we need the label if we're going to study anything at all.

But I keep tripping over an asymmetry: an electron doesn't know what physicists call it, and the act of classifying it doesn't change how it behaves, whereas these kids know exactly what we call them. If a classification loops back and reshapes the people it classifies, in what sense is it a neutral description of them? Is that a reason to stop using the label, or is that just how categories about people work? I'd like to know if my unease is philosophically coherent or if I'm overthinking it.
