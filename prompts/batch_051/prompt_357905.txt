I've been a software engineer for about 15 years, mostly backend stuff, build systems, dependency management, and I dont have any philosophy background. Lately I've been chewing on why I believe what other developers tell me — a maintainer says a release is safe, a stranger on a forum says a library is unmaintained, a coworker says they reviewed a patch. My unprincipled working rule is basically to trust the README and the CI badge unless the dependency is on a critical path, and I know that isnt really a rule.

I ended up reading a bit about the debate philosophers call reductionism vs anti-reductionism about testimony. As I understand it, the reductionists (Hume, I think) say that trusting what someone tells you is only justified because youve built up background evidence from your own perception, memory, and reasoning that people and their reports are generally reliable. The anti-reductionists (Reid?) say testimony is its own basic source of justification, like perception, and youre entitled to trust what people say by default unless you have a specific reason not to. The anti-reductionists also say the reductionist position is somehow circular or impossible, because you cant check how reliable testimony is in general without already relying on testimony, and that reductionism would make kids unjustified in trusting what their parents say, which seems wrong.

Treat "trust what someone tells you" as something like "accept a claim about a package without reading the code yourself." Be as faithful and systematic as you can, and flag places where you have to stretch the analogy.

Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' debate map cleanly onto dependency trust, which parts only map if you distort them, and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary? And more importantly: is the residue a sign that my dependency-trust way of thinking is missing something that matters, or that the philosophical debate is partly an artifact of talking about people instead of packages? I keep going back and forth on whether my case-by-case, critical-path-only rule is just pragmatic risk management or if Im actually an anti-reductionist at heart.
