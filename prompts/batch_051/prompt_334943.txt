I have been reporting on a running argument among computer scientists, cognitive psychologists, and philosophers about whether learning to program changes something deeper than a person's skill set. One claim keeps surfacing in different forms: that a programming language, once it has been internalized, functions as something like a language of thought for the programmer. On this view, the language is not just a notation for instructing a machine or a way of writing down plans one already has; it becomes a representational system that shapes which problems a person can formulate at all, and which distinctions they can notice in the first place. A skeptic at one of the events I covered pushed back by saying this sounds more like a flattering metaphor for programmers than a testable claim, and I have not been able to stop thinking about whether he was right.

What I would like from you is a serious attempt to make the claim empirically tractable. Design the best concrete test you can of the hypothesis that acquiring a programming language changes the structure of someone's thinking, rather than merely giving them a new technique for expressing thoughts they could have had anyway. Be specific about the population you would study, the manipulation or comparison you would use, the outcome measure you would record, and the contrast that would separate the strong claim from weaker ones, such as the claim that programming merely speeds up existing reasoning or gives people a useful new vocabulary for it. Then say exactly what a positive result would establish and what it would still leave open, including questions about whether the underlying cognition is genuinely languagelike, whether the change is a gain rather than a restriction, and whether the effect comes from the formal language itself or from the practices and communities built around it. Finally, identify what no empirical test of this claim could settle even in principle, and why the dispute may still be partly conceptual rather than purely factual.
