I've started using this rule for updating my mind, and I want to know if it's actually defensible or just a way to skip thinking. In my reading group I'm almost always the last person to change my mind. I'll defend my initial take during the meeting, then a couple days later I'll notice I've drifted most of the way to where the group ended up—like I needed to hear myself say the old view out loud once before I could let it go. So now the rule is: as soon as I can predict where I'm going to land, I just go there. If I look at some argument and I can tell, from how similar debates have gone for me or from how I'm already reacting to the first page, that in a week I'll assign it 0.75, I set my current credence to 0.75 immediately. My reasoning is that my future self is still me, and if I could download his credence now, I would; refusing to move until I've "arrived there myself" treats my future self like a stranger, which feels incoherent. I half-remember this is basically van Fraassen's Reflection principle from formal epistemology, defended by some diachronic-Dutch-book thing, but I might be misusing it.

The trouble is my roommate. He says by the exact same rule I should, on the night before a party, adopt the beliefs drunk-me will have about my pool skills, and obviously I shouldn't. He also brought up my grandpa, whose later dementia-stage beliefs were wildly worse than his earlier ones; nobody thinks he should have deferred to those ahead of time. I want to say my rule is only about future-me who has more exposure to the arguments and hasn't been cognitively trashed, but I'm not sure I can draw that line without giving up the principle. Is the policy defensible, and can it be restated so it gives me the speed but doesn't commit me to trusting the guy who's about to play pool after four beers?
