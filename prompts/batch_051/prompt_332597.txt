For a political philosophy reading group, I have been asked to write down my current commitments about democratic institutional design, and I want an audit of whether they hang together before I circulate the list. The commitments are these:

1. I hold that at least one legislative chamber should be replaced by a citizens' assembly chosen by random selection from the citizenry—a lottocratic chamber—because it would better reflect the population and deliberate more honestly than an elected chamber.
2. I think the main thing elections are actually good for is retrospective accountability: allowing voters to punish or remove incumbents who have governed badly, rather than selecting members who are especially wise or representative.
3. I believe a legislature is legitimate only if citizens can identify which decision-makers are responsible for a policy and can sanction those decision-makers at the next election.
4. I regard descriptive representation as important mainly because the range of perspectives in the room determines which problems and options get taken seriously—not because representatives act as agents for particular constituencies.
5. I am convinced that structured deliberation among ordinary citizens works: given adequate time, information, and facilitation, randomly selected citizens converge on evidence-responsive and public-spirited judgments. This makes the competence of individual legislators less urgent than the design of the deliberative setting.
6. I also favor direct referendums for constitutional amendments, on the ground that citizens should retain a participatory veto outside the ordinary legislature.

Please audit the set rather than giving a general overview of sortition. Go through the commitments pairwise and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and state what would have to be given up in each case. Conclude by saying which single revision would restore the most coherence, and at what cost.
