I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and for the past few months I have been sitting in on hearings in a mid-sized city that is weighing a guaranteed-income pilot for people exiting homelessness. Two testimonies in favor of the same proposal, given on the same evening, keep nagging at me. An economist argued that the program should be funded because the downstream savings in emergency departments, jails, and shelters would more than repay the outlay—an argument that quietly mixes empirical projections with a prior commitment to choosing among public programs by measurable welfare gains. A volunteer coordinator at a church-based shelter argued that the program should be funded because the city is answerable to God for how it treats its poorest residents. No one doubted that both speakers were sincere, and no one doubted that both were offering what they took to be reasons rather than mere preferences. Yet the economist's testimony was received as a perfectly proper contribution to public deliberation, while the coordinator was asked, with visible discomfort in the room, whether she could restate the point in terms everyone present could accept. Both speakers were appealing, as a reason, to a comprehensive worldview—an account of what is ultimately valuable and how one should live—that many citizens in the room do not hold. The asymmetry, stated plainly: in official political argument we routinely admit secular comprehensive doctrines, utilitarianism and libertarianism and Kantian theories of dignity and Marxist theories of exploitation, as if they were public currency, while treating religious comprehensive doctrines as private coin that must first be exchanged for secular equivalents. Is anything actually justifying this asymmetry, or have we simply learned to experience a secularist double standard as good civic manners?

I would like you to answer that question directly. First, construct the strongest principled defense you can of treating religious reasons differently from equally contestable secular moral doctrines in official deliberation; give the defense its best form rather than setting up a weak version. Second, test that defense against nearby cases: a self-described secular ideologue whose cost-benefit conclusion is plainly immune to counterevidence, a religious argument that coincides with a widely shared secular value such as human dignity, and a citizen whose only sincere reasons for supporting the measure are religious. Third, give me an honest verdict. Should the asymmetry be kept as it is, kept but with the boundary redrawn, reversed, or abandoned—and what would your recommendation cost the citizens it disappoints?
