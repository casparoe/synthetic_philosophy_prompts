Last night I got into a long arguement with a friend about A.J. Ayer and whether his early verificationism leaves any room for rational belief in life after death. My friend thinks people let Ayer off too easy, so he reconstructed what he called Ayer's real position and sharpened it into the following argument. (1) A sentence is literally meaningful only if there could in principle be observations that raise or lower its probability. (2) The sentence 'we survive death' is put forward as literal. (3) If 'we survive death' means a non-empirical soul continues to exist, then no possible observation could bear on it, so the sentence is meaningless. (4) If it means our same conscious mental life continues in some empirically detectable form, then it is a plain hypothesis about the world. (5) But all our evidence says conscious mental life is dependant on brain function, so that hypothesis is very unlikely. (6) So either the claim is meaningless or it is probably false. (7) So a rational person shouldn't believe in life after death. My friend then added that Ayer himself later had a near-death experience and, while he didn't become religious, he admitted it weakened his conviction that death is the end.

I suspect the argument slides between two senses of 'meaningful' and maybe between two readings of 'survival', but I can't quite make the diagnosis stick. Could you act as a professional philosopher who knows Ayer's work well, think carefully, maybe brainstorm the strongest readings of each premise first, and then tell me whether this argument is valid? If it isn't, which step fails and why? If it is valid, is the conclusion acceptable? Also, does Ayer's later near-death experience undermine his early view, or just his personal confidence? I'd like your answer as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, like an email or letter.
