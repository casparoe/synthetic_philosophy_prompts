I've been chewing on the no-trade theorem and what it does to the idea that markets are good at pooling dispersed information. The theorem, as I understand it, says that if two rational traders share the same prior and are just maximizing expected utility, then private information alone shouldn't make them trade: as soon as one of them is willing to bet, that willingness leaks information, and the other updates away from the deal. On my read that's mainly bad news for the epistemic case for markets. Heavy speculative trading has to come from somewhere else — divergent priors, overconfidence, noise traders, liquidity needs — and if prices are mostly summarizing those, then prices aren't the truth-trackers the market-knows-best crowd says they are. I want you to pick at that position the way a careful defender would. Which assumptions of the theorem could fail in real markets, and wether those failures actually rescue the epistemic story or just move it somewhere else? What observations, results, or arguments would count against my view, and which would be merely awkward? Rank the escape routes for the markets-aggregate-information story from fatal to minor, and be honest about whether my position is even exposed at all.
