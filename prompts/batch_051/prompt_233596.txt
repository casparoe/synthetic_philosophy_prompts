Take three cases. On Sunday a congregation learns that one of its members will undergo serious surgery on Tuesday, and through Monday and Tuesday they pray for a successful outcome. On Wednesday they learn that another member underwent similarly serious surgery on Tuesday, though it had not been announced, and that the outcome will not be known until Thursday; in the interval they do not pray for it. A family, meanwhile, learns that a relative's aeroplane has gone missing over the ocean, and they pray through the night that she will be found alive, even though whatever has befallen her may already lie in the past when they begin.

All three are cases of petitionary prayer to a God who is, on the standard theistic picture, omniscient and immutable. In each case the outcome is therefore already infallibly known and already fixed in the divine plan, so no prayer supplies God with information or moves him from one intention to another. Yet the three cases are treated differently: prayer about the known future event feels appropriate, prayer about a past event whose outcome is not yet known feels pointless, and the temporally ambiguous case feels appropriate unless and until it turns out to have been past-directed all along.

Is there a defensible rationale for this asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency in the practice? Your answer should do three things. First, state precisely what distinction, if any, the practice is tracking—between past and future as such, between our epistemic relation to each, between events whose outcomes are already settled and those that are not, or something else. Second, set out the best candidate justifications, including non-causal accounts of petitionary prayer on which its point is not to alter the world but to align the petitioner's will with God's, to express dependence, or to deepen a relationship, and test them against the temporally ambiguous case. Third, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Keep the answer to at most 1,000 words.
