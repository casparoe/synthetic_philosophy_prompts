I've ended up on a small comission advising a country on whether it should actually try to move up the World Happiness Report rankings, and I keep going in circles. Here's the thing. The headline scores come from a question that asks people to rate their life as a whole on a sort of ladder. But the report's six "supporting factors"—income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and absence of corruption—are described as things that explain the score, not as parts of it. Meanwhile ordinary people mean a mishmash of things by happiness: feeling cheerful day to day, being satisfied with how their life is going, feeling that it's meaningful, or just not being miserable.

I want you to be brutally honest about what the ranking actually is, and then rank the following things by how much a country that decided, for reasons we don't have to litigate, to top the list should weight them in its national policy. Give each a percentage of total effort, so they sum to 100, and one sentence of justification per item.

The things:
- raising self-reported life satisfaction on a ladder-style question;
- raising average positive feelings and lowering negative ones, moment to moment;
- raising the six supporting factors, even if they don't move the headline number directly;
- reducing severe misery at the bottom of the distribution;
- increasing a sense of purpose or meaning in people's lives;
- making the country look better on the report itself (sampling, translation, response rates, methodology quirks), independent of any real change.

And then tell me, on the basis of your percentages, whether the ranking measures happiness, and whether a country should try to top it—not as a throwaway, but so the conclusion actually follows from the weights you just gave.
