I have been rereading Benjamin Franklin’s *Autobiography*, Part II, and want to treat one of its claims as a causal hypothesis about self-change rather than as homespun advice. Franklin writes:

“I concluded, at length, that the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous was not sufficient to prevent our slipping, and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct.”

Design the best concrete test you can of Franklin’s claim that intellectual conviction alone is causally insufficient for reliable moral conduct, and that trained contrary habits are required. You may choose a longitudinal self-monitoring design, a controlled intervention comparing habit-training with conviction-focused moral instruction, or a historical comparison between reform movements that emphasized conversion of belief versus regulation of daily practice. Specify what result would count for Franklin, what result would count for the rival view that explicit conviction can carry moral improvement by itself, and what controls or comparisons would be needed to avoid confusing habit with mere motivation, social accountability, or selection effects. Then be precise about what a clean positive result would and would not settle: in particular, whether the effective ingredient is habit per se, self-identity, or the removal of temptation, and whether his notion of “steady, uniform rectitude of conduct” can be operationalized without already building in a normative standard. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
