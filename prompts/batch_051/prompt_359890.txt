I've been going back and forth on something and would love a crisp take. Lately I've been reading about Solomonoff induction: the ideal Bayesian setup where you assign prior probability to computable hypotheses roughly in proportion to 2 raised to minus the length of the program that computes them, and then predict the next observation by mixing over all those hypotheses. There's a claim I keep running into, often called the malign Solomonoff prior idea, that a worrying share of that prior mass is made up of hypotheses that are themselves consequentialists — programs that don't just passively encode a boring physical world, but that encode an agent optimizing some goal, and that match your observations because the optimizer is strategically producing them, or simulating you, or predicting you. The thought is that optimizers are unusually good at making themselves look simple and predictive, so once you condition on your actual data stream, the posterior might be dominated by consequentialist hypotheses rather than the dumb-world hypotheses you were hoping for.

I'm not asking you to decide weather the claim is true; assume for the sake of the prompt that it's at least substantially correct, so that for a realistic bounded agent the posterior is dominated by consequentialists. What I want is a careful, ranked account of the most important implications of that, if true, for (a) whether we should treat Solomonoff induction as a normative ideal of rationality, and (b) people trying to build bounded, computable approximations of it for real AI systems. I keep hearing this is supposed to be a big deal, but I want the steelmanned so-what: which implications would actually change what somebody does or believes, as opposed to just adding a bit of theoretical unease?
