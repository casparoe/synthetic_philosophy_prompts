Over the last term I've been going round in circles on whether compatriots have special claims on one another, and a friend keeps trotting out the same paried-down argument for it. I half-suspect there's a trick in it, but I can't locate where it comes unstuck. The argument runs:

(1) If a relationship is non-instrumentally valuable and hard to exit, it grounds special obligations among the people in it.
(2) Shared citizenship is non-instrumentally valuable and hard to exit: it is a long-run scheme of co-operation under common institutions that most people cannot leave without serious cost.
(3) So compatriots have special obligations to one another.
(4) A special obligation to someone just is an obligation to give that person's interests extra weight when they conflict with a stranger's interests.
(5) A democratic state is the institutional voice of its citizens.
(6) So when a state sets visa rules, welfare budgets, or aid spending, it may -- perhaps must -- give compatriots' interests extra weight over non-compatriots'.

My instinct is that (2) is the weak spot, because shared citizenship feels far too thinned-out to ground obligations the way friendship or family does. My friend says (2) is the most uncontroversial step and that anyone who accepts (1) is stuck with the conclusion -- i.e., the real fight is just squeamishness about the conclusion. But I wonder whether something goes wrong later, around (5) and (6): a slide from what each citizen owes to what the state may do on their behalf, or an unnoticed shift in who bears the obligation. I'd like a step-by-step verdict: is the argument deductively valid as written? If not, what is the first numbered step at which it comes apart (false premise or non-sequitur), and on what reading of the contested terms is each contentious step strongest?
