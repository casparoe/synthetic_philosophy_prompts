I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like a rigorous dissection of an argument that has been circulating at my workplace about whether virtual items are genuine things. We build online games, and in a design review someone claimed that virtual theft is impossible because a virtual sword is just a bit pattern. The claim was compressed into the following step-by-step argument, which I find plausible at each step but wrong as a whole. I need to know exactly where it breaks.

(1) A virtual item is nothing over and above a bit pattern in a server's state; there is no additional ingredient to the item.
(2) If two servers hold the same bit pattern, then they hold the same virtual item.
(3) Two servers in different physical locations can hold the same bit pattern.
(4) Therefore, one and the same virtual item can be wholly present in two different places at once.
(5) No ordinary single object can be wholly present in two different places at once.
(6) Therefore, a virtual item is not an ordinary single object, and it lacks the determinate identity that ordinary single objects have.

If (6) is right, it seems to follow that a virtual item is not the kind of thing that can itself be owned, stolen, or harmed, and that the loss a player suffers when an item is deleted is only the loss of an arrangement of bits. That conclusion feels too quick, but I cannot articulate the error. I half-suspect that steps (2) and (4) slide between saying that the two servers run the same kind of state and saying that they share one particular item, but I do not trust my own diagnosis.

Please determine whether the argument is valid. If it is not, identify exactly which step is false or which inference depends on a shift in the meaning of a term. Then give the strongest reading of the problematic step on which it is true, and check whether the argument goes through on that reading.
