I'm a journalist writing a piece about what machine learning and evolutionary biology can learn from each other, and I keep hearing the same analogy used to shut that conversation down. An ML person will tell me natural selection is just gradient descent on a fitness landscape: mutations are random steps, selection plays the role of the gradient, fitness is the loss, and evolution is a kind of stochastic hill-climbing with no idea where the peaks are. From there they conclude evolution is a stupid optimizer — it never sees a true gradient, gets stuck on local optima, has no global design plan, and so we shouldn't be too impressed by what it built, and maybe shouldn't bother mining it for algorithmic tricks. A biologist then tells me I have it backwards: evolution runs parallel populations, recombines variants, exploits neutral drift, and has been exploring a huge high-dimensional space for a very long time, none of which standard gradient descent can do.

I want the analogy audited. Give me the correspondence table — what in natural selection is supposed to play the role of the gradient, the loss surface, the learning rate, the batch, the local minimum — and then spell out the places where the mapping breaks. Seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the 'stupid optimizer' conclusion from the ones that don't, and tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing most of the work.
