In my medieval philosophy class we've been working through Abelard's Ethics, and my professor has this slogan he reaches for whenever the discussion gets messy: "the sin is in the consent, not the act." He says it like it just ends the argument. Last week someone brought up a case where you fully decide to leak your roommate's private messages, you're basicly about to hit send, but the roommate moves out the night before so nothing gets leaked. The prof said obviously still a sin, because the sin is in the consent, not the act. Okay. Then in the same class someone mentioned hitting a cyclist with your car door because you didn't look — real damage, but no decision or bad plan at all. The prof just shrugged and said the same thing: the sin is in the consent, not the act, so no sin.

I can see the phrase points at something important, but I'm not sure it says one consistent thing, and I'm especially confused about how much the outward act is supposed to matter after that. Like, is the act adding literally nothing? If I fully consent to something awful but get stopped by luck, is my sin exactly the same size as if I'd done it? And what counts as consent anyway — does dwelling on a revenge fantasy and really enjoying it count, or is that just temptation, or is there a line in between? Could you lay out the different precise claims this slogan could be making, from the boring/trivial ones to the radical ones, and say for each whether it's just a definition, whether Abelard would actually endorse it, and whether it would actually get my professor his conclusions in the two cases?
