Working on a novel set around a local ballot fight over switching city council seats from winner-take-all districts to proportional representation. One of my characters, a retired racehorse owner, keeps leaning hard on the name 'first past the post.' His line, roughly, is that first-past-the-post is just the logic of a race: there's one finish line, the first one across wins, and you don't go back afterward and split the trophy according to how many people betted on each horse. The reformer in the book wants to flatten him, but I don't want either of them to be a strawman, so I need to know how much weight the analogy can actually bear before I write the scene. Give me the mapping first — what's the race, the horses, the finish line, the bettors, the trophy, and what would 'splitting the purse' correspond to — then the places the mapping breaks. In particular: is the bettor/spectator difference fundamental, since the people who back a losing horse in a real race just go home disappointed, while people who back a losing candidate still have to be governed by the winner? And is that the disanalogy that does the most damage, or is the deeper one that a race has a single agreed finish line and an election doesn't? Don't give me a menu of views and walk away — take a definite position on whether the horse-race analogy can support his conclusion that winner-take-all is the only honest way to pick a representative, and if it can't, say what a fairer analogy for his side would be, or whether the whole image is just smuggling in the thing he's trying to prove.
