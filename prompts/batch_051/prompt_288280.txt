I have been having a recurring argument with a friend who works on macroeconomic modeling, and I have realized that the whole dispute rests on an analogy that I have never seen examined carefully. His side says that a society or an economy is like an organism — a body, a beehive, an ant colony — and that the demand that social science always bottom out in the actions of individuals is like demanding that cardiologists derive the heartbeat from the physics of individual cells before they are allowed to talk about circulation. The conclusion he draws is a strong one: not just that macro-level description is useful, but that social wholes such as economies, institutions, languages, and norms support genuinely autonomous explanations, and that the demand for microfoundations is a reductionist fetish. I find the analogy intuitively gripping — I have absorbed enough reductionism-versus-emergence material from blogs and forums to know both the pull and the failure modes of this kind of move — but I think the organism picture may be doing more work than its defenders notice, quietly importing harmony, functional organization, unified purpose, and aggregate-level interests that a society may not have.

I would like you to audit the organism analogy: what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, what the analogy imports that would otherwise need to be argued for independently, and whether the holist conclusion it is used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Open with a one-sentence bottom line, then work through the following sections in order, at roughly the lengths indicated.

(1) The mapping (150–250 words): an explicit table of correspondences — what plays the role of the cells, the organs, the physiology, the adaptive behavior, and the organism itself.

(2) Where the analogy earns its keep (100–200 words): the respects in which society is genuinely organism-like and the modest conclusions those respects can support.

(3) Where it breaks (150–250 words): the disanalogies that matter, including at least individual intentionality, internal conflict, selection, and reproduction.

(4) What it smuggles in (100–200 words): the features of organisms that the picture imports into the social case without independent argument.

(5) Verdict on the conclusion (150–250 words): whether the strong holist conclusion survives, what a weaker defensible version would say, and whether a better analogy exists or the argument should be made without one.
