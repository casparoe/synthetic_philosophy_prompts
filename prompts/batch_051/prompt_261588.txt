For my philosophy of religion course we've been doing the unit on whether spirituality without religion is a real thing or just religion with the awkward bits taken out. I've ended up with a little theory and I want someone to be honest with me about it.

I've been calling it uncashed awe. The claim is that spiritual experience and religious experience run on the same stuff — a felt sense of significance, smallness, connectedness, of being addressed — but religions cash that feeling into claims about what it's a sign of (God, the order of things, karma, whatever). Spirituality without religion keeps the awe and refuses to deposit it. The experience is pointed but never arrives at anything it points to: you get the hush without the addressee. And my strong claim is that the refusal isn't a deficiency or a halfway house. The not-settling is the positive feature, the thing that makes it a distinct kind of life rather than failed religion.

Two examples. I'm an atheist, but the hush in a cathedral still moves me. It has a shape — reverence, being made small, feeling called on — with nobody doing the calling. I don't think that's nostalgia or just liking a nice room. And most mornings I go outside and look at the sky for a minute. It matters, but if you ask what it means, the honest answer is that it doesn't mean anything, and that's kind of the point. It means without meaning. My rough diagnostic is this: an experience counts as uncashed awe if the person having it feels that any definite statement of what it was about would be a loss rather than a gain.

Is this a real distinction or just a metaphor? It sounds close to things like the numinous, the oceanic feeling, or the James line that religion is at bottom about individual experience rather than institutions, so has someone already got this under another name? Tell me what it gets right, then give me three strong objections and a verdict on whether any of them is fatal. Also say where it breaks: what does it wrongly count as spiritual, and what does it misdescribe about the people who actually live this way? And please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested view as the consensus, and tell me where I'm being sloppiest.
