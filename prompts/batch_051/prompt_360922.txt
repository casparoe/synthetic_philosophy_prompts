A public-facing essay I read recently summarizes the theory of legitimacy behind populist politics in roughly the following way. Populism, the essay says, is the view that political authority is legitimate only if it expresses the will of the people, where the people is understood simply as ordinary citizens arrayed against a self-serving elite. On this account, the people’s will just is what a majority of ordinary citizens currently favors, as revealed by elections, referenda, or opinion polls. Populists, therefore, want to replace representative government and its constitutional constraints with direct decision by the majority, typically through plebiscites. The essay then objects that no collection of persons literally has a will—only individuals have wills—so the whole idea of a popular will is incoherent. It concludes that populism is therefore incompatible with constitutional democracy, because it rejects any check on what the majority happens to want.

I find parts of this account persuasive, but I suspect it runs together distinct claims and overstates others. Is the essay’s summary fair? I would like a careful accuracy audit: which elements of the summary would a theorist of populism accept, which are subtle misrepresentations or conflations, and does the essay’s concluding claim about populism’s incompatibility with constitutional democracy survive once those points are corrected?
