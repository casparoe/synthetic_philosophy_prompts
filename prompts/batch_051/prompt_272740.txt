I'm a junior faculty member running a graduate seminar on philosophical methodology, and over the semester I've ended up defending a view I used to think was obviously question-begging: when a general principle conflicts with intuitions about particular cases, the principle should generally win, provided it has the right formal profile — transitivity in its rankings, simplicity (the fewest independent principles, the least ad hoc qualification), and the capacity to generate verdicts across novel cases without case-by-case patching. What moved me is that case intuitions look suspiciously labile: they shift with framing, order of presentation, and which comparisons are made first, whereas the demand that one's commitments not generate cycles or require a standing list of exceptions looks less like an intuition to be weighed and more like a condition on having a coherent view at all. A cycle of all-things-considered judgments, for example, strikes me as near-decisive against a view even when each pairwise judgment in the cycle feels solid, because the pattern itself tells us at least one of the feelings is unreliable; but nothing analogous seems to undermine the transitivity requirement itself.

I'd like you to take this principle-first methodology as the position and tell me what would genuinely count against it — not a survey of options, but the defeaters a committed defender should themselves regard as dangerous, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in the possibility that the formal virtues are themselves just case intuitions laundered into a respectable vocabulary: my conviction that rankings must be transitive may be nothing more than a trained aversion to a certain structure, and my preference for simplicity may be an aesthetic case judgment doing evidential work under another name. I'd also like to know what evidence from cognitive science, or from the actual history of formal methods, would bear on whether training in such methods makes judgments more or less reliable. Please give a definite verdict: does the view survive its strongest counterevidence, does it need to be qualified into something like the claim that formal virtues are evidence only when the case-based pressure against them is unstable under debiasing, or should it be abandoned? I would rather have a wrong but committed answer than a catalogue of things I might think.
