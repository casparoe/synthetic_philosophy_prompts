Analyze the British abolition of the slave trade and then of slavery in the British Empire as a case for Nietzsche's genealogy of morality. Take the standard history as given: a movement that began among Quakers and evangelical Anglicans, was joined by free Black abolitionists, mobilized mass petitioning and sugar boycotts, and eventually overcame fierce resistance from the West Indian planters and their parliamentary allies to abolish the slave trade and then legislate emancipation in the 1830s. I want a disciplined mapping of this episode onto Nietzsche's story of the slave revolt in morality, in which a priestly caste transvalues the values of a noble class so that what the strong call good becomes evil and what they despise becomes the sign of virtue, with ressentiment carrying much of the psychological work. Apply the template to the episode: identify who occupies the structural roles of noble, slave, and priest, and where the fit is strained; state what a transvaluation of values would have looked like in this case and how much of it the historical record actually shows; and say what ressentiment would predict about the abolitionists' motives, rhetoric, and self-understanding, and set that against what the standard account says the movement was actually driven by. Then give an honest account of what the genealogy captures and what it misdescribes or cannot touch, paying particular attention to the gap between Nietzsche's metaphorical slaves and the actual enslaved, and to the fact that the abolitionists' evangelical Christianity is itself one of Nietzsche's central exhibits. End by saying what the episode shows about the theory: is British abolition a confirming instance of the slave revolt in morality, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built to handle? Where the theory would have to be stretched or kept silent, say so explicitly rather than forcing it.
