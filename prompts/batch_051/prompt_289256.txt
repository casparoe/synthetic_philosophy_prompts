I am a PhD student working on the ontology of social groups, and a line of argument I developed for a chapter on generational categories — Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, Generation Z — now seems to prove too much. I would like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong, if it does, and what each available fix would cost.

(1) For a grouping of people to be a real kind, its boundaries must be non-arbitrary: something about the members and non-members themselves must justify drawing the lines where they are drawn, rather than mere convenience or stipulation.

(2) A boundary is non-arbitrary only if it tracks a difference in kind, not merely a difference in degree — that is, adjacent cases across the boundary must differ qualitatively from one another.

(3) The properties standardly cited to individuate generations — formative historical events, media environments, economic conditions, prevailing child-rearing practices, and the like — are distributed across birth cohorts as gradients rather than qualitative breaks.

(4) So for any proposed generational boundary, whether placed in the mid-1960s, the late 1990s, or elsewhere, the cohorts on either side differ only by degree in the relevant properties.

(5) If the cohorts on either side of a boundary differ only by degree, then any line drawn between them is arbitrary: there is no property-based reason to draw it at one year rather than a year earlier or later.

(6) A grouping whose boundaries are arbitrary is not a real kind; at best it is a conventional label.

(7) Therefore generations are not real kinds.

(8) The same logic extends to social classes, neighborhoods, and dialect communities, since class position, spatial belonging, and speech features also shade continuously into one another.

(9) So social classes, neighborhoods, and dialect communities are not real kinds either.

(10) That conclusion is too strong: these groupings support robust generalizations, self-understanding, and causal explanation in ways that merely stipulated labels do not.

(11) The argument must therefore fail somewhere. I would most like to keep (10), and I also find (6) hard to deny because it seems nearly definitional; but I do not see how to keep both without making “real kind” vacuous. What are the precise options for rejecting a premise or step, and what does each cost? Does the move that saves social classes and dialect communities also save generations, or does it leave generational labels conventional after all?
