Design a ninety-minute classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy-of-mind seminar of about fifteen students, on cognitive penetration of perception. Participants should come away having felt, before it is named, the central puzzle of the field: some perceptual illusions (for example, the Müller-Lyer arrowheads, where two equal lines look unequal even after you measure them) persist despite full knowledge, which suggests perception is insulated from belief — yet in other cases what someone was just primed to expect seems to change what they literally see. The activity must be runnable with paper handouts, a projector, and small non-monetary stakes such as points or sweets, and it must be arranged so that students generate the puzzle themselves through the rules or the sequence of tasks, before terms like "cognitive penetration," "attention," or "criterion shift" are introduced. Include exact rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, roles where useful, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto three distinct hypotheses: (i) the perceptual experience itself changed, (ii) attention moved, and (iii) participants only changed how willing they were to report or classify what they saw. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run — for instance, a priming manipulation too weak to shift results, students guessing the point and performing to it, or an order effect between the illusion and the priming tasks — and how to handle each. Be explicit about what the activity cannot establish: it produces differences in reports, not direct evidence that experience rather than judgment changed, and the instructor will have to supply that step. End with a one-sentence summary of the design as the very final line, beginning "Verdict:".
