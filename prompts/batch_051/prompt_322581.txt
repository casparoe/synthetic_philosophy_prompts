For a reading group on Darwin and the idea of cultural evolution, I want you to build a decision tree or sorting quiz (with a scoring key, whichever format actually works better) that takes people from a starting commitment to a named position on the Darwinian theory of culture. Use Darwin's discussion in The Descent of Man as the anchor — you know the bit, where he says, roughly (I'm paraphrasing, not quoting), that the struggle for existence is constantly going on among words and grammatical forms, and the better, shorter, easier forms gain the upper hand, and that languages can be classed like organic beings. I want the tree to be built around whether that analogy can be cashed out literally or whether it breaks at some specific point.

Assume everyone answering already accepts the minimal starting point: cultural items (words, phrases, ideas, practices) vary, are transmitted, and some spread while others die out. The quiz is about what follows from that.

The leaves I'd like to end up with are at least these five: the meme's-eye view where cultural items are the real replicators and humans are their vehicles; the dual-inheritance view where culture is a second inheritance system that coevolves with genes but can't be reduced to them; the cultural-attraction view where cultural stability comes from shared psychological biases rather than high-fidelity copying; the loose-analogy view where cultural selection is only a metaphor and the real selection operates on biological hosts; and a deflationary view that says the whole Darwinian vocabulary is just re-description with no predictive added value. If your question space generates other coherent positions, include them too.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. That is: first lay out the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, and flag unoccupied combinations with a short note on why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, rather than pretending they're cleanly distinct.

The diagnostic questions should each be a concrete case or claim that the positions genuinely come apart on, phrased so someone can answer without knowing the labels. Dont ask things like "do you think memes exist?" Use cases: a song hook spreading through a population, a chain letter that mutates as it's forwarded, a pronunciation change sweeping through a dialect, a scientific concept that shifts meaning as it gets taught, a religious taboo that persists despite costly compliance, a fad diet that burns out while a cooking technique survives. Score people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like.

Audience is a mixed reading group — some evolutionary biologists, some cultural theorists — so the questions should not presuppose insider knowledge of memetics or dual-inheritance theory. Eight to ten questions is about right. After the tree or quiz, give the scoring key and then walk through how you've placed each of the five named leaves in your logical space, and which feature of each diagnostic question is doing the separating work.
