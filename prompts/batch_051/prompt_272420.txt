I am a novelist researching the ethics of discounting the future for a plot in which a government economist must decide whether to prevent a slow-moving environmental catastrophe that will mostly harm people a century from now. A philosopher character challenges the economist’s use of a standard discount rate, and in preparing that character’s position I encountered the following summary in a friend’s notes on Henry Sidgwick’s The Methods of Ethics. She writes that Sidgwick maintains, in essence, that once the uncertainties of future life and our limited ability to affect it are set aside, mere remoteness in time provides no justification for caring less about a future benefit or harm than about the same benefit or harm experienced now; that he treats any pure time preference as a violation of rational impartiality, akin to caring less about distant strangers simply because they are far away; and that, consequently, Sidgwick’s argument rules out the kind of positive discount rate on future well-being that underlies most cost-benefit analyses. My philosopher character wants to put this summary almost verbatim into his dialogue. Before I let him, I would like an accuracy audit: which parts of this summary are faithful to Sidgwick’s position, which overstate or subtly distort it, and would a careful defender of Sidgwick accept the leap from his argument about pure time preference to the rejection of a positive social discount rate in policy analysis?
