I'm a retired field ecologist — spent most of my career on salt marshes and long-term intertidal surveys — and I've been reading environmental philosophy for the last couple of years. I've ended up with a set of commitments that feel more like an accumulation than a worked-out position, and I'd like someone to poke at whether they actually hang together.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Some natural things — species, long-evolved habitats, particular places — have value that doesnt depend on whether any human values them or gets anything out of them.
2. But when I actually argue for protecting a place, I reach for the way it matters to the people who work it, fish it, walk it, or grew up beside it. I don't think this is just a political compromise; I think those relational considerations are doing genuine normative work.
3. I'm suspicious of the idea that pristine wilderness deserves special protection as such. I think the category is partly a cultural artefact, and I'd defend a well-managed working landscape or a restored site over a hands-off remnant more often than most environmentalists I know.
4. I also think a newly created wet woodland, or a decommissioned reservoir that's been turned into a reedbed, can be worth every bit as much as an ancient site of the same kind — even though one is more or less a human artefact and the other isn't.
5. I support culling invasive species (grey squirrels, mink, Himalayan balsam) without much hand-wringing, even though I think the suffering of an individual culled animal matters in itself.
6. At the same time, I think the value of a species or a place has something to do with its not being ours to make — with its having a history and a way of carrying on that's independent of human intentions. That's a big part of why I'm not an instrumentalist.
7. And I want to say a system's value isn't mainly about what it does — its productivity or its functioning. But then I catch myself defending a restoration project by pointing to its functions, and I can't tell whether that's just loose talk or whether function has to come into the value story after all.

So, put those all on one page: am I holding two or three positions that can't all be true, or is this basically one view with some loose ends? I'd like the tensions ranked by how serious they are, each with a sentence or two on what would have to give, and for the ones that are real, say what the least costly repair would be and what it costs. And don't just flag everything — tell me which pairs are only apparently in tension and which are genuinely pulling against each other.

Also, I'm not a professional philosopher, so I'd be greateful if you could be clear about which parts of your answer are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and not dress a contested view up as the obvious consensus. British English spellings, please.
