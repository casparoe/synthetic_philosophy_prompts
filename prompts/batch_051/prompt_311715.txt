Friends keep telling me that an omission can be a genuine cause: the dry rot happened because I didnt repair the gutter, the patient declined because the night nurse never checked on her, that sort of thing. I keep replying that this is blame wearing a causal costume—once you strip away the norm (he should have repaired the gutter), all that's left is a bare counterfactual, and counterfactual dependence is much too cheap to count as causation. They reply that counterfactual dependence is exactly what causation is, and around we go. Someone then pointed out that we might never get a clean empirical handle on this because norms and counterfactual structure are usually manipulated together in the obvious vignettes: when someone fails to act and the outcome follows, the same background that made the failure violate a norm is often what made the failure necessary for the outcome. I want to know whether there is any test that could seperate the two readings of the claim that omissions are real causes. Design the best experiment or observational study you can, say exactly what result would favour the real-cause reading and what result would favour the blame-in-costume reading, and be explicit about what a positive result would leave unsettled—in particular whether it would bear at all on whether omissions are items in the causal order, or only on how people police each other with the word 'cause'.
