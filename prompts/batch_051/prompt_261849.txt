I keep reconstructing an argument against the promise of recursive interpretability — using AI systems to interpret other AI systems layer by layer until the stack becomes transparent — and I cannot tell whether it is a real proof or a paranoid over-demand. Here it is, as cleanly as I can state it:

(1) We are entitled to trust an interpretation of a model's internal computation only if we have reason to think the interpretation tracks the model's actual internal features, not merely spurious correlates of the model's outputs.

(2) When the interpretation is produced by an AI, the interpreter's agreement with our judgments on test cases cannot by itself supply that reason: a black-box interpreter could be latching onto superficial correlates.

(3) The only way to rule out such spurious correlation is to know how the interpreter itself computes the interpretation; in other words, to interpret the interpreter.

(4) But an interpretation of the interpreter faces the identical problem: its agreement with our judgments on test cases still cannot rule out that it, too, tracks spurious correlates of the interpreter's behavior.

(5) Therefore, every AI-produced interpretation requires a further AI-produced interpretation before it can ground entitlement, and this demand iterates without end.

(6) Since finite agents cannot complete an infinite chain of interpretations, no finite agent is ever entitled to trust an AI-produced interpretation of a model's internal computation.

The conclusion seems too strong, because a parallel argument would appear to show that no one can ever trust an expert's account of any system too complex to inspect directly. Yet I cannot pinpoint the failure. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, under what reading of that step, and how it should be revised or resisted. If it is valid, say what it actually establishes and where a defender of recursive interpretability can still stand.
