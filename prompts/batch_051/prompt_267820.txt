My friend has been reading Lucretius and walked me into an argument I can't find the exit from. It goes:

(1) Before I was born there was an infinite strech of nonexistence, and nobody thinks that stretch was bad for me.
(2) Being dead is just another stretch of nonexistence, after my life instead of before it.
(3) If two periods are alike in every respect relevant to whether they can be bad for a person, and one isn't bad for that person, then the other isn't bad for them either.
(4) So being dead isn't bad for me.
(5) But the same reasoning says that if a machine could erase me from existence for a year — no body, no mind, no trace — and then stitch me back together, that year of nonexistence wouldn't be bad for me either, even if my partner died in that year, my kids were taken away, or everyone forgot me.
(6) But that year obviously is bad for me, so something went wrong in the first five steps.

Which premise should I give up? I'd like to keep (1), because it seems right that pre-birth nonexistence wasn't a misfortune, but I can't see how to keep it without also accepting the absurd conclusion about the machine. What would rejecting each premise cost? Where does the argument slip from the boring past to the scary future?
