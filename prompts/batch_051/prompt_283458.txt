You are a philosopher of mind with a working knowledge of cognitive neuroscience, and you have been asked to referee a long-running dispute rather than to take sides in it.

For decades, a family of experiments now standardly associated with Benjamin Libet has been read by some researchers as showing that the brain commits to a movement before the person consciously decides to move. In the basic setup, participants watch a fast-moving clock, flex a wrist whenever they choose, and report the moment at which they first became aware of the intention to move; meanwhile, scalp electrodes record a slow buildup of electrical activity, the readiness potential, that begins before the time subjects report as their moment of decision. One camp takes this as evidence that conscious will is an illusion, or at best a late after-the-fact commentary on actions the brain has already initiated. The opposing camp says the experiments are simply irrelevant to free will: the task contains no genuine choice, the relevant decision was already made when the participant agreed to flex at some unspecified time, and the measured gap only shows that introspective timing is imprecise, not that conscious intention comes causally after the neural preparation of the movement. Both sides have had decades to refine their positions, yet each new study seems to leave the disagreement exactly where it was.

Your task is to diagnose why this debate has stalled, not to declare a winner. In particular, evaluate the following working hypothesis: the debate has stalled because it fuses at least three distinct questions that need not have the same answer — (1) an empirical question about the timing, source, and functional role of the readiness potential and of the neural processes that produce voluntary movement; (2) a conceptual question about what would even count as the moment of decision, and whether an intention is the sort of thing that can have a temporal onset accessible to introspection at all; and (3) a normative question about what kind of control, if any, moral and legal responsibility requires.

Think slowly, and begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses: that the disagreement is merely verbal, that it is an ordinary empirical dispute awaiting better data, that it is a clash of unargued starting intuitions about the self or about what an explanation must deliver, or that the question itself is ill-posed. Then commit to the diagnosis you find best supported and defend it. Along the way, make clear which of the three questions in the working hypothesis are genuinely load-bearing for the disagreement and which, if any, are distractions. Be precise about what each side actually claims, and be honest about what could count as progress: spell out a specific experimental result, conceptual distinction, or reframing that would genuinely move the debate forward — or argue explicitly that none would, and say what follows if the debate is better dissolved than settled.
