I have been trying to work out on my own when examining my own thoughts and feelings is good for me and when it makes things worse. Weighing concrete cases has seemed more honest than starting from a rule, so here are the ones I keep returning to, with my verdicts.

1. An hour after a bad row with my mother, I write down what each of us said and where it went wrong. I see that I had been pressing a point she cannot hear, and I decide to wait and write to her instead. Useful, and the next conversation went better.

2. After the same row, I then spend three nights in bed replaying the one sentence I most regret, wincing each time, reaching no new thought. That is plainly harmful, though it looks almost identical to the first.

3. After being turned down for a promotion, I ask what I could honestly have done differently. I list two realistic things and begin them the next week. This dulls the disappointment and I count it as good.

4. After the same rejection I spend weeks asking why I am not as driven as other people and whether something in me is broken. The question never resolves, and I start avoiding work. Bad.

5. I keep a mood diary, and after a few months I notice that my worst stretches follow a week of poor sleep and no exercise. That gave me something I could act on, so I count it as good.

6. A friend records how she feels several times a day and each time asks why she feels exactly that way at that moment. She says it makes every mood bigger and more fragile, and she has stopped. I lean towards bad, though it resembles my diary in case 5.

7. Once, while doing something generous, I stopped to ask whether I was only being kind because I wanted to be liked. It left me deflated for days, but it also made me more honest about my mixed motives, and I count that as good overall.

8. When I tried the same move on every kind thing I did for a week, I ended up doing fewer kind things and felt sour rather than honest. Bad.

I keep half-believing a rule: self-examination is good when it turns a vague feeling into something I can see clearly and act on, and bad when it is circular, asking the same question and receiving the same answer or none. But that rule does not obviously explain the difference between 7 and 8, and I am not sure whether the harm in 4 and 6 is really circularity or something else.

I would like you to find the best rule, stated precisely, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible; tell me which of my verdicts it cannot keep and why; and apply it to three further cases. The further cases are: spending an evening going over an embarrassment from years ago that can no longer be acted on at all; sitting down each night for twenty minutes to review the day and write one thing to do differently, even on uneventful days; and asking myself, after a friendship cools, exactly which of my habits drove the person away.

Present your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the rule; how it sorts my eight cases; which of my verdicts it overturns; its verdicts on the three further cases; and the place where the rule is softest and might need a qualification.
