A widely recommended book on global inequality gives, as I remember it, roughly the following account of why some nations are rich. The debate is a contest among three explanations: geography (climate, disease, natural resources), culture (values like trust and work ethic), and institutions (the rules and their enforcement that shape economic incentives). According to the book, the institutions view has won decisively. The decisive evidence is the natural experiment of European colonization: where settlers faced high mortality they stayed in small numbers and built extractive institutions, while where they could settle in large numbers they built inclusive institutions protecting property and constraining the state. Because which kind of institution a colony got was determined by settler mortality rather than by anything about the colonies' later economies, the comparison is effectively randomized. These early institutions persisted and predict national income today, so we can conclude that institutions are the fundamental cause of prosperity, and that geography and culture matter only insofar as they shaped institutions. The obvious worry that the causal arrow runs from wealth to institutions is ruled out because the institutions in question were imposed centuries before the wealth differences emerged. I half-remember the real debate as being more unsettled than this, but the book is widely cited. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate from what is subtly wrong or overstated, say how a careful defender of the institutions view would want to qualify the account, what a proponent of the geography or culture view would reject, and which of the book's conclusions survive the corrections.
