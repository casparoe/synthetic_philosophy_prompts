I am an undergraduate in a seminar on rational models of conflict, and the course has been organized around the puzzle that anchors the rationalist literature: because war is costly, there ought to be some negotiated settlement both sides would prefer to fighting, so the interesting question is why bargaining ever breaks down. The framework we have been using, associated with James Fearon, attributes bargaining failure to three broad mechanisms: actors hold private information they have incentives to misrepresent, actors cannot credibly commit to abide by a settlement later, and certain stakes resist division. I have become interested in pushing that framework into territory it was not designed to cover: AI development races, in which firms or states pour resources into frontier capabilities and end up in what many describe as a race to the bottom, even when every party claims it would prefer a slower, safer, coordinated trajectory.

The transfer seems awkward in ways that strike me as theoretically promising rather than merely confusing. Information about algorithmic progress and model capabilities is genuinely hard to observe and easy to spin; the relevant 'settlement' is obscure because a verifiable division of a future capability, or a binding promise to forgo it, may not be feasible; and some of the pathology may be less a bargaining failure than a negative-externality problem, since one lab's safety investments tend to benefit its rivals. At the same time, the commitment-problem analogue feels as though it is doing most of the intuitive work: whoever defects on a slowdown agreement may lock in an irreversible lead, which gives everyone reason to defect preemptively.

Suppose a small group — two people who work on rationalist conflict theory, two on AI governance, and one decision theorist — had a year and a modest budget to produce the conceptual groundwork before any larger empirical or formal project builds on it: a precise account of what an 'AI race' is, an explicit mapping of the three mechanisms onto the technology case, and a judgment about which mappings carry genuine explanatory weight rather than merely analogical surface. What open conceptual questions should they prioritize, and in what order? I am more interested in the reasoning than in a list: for each question, why it is upstream of or dependent on the others, how tractable it is for people whose tools are rational-choice theory and careful case comparison rather than experiments or access to proprietary lab information, and what argument or evidence in the next year would move it up or down the ranking. I would also like an honest statement of where the transfer itself is misleading, and whether some features of AI races are better posed outside the rationalist-bargaining framework altogether.
