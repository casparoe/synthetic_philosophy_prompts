I'm a PhD student in religious studies, and I keep coming back to William James's The Varieties of Religious Experience. There's a section in the later lectures where he refuses to dismiss mystical or conversion experiences just because they have neurological or psychological causes. Instead he proposes something like a pragmatic test: assess such experiences by what they do in a life, not by where they come from. I've been thinking about how this applies to people who say they're spiritual but not religious. My advisor thinks that when you drop the theological framework, all that's left is a vague mood or aesthetic preference. But James seems to leave room for the idea that even unorthodox, non-creedal experiences could be truth-revealing in some practical sense.

So here's what I want to work through. Suppose I take James's pragmatic claim seriously: a spiritual experience is to be judged by its fruits, meaning whether it produces lasting changes in a person's orientation, such as more patience, less fear of death, a stronger sense of connection, or altered priorities. How would one actually test whether non-religious spiritual experiences—say, a powerful experience in nature, during meditation, or at a concert—are doing the same kind of work James attributes to classical religious experience? I want a concrete design: what would we measure, what would a positive result show, and would it show that the spirituality is more than just a mood? Also, be honest about what the test could never settle: even if the behavioral fruits match, maybe that just means pleasant experiences can motivate people, not that the experiences themselves have any insight. I'd like the answer to tell me how confident it is in each part of the proposed test and to flag the point it is least sure about.
