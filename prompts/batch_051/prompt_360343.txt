For most of my career as a journalist covering science and ideas, I have worked under a self-imposed rule of doubt. I do not treat a finding, paper, or argument as ready for print unless I can articulate, in my own words, at least one serious objection or alternative explanation that a well-informed critic could press against it; if I cannot produce one after genuine effort, I treat the piece as incomplete rather than the claim as settled. The rationale has always been that premature certainty is my profession's characteristic failure, and that a story should not be imposed on a reader until it has survived an adversarial test by the person who wrote it.

Lately I have begun to worry that the rule has become a ritual rather than a discipline. There are stories where the honest result of my check is that no live objection remains — the safety record of a widely used drug, the fact that a vaccine does not cause the disease it prevents, the age of the Earth — and in those cases I sometimes catch myself manufacturing a doubt purely to satisfy the procedure. I have also noticed that I doubt claims that flatter my assumptions much faster than claims that unsettle them, which suggests the rule may be tracking my biases rather than the evidence. I would like to know whether the policy, as stated, is rationally and morally defensible, or whether I have converted a useful form of critical thinking into an unfalsifiable demand for perpetual suspicion. Is there a principled way to distinguish doubt that improves a piece of reporting from doubt that merely performs open-mindedness? Can the rule be restated — for instance, by separating doubt as an investigative probe from doubt as a standing attitude — so that it keeps the benefits of adversarial checking without the vices of manufactured skepticism and without giving editorial cover to false balance? Please answer in American English.
