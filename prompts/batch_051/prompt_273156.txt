I was talking to a friend who used one of those apps that lets you keep chatting with a simulation of someone who's died, and I've ended up with a set of views I'd like pressure-tested for coherence. It's not abstract either: a cousin is thinking about building one for my late grandmother, so I don't want to coast on vibes.

Here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) The dead can't be harmed. Once a person is gone there's no subject there to suffer or be wronged; talk of posthumous harm is at best metaphor.
(2) It would be seriosuly wrong to make a griefbot of someone who explicitly said, while alive, that they would hate being turned into one. Ignoring a declared wish like that feels almost like desecration, not just rudeness.
(3) If someone never said anything either way, making a private griefbot for your own consolation is fine, even though it will say things the person never actually said.
(4) Even in that 'never said anything' case, it would be wrong to deliberately make the bot say things the person would surely have rejected — say, using it to vent political views they spent their life opposing.
(5) Any genuine wrong here has to cash out in terms of the living: distress to relatives, who controls the person's image and legacy, the effect on how society treats the dead, and so on. There's no separate kind of wronging 'the memory'.

Each of these feels right on its own, but I have a nagging sense they don't all pull the same direction. Before I commit, I'd like an audit: where are the real tensions, where am I only imagining a tension, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency?

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, ordered from the most serious tension to the least. For each point say which commitments pull against each other and what would have to give. And write it in British English, not American.
