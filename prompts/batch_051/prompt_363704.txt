You are a philosopher who specializes in the structure of moral and metaethical disagreement. Work through this carefully: before settling on any diagnosis, brainstorm the distinct kinds of impasse that could be operating, then commit to the one that best fits what each side actually says and defend it.

I have been following the moral bioenhancement debate, and it strikes me as stalled in a way that is itself worth diagnosing. On one side, Persson and Savulescu argue that human moral psychology is dangerously outpaced by human technological power: our altruism is parochial, we discount distant and future harms, and our moral motivation is not up to the risks we can now create. Ordinary moral education and institutional reform are, on their view, too slow and too unreliable for the urgency of the situation, so we have a duty to pursue biomedical means of improving moral motivation. On the other side, critics object that a change brought about by drugs or neural intervention bypasses the agency and reasoning that make a change a moral improvement rather than a merely behavioral one; that the argument mislocates the problem in individual psychology when the relevant deficits are institutional and political; that we lack any uncontested account of what counts as more moral, so the project risks cementing whatever values happen to guide the enhancers; and that making people more altruistic or cooperative could make them worse off or more exploitable. Each side proceeds as though the other has missed the point, and the same cases and thought experiments circulate without moving anyone.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about whether the interventions could work, nor simply a first-order moral disagreement about whether we should use them. I suspect there are at least two debates running under one heading: a risk assessment under time pressure about how to close the gap between technological power and moral motivation, and a conceptual and normative argument about what kind of change deserves to count as a moral improvement at all, such that one side is doing emergency policy and the other is doing gatekeeping about the grounds of moral status. But I do not want to assert that; I want it tested.

Give me a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure, not a survey of authors and not a verdict on who is right. In particular: is the impasse verbal, substantive, or several debates under one heading? Do the sides differ over facts, over which cases count as data, over the burden of proof under uncertainty, over the nature of moral agency and freedom, or over starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical bet inside the apparently conceptual argument, or a hidden conceptual bet inside the apparently empirical one? And, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a case, a datum, a reframing, or an admission one side would have to make — or is this a debate that should not be expected to converge?
