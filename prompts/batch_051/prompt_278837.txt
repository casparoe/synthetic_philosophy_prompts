I'm a backend engineer, and somehow I've ended up owning the identity-resolution logic for our multi-tenant SaaS product. We get identifiers for the same person, account, or team from all over the place: SSO providers, invite links, a legacy database key from the old on-prem product, support tickets, third-party integrations. Every week we make maybe two dozen decisions about whether two strings refer to the same entity and whether we can merge or canonicalize them. These decisions are hard to reverse and the stakes are real: we once merged what turned out to be a personal email and a shared team inbox, and someone's billing history got sent to a whole department. I need a reusable procedure the on-call engineer can run in about ten minutes, because we can't exactly have a philosopher sitting in on every ticket.

Recently I went down a rabbit hole reading about Frege's puzzle — the thing where the morning star and the evening star both turn out to be Venus, so the morning star is the morning star is trivial but the morning star is the evening star is a discovery. The sense/reference split felt weirdly relevant to our merging problem: some of our identifiers look like different senses that pick out the same reference, and some are doing more than that. But I dont have any formal philosophy background, so I don't trust myself to turn the idea into something a tired on-call engineer can follow.

Can you write me a decision procedure, grounded in Frege's actual distinction between sense and reference, for deciding when two identifiers should be treated as the same entity and when not? I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exact step where you stop with dont-merge versus merge-but-keep-both-as-aliases-with-metadata, and the points where the procedure has to hand the call to a human because the issue has stopped being about identity and turned into a question of business meaning. Then run it on two or three of our realistic cases: (a) a personal gmail address and a work email that both belong to the same customer, (b) a legacy customer_id from the old billing system and a user_id from the new one that point to the same human, except the old customer_id actually refers to a billing account shared by two spouses, and (c) two provider-unique IDs that resolve to the same person even though the names attached to them differ. Please include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like — like telling us not to merge something that looked obvious — and be honest about what the procedure can't settle.
