I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company and somehow got roped into our sustainability committee, which mostly argues about whether to go carbon-neutral in the office and how many meatless days the cafeteria should have. We're now stuck on a concrete decision: whether to replace one of the lunch stations with an insect-protein option—cricket flour patties, mealworm bolognese, that kind of thing. The environmental case looks strong: way less land, water, and emissions than beef or chicken. But a few members are uneasy about the ethics of farming and killing enormous numbers of insects, and one of them keeps pushing something he calls the scale argument. As I understand it: even if you assign each individual insect only a small probablity of being sentient and capable of suffering, the numbers are so astronomical that a tiny probability still translates into a huge expected amount of suffering—so switching to insect protein might end up ethically worse than the status quo once you aggregate. Others reply that invertebrates are neurologically simple enough that the probability is basically zero for practical purposes, and that it's bizarre to block a climate-friendly policy on the back of a low-credence possibility we can't measure. A third group says the whole expected-value approach is the wrong tool here, and that the commitee shouldn't pretend to resolve a debate philosophers haven't settled.

I've been asked to draft the policy memo that we'll actually vote on next month. You're a professional philosopher who does applied ethics work for companies and non-profits. Please write this as a real memo to the committee: recommend a concrete policy for the cafeteria (adopt, reject, pilot, phase in, whatever you think is right), give us criteria we can apply when someone raises a new animal-welfare objection down the line, and take the strongest version of the losing side seriously rather than strawmanning it. Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the relevant considerations before you settle on a recommendation. And don't just survey the options and stop—we have to vote on something, so the memo needs an actual bottom line. Also, I don't have any formal philosophy background, so feel free to explain concepts but don't hide behind jargon. One more constraint: "we need more research on insect sentience" can't be your entire answer, since the committee has to make a call regardless.
