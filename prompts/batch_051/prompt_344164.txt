Turing's proposal for reaching machine intelligence was to build a child machine and educate it, rather than to program an adult mind directly. The load-bearing idea was roughly this: specifying the adult mind is intractable, specifying a child-like initial state is more tractable, and education is a known process that can transform the one into the other. Modern large language models appear to have taken a third route. They are not assembled out of adult-level rules, but they are also not educated the way a child is; they are pretrained on an enormous corpus with a generic next-token objective and then steered by a comparatively thin layer of feedback.

I want to know whether Turing's argument survives this development, becomes inapplicable, or can be repaired. Specifically: which premise of the child-machine argument does pretraining-plus-feedback most directly bear on, and is there a principled sense in which that pipeline is still an implementation of the build-a-child-and-educate-it strategy rather than a refutation of it? If the argument needs repair, give me the repaired version, and say what it would predict about how future training regimes ought to differ from current ones. I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in a policy recommendation.
