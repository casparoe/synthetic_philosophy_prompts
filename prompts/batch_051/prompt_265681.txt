I am an undergraduate currently taking an epistemology course on the regress problem, and I am writing a term paper on how institutional decisions can embody positions on epistemic justification. For this paper, I would like you to act as an expert in social and institutional epistemology and think carefully about the following counterfactual.

Suppose that in 2028 a medium-sized, wealthy democracy—say, the Netherlands—responds to a widely publicized epistemic crisis by enacting the Justification in Public Institutions Act. From 2030 onward, no statement may be asserted as knowledge in public schools, court rulings, parliamentary debates, or government-funded science communication unless it is either a certified basic belief, limited to perceptual reports, elementary logical and mathematical truths, and independently vetted introspective reports, or it is linked to such certified beliefs by an explicit, registered chain of deductive or inductive inferences. Crucially, the Act states that mutual support, explanatory coherence, and wide reflective equilibrium cannot count as sources of justification, and any attempt to stop a justificatory regress by appealing to coherence is inadmissible in state institutions. The Act does not regulate private speech, but it gives its enforcement office strong control over curricula, expert testimony, state-funded research reporting, and administrative rulemaking.

Predict, with visible reasoning, how the epistemic and institutional landscape looks in 2080. In particular: which domains of knowledge absorb the foundationalist rule most easily; which domains quietly circumvent it, fragment, or migrate outside state institutions; what new professions, gatekeeping bodies, or informal practices emerge; and whether the rule plausibly achieves its designers’ presumed aim of restoring public trust in shared knowledge. Begin by laying out the mechanisms and incentives you expect to operate, then trace them over the fifty-year period. For each major predicted effect, state whether you regard it as robust or as a guess, and identify what would have to be true for the prediction to be wrong. Finally, say how confident you are in the overall picture and single out the point you are least sure of.
