Since I retired from a research career in the natural sciences, I have been spending much of my reading time in a philosophy-of-science group, and we have lately been working through the Doomsday Argument and the dispute between the self-sampling assumption (SSA—the rule that one should reason as though randomly selected from one's reference class) and the self-indication assumption (SIA—the rule that one's own existence is itself evidence favoring hypotheses that contain more observers). An old colleague who spent his career as a statistician has been defending SSA with an analogy that speaks to my professional habits while also troubling me, and I would like it audited before I accept what he draws from it.

His analogy goes as follows. Imagine an urn containing one ball for every human who has ever lived or ever will live, each ball marked with its owner's birth rank. You reach in blind, draw your own ball, and find that it reads roughly a hundred billion. In an ordinary sampling problem, drawing ball number 7 from an urn of unknown size favors the small urn over the enormous one, since the observation is far more probable under the small-urn hypothesis. Likewise, he says, reading a birth rank near a hundred billion favors "doom soon"—a total human population not much larger than the present one—over "doom late." SSA is simply the instruction to treat your birth rank as that sort of draw. He then turns the urn against SIA: SIA, on his telling, is the elementary error of taking the mere fact that a ball was drawn as evidence about the urn's size, when the drawing was guaranteed by the fact that one is alive to pose the question. He also dismisses the presumptuous philosopher worry—that under SIA two empirically identical theories differing only in how many observers they produce would be illegitimately distinguished by one's own existence—as a confusion the urn picture prevents.

My uneasiness is that the analogy appears to supply, without argument, the very things that are in question. In a genuine sampling problem there is an actual urn, a sampling frame, a stated selection rule, and a real chance of not having been sampled. Here there is no urn and no frame; the choice of the class from which one is supposedly drawn—all humans, all observers, all sentient beings, all possible versions of oneself—seems to be doing enormous work and is never justified; and it is unclear that the urn can represent SIA fairly, since the picture seems constructed to exclude SIA's key move from the outset.

I would like a full audit. First, give the explicit mapping: what corresponds to the urn, the balls, the labels, the draw, and the sampling protocol. Second, list the disanalogies and separate those that matter for the conclusions the analogy is being used to support from those that do not. Third, address three specific questions. Is the absence of any real sampling frame a fatal disanalogy, or merely a redescription of the assumption under dispute? Can the urn model state SIA and the presumptuous philosopher without caricature, and if not, what does its construction beg? Once the disanalogies are admitted, does the Doomsday shift survive, does the dismissal of SIA survive, and what would a better analogy—or a more honest formulation—look like?
