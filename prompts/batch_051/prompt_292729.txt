I hold the view that the normative force of privacy is best grounded in the distinctive kind of harm Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis described in The Right to Privacy (Harvard Law Review, 1890). The passage I rely on is this:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

I accept the position this suggests: privacy matters because unwanted exposure, intrusion, and the loss of a retreat from the world cause a distinct form of mental pain and distress, and that harm—not bare autonomy, property, or information control—is the primary reason a legal and moral right to privacy is justified. I want to know what would count against this harm-based account. I do not mean only the standard objection that some privacy violations are not felt as painful at the time. I want a systematic map of the view's exposure: which ordinary cases, empirical findings, philosophical arguments, or conceptual results would have to be true—or would have to be shown to be true—for the view to be false or to collapse into a rival account, and which can the view absorb as mere complications. Rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, say what a committed defender of this account would themselves concede counts against it, and give an honest assessment of whether the view, as I have stated it, has been insulated against refutation and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
