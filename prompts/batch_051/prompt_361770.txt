I am currently taking a seminar on intellectual property and the ownership of ideas, and I have noticed that the move everyone keeps making — classmates, a guest lecturer last week, and my own first instincts — is to reason about a novel, a piece of software, or a patented drug as if it were ordinary physical property. On this picture, the creator is the landowner, the work is the land, the copyist is a trespasser, and unauthorized copying simply is theft, which is then taken to settle the question in favor of strong, near-absolute rights of control over the work. I have come to suspect that the analogy is doing most of the argumentative work while quietly hiding the real issues, but a suspicion is not an analysis. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Begin by making the mapping explicit, element by element: the work, the creator, the copyist, the public domain, the legal term of protection, and whatever in the target is being asked to play the role of land, fence, owner, trespasser, commons, and lease. Then distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support from the disanalogies that do not — for example, non-rivalry, independent creation, the cumulative or standing-on-shoulders character of ideas, term limits, fair use, and the public domain. I especially want to know which features of the physical-property picture the analogy quietly imports — scarcity, the naturalness of exclusion, the theft framing — that a work of authorship or an invention lacks, and whether the rights-based conclusion survives once those features are named. If the analogy cannot carry that conclusion, say what a better analogy would be, and what that better analogy would license instead. Please answer in American English.
