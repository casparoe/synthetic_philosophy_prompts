Two historians of moral change are entering an adversarial collaboration. The first defends a version of the economic-interest thesis associated with Eric Williams: the abolition of Atlantic slavery is best explained by shifts in material interests — above all the declining economic weight and profitability of the British West Indian plantation complex relative to industrializing Britain — rather than by the causal force of humanitarian argument. The second defends a version of the honor thesis associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah: the great moral revolutions — the end of dueling in Europe and the United States and the abolition of Atlantic slavery — are best explained by transformations in honor, in what counts as respectable or contemptible and whose esteem matters, rather than by disinterested moral argument or by economic interest alone. Both camps grant that argument, interest, and honor were all present in these episodes; they disagree about which did the explanatory work, through what mechanisms, and what the counterfactuals would show.

Write the document this collaboration would produce after sustained work. It should include:

(1) a numbered agreed-claims section: substantive claims about the cases that both sides can sign without hedging — for example, about the timing of British abolition relative to the profitability data, the social class and status position of leading abolitionists, whether slaveholders were compensated, and the sequence by which dueling spread socially before it collapsed;

(2) a disagreements table: each live dispute stated first in neutral wording both sides accept as accurate, then in the phrasing each side insists best captures its own view, with no caricature;

(3) the cruxes: the empirical or conceptual sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side, each paired with the concrete evidence or comparison case both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Possible cruxes include whether the British Caribbean sugar economy was still profitable and expanding in the years just before British abolition, and whether dueling collapsed because it had democratized and lost its honor-conferring function rather than because its opponents had won the argument.

Throughout, separate disagreements that are substantive from ones that are merely verbal — for instance, over whether honor should be classified as an interest — and say precisely which part is verbal and which remains substantive. The goal is a document neither vacuous nor slanted, which both parties could actually sign and recognize their own position in.

Begin with the document itself, with no preface or commentary.
