I'm on my school's mock trial team, and while preparing a civil case I noticed something odd about how the preponderance-of-the-evidence standard gets applied. We were told that the plaintiff must prove each element of the claim—say, in a negligence case, that the defendant owed a duty, breached it, and thereby caused the plaintiff's injury—and that each element must be shown to be more likely true than false. But the elements are assessed separately: the jury is not invited to multiply the probabilities and determine whether the conjunction of all the elements is itself more likely true than false. So a plaintiff could establish two independent elements at 70 percent each, while the chance that both are true would be only 49 percent, below the threshold the law elsewhere treats as sufficient proof. This looked to me like a legal cousin of the lottery paradox, and perhaps the preface paradox as well: the court accepts each member of a conjunction as sufficiently probable while refusing to treat the conjunction as proven.

I am writing a paper on what this practice presupposes about legal proof and evidential support. Does the practice presuppose that proof in court is not the same thing as graded probabilistic belief, or that probabilities over separate elements cannot simply be multiplied into a probability for the whole claim? Is the practice coherent, or does it bring the lottery and preface paradoxes inside the courtroom in another form? Reconstruct the implicit theory of evidence or belief that the practice presupposes, say whether the practice strictly needs that theory or is compatible with several rival theories, and explain what would have to change in how trials are conducted if the law adopted a straightforwardly probabilistic rule instead.
