I keep seeing the Condorcet jury theorem used as a philosophical master key: one side waves it around as a proof that many heads are better than one, the other side rattles off its assumptions (independence, uniform competence, sincere voting, binary choice) as if listing them were itself a refutation, and neither side says what a serious epistemic theory of majority decision would have to accomplish in the first place. I want the specification before I read any further in this debate. Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of when majority decisions track the truth must meet: a theory of when a majority verdict is more likely to be right than any individual voter's, and under what conditions the probability of a correct majority approaches one as the group grows. Treat Condorcet's theorem as the result such a theory must recover, refine, or deliberately give up on, not as the whole theory.

For each desideratum, give the consideration or motivating case behind it. I especially want to see: how the theory handles unequal competence, since real voters are not identical coins biased the same way; how it distinguishes independence-violating dependence that helps (shared evidence pointing toward the truth) from dependence that merely adds redundant votes (copying, deference, herding); what it says about strategic voting and the incentive to become informed when a single vote is almost never pivotal; how it extends beyond exactly two options, where cycles and agenda effects enter; whether it can distinguish the jury setting, in which there is a shared interest in getting a determinate question right, from elections and referenda, in which interests conflict; and what work deliberation does, since discussion can pool information or produce correlated error depending on how it goes. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp. Then say which subset of requirements is jointly hardest to satisfy, and which single requirement does the most to exclude sloppy uses of the theorem.

End by stating how confident you are in the details of the theorem and its standard extensions, and single out the point you are least sure about. Keep the whole answer to at most 900 words; one table is fine if it helps.
