The extended mind thesis, as introduced by Clark and Chalmers, was built around the idea of parity: if an external process plays the kind of functional role in a person's cognitive task that we would unhesitatingly count as cognitive if it were performed inside the head, then it is properly considered part of the cognitive system. Their central illustrative case is Otto's notebook, treated as a passive, stable, transparent store: Otto records information in it, consults it when needed, and automatically accepts what it says. The background conditions that make the notebook more than a mere tool are standardly summarized as reliable availability, ready access, and automatic endorsement.

Now suppose the external resource is no longer a passive store like a notebook or a photo archive, but a generative assistant: a language-model-based system that the user consults constantly and fluidly in the course of planning, writing, and deciding, and that does not simply retrieve stored records but summarizes, reconstructs, elaborates, reformats, and sometimes introduces or garbles content in ways the user cannot precisely predict or retrace. Many people now rely on such systems in something close to the way Otto relies on his notebook. Assume for the question that the assistant is generally accurate and well-calibrated; the pressure under discussion comes from the fact that it generates rather than merely stores, not from the fact that it can err.
