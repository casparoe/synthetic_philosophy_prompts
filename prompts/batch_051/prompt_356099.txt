I’m writing up my philosophical commitments on appeals to “nature” in debates about sex and gender for a small workshop, and I’d like an audit before I present. I start from Mill’s statement of his object in *The Subjection of Women*:

“That the principle which regulates the existing social relations between the two sexes—the legal subordination of one sex to the other—is wrong in itself, and now one of the chief hindrances to human improvement; and that it ought to be replaced by a principle of perfect equality, admitting no power or privilege on the one side, nor disability on the other.”

Against that backdrop, here is the set of views I want to check for consistency:

(1) Arguments from nature to social arrangements are almost always question-begging: what gets called “natural” in such arguments is usually just what is customary under existing arrangements, and the move from “is natural” to “is right” depends on a suppressed normative premise.

(2) But I also think “nature” is not an empty concept. Human beings have basic capacities, developmental needs, and vulnerabilities, and some social arrangements frustrate or support those better than others. So I treat “this is against nature” as sometimes being a compressed empirical claim about blocked human capacities, assessable like any other empirical claim.

(3) I endorse Mill’s epistemic point in the first chapter: when one group has always lived under legal subordination, observed differences cannot ground confident claims about that group’s innate or natural traits, because the conditions producing the observations are contaminated by the subordination itself. I therefore regard most confident claims about innate sex differences with deep suspicion.

(4) But I also hold that the case for legal equality should not ultimately depend on our ignorance about innate differences. If reliable average differences relevant to some social role were established, the utilitarian case for equal treatment might have to be made differently, and in some edge cases it might not go through at all.

(5) I find the passage above slightly unstable: “wrong in itself” and “hindrance to human improvement” suggest two different justifications, one more deontic and one consequentialist, and they can come apart. I want to say the subordination is wrong in itself even if it did not hinder improvement.

(6) I also hold that when an institution has historically subordinated a group, the burden of proof should always fall on those defending the institution, and this procedural principle does not depend on any evidence about nature or utility.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only appear to be. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, explain what each would force me to revise, and say what the cheapest fix is in each case. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent. Finally, I’d want to know what would change your mind about the audit: which line of argument or counterexample would most plausibly lead you to a different ranking, or where your diagnosis is most likely to break down.
