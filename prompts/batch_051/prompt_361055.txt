I keep meeting the debate over why some nations are rich and others poor, and after the first few chapters it stops feeling like an argument and starts feeling like three fields passing each other on different tracks. The institutionalist side says the decisive variable is the rules of the game: secure property rights, constraints on rulers, inclusive rather than extractive political and economic arrangements; it leans on colonial histories and natural experiments in which institutions changed while geography and culture stayed roughly fixed. The geography side replies that institutions are themselves downstream of soil, climate, disease burden, and location, which shaped agriculture, settlement, and access to trade, so citing institutions only pushes the puzzle back a step. The culture side replies that both miss the deeper layer: norms about trust, work, saving, and cooperation determine which institutions are adopted and whether they function, so the same formal rules can mean different things in different societies. All three camps seem to accept the same broad correlations, and yet nobody concedes. My working hypothesis is that this is not mainly a factual disagreement but a disagreement about what can be shown to be a cause from a single, unrepeatable historical record: the key variables are endogenous, measured by proxy, and each camp trusts a different style of evidence — cross-country regressions, natural experiments, or narrative history. Is that diagnosis correct, does it overstate how much the camps agree on the facts, and what kind of disagreement is left once the methodological differences are factored out? I am not looking for a survey of the three positions; I want a diagnosis of the impasse and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as genuine progress.
