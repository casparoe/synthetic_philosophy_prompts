So I've been down a rabbit hole about bivalence — the rule that every statement is true or false — because of an argument with two friends where one kept saying "that's not even false, it's just a category error" and the other said "no, it's unsettled, the world hasn't made up its mind." It hit me that they were using one phrase for what felt like two different situations. I started keeping a list on my phone, and I've landed on a distinction I made up that I want someone to kick around.

I call them "misfires" and "unanswered questions." A misfire is when a sentence quietly depends on a background yes/no question, and that background question has a perfectly good answer — but the answer is the kind that cancels the main sentence before it gets going. "The present king of France is bald" is my standard case: it smuggles in "is there a present king of France?", the answer there is plainly no, and so the baldness question never actually gets asked. An unanswered gap is different: the background question itself has no yes/no answer yet, which is what I think is going on with future contingents ("it will rain on my birthday next year") and maybe with borderline uses of vague words.

I've also got a test for separating them: make the background question explicit and see whether the gap survives the repair. With the king case, the repair — something like "there is no present king of France, so the baldness question never got asked" — is just plain true, which tells me the original gap was only a misfire and bivalence was never really threatened. But with the rain case, the repair still bottoms out in a question with no answer yet, so there's a real gap left over. My actual claim is that most everyday "neither true nor false" cases are misfires, and only the second kind poses a real problem for bivalence.

I know this is probably just me re-inventing the presupposition literature, and I'm not after a reading list. I want an honest assessement: is the distinction actually doing work, or does my repair test just move the gap one level down? What's the strongest example where my test says "misfire" but you'd still feel like bivalence has been violated? And am I just relabeling a familiar idea and calling it new?
