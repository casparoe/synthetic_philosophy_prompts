I grew up in a church that was big on biblical inerrancy, and lately I've been trying to reverse-engineer the actual intellectual habits that came with the commitment. The one I keep coming back to is harmonization. Whenever two passages seemed to contradict each other — the resurrection accounts disagree about who went to the tomb and what they found there, the two genealogies of Jesus don't line up, and the post-resurrection appearances happen in what sure look like incompatible places — the move was always to postulate some extra fact that makes both texts true: maybe the women made two trips, maybe one genealogy traces Joseph and the other traces Mary, maybe Jesus appeared in one town and then another. The rule was never stated, but it operated as an absolute: no apparent contradiction is allowed to stand, and you keep adding auxiliary hypotheses until the tension dissolves.

The thing that bugs me is that these same people would also say a parable isn't making literal truth claims, and that the days of Genesis 1 needn't be 24-hour periods. So how literal a text is seems to get turned up or down depending on what's needed; that looks like it should cash out into a real epistemology of interpretation, not just a reading preference. I suspect there's a coherent philosophical picture underneath — something about what a text's meaning consists in, what it is for two assertions to be compatible, what authorship does to a text's truth — but I can't make it explicit.

So: reconstruct the theory of meaning (and maybe of authorship) that the practice of harmonization presupposes. Then pull apart which parts it strictly presupposes and which parts it merely fits with — for instance, could a non-inerrantist who just wants to be charitable to ancient authors harmonize in the same way? And if you swapped in a more critical view, where apparent contradictions are allowed to be real contradictions between fallible human authors, what would the reading practice actually look like on the ground?
