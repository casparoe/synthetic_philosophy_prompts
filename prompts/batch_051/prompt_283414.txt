Suppose a state lacks the capacity to govern a remote, resource-rich territory and responds by chartering a for-profit corporation to govern it for a renewable forty-year term. The charter lets the corporation issue civil regulations, operate a police force and first-instance courts, and levy a capped land-value tax; it must also issue each adult resident a non-voting share whose dividend tracks the territory's fiscal surplus, may exclude residents only for breach of a published and externally reviewable code of conduct, and must pay a defined exit sum to any resident who chooses to leave. Analyse how this arrangement would actually behave. What would it be incentivised to do about public goods, dispute resolution, and the composition of the resident population? Where is it likely to be gamed first, by residents, by the corporation's managers, by the chartering state, or by outside parties, and with what second-order effects? Which failure modes could be corrected by rewriting the charter, and which are inherent in corporate sovereignty itself rather than in any particular drafting? Throughout, please distinguish what is settled in the historical and theoretical work on chartered companies and corporate sovereignty from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested interpretation as the consensus.
