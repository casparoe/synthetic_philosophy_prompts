I've been collecting my own causal judgments on little cases, and now I have a pile of verdicts that don't obviously hang together. I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A boulder dislodges and smashes a cabin. I say the boulder's falling caused the destruction. The cabin's being made of wood and the freeze-thaw cycle that loosened the boulder were every bit as necessary, but I don't want to call them causes; they're background.

2. A building catches fire. The match the arsonist tossed caused the fire. The fire engine that normally would have been there didn't arrive because of funding cuts. I want to say the engine's not arriving caused the building to burn down too — even though that's an absence, not an event. But I wouldn't say every absent fire engine in the county caused it.

3. Two hitmen, Adams and Bennett, are independently stalking the same target. Adams shoots and kills him. Bennett, who would have shot a second later, sees the target go down and quietly leaves. I say Adams's shot caused the death and Bennett's didnt. But if Adams hadn't shot, the target would have died anyway from Bennett. So the death doesn't counterfactually depend on Adams's shot, and my verdict seems to break the but-for test I keep reaching for.

4. A lifeguard doesn't show up for his shift, and a swimmer drowns. I say the missed shift caused the drowning — an absence cause. A tourist standing on the shore who also didn't jump in didn't cause the drowning. The only difference I can name is that the lifeguard had a duty and the tourist didn't. But that makes causation sound weirdly moral or conventional.

5. A worker is exposed to asbestos for years and gets lung cancer. I say the exposure caused the cancer, even though I can't point to which fiber did it and can't say he would have been cancer-free otherwise — only that the exposure raised the risk a lot. Yet if a person's cancer is weakly associated with diet soda, I don't say the diet soda caused it. That feels like a difference of degree, but maybe I'm fooling myself.

Is there one account of causation — or a small set of candidates — that gets all five, or close? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? Please also say what your favored account predicts about a case I genuinely cant decide on: a doctor prescribes a sedative to a patient who is dying of a terminal illness; the patient dies an hour later from the sedative combined with his frailty, but he would have died within days anyway from the illness. Did the sedative cause his death?

One more background worry. When I think about the boulder case, causation feels like something fully real in the world, but when I think about the lifeguard and the tourist, it starts to feel like a way of organizing a story we tell, with duties and expectations built in. Does the account you land on force me to pick one of those pictures, or can it have both?
