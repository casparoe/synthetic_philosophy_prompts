Question: In The Subjection of Women, Mill attacks the argument that women's subordination is justified by their 'nature': roughly, women are everywhere observed to be subordinate and to have certain traits, so the subordination must suit their nature. Reconstruct Mill's criticism of this line of thought. In particular, explain whether his criticism requires him to deny that there are innate differences between men and women, or only something weaker, and state precisely what the defender of the nature argument would need to establish for the argument to succeed, on Mill's view.

Student answer:
Mill's main complaint is that the nature argument is circular. Defenders point to how women actually behave under subordination and then call that behavior natural. But they can't do that, because the social system itself shapes women's behavior: it represses some capacities and artificially encourages others, so much of what gets labeled 'nature' is an artifact. That's also why he thinks the appeal to experience fails: nobody has run the experiment of treating women and men equally, so the 'experience' the defenders invoke is experience of only one regime. He adds that the subordination of women is an ancient and universal custom, and that's precisely why going against it feels unnatural to us — not because anything in particular about women's nature has been discovered. And he says that if women were naturally incapable of certain roles, it would be unnecessary to forbid them by law. From all this, Mill concludes that there are no innate differences between men and women: once you strip away the artificial constraints, what remains is sameness. So the burden is on the defender of subordination to point to a natural difference, and until they do, equality is the default.
