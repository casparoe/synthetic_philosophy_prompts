I'm the head of a reliability-modeling group at a manufacturing firm. A few times a month my engineers have to assign probabilities to failure modes or unknown input parameters for a new component when there is no operating data. Their default is the principle of indifference: equal probabilities to each failure mode, or a uniform distribution over some continuous parameter, which they call "uninformative." I've read enough about Bertrand's paradox (the circle where different ways of saying "at random" give 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4) to know this isn't neutral: equal probabilities smuggle in a way of carving up the possibilities, and reparameterizing (uniform over a failure rate vs uniform over the log of the rate) changes the answer. But I cant just tell them "be careful," because they need numbers in models on a deadline. The stakes are real — wrong probabilities lead to overbuilt parts or a bad warranty exposure — and the senior engineer or I sign off on the final numbers.

Write us a decision procedure for when to assign equal probabilities and when to refuse. It should be runnable by a competent engineer in under an hour without reading philosophy, and it must mark the points where judgment or sign-off is required rather than pretending the procedure settles them.

Sections: "The procedure" (about 350 words), as ordered steps with what counts as passing each step and where the procedure stops with a no; "Worked example 1" (about 200 words) on a new sealed bearing with three possible failure modes and no supplier data; "Worked example 2" (about 200 words) on an unknown continuous failure rate between 0 and 1 where the parameterization changes the answer; "Where this misfires" (about 150 words). Please write it in American English.
