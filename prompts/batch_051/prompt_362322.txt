I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics, fourteen students with a solid conceptual background but not necessarily a mathematical one, and I have a two-hour block for the measurement problem. They already understand superpositions, unitary evolution, and the broad Everettian proposal that every term in the final state is real and collapse is not needed, but they have not yet met the Born rule problem framed as an objection. I want a structured in-class branching game that makes them feel the force of the problem before I name it, rather than a lecture that announces it. The rough idea I have in mind is this: each participant controls a bankroll of tokens, and a stylized splitting device with two outcomes creates two successor versions of them, each of which inherits the bankroll; before each split they must allocate tickets whose payoffs go to one successor or the other. Crucially, the device should come in two modes. In a duplication mode the two outcomes are realized as different numbers of successor copies, so counting successors gives them a coherent way to price tickets and they can settle into it comfortably. In an amplitude mode the device displays a magnitude attached to each outcome, shown visually as bars of different length, while still producing exactly one successor per outcome, and the rules should force them to choose between counting successors and deferring to the displayed magnitudes where the two policies conflict, with real small stakes so the choices are not idle. By the end they should have experienced on their own terms the sense that the magnitudes seem to matter for how they should act even though the unitary-only picture does not say where that mattering comes from.

The deliverable should include the exact rules and materials, the payoff scheme made from tokens rather than real money, the number of rounds and the timeline, how to rotate roles so no one stays the odd one out, and the debrief. The debrief should reconstruct from what the participants actually did the two candidate policies, counting branches versus weighting by the displayed magnitudes, show that the duplication rounds licensed the first policy while the amplitude rounds put pressure on it, and only then introduce the Born rule problem: a deterministic branching dynamics seems to require rational agents to treat the magnitudes as probabilities, yet nothing in the dynamics alone has earned them that role. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I suspect people will quietly reintroduce a single future self, or bet as though one displayed outcome will not really happen, or drift into arguing about personal identity rather than probability, and I want concrete ways to contain each tendency. Be frank about the one feature the game cannot manufacture for itself. I think the game can expose the gap but cannot by itself generate the normative force of the weights out of the branching facts alone, and I want you to say exactly what your design has to import from outside to make it run, rather than pretending the activity solves the problem it stages. Write the answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a careful email to a colleague, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Do not move beyond the setup into a defense of any particular solution, since I will bring in the decision-theoretic and self-locating proposals in the following session.
