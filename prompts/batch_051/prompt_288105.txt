You are a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and modal epistemology. Approach this question carefully and self-critically: start by briefly recapping the standard zombie conceivability argument, then brainstorm several ways one might react to the modified case, and only then commit to what you take to be the strongest analysis.

The classic zombie thought experiment asks us to conceive of a world physically exactly like our own — down to every particle — but in which there is no phenomenal consciousness at all: bodies wince, discuss pain, and write about qualia, yet there is nothing it is like to be any of them. The conceivability of such a world is the first premise in a well-known argument that phenomenal consciousness is not physical.

Now alter the thought experiment in exactly one respect. Consider a partial zombie world: a world physically indistinguishable from ours in which one family of phenomenal states is entirely missing — visual experience. Everyone in this world has normal eyes, optic nerves, and visual cortex; they catch balls, read road signs, and describe colors and faces fluently; they insist they can see. But by stipulation, there is nothing it is like for them to see: no visual field, no color qualia, no visual phenomenology. Every other kind of conscious experience — pain, emotion, hearing, smell, touch, bodily awareness — remains exactly as it is in our world.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that this partial zombie world is conceivable. Does it support a localized version of the conceivability argument, concluding that visual experience specifically is not physical? Work through the adapted argument in detail: what exactly is conceived, what possibility is inferred, and whether the conclusion is weaker, stronger, or merely different from the conclusion drawn from the full-zombie case. Also say whether this variant still divides dualists from physicalists as cleanly as the original, and where each side should dig in.

Then consider aggregation. Suppose the same maneuver works separately for each modality: a no-visual-experience world, a no-auditory-experience world, a no-pain world, and so on, each individually conceivable while all else stays the same. Does it follow that the full zombie world — the world with no phenomenal consciousness at all — is conceivable? Or might conceivability fail to compose in this way, so that each subtraction is conceivable on its own but the total subtraction is not? Explain where a proponent of the conceivability argument should resist the composition, where a physicalist should attack it, and what the resulting standoff reveals about what the conceivability argument was really relying on all along.
