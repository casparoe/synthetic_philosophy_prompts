I've been reading the rationalist blogs for years, and I'm fluent in Bayesian epistemology as that community uses it: priors, posteriors, likelihood ratios, model evidence, calibration, hypothesis classes, and the difference between updating inside a model and replacing the model wholesale. My philosophy of science is spottier. I know the broad outlines of Popper and Kuhn but not the fine details of their arguments, and I don't fully trust my own paraphrase of them.

I'd like you to restate the Kuhn–Popper dispute over whether science is rational during a scientific revolution in that Bayesian vocabulary, as a translation exercise rather than an analogy. Give Bayesian renderings of at least these elements: Popper's falsificationism and his asymmetry between refutation and confirmation; Kuhn's picture of normal science as puzzle-solving within a paradigm; the accumulation of anomalies and the onset of crisis; the revolutionary switch to a new paradigm; and incommensurability, including the idea that rival paradigms define evidence and standards differently and that no neutral algorithm settles the choice between them.

Then give me a residue report. For each element, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be expressed in the Bayesian framework at all. In each case, indicate whether the leftover is a limitation of the Bayesian vocabulary or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it seemed. Finally, after the translation: is the question "was that revolution rational?" answered, dissolved, or still open? If it is still open, locate the hard part precisely — in the choice of priors, in the choice of hypothesis space, in specifying what counts as evidence, or somewhere else.
