Having spent my career in the lab, I can't help trying to operationalize the harm principle I've been reading about in Mill. Suppose a big professional society adopted this rule for its yearly meeting: you can ask them to disinvite a speaker or cancel a session only if you belong to the specific group the speech suposedly harms; the request then goes to a panel of 11 members picked by lot, which can bar the talk only by a vote of 8 to 3 or better, and only on the grounds that the speech is more likely than not to cause concrete harm to identifiable people and that no lesser measure would prevent it; and the panel's written ruling lapses after 12 months and can never be cited as precedent. The idea is to make restriction track the harm principle — no restriction except to stop harm to others — and load the dice in favor of letting the speech go ahead. But I don't want to know whether this is a good rule in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually run: who gets through the standing filter and who doesn't, how people game the lottery or the 8–3 threshold, what happens the week after a ruling lapses, and which of these failure modes can be fixed without turning the whole thing into a paper tiger. I expect the real philosophical action is in what the panel ends up counting as concrete, likely, and no lesser measure, and who ends up controlling those definitions.
