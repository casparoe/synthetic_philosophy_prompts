I keep going in circles on a thing that came up while reading about Pearl's causal hierarchy. A friend walked me through the following and I can't find the grab-point:

1. Everything we ever get from a dataset is, at bottom, a table of numbers. Variables took values and we wrote them down. So everything we learn from data is a fact about some joint distribution P(...) — the bottom rung, the seeing level.
2. A randomized controlled trial is no exception. It's just a joint distribution over a slightly bigger set of variables — the randomizer R, the assigned treatment T, the outcome Y, covariates, whatever. The fact that R was independent of the confounders is either an independence in P (if you measured the confounders) or a claim about unmeasured variables that the data themselves can't check. Either way, all you've got is seeing-level facts.
3. Pearl's ladder says the doing level is strictly more informative than the seeing level: P(y | do(x)) is not in general P(y | x), and no amount of seeing-level facts reconstructs a doing-level fact unless causal assumptions are smuggled in.
4. But to identify P(y | do(x)) from data, you already need the causal graph, or enough of it to know which arrows the do-operator severs and which backdoors remain.
5. So the causal assumptions supply exactly the knowledge that was up for grabs, and the do-calculus just propagates those assumptions into new notation.
6. Which makes structural causal models look unfalsifiable by data: whatever fit you get, a different graph can be built to match the same associations — flip some arrows — so the causal story is always your choice, not the data's.
7. So causal inference from data is either circular (you recover only the causal assumptions you put in) or impossible (you can't get causal conclusions out of purely associational data). And the do-calculus, which was supposed to license causal claims from data, can't.

I can't accept (7) — epidemiology and social science do seem to learn causal facts from data — and yet (3) looks right to me, and (5) has the ring of something people say approvingly. Where does the argument first go wrong, and what do you give up by rejecting that step?
