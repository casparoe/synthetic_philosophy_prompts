I have been trying to work out whether prosperity is good only through what it buys — comfort, freedom, security, the capacity to build things — or whether there is something good in the bare state of having more, even when none of what having-more normally produces ever reaches anyone. I do not think I believe that wealth is valuable in the way pleasure or knowledge is valuable; my question is narrower and, to me, harder: do my own judgments about wealth and abundance obey any principle, and if they do, which one?

I have noted down my rather stubborn first reactions to a set of cases. (1) A country discovers a large oil reserve that it could extract within a few years, but the discovery is kept secret and a moratorium on extraction will, as things actually go, never be lifted. I think the country is still better off. (2) The same discovery, except the oil sits in formations from which no feasible amount of energy could ever be recovered. I feel no gain at all. (3) A miser hoards a large fortune, never spends it, never gives it, has no heirs, and draws no comfort from it; his days are exactly as they would be if he were poor. I do not think the money made his life better. (4) Another person's outward life is just as frugal, but she is quietly sustained every day by knowing she could absorb a disaster if one came. I think her life is clearly better. (5) After a long run of good harvests, a village fills a granary that, by the conditions of the case, will never be opened and never needed; no one expects shortage, no one feels safer, and no one takes any pleasure in the granary itself. I still keep having the reaction that the village is better off for the abundance, though I cannot say how. (6) An economy doubles its measured output, and the entire increase goes into a vast monument no one visits, no one finds beautiful, and that collapses before anyone can maintain it. I do not count that as prosperity at all.

Notice that cases 1 and 3 look to me like the same structure — a real capacity, never used, never felt — and yet I judge one a gain and the other a non-event, and I am not sure I can say what separates them. My best attempt at a principle is this: wealth adds to a life or a society only when it makes possible some good that would not otherwise have been available, whether the good is actually obtained, would be obtained if circumstances changed, or is presently felt as confidence in being able to obtain it. But that principle seems to call cases 1 and 5 wrong, and those are the very judgments I am least willing to surrender — a friend of mine says, not entirely kindly, that I am confusing a balance sheet with a life.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, and what does it say about the case I keep circling: a person who spends a lifetime converting every surplus dollar into gold bars that he seals in a vault, intending never to spend them, and whose vault is walled up untouched after his death? I swing between thinking the hoard added something to his life and thinking it was dead weight. If the cleanest principle is a simple one, which of my verdicts does it force me to give up, and what exactly am I paying?
