I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and I keep having the same argument with my flatmate. A few months after moving to Bristol he met someone who shared his birthday and his first name, and who'd grown up in the same small Borders town as his mum. He's now conviced it can't be chance: he multiplies 1/365 by the frequency of his name (he reckons roughly 1 in 1,500) by the population of that town relative to the UK (he gets roughly 1 in 4,000) and comes out somewhere around one in two billion. To me this looks like the birthday paradox turned inside out, but I'd like to be precise about where his reasoning goes wrong instead of just waving at 'coincidences happen.'

Here's the task. Treat the situation as a variation on the classic birthday puzzle. The classic result says that in a room of 23 people the chance that *some* pair shares a birthday is about fifty-fifty, even though the chance that someone shares *a specified* birthday is much smaller. Now add a second wrinkle: suppose you meet N=200 people, and for each person you could in principle record F=50 independent categorical facts, each with 365 equally likely values. A 'spooky coincidence' is then defined, only after looking at the data, as any two people agreeing exactly on some triple of these facts. Work out (i) the probability that a pre-specified pair agrees on a pre-specified triple; (ii) the expected number of coincidences if you scan all pairs and all triples; and (iii) the expected number if one person is held fixed, as in 'someone matched me', so he is compared only against the other 199. Then explain which of these corresponds to what my flatmate actually did, how the classic some-pair-versus-specified-birthday distinction generalises to the many-feature case, and what the corrected null model does and doesn't show about his anecdote. I'm not after 'therefore it was meaningless' or 'therefore it was fate' — I want to know how much of his surprise survives once the post hoc selection of features is modelled properly, and where the remaining uncertainty lives (dependence among facts, non-uniform values, common causes like social circles and ancestry). Lead with a one-sentence bottom line, then the maths, then the epistemic bit. Hard limit: 700 words. British English, please.
