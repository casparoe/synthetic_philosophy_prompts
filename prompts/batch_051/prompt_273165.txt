You are an expert philosopher of science who works on idealization in scientific models and on the question of when false assumptions are epistemic vices rather than permissible instruments. Treat me as a serious interlocutor, but before answering, brainstorm several different ways the two cases below might be mapped onto one another; then think through the implications of each mapping carefully before settling on your view.

I am an undergraduate taking a philosophy of science course on models and idealization. We have been working through Friedman's "as-if" methodology — what the course calls the F-twist — and the standard objections to it. I also follow some work on interpretability and alignment in machine learning, and I cannot shake a parallel between the two.

Roughly, the F-twist says that the realism of a model's assumptions is not what matters when we assess the model; what matters is whether it yields good predictions for the class of phenomena it is meant to cover. Friedman's stock examples describe systems as if their parts were solving optimization problems well beyond their actual capacities: the firm as if maximizing a profit function, an expert billiards player as if planning the shot, leaves arranged as if each one sought to maximize sunlight exposure. In each case the optimization assumption is literally false as an account of the internal process, but it organizes prediction well, and Friedman's point is that this literal falsity is not a defect.

Now take a cluster of practices in machine learning and AI safety. Researchers often model a trained network — a language model or a reinforcement-learning policy — as a coherent expected-utility maximizer whose objective can be more or less recovered from behavior. They infer its "goal," reason about what an agent with that goal would do under distribution shift, and use "the model is optimizing X" as an explanatory and predictive idiom. But at the level of weights, activations, and training dynamics there is no literal utility function being computed; and the objective used in training — the loss or reward — need not coincide with the objective the network's behavior looks as if it is maximizing. So this, too, is as-if optimization: an admittedly false description of the system's inner procedure, justified by apparent predictive and explanatory usefulness.

I can see two readings. On one, it is the same methodological move: a selection-like process (market competition for firms; gradient-based training for networks) makes behavior approximately optimizational in the relevant domain, and what licenses the as-if description is predictive success rather than mechanistic accuracy. On the other, the cases may come apart. The market story concerns populations of firms, and its limits are understood at that level; the ML story, by contrast, is being asked to support claims about one particular system's goal and about its future behavior under distribution shift — precisely where the as-if idiom often fails. Gradient descent does not select among independent firms the way market exit does; it iteratively modifies one set of parameters, and the inferred "goal" is a further modeling choice, not the objective the training process literally pursues at the behavioral level. Friedman's program also resists judging assumptions directly, whereas much interpretability work tries hard to test, and sometimes mechanistically validate, the goal-ascriptions it makes.

My question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Specifically: (1) Give the strongest statement of the structural identity between the two as-if moves — that is, what would have to be true of both sides for an objection to the F-twist to transfer automatically to the ML case. (2) Show where the mapping breaks; name at least one claim about the ML case that the economic parallel would license but that actually fails, or the converse. (3) Say whether the ML practice is best understood as a legitimate heir of the F-twist, an illegitimate borrowing of it, or a genuinely different methodological claim that merely shares its vocabulary — and if it is different, give the cleanest name for the difference. Throughout, be explicit about how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
