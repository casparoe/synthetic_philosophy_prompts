Im in a metaphysics class and were reading Locke on identity. In the Essay Concerning Human Understanding II.xxvii, Locke distinguishes a mass of matter from a living body: as I understand him, he says a mass stays the same only while its particles are the same, but a living body stays the same through continued life even as particles are lost and replaced. I am paraphrasing, not quoting. The standard statue-and-clay puzzle is that a clay statue and the lump of clay coincide but can come apart: squash the statue and the statue is destroyed while the lump survives. But change the case like this. On Monday a sculptor makes a clay statue. Over the next year she replaces every molecule of the original clay one at a time with new clay, always keeping the shape intact, and saves all the removed molecules in a box. At the end, none of the original molecules remain in the statue. Then she uses the saved original molecules to make a perfect duplicate statue. Now there are two statues: one whose shape and location have been continuous from Monday, and one made of exactly the molecules that composed the original statue. On Lockes distinction, which object is the same mass of matter as Mondays clay? Which is the same statue? Does the usual point that the statue and the lump can come apart survive this kind of gradual replacement, or does the mass-body distinction become less useful here than in the squashing case?
