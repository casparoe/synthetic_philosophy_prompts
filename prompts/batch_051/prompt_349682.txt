I am trying to work out what, if anything, makes negative emotions worth having, and I would like you to help me turn my case judgments into a principle. I am not asking whether sadness or grief can be useful—plainly they can, as signals or as motives. The question is whether some sadness or grief is worthwhile apart from any information or behavior it produces, and whether a person who retained all love and knowledge but simply lacked these states would be missing something.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

A. M's husband of forty years dies. She grieves deeply and for a long time. The grief gives her no new information—she already knows exactly what she has lost—and it improves no decision or action that anyone can identify. At the worst point she is offered a treatment that would remove all grief for a week while leaving memory, cognition, and ordinary sadness at new losses intact; she refuses, and later says the grief was part of what the marriage meant. I think she was right, and that treating the grief as a mere cost would be a mistake.

B. N embezzled money from a friend years ago, confessed, repaid it, and was forgiven. His residual remorse is painful, but it is the very thing that has kept him scrupulous in his later job handling other people's funds. I count his remorse as clearly valuable, and I am willing to allow that its value may be entirely instrumental.

C. O has followed a serialized story for years and loves one of the characters. When the character dies, O weeps for an evening; the sadness changes no belief and no behavior. I do not think anything of value would have been lost if O had missed those episodes and never mourned the character.

D. P loved his older brother, but when the brother dies suddenly P feels almost no sadness—not because of shock, medication, or suppression, but because he seems simply to lack the response. He continues to honor promises he made to the brother, speaks of him with warmth, and says the brother mattered more to him than anyone else. I find something missing in P's relation to his brother; the fact that he feels fine is, by itself, a flaw.

E. Q has a depressive condition that he did not choose. His sadness is full of false content—it tells him he is worthless and that his friends despise him—and it loops back on itself. It has shown no informational or motivational value despite years of effort, and it markedly impairs his agency. I would remove it by any safe means without hesitation.

The pair that troubles me most is A and C: in both cases someone feels sadness over losing something they love, and in neither case does the sadness change anything, yet I treat one as worthwhile and the other as trivial. I need a principle that either respects that difference or shows that one of my verdicts is wrong.

The half-formed principle I keep returning to is this: negative emotions are worthwhile to the extent that they are the way a person stays properly oriented to something they love. But as stated it seems to cover C, and every crucial term in it is vague.

Please do the following. (1) State the best precise principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures as many of my five verdicts as possible without being gerrymandered to them; for each clause in the principle, say which case motivates it. (2) Tell me which of my verdicts each candidate principle cannot accommodate, and say whether saving all five would require a principle so complicated or ad hoc that I should instead drop one of the verdicts; if so, identify the verdict and explain why. (3) Apply the account you end up with to a further case I have not listed: S is voluntarily childless, and every few years she feels a brief, sharp sadness at the thought that she will never raise a child; the sadness never changes her decision and passes in minutes. Is that sadness worthwhile or merely a cost, and which feature of the case decides the matter?
