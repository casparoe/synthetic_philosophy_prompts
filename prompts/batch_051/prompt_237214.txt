i retired from condensed matter physics a few years back and have only recently started reading philosophy, so forgive me if i mangle the vocabulary. my whole working life i was an unreflective reductionist: the right description of any system is the underlying microstructure, and the macroscopic story is just a convenient summary of what the particles are doing. i never questioned it, mostly because it works — you get the liquid from its molecules, and so on.

but the closer i look at the things i actually studied, the less tidy it gets. at a continuous phase transition, the same set of critical exponents shows up in a ferromagnet and in a liquid-gas mixture — systems that could hardly be more different at the particle level. universality classes lump physically different systems together, and the renormalization group story says that as you coarse-grain, almost all of the microscopic detail flows away; the interesting macroscopic behavior depends on only a few broad features like dimensionality and symmetry. so the tool reductionism itself gave us seems to say that the lower level is, in the technical sense, largely irrelevant to the behavior we care about.

and yet i have a firm intution that the transition, or the pressure, or the magnetization, is not a second-class fact — that there is something genuinely there at the macroscopic level that you miss if you insist that really there are only particles. i cant shake it, even though it sounds like the wooly holism i used to make fun of.

so here's my problem. i'm drawn to reductionism because of its system: one fundamental level, one set of laws, no mysterious downward causation, a clear test for what exists. and i'm also stuck with the intuition that higher-level features are real and explanatory in a way that isnt just my ignorance or my finite computing budget. which should yield? is this a case where the intuition ought to be debunked — say, it is an artifact of how a slow creature summarizes a system it cannot compute — or a case where the theory has to be amended, so that being reduced to the parts no longer means being nothing but the parts? or is there a reading on which both survive once i distinguish, for instance, a complete description of a system from an explanation of its behavior? give me the strongest version of each move, and tell me what kind of consideration should make me take one rather than another. if the intuition is the thing that has to go, i want to know what sort of argument could actually make me let go of it.
