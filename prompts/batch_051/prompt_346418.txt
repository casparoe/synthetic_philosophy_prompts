My philosophy advisor keeps ending arguments with "truth is what works," and until last week I thought that was just a dumb slogan. Then he said the real problem is that people conflate the Peircean and Jamesian versions, and the slogan picks up different content depending on which one you're smuggling in. Can you seperate out the distinct precise claims "truth is what works" could be making, from the trivial ones (roughly, that beliefs which survive practical tests are usually reliable) through to the radical ones (roughly, that truth is just whatever it's advantageous to believe, full stop)? For each reading say whether it's a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or just false. Then sort the readings into what feels Peircean (truth as where inquiry would eventually settle, the long-run stable opinion) and what feels Jamesian (truth as a kind of good, beliefs made true by their practical fruits), and tell me which reading, if any, would actually justify using the slogan to shut down the specific question of whether there are truths that nobody will ever be able to verify.
