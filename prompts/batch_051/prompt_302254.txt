Ok so my flatmate and I got cornered by this at 2am and neither of us can find the exit. We were going through Al-Ghazali's occasionalism, and she reconstructed it as an argument that seems way too strong:

(1) When cotton touches fire and burns, the fire isn't what makes it burn. God creates the burning directly, right at the moment of contact.
(2) So there isn't any necessary connection between fire and burning, and burning isn't even part of what fire is.
(3) If there's no connection of that kind, then all the times I've seen fire burn things give me no more reason to expect burning next time than to expect the cotton to turn into a small goat.
(4) So anyone who accepts Al-Ghazali's view about causes can't rationally rely on fire burning cotton, food nourishing them, or the sun coming up tomorrow.
(5) But Al-Ghazali clearly doesn't think ordinary knowledge collapses; he says God runs the world by a stable custom that we can rely on.
(6) So the view undercuts itself: it makes ordinary expectation irrational, then helps itself to ordinary expectation anyway.

shes pretty sure the argument is airtight, and I want to keep (1) and (5) if there's any way to do it. Which step should I reject, and what do I actually pay for rejecting it? Whenever I try, I end up saying "well, its still probable that fire burns cotton," but I have no idea what "probable" is supposed to mean here if there's no necessary connection backing it.
