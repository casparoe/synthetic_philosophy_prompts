I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, and I'm writing this year's final exam. I want one essay question on costly signaling and the handicap principle, pitched at students who have worked through the standard material on the handicap principle, the differential-cost formalization of it, and the distinction between costly signals, indices, and conventional signals. The question must not be answerable by reciting the peacock or the gazelle: it has to force students to say what, exactly, the cost in costly signaling is buying.

Please draft the question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks. The question should present short descriptions of three fictional display traits in some species, constructed so that one trait is honest because its cost is differentially borne by higher- and lower-quality signalers (a genuine handicap), one is honest because its intensity is mechanically or causally tied to the advertised quality (an index), and one is cheap to produce but honest because dishonesty is punished by receivers (a conventional signal kept honest by receiver-imposed costs). For each trait, ask the students to identify which mechanism best explains its honesty and to explain what role, if any, the signal's cost plays. Then add a general question asking them to articulate the condition under which a costly display is a handicap, and to explain why "the display is expensive" is not by itself an explanation of its honesty.

The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one, with marks for each deserved move. In particular, it should reward the following: (i) stating the differential-cost condition precisely — the marginal or viability cost of a unit of display must be lower for higher-quality signalers; (ii) explaining why costs that signalers bear uniformly cannot by themselves make a signal honest; (iii) distinguishing an index from a handicap, and explaining why an index's production cost is incidental to its honesty; (iv) recognizing that receiver-imposed costs can keep a cheap signal honest and that this is not a pure handicap; and (v) handling boundary cases, such as a trait that is both condition-dependent and causally constrained. For each criterion, add a one-line note on the specific mistake students typically make — for example, treating any costly signal as a handicap, or classifying a mechanically constrained signal as a handicap because displaying it is "costly."

Do not ask for readings, bibliographies, or citations. The question should be answerable entirely from the conceptual distinctions, and the fictional traits should do all the work of forcing those distinctions.
