I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a workshop on economic inequality and the moral psychology of resentment. The audience will be a mix of philosophers, policy researchers, and community organizers. I would like the speech to be organized around Rawls's distinction between resentment, which he treats as a moral reaction to a distribution one takes to be unjust, and envy, which he treats as a non-moral hostility toward another's greater good, together with his under-discussed claim that envy becomes excusable when inequalities are so deep that they undermine the less advantaged's self-respect. The speech should use these ideas to pose the workshop's organizing question: when is the anger of the less advantaged a vice to be overcome, and when is it a signal of background injustice that a well-ordered society has reason to take seriously? Please let the distinction do real structural work: the remarks should neither simply condemn envy as a failing nor simply vindicate resentment as righteous, but should explain what makes envy excusable and what it would mean for institutions to respond to that fact. Aim for roughly 800 words, and pitch it so the organizers and policy researchers can follow it while the philosophers still find it precise.
