I'm assembling a unit on the philosophy of artificial intelligence and want to set E.M. Forster's The Machine Stops alongside the recent discussion of epistemic dependence and the erosion of understanding. The novella gives us a society in which people live alone in underground cells and have handed over nearly every material and intellectual task to a single, centralised Machine: it carries them, communicates for them, and selects what they need to know, so that knowledge tends to reach people second-hand. Vashti is largely content with this arrangement, while her son Kuno insists on the difference between having an answer delivered and knowing the thing for oneself. I'd like a careful reading of the story through the lens of the contemporary worry that outsourcing cognition to AI can erode understanding rather than merely extend it: the idea that a person can have every answer available at hand without grasping the matter, and that a society can quietly lose the second while retaining the first.

My working hypothesis is that Forster dramatises something the philosophical literature often underplays, namely that the loss of understanding is not simply a cognitive deficit but a shift in what people value, trust, and find intelligible. At the same time, I am wary of flattening the novella into a straightforward allegory for the present day: the Machine is a single, dependable, centralised authority, whereas current AI systems are distributed, fallible, and commercially shaped, and the story's ending turns on a total infrastructural collapse that does not map cleanly onto present risks. I'd therefore like a reading that is faithful in both directions: show where the story illuminates or complicates the philosophical worry about epistemic dependence on AI, and where an honest critic should resist the analogy. Finally, say what the fiction supplies to the debate that the arguments alone do not. Please answer in British English.
