I'm not any kind of lawyer or philosophy person, I just got picked for jury duty a while back and since then I've been reading up on criminal law cases, and I keep noticing I have strong feelings about punishing people who didn't mean to do anything and couldn't have known better. I wrote down my verdicts on a few cases and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent. I've seen people call this strict liability.

A trucker does all the inspections he's supposed to, but a hidden crack in a brake line that nobody could have caught causes a wreck that kills someone. I don't think he should be punished. The company that did sloppy maintenance records feels different to me.

A bartender cards a kid who hands him a flawless fake ID, he checks it the way he was trained, and the kid turns out to be seventeen. I still think the bartender should be cited or fined. If you take the job of being the person who checks IDs, you own the mistake even when it's not really your fault.

A guy borrows a friends car and there's a baggie of drugs under the spare tire from the friend. He doesn't know anything about it. He gets charged with possession. I think that's clearly wrong. Possession should mean you actually know you have the thing.

A food company executive signs off on shipments from a plant where, without him knowing, a manager under him cut corners, and some jars get contaminated and kill a couple people. He had decent systems in place and didn't cut the corners himself. Part of me hates the idea of putting him in prison, but I also feel like if the person who signs can always hide behind not knowing, companies will just organize themselves around ignorance.

A factory dumps waste that their own hired expert said was harmless, and it turns out to poison a towns water. I think the company should pay, obviously, but I'm genuinely torn about whether anyone should go to jail for it.

So my gut says some no-fault punishment is fine (the bartender, maybe the executive) and some isn't (the trucker, the borrower). The line in my head is something like: it matters whether you volunteered for a job where the whole point is being the guarantee that the bad thing never happens. But the factory case messes that up, since they did volunteer to be careful and hired an expert, and I still don't want to send anyone to jail. Is there a better principle that gets all of these, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? What would it say about a case I haven't thought of? Also tell me what would change your mind about the principle you end up with — what kind of case or argument would make you abandon it, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
