I keep flip-flopping on this, honestly. A friend and I have been arguing about whether moral revolutions are actually caused by better arguments, and we keep talking right past each other. One of us says they end because good arguments eventually win out: people were given reasons to think slavery or dueling was wrong, those reasons spread through pamplets, sermons, courts, and novels, and once enough people accepted them the practice lost it's legitimacy and collapsed. The other says arguments are mostly the soundtrack, not the engine. On this view what actually ends a practice is a shift in who gets honor and who gets shame, plus where material interests move. Dueling didn't end because someone finally proved it was wrong—everyone already knew it got people killed—but because it started to look ridiculous and unchivalrous. Footbinding ended when status competition among families got attached to something else. Abolition needed slave resistance and the interests of industrializing economies that wanted wage labor; moral argument was more the public story told after the causal work was done.

I'd like to see what a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce. So draft the joint statement the arguments-first camp and the honor-and-interests camp could both sign. Don't flatten either side into a caricature: the arguments camp should concede that arguments often go nowhere for centuries, and the honor/interests camp should concede that arguments do real work in coordinating new expectations and giving people a language for the new order. Format it as: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section, (2) a disagreements table—say three or four rows—where each side's view is put so precisely that its own side would accept that wording, (3) a cruxes list, meaning the specific sub-questions where settling them would move at least one side, and (4) for each crux, one historical case or natural experiment both sides would accept as genuinely bearing on it (dueling's decline in England, footbinding in China, British abolition, or similar), with each side's prediction about the case written down in advance. Keep the agreed claims non-vacuous but not slanted, and try to separate real disagreements from ones that were just about words.
