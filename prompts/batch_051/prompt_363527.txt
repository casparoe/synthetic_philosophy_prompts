My friend and I stayed up way too late last night arguing about embryo selection, and I walked away with a position I'd defend but not confidently. Roughly: if you're already doing IVF and have to choose which embryo to transfer, and all else is equal except that one is expected to have a much better quality of life than the other, then you should pick the better-off embryo. The reason isn't state policy or improving the world—it's that you'd be acting on behalf of the child you end up having. But I don't want this to cover parental-preference traits like height, eye colour, or IQ when its mostly about status or taste. So it's a weak, welfare-only, no-coercion version of procreative beneficence with a hard line against full eugenics.

My friend kept saying the welfare/preference line is unstable and that I'm really defending liberal eugenics with better branding, and also kept pressing the non-identity point—that the child who ends up existing is a different person either way, so "on behalf of the child" is shakier than it sounds. I sort of feel that but don't know how much it matters.

What I actually want is a map of what would count against this position. I already grant the horrible history of eugenics. What I mean is: what would have to be true about well-being, disability, parental love, diversity, or how selection plays out in practice for my version of the principle to be wrong? I'd like the defeaters separated into ones a defender would have to accept as real—fatal or at least deeply shaking—and ones they can shrug off as bullets they're already biting. And tell me honestly whether my qualifications—only within existing IVF, only welfare, no coercion—have made the view unfalsifiable, and whether that's itself a problem. Personally, the thing that would move me most is if selecting for expected welfare turned out to make parents worse at loving or accepting the child they actually get; that's the part I'm least settled on.
