I have been trying to determine whether video-game characters matter morally, and I have collected a set of my own verdicts that I cannot yet fit under one principle. I would like you to either extract the principle behind them or tell me which verdicts to abandon.

Here are the cases.

1. In an open-world game, procedurally generated pedestrians can be run over. There is no blood, no screaming, and no lasting consequence. I do this casually, and I do not think it is wrong.

2. In a narrative RPG, a companion character has accompanied my player-character for around sixty hours. There is an option to sacrifice the companion for a large mechanical benefit. I cannot do it. I know the companion is a script and has no inner states; I do not think taking the mechanical benefit would be a moral wrong, exactly, but sacrificing the companion would feel like a betrayal, and I would accept a real in-game cost to avoid it.

3. In a sandbox game, I build a functioning village and later delete that entire world to free storage. I feel nothing, and I take this to be obviously fine.

4. In another game, NPCs have what I would call simulated welfare: numeric hunger and injury variables plus behaviors — they forage when hungry, limp when injured, moan when in pain, and visibly languish. They are not sentient; the "welfare" is a state variable and an animation. I discover that I can cage a group of them in a room where hunger rises but cannot fall, producing an endless loop of distress displays. I find this repulsive. I think a player who spends real hours doing this for its own sake has done something wrong, or at least something defective — though I cannot say who has been wronged.

5. I learn that the "NPCs" in a game are actually avatars of human actors who are paid to play roles. Striking one to make it yelp is now, I think, straightforwardly wrong, because the yelp is a real person's performance. The on-screen behavior is identical to the pedestrian case, but the cases seem to me entirely different in kind.

My working hypothesis is that only sentient beings are moral patients, that in case 5 a sentient being is affected, and that in the other cases the only question is what a player's repeated choices express — whether a disposition is being cultivated. That hypothesis fits 1, 3, 4, and 5, but it threatens to make case 2 simply confused, and I am not sure I want to give case 2 up.

The case I keep returning to is a hybrid: a mod replaces the procedurally generated pedestrians with photorealistic figures who make eye contact, beg, and audibly suffer, while the underlying program remains exactly as non-sentient as before. Do my case-1 intuitions survive that mod, and should they?

Is there one account — about moral patienthood, simulated welfare, expressivist or virtue-theoretic wrongness, or something else — that delivers or at least explains all five verdicts? Where does it clash with my working hypothesis, which verdicts does it force me to revise, and what does it say about the mod case that I have not settled?
