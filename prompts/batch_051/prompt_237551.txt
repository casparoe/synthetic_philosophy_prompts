I got into a long argument with a friend last night about Sen's liberal paradox, and I'm still turning it over. He was insisting that the paradox exposes a real conflict inside liberalism between two things liberals want: respecting individual liberty and respecting unanimous preferences, i.e., the Pareto principle. I'd always thought Sen's point was more subtle—that the theorem mostly shows how badly social choice theory models rights, not that liberty and Pareto genuinely clash. But when I tried to make that reconciliation precise, I couldn't, and now I'm not sure which of us was closer.

Here's the setup as I understand it. Sen starts with two conditions. One is a Pareto condition: if every individual prefers social state x to social state y, then society should rank x above y. The other is a minimal liberty condition, something like: each person should be decisive over at least one pair of alternatives that is in some intuitive sense their own business. In Sen's example, Lewd gets to decide between "Lewd reads the book" and "nobody reads," and Prude gets to decide between "Prude reads the book" and "nobody reads." Fill in the standard preferences—Lewd ranks Prude reads > Lewd reads > nobody reads, and Prude ranks nobody reads > Prude reads > Lewd reads—and you get a cycle: Lewd's right forces Lewd reads > nobody, Prude's right forces nobody > Prude reads, and Pareto forces Prude reads > Lewd reads, since both prefer Prude reads to Lewd reads. So the social ranking goes in a circle, and there's no coherent way to satisfy both conditions at once.

Can these two principles actually be reconciled, or is the conflict genuine? Give me the candidate reconciliations you can think of—rights aren't preferences over outcomes, Pareto should ignore meddlesome preferences, the example is too degenerate, the formal setup conflates different senses of "liberty," that kind of thing—and say which ones you'd actually endorse and which feel like special pleading. I want a verdict, but I also want you to be explicit about how confident you are in it and what would change your mind. And if I've garbled the theorem or the example, tell me that plainly instead of working around it; I'd rather find out I'm confused than get a forced reconciliation.
