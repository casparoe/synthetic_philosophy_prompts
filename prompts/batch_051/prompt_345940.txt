After a long argument with a colleague about whether functional programming is genuinely easier to reason about or just better advertised, I've been trying to get clear on why mutation in ordinary imperative programs is so much harder to think about than the size of the operation would suggest. The position I've arrived at is this: the difficulty is not fundamentally about assignment, performance, or surface syntax. It is about the way mutation introduces state and identity behind the back of the value-level language. A mutable cell is one thing that persists while its contents change; two mutable cells with identical contents are nevertheless different things. So equality of current values no longer licenses substitution. Two variables can hold the same number while assigning to one of them leaves the other alone, and f(x) + f(x) need not equal 2 * f(x) once f can mutate hidden state. On this diagnosis, an expression's meaning depends on a hidden history, and sound reasoning about mutation has to run over evolving states and object identities rather than over values.

I want this diagnosis stress-tested rather than affirmed. The question is: what would have to be true for this account of why mutation is hard to be false or importantly incomplete? I would like a map of the position's exposure, not the standard functional-programming talking points. Which empirical findings about how programmers actually fail, which language-design results, and which formal developments would count decisively against the view, and which would merely force a qualification? For example, would it be a serious problem if novices struggle just as much with persistent immutable data structures, or if a language with mutation but no observable aliasing turned out to be easy to reason about? Would evidence locating the core difficulty in aliasing, evaluation order, or available reasoning tools rather than in mutation itself distinguish this account from its rivals or collapse it into one of them? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of the position should themselves concede, and give a committed verdict on whether the position is well insulated — and, if it is, whether that insulation is a strength or a sign that the view has stopped making a substantive claim.
