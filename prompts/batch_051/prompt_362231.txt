So I've ended up in a lot of late-night arguments with my friend Maya, who's doing a PhD in behavior genetics, and I've been collecting her one-line rulings on claims people make about heritability. The ones I'm pretty sure about are these. She says it's wrong to say that if height is 80% heritable then 80% of your height comes from your genes and the rest from your environment. She says it's wrong to go from "this trait is substantially heritable within each of two populations" to "the average difference between those populations must be partly genetic." She says it's wrong to say a high heritability means environmental interventions won't do much to change the trait. She says it's right that heritability is a property of a population in a given range of environments, not a claim about how much an individual's genes caused their own trait. She says it's right that, other things equal, making everyone's environment more uniform would push heritability up. And she says it's wrong that "heritable" just means "runs in families," because a shared family environment can also make traits run in families without it being genetic variance. She'll also say heritability is the fraction of variance in a trait in a population that's attributable to genetic variance, where "attributable" is just variance decomposition, not a deep causal story.

Somebody wrote a blog post about a thought experiment where you take a sample of people, put them all on a strictly identical diet for a year, and the heritability of body weight in that sample comes out higher than in the general population where diets vary. The blogger's punchline is: "When you make environments uniform, genes become more important determinants of who weighs more."
