I am currently enrolled in an upper-division philosophy of mind course, and I have ended up defending a version of epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that qualia—what it is like to feel pain, see red, or be angry—are real, are produced by the physical, but have no causal powers of their own. On this picture, every bit of behavior ordinarily attributed to my pain—withdrawing, grimacing, saying that it hurts—is fully caused by the underlying physical state, and the pain itself is causally inert; the same physical causes would have produced the same behavior had the pain been absent or qualitatively different. I take the most serious objection to be the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If my conscious experiences never cause anything, then the fact that I judge, report, and even theorize about those experiences looks like a massive coincidence, and my own confident conviction that I have them starts to seem either inexplicable or self-undermining.

What would count against this position? I would like a ranking of considerations from those that would come closest to showing the view is false to those that would merely show it is costly. For each consideration, say whether it is a problem that a committed epiphenomenalist should themselves regard as genuine trouble, and not just an argument a critic would press. Which arguments, findings, or cases would show that epiphenomenalism is not just unappealing but unstable or false—for instance, because it cannot explain why introspective reports track experience so reliably, because it collapses into physicalism or eliminativism once it grants that the physical does all the causal work, or because evidence from conscious perception indicates that phenomenal character carries information with no underlying physical difference and that this information affects behavior? Which would show only that the view pays a price? I would also like a division of labor between empirical and a priori considerations: could any neuroimaging or psychophysical result actually settle the question, given that every piece of data must ultimately be reported by a conscious subject? Finally, give an honest assessment of the standard replies to the paradox of phenomenal judgment—for example, that we have a non-causal acquaintance with our own experiences, or that judgments about them are caused by the underlying physical states rather than by the experiences themselves. Do those replies protect the view from refutation, or do they insulate it by gradually qualifying the thesis until no possible outcome would count against it? If the latter, say whether that is a serious defect for a view of this kind or a defensible response to a question that may not be empirically decidable.
