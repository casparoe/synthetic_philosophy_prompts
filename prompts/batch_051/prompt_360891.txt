For a course on philosophy of religion, our teaching assistant circulated a one-paragraph summary of the three standard positions about how religions relate to one another. The summary strikes me as suspiciously tidy, and a friend drew an inference from it that seems too strong, but I do not have the distinctions nailed down well enough to say exactly why. The sheet reads as follows:

"Exclusivism is the view that only one religion is true, and therefore only its explicit adherents can be saved. Inclusivism is the view that other religions contain genuine but partial truth, and that their adherents can be saved, though only through the grace of the one true faith working quietly inside their own traditions. Pluralism is the view that no religion has a monopoly on truth: the great world religions are equally valid paths to the same divine reality, and their apparently conflicting doctrines are simply different descriptions of that one reality."

My friend then concluded: "So pluralism is just relativism with better manners. It makes every religion equally true, and a consistent pluralist is not allowed to regard her own tradition's claims as more correct than anyone else's." I would like an accuracy audit of the sheet and of my friend's inference. Please separate what is defensible from what is a common simplification, note which parts a defender of each position would protest, and say whether pluralism as it is usually worked out really commits one to holding that all doctrines are equally true. It would also help if you flagged where the summary runs together a view about truth and a view about who is saved, since I suspect those are not the same dimension.
