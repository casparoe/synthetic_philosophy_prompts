I'm writing a paper on standard-form adhesion contracts, and I keep getting stuck on a philosophical question about the clickwrap case. Courts in the United States generally enforce agreements in which a person clicks a button marked I agree and is bound to pages of terms they almost certainly did not read, terms drafted entirely by the company and often amendable later by the company alone. This seems to conflict with the rest of contract law, which usually refuses to treat silence as acceptance, treats a paper signature as binding partly because the signer had the document in front of them, and sometimes asks whether the parties genuinely reached a meeting of the minds. Reconstruct the view of agreement or consent that the practice of enforcing clickwrap presupposes: what must agreement be, such that a recorded click can count as agreeing even though there is no actual meeting of minds, no negotiation, and no realistic chance the terms were read and understood? Be precise about what the practice strictly requires of that view, as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in the practice if we instead required consent to be knowing and given term by term. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
