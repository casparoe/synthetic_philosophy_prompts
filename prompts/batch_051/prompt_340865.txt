Here's a question I can't get out of my head. The knowledge norm of assertion — roughly, you shouldn't assert p unless you know p — was originally defended by pointing at ordinary exchanges between individual speakers. If you say it's raining but I don't know that it is, that sounds defective in a way truth or belief norms don't quite capture, and your audience always seems entitled to ask how do you know? The background picture was an individual speaker with beliefs and evidence who can be challenged on the spot and either produce grounds or retract. Now suppose an increasing share of what people read in a day comes from large language models: systems without beliefs, without knowledge in the usual sense, with no accessible body of evidence, and whose providers explicitly disclaim the truth of the outputs. There is no particular person who can answer the how-do-you-know question on behalf of the string, and no one treats themselves as having vouched for it. Does the knowledge-norm argument survive this development? I can see roughly three moves: (a) the norm still goes through but now attaches to the provider or deployer as a kind of proxy asserter, (b) these outputs simply aren't assertions so the norm is untouched but now covers less of our actual information environment, or (c) the norm was never meant for this and we need a successor, like a norm requiring the system or provider to signal the grounds and reliability of each output. Which premise of the old argment is actually under pressure, and what's the strongest defensible version of the view once you've absorbed the change? Don't just say it depends; pick a position and tell me why the alternatives fail.
