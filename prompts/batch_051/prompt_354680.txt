The position I want stress-tested is this: the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — Dutch book arguments, representation theorems, and the fact that violating the axioms leaves an agent open to a sure loss or a money pump — successfully establish that any preference structure not representable as maximizing expected utility is irrational, at least for an ideally rational agent. I want you to map where this position is vulnerable, without rehashing only the familiar objections. Break the argument into its load-bearing pieces — completeness, transitivity, independence or the sure-thing principle, sharp probabilities, invariance under how a choice is described — and tell me, for each, what an agent or a world in which that piece fails would actually look like, and whether that failure is merely conceivable or is something we have reason to take seriously. Then take the main empirical violations — Allais-type choices, Ellsberg-type ambiguity aversion, preference reversals, framing effects — and give me a principled test for when such behavior counts as evidence against the norm rather than as subject error or an artifact of the experiment. Say whether there are formally coherent ways to be ambiguity-averse or risk-sensitive that avoid exploitability without maximizing expected utility, and whether their existence undermines the inference from coherence to expected utility specifically rather than to some weaker norm of consistency. Rank the candidate defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and identify which of them a committed defender of the position should concede as genuine and which that defender should dismiss as attacking a strawman. For my part, I would find a successful case that ambiguity aversion is rationally permissible the most persuasive against the position, and I suspect that framing effects are the least threatening; tell me whether that ordering survives scrutiny. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — by restricting it to ideal agents, to normative rather than descriptive claims, or to fully specified choice problems — and, if so, whether that insulation is a problem or a defensible feature of a view of this kind.
