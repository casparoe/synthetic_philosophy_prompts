Help me out with an argument a friend has been pressing on me, because I cant find the exit and the conclusion is stronger than seems sane. The issue is the old one about infinite regresses: why are some vicious and others benign? The standard examples are the truth regress — the claim that p is true, the further claim that "p is true" is true, and so on — which is supposed to be harmless; and the homunculus regress — you see because a little man in your head sees the image on your retina, and he sees because a smaller little man sees his image, and so on — which is supposed to be the paradigm of a bad one. My friend says the usual distinction cant survive its own test. His argument:

(1) A regress is benign exactly when nothing in the chain has to wait for the chain to bottom out: each member is settled at its own level, and the next member is just something that follows from it or is prompted by it, not something it depends on.
(2) That's why the truth regress is benign: the first truth does not wait for any later truth, because its already true and there is no last truth to wait for.
(3) But by the same test, the homunculus regress is benign. The first man's seeing is settled at its own level — he does see; the fact that his seeing gets explained by a further man's seeing is a matter of what happens further down the chain, and no later member has to be reached for him to see.
(4) If the homunculus regress is benign, then the regress cannot be the reason to reject the explanatory strategy behind it — explaining a creature's seeing by positing an inner seer. The regress is no objection to homuncular explanations.
(5) By the same move, no regress in which each member already possesses the property in question can ever be vicious. Vicious regresses, if they exist, would be only those where each member's very existence depends on the next.
(6) But that cant be right. "He sees because an inner seer sees" is an empty explanation precisely because it re-uses the very capacity it was supposed to explain. Somewhere between (1) and (5) the difference between a harmless iterating chain and an explanation that never discharges its job has gone missing.

I have a hunch the trouble is in how the test gets applied to the homunculus case, but I dont want to just insist that the homunculus regress is vicious by fiat. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the homunculus case as vicious than defend (1) as stated. Can you name the step to reject or repair, say why it looked fine, and say what the correct criterion for viciousness is after the repair — and what I'd pay if I kept (1) instead? Lead with the step you'd reject in one sentence, and keep the whole diagnosis under 300 words.
