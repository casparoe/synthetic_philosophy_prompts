Not long ago a student came to my office hours and described a puzzle I have been turning over ever since. She and her partner had chosen each other, she said, by an explicit inventory: each could state what they brought and what they required, and on every item the match was good. Years later the list had not changed and the partner had not changed, yet the reason for staying seemed to her to have changed altogether — she stayed because this was her person, not because the qualifying properties were still in place. What struck me is that much of our romantic practice appears to presuppose one view of love's reasons, while the institution of marriage appears to presuppose another. The machinery of modern partner selection — platforms that filter by education, income, religion, and views about children, the advice to know one's non-negotiables before a first date, the habit of asking whether someone is good on paper — treats the reasons for loving someone as ascertainable in advance, impersonally checkable properties of the person, so that a better match is always conceivable and one should keep searching until the criteria are met. The marriage vow, by contrast, binds spouses to one another precisely when the favorable properties are absent or destroyed — through poverty, illness, or failure — and is widely understood to make the relationship less, not more, revisable by a better offer.

I would like to understand this tension rigorously. Philosophers sometimes mark it as the difference between an appraisal view of love, on which love answers to the beloved's antecedent value, and a bestowal view, on which love confers a value the beloved would not otherwise have for the lover. Which of these does each practice strictly presuppose, and which does it merely fit well with? Where is each practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several different theories of love's reasons would license it? Can the two practices be read as governing different stages of love, so that the apparent inconsistency dissolves? And what would each institution look like if it were rebuilt on the rival assumption — a matching practice designed on the bestowal view, and a marriage vow designed on the appraisal view?
