I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been trying to work through Rawls's later account of religion in democratic politics—not as a specialist, but as someone who has to explain to readers why the dispute matters. The account attracts me for its systematic virtues. It offers a sharp criterion of legitimacy: coercive laws must be justifiable to reasonable citizens by reasons they could accept, not by invoking a comprehensive religious or philosophical doctrine they cannot be expected to share. It explains how a free society can remain stable among people who disagree about God, the good, and the afterlife. And it does not simply banish religious speech; it gives it a disciplined role under a proviso that sectarian reasons may enter public argument if, in due course, reasons framed in shareable political values are offered as well.

Yet I cannot shake a contrary conviction. Demanding that a devout citizen recast her deepest reasons in a public idiom before they may count politically seems not to be a neutral requirement but a substantive philosophical position about what is allowed to count as a reason at all. It risks treating as peripheral the very thing that makes some citizens' political commitments morally serious to them, and in that sense it may fail its own test of being acceptable to all reasonable people. I am not asking for a verdict between the theory and the intuition. I am asking what it would take for each side to yield: what would have to be true for the intuition to be a mere discomfort that the theory rightly teaches us to override; what would have to be true for the intuition to reveal a defect that the theory must repair; and whether there is a version of the account that preserves both by distinguishing forums, roles, or subject matters—for example, by binding judges and legislators more strictly than citizens in the background culture, or by applying it to constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice rather than to every legislative choice. In answering, please distinguish what is solidly established about Rawls's view from what is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
