I've been re-reading Rousseau's *The Social Contract*, and I keep getting stuck on a passage in Book II, Chapter 3, in the G. D. H. Cole translation. The passage runs:

“It follows from what has gone before that the general will is always right and tends to the public advantage; but it does not follow that the deliberations of the people are always equally correct.”

Rousseau then adds, roughly, that our will is always for our own good but we do not always see what that is; the people is never corrupted, but it is often deceived, and only then does it seem to will what is bad.

I want to defend a particular reading of this passage, and I want you to tell me what would count against it. Please answer in British English.

My position is this. Rousseau is making an error-theoretic claim about democratic deliberation, not just drawing a definitional contrast between the general will and the will of all. He distinguishes two ways collective judgement can go wrong. The people can be corrupted, meaning their will itself is no longer directed at the common interest. Or they can be deceived, meaning their will remains properly oriented but their practical judgement about what the common interest requires is factually or deliberatively mistaken. The passage I quoted is committed to saying that, for a people that still has a general will, only deception, not corruption, is ever genuinely possible. The general will is always right because it is defined as the will that aims at the public advantage; actual deliberation can fail only by failing to know what that advantage consists in.

On this reading, the central question is whether the distinction between corruption and deception can bear the weight Rousseau puts on it. I want a map of what would have to be true for that position to be false, not just the standard objections about the Legislator or the supposed vacuity of the general will.

Specifically:

1. If the corruption/deception distinction is not stable—if, for example, systematic self-serving bias in a large group is better described as the group's will being corrupted rather than the group's being deceived—does the position collapse, or can it absorb that by reclassifying such cases as failures of the epistemic conditions for a general will to emerge?

2. If the general will is always right only because it is defined as the will aiming at the common advantage, then the position seems unfalsifiable at the level of principle. Would a defender of this reading treat that unfalsifiability as a harmless feature of a normative ideal, or does it become a genuine problem because it makes the theory unable to be tested against actual democratic outcomes?

3. Which results from social psychology, voting theory, or information aggregation would count against the view that popular deliberation goes wrong mainly through deception rather than corruption? I am thinking of preference reversal under framing effects, systematic polarisation, or majority cycles. Are these cases of the people being deceived, cases of their will being corrupted, or neither, and what does it mean if the theory has no natural category for them?

4. Suppose it turned out that a group's actual preferences diverge from the general will not because people are misinformed about the common good but because their private interests are structurally dominant—say, in a society with stable majority coalitions that reliably extract from a minority. Would that show the position is false, or only show that the conditions for a general will to manifest are almost never met in real politics? If the latter, does the position still risk anything?

5. Rousseau says in the passage that the people is only deceived, and on such occasions only does it seem to will what is bad. What would be a clear case where the people seems to will what is bad but cannot plausibly be described as deceived? If such cases are possible, is that a decisive defeater or merely an awkward finding?

I want you to distinguish between decisive defeaters, merely awkward findings, and things that a committed defender would dismiss as misdescriptions. I would also like an honest assessment of whether this reading has been insulated by qualifications, and whether that insulation is a vice in a political theory of this kind. If the position is well insulated, does it still have normative or predictive content? Finally, tell me whether you think the corruption/deception distinction is doing real work here or simply redefining the general will into infallibility.
