I'm an analyst at a regional disability-services authority that administers an annual independent-living fund for adults with disabilities. Eligibility is settled elsewhere; the fund pays for personal assistance hours, home modifications, assistive technology, and transport subsidies, and each year it receives roughly three times as many eligible applications as it can finance. Case managers are qualified social workers but have no background in political philosophy, spend about ninety minutes per file, and their ranking decisions are audited for consistency and can be appealed to an administrative judge. I want you to turn the equality-of-what debate into a decision procedure the case managers can actually run.

The procedure should take seriously three candidate currencies, which I will summarize:
- Welfare: the relevant unit is the difference a benefit makes to how well the person's life actually feels or goes — pain, distress, satisfaction, mood. A distribution is fair when it tends to equalize or improve these states.
- Resources: the relevant unit is a fair bundle of general-purpose external means, with adjustments for unchosen disadvantage, and neutrality about what people do with the bundle. After resources are fairly allocated, how much welfare or functioning someone derives is not the agency's business.
- Capabilities: the relevant unit is the real freedom to achieve a set of basic functionings — mobility, communication, bodily integrity, social participation — not the means and not achieved satisfaction. Fairness is about closing gaps in substantive opportunity, not equalizing feelings.

Write the procedure as an ordered set of questions a case manager can answer from a case file, with a yes/no or numeric output at each step and clear stop rules. For each step, say in plain language what question is being asked, what in the file counts as a sufficient answer, and which currency the step operationalizes. The procedure must face the standard hard cases head-on rather than hiding them: a cheerful person with severe limitations, an isolated and miserable person with intact capabilities, a person whose same impairment converts resources into functioning unusually poorly, and an applicant who requests far more than the minimum that restores a functioning. Say explicitly how conflicts among the three currencies are resolved — by a fixed priority rule, a dominance rule, or a handoff to a supervisor — and mark every point where a policy judgment is being dressed up as a technical step.

Then run the procedure on three applications:
1. Two applicants with the same spinal-cord injury and the same need for a bathroom modification: one lives with a large family, has a spouse's income, and reports high life satisfaction; the other lives alone, has no family support, and scores badly on standard distress and loneliness measures.
2. An applicant requesting a $95,000 through-floor elevator when a $17,000 stairlift would restore the same indoor mobility, with the extra cost defended mainly by greater comfort and dignity.
3. A person with severe, chronic grief and no relevant impairment, requesting ongoing funding for a companion animal and counseling, whose projected welfare gain per dollar is large but who has no capability gap on your functioning list.

For each, show where the three currencies would rank the application, what the procedure instructs in light of the conflict rule, and whether the result is one the caseworkers are likely to dislike. After the worked cases, say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide — the kinds of cases where no reformulation of the questions would remove the need for a value choice that belongs to an elected body or a judge. Finally, state how confident you are that the procedure faithfully tracks the distinctions the three views actually draw, and identify the single step or judgment you are least sure of, including which alternative reading of one of the views would most change your answer.
