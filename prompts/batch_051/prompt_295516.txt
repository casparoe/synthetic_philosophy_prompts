I keep returning to a puzzle I first encountered in rationalist discussions of decision theory, and I would like a serious essay on it rather than a survey. The Pasadena game runs as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^{n−1} · 2^n / n dollars: +$2 if heads comes on the first toss, −$2 if it comes on the second, +$8/3 if on the third, and so on. Enumerating the possible outcomes in their natural order and summing probability times payoff gives the alternating harmonic series, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, roughly 69 cents. But that convergence is only conditional: the positive terms and the negative terms each sum to infinity, so the expectation is undefined in the usual sense, which defines it via positive and negative parts separately; and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, a suitable reordering of the same terms can be made to converge to any real number, or to diverge.

That seems to force a choice among genuinely different views. One could hold that the natural enumeration is not arbitrary, since it tracks the actual order of the coin tosses, so the game simply is worth about 69 cents. One could hold that a quantity which changes under rearrangement is not an expected value at all, and that the Pasadena game has no determinate fair price until extra structure is imposed. One could also defend an intermediate position: adopt a weak notion of expectation relative to some privileged ordering, bound the utility function, discount distant outcomes, or replace numerical expectation with a partial order based on dominance. Each move has costs elsewhere in decision theory, and I want those costs made explicit.

Please write an essay on how decision theory ought to handle the Pasadena game and conditionally convergent expectations more generally. Lay out the strongest case for taking ln 2 seriously as the game's value, the strongest case for treating its value as undefined, and any intermediate proposals you find defensible; then reach and defend an explicit verdict rather than ending non-committally. Engage the losing positions at their best, and avoid citations or reading lists—I am interested in the argument itself, not in learned references.
