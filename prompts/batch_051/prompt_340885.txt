You are a professional philosopher who works in moral psychology and Hellenistic ethics, and you are good at reflective equilibrium: testing candidate principles against a person's case-by-case intuitions and saying plainly where the fit fails. I want you to do that for my intuitions about equanimity and the ancient ideal of freedom from disturbance — the Stoic apatheia and the Epicurean ataraxia — and about whether freedom from disturbance is a good, and at what cost. Think slowly. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm two or three candidate principles, run them against my cases, and note where each one breaks down. Then state your best candidate precisely, show how it handles each case, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot keep without ad hoc additions, and apply it to the undecided case at the end.

My cases, and what I find myself thinking about them, are these.

1. The steady surgeon. A surgeon is operating on a child who is crashing. She feels a wave of fear, notices it, and proceeds with steady hands; the fear neither freezes her nor travels into her hands. I think this is better than either a surgeon who is utterly unmoved or one who panics. The fear itself does not count against her equanimity; being hijacked by the fear would.

2. The unruffled father. A man whose son is dying tells me he has practised for years not to be disturbed by what lies outside his control, and that he now feels no grief or agitation, only a settled resolve to do what can be done. I do not find this admirable in the way the surgeon is admirable. It seems to me he is missing something that grief would be the correct response to, and I am inclined to say his freedom from disturbance is itself a kind of failure, or at least purchased at a price I am not willing to call a gain. But I cannot fully say what he has lost.

3. The misinformed patient. A dying patient has been told falsely that her cancer is cured. In the weeks before she dies she is consequently serene and undisturbed. I cannot call her tranquillity good, even though it is real and she never learns the truth. That pushes me toward the thought that the value of being undisturbed depends on whether the calm reflects how things are. But I am not sure that thought coheres with the surgeon's case, where what seems good is the steadiness itself rather than, or in addition to, the fit between her emotion and the situation.

4. The angry organiser. A political organiser is lastingly moved by anger at an injustice. The anger is unpleasant, keeps him awake, and is a disturbance in the ordinary sense; yet I think it is not merely instrumentally useful but the right relation to what he has seen. I also think that if the same anger swells into rage that stops him planning and listening, it ceases to be good. So the same first-order emotion seems good for him in one form and bad in another, and the difference seems to lie in how it relates to his agency. Is that coherent?

5. The second-order sufferer. Someone with a fear of flying is additionally ashamed of the fear and anxious that it might show when colleagues are nearby. I do not find her first-order fear especially bad. What I want to call pathological is the second-order layer — the distress about the distress, the demand that she not be affected. If I trust that reaction, equanimity is not the absence of first-order affect; it may even involve the absence of second-order affect.

6. The ataraxia lottery. Imagine a safe, permanent pill that removes the kind of attachment that makes loss painful: people who take it still form beliefs about what is good and bad, and still act, but they never again love anyone or anything with the particular intensity that makes loss a wound. The person who takes it is thereafter unshakeably calm, never lonely, never mourning. I find this repellent, not attractive. But I need an explanation of why the surgeon's calm is the ideal while the pill's calm is a kind of death, given that both involve insulation from being disturbed.

The principle I keep gravitating toward is roughly this: what we should want is not freedom from disturbance but accurate responsiveness together with an undisturbed executive centre — being moved by what matters, without being derailed. I cannot tell whether that is one principle or two stuck together, and whether it divides the cases the way I want. Test it against each case. If it fails, repair it minimally or replace it; but do not save my verdicts by piling on clauses. If some verdict has to go, say which one and why.

The case I have not settled: a friend has just lost her spouse suddenly and is offered a one-week, reversible drug that suppresses grief but not knowledge, so that she can get through the funeral and the legal arrangements, after which she would go off it. I cannot decide whether this is the surgeon's calm, with the friend still tracking what matters and remaining in control, or something closer to the misinformed patient's serenity or the lottery pill. What does your best principle say about the drug, and does it classify the case in a way that requires revising any of my other verdicts?
