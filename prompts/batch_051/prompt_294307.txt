I'm prepping a philosophy of language unit on prescriptivism vs descriptivism, and I keep feeling like 'is there such a thing as bad grammar?' is a loaded question. So I want to attack it from the back: before anyone argues about whether bad grammar exists, I want a real list of what any account of bad-grammar talk has to get right.

Here's the assignment. Write the desiderata that a theory of bad grammar has to meet. Don't give me the obvious four-bullet version — give the full list, with the case or consideration behind each, and say which requirements are load-bearing and which are just one camp's taste. The phenomena I most want it to handle: hypercorrection ('between you and I'), stable nonstandard systems like negative concord in AAVE, performance slips versus competence, singular they, rules no one actually follows in speech (split infinitives, stranded prepositions), and the way yesterdays error becomes today's unremarkable usage. It has to respect the descriptive-linguistics point that plenty of so-called errors are rule-governed in their own variety, so any theory that writes them off as sloppy is already dead. But I also don't want the opposite cop-out where nothing can ever count as a mistake because its all just register — an account that can't call a non-native slip or a genuinely impossible string an error isn't a theory of bad grammar anymore.

Say up front whether the theory is aimed at sentences or at speaker judgments about sentences, since the two come apart in hypercorrection and code-switching. Then I want to know whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — like wanting both 'usage sets the standard' and 'some common usages are errors' — and if so, which one you'd sacrifice. And tell me which single requirement does the most to kill off the bad theories. Commit to the list; don't survey what the camps would say, I want the one you'd defend.
