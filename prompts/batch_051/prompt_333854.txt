I need a working definition of knowability — the property a true proposition has when it is possible for someone to know it — that gets the six cases below right and, more importantly, derives each verdict from the definition rather than stipulating it. I plan to use this definition at the start of a lecture on Fitch's paradox of knowability, so please give it in ordinary language; standard modal vocabulary such as "possible" and "could" is fine, but do not presuppose any particular axiomatic modal logic. You may relativize knowability to a world, a time, an agent or community, or a sense of "could" (logical, metaphysical, nomological, evidential); if you do, say exactly which relativization is doing the work in each case. Do not treat "knowable" as a synonym for "known" or "not yet known," and do not rule out awkward cases by definitional fiat.

The difficulty I am trying to get at comes from Fitch's paradox. Let p be a true proposition that is not known; for concreteness, p might be that an odd number of leaves fell on a particular uninhabited island during a particular storm long before written records, and suppose no one knows it. Now form q: "p is true, and it is not known that p." (Read "it is not known that p" as a claim about the situation in which q is evaluated, not merely about the present.) q is true, since p is true and not known. But q cannot be known. If someone knew q, they would have to know both conjuncts; knowing the first conjunct would be knowing p, and that would make the second conjunct false, since it would then be known that p. Because one cannot know a falsehood, no one can know q. So q is a true proposition that is not knowable, and the unrestricted knowability principle — every true proposition is such that it could be known — is false. A serviceable definition of knowability should locate the exact feature of q that makes it unknowable, and it should not do so by banning q merely because it contains the concept of knowledge or by calling it self-referential: q does not mention itself; it mentions p and the epistemic status of p.

Here are the cases.

1. The Fitch conjunction q just described. My verdict: true, and not knowable. The definition must return "not knowable" and explain why.

2. The embedded proposition p — that the odd number of leaves fell on that island during that storm. It is true, but no one will ever know it. My verdict: knowable. Someone in a better epistemic position could have known it, and knowability should not be hostage to whether the actual world happens to contain a knower or the right records.

3. A true but as-yet unproved mathematical proposition that is provable by ordinary means — say, an ordinary number-theoretic conjecture that is in fact true and has a proof no one has yet found. My verdict: knowable.

4. A Gödel sentence for arithmetic: a sentence of arithmetic that is true but cannot be proved from the standard formal axioms of arithmetic. My verdict: knowable — a mathematician can know it by reasoning about the system from outside — so knowability must outrun provability in any one fixed formal system.

5. The qualitative character of another person's headache at a particular moment, where the sufferer knows it but reports it to no one. My verdict: knowable by the sufferer; I have no firm view about whether it is knowable by anyone else, and I am unsure whether "knowable" makes sense without specifying a possible knower. I want your definition to settle both questions.

6. A true proposition that was known in the past but is now known to no one — say, the location of an unmarked burial known only to a person who has since died and left no record. My verdict: it was knowable; I have no firm view about whether it is still knowable now, and I want the definition to decide that and say whether knowability is tensed.

Please propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it had to be revised or where it surprised you. Then answer three questions: (a) On your definition, does the knowability principle hold for any interesting restricted class of truths, and if so, what restriction excludes the Fitch case? (b) Which case is doing the most work against the simple "someone could know it" definition? (c) Which of your verdicts is least secure, and what would change your mind?
