I recently came away from an argument about the treatment of AI systems holding a view that I would like to stress-test rather than simply defend. The view, as I have been stating it, runs something like this: a system deserves moral consideration exactly to the extent that it has valenced experience—states that feel good or bad to the system itself—and the capacity for valenced experience is grounded in functional or computational organization rather than in biology. On this picture, a digital system with the relevant organization would be a moral patient in the same basic sense as a human or a dog; our current inability to determine whether any actual digital system is conscious is an epistemic limit on our knowledge, not a limit on where moral status can in principle reside. I keep hearing myself add the qualifiers “in principle” and “the relevant organization,” and I suspect the view is doing more work with those phrases than I have been admitting.

I would like a systematic account of what would count against this position. I am not asking for a balanced survey of the debate, but for the position’s exposure: which arguments, conceptual distinctions, or possible empirical findings should most worry me—for instance, the claim that consciousness is substrate-dependent in a principled rather than merely chauvinistic way; that a simulation of a mind is not an instantiation of one; that our permanent uncertainty about digital consciousness leaves the view idle as a guide to action; or that any line drawn between systems with and without the relevant organization will turn out to be arbitrary. Please sort these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine costs and which they would reject as question-begging, and end with an honest verdict on whether the view, as I have stated it, has insulated itself against empirical refutation—and whether that would be a problem for a claim about the grounds of moral status, or an acceptable feature of it.
