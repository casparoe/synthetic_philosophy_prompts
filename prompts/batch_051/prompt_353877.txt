I am putting together a seminar unit on just war theory and the moral equality of combatants. One recurring difficulty is how to reconcile the classical requirement that a war have a just cause with the modern convention that ordinary soldiers on both sides fight under the same permissions and prohibitions. A common defense of that convention is that soldiers cannot reliably determine whether their side's cause is just, so their conduct should be judged mainly by how they fight rather than why they fight. A common revisionist objection is that this lets an unjust state launder wrongful killing through military discipline: if the war itself is unjust, then killing in its service is not morally cleaned up merely because it was ordered.

I want to use one passage from Aquinas to complicate both positions. In *Summa Theologica* II-II, Question 40, Article 1, Reply to Objection 1, he writes:

"As Augustine says (Contra Faust. xxii, 70): 'To take the sword is to arm oneself in order to take the life of anyone, without the command or permission of superior or lawful authority.' On the other hand, to have recourse to the sword (as a private person) by the authority of the sovereign or judge, or (as a public person) through zeal for justice, and by the authority, so to speak, of God, is not to 'take the sword,' but to use it as commissioned by another, wherefore it does not deserve punishment. And yet even those who make sinful use of the sword are not always slain with the sword, yet they always perish with their own sword, because, unless they repent, they are punished eternally for their sinful use of the sword."

Please write an essay interpreting this passage in the context of the debate over the moral equality of combatants. In particular: does Aquinas's distinction between "taking the sword" and using it "as commissioned by another" imply that a soldier who fights under legitimate authority is shielded from the wrong of killing even when the war's cause is unjust? If not, what kind of wrong remains, and where should it be located? Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, but do not end on a merely concessive note—defend a specific reading of Aquinas and a considered conclusion about whether this passage supports, undermines, or qualifies the moral equality of combatants.
