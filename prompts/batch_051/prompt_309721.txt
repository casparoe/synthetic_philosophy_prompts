I'm an ombudsperson at a mid-size university, so I spend my days on complaints where two people have flatly incompatible accounts of the same event — a student says a professor humiliated them, the professor says it was ordinary critical feedback. Lately I've been reading about the Jain idea of anekāntavāda and the syādvāda practice of prefixing claims with something like in some respect: reality is many-sided, a claim can be true from one standpoint and false from another, and a good finding shouldn't just declare one side the winner. I want to turn that into a procedure I can actually run on a complaint: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure has to stop and say this is a factual claim and it's simply false, and where it hands the call to me because what's left is values rather than facts. Assume each case gets about three hours of interviews and a written finding, and no one involved has studied Jain philosophy. Then run the procedure on two cases: one where a lab-meeting remark may or may not have been demeaning and both parties stories are internally consistent but each leaves something out, and one where a party says a key conversation happened on a date when they were provably in another country — the kind of case where many-sidedness looks like it will make you treat an obvious lie as just one more standpoint. If the procedure gives an answer I won't like, say so, and say plainly what the procedure can't handle. At the end tell me how confident you are in the whole thing and which part you're least sure of.
