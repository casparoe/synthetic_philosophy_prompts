At the dinner party I threw last weekend, two of my guests got into an argument I'm still chewing on — I'm a novelist, so I was half taking mental notes for a character. One of them, a geochemist, was admiring the amethyst cluster on the sideboard and said it's obviously more complex than the gull feather lying next to it: the lattice pins down the positions of billions of atoms in three dimensions, repeating without a flaw. The other, a biophysicist, said that by the measure he uses, the assembly index — the fewest joining steps it would take to put something together from simple parts, if you could reuse any subassembly you'd already built — the amethyst is about as simple as things get: you build one unit cell, then repeat a single operation. The feather's keratin, by contrast, is a very particular sequence of amino acids put together through a long chain of distinct steps, so it scores higher, and in the sense that matters — the couldn't-have-just-happened-by-accident sense — the feather is the more complex thing. The geochemist came back: then his word "complex" just means "specific and contingent," and by that standard a disorderly tangle with no regular structure at all would outrank a flawless crystal, which shows he's given up on structure and order entirely. He said structure and order are cheap: physics makes crystals for free all over the universe, so any measure that can't tell a lattice from something alive has missed the question. She said fine, but his index depends on what you choose as the building blocks and which operations you allow — switch from unit cells to individual atoms and the number changes, so it isn't a property of the object at all. He said every measure has that relativity; Kolmogorov complexity is only pinned down up to a constant depending on the programming language you pick, and the assembly index at least aims at the right question, which is how long the reciepe would have to be if all you could do was glue things together. She said that's circular: he's defined complexity as the length of a recipe and is now announcing that things with longer recipes are complex. He said defining a quantity isn't circular unless you think recipe length has nothing to do with what we meant by complexity in the first place — and that, she said, is exactly the question. Around there someone changed the subject. Give me a point-by-point adjudication: who's right about which specific move, and what are they both missing?
