Since I retired I've been reading a bit of philosophy of value, and there's a practice I keep bumping into that I can't make hang together. Where I live, the state will pay to make recordings of the last few fluent speakers of a dying language chatting about their childhood, and to film a master weaver's technique, but it does almost nothing to keep that language spoken in daily life or to line up apprentices for the weaver. The tapes go into a digital archive that basically nobody opens; I've heard UNESCO does something similar for intangible cultural heritage. As a retired geologist I keep wanting to ask: what view of value does this practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if languages and techniques have some value beyond the people who currently hold them, or could it be justified entirely by future benefits, or just by the wellbeing of the recorded speakers themselves while they're still around? And is the practice coherent? If the song or the pattern is the valuable bit, why not spend to keep it alive instead of embalming it; if the living activity is what's valuable, why does a recording in an unopened archive count as preserving anything? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value, sort out which assumptions the practice strictly needs from the ones that merely happen to travel with it, flag which rival views of value it quietly rules out (or should make everyone uncomfortable holding), and then say what would have to change if the people running these programs actually adopted (a) a strict preference-satisfaction view of value, or (b) the view that only the welfare of currently existing people matters. Dont worry about history or references; I want the commitments forced into the open.
