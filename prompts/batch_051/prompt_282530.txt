I'm a linguistics PhD student, and a couple weeks ago at a department happy hour two of my friends got into an argument that nobody else would referee, and I've been turning it over since. An undergrad in our intro class had apparently said, "Me and her went to the store," and Dana said it was just bad grammar. Isaac said its a category mistake: linguistics studies the grammar people actually have, so a form produced systematically by millions of native speakers isn't wrong in any scientific sense, only nonstandard.

Dana came back that the same speakers would never say "Me went" or "Her went," so this isn't a dialect with different case rules—it's a slip, and people know the rule but fail to apply it. Isaac said that's the interesting part: English seems to allow accusative (or default) pronouns specifically inside coordinate noun phrases, so the contrast between "Me went" and "Me and her went" is a pattern, not an error, just one that most textbooks don't describe. Dana said if systematicity is enough to make something grammatical, then ain't, negative concord, and "between you and I" all get a pass, and the idea of bad grammar dissolves entirely. Isaac said that isn't a reductio—that's the point: "bad grammar" marks a social judgment, not a property of the sentence, though you can still call it nonstandard or say it's bad in a job talk without treating it as broken.

Dana said that move concedes everything while hiding behind terminology: the construction is marked, listeners judge it as a mistake in formal contexts, and an undergrad should be warned not to use it in an interview. Relabeling "ungrammatical" as "nonstandard" doesn't change the advice. Isaac said the relabeling is the argument, because "ungrammatical" sneaks in the claim that the standard dialect is the grammar and other varieties are defective copies of it. Dana replied that ordinary speakers just mean "not in the prestige standard" when they say bad grammar, so they aren't confused—linguists are the ones trying to redefine the term. At that point everyone else had drifted off to the bar.
