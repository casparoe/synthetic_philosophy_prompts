I've been going down a rabbit hole about the identification problem in philosophy of math — the Benacerraf one about multiple reductions, not the epistemic access one — and I've ended up with a distinction I want someone to stress-test honestly. You know the setup: if numbers are sets, there are too many ways to do it. Von Neumann makes 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4 comes out true; Zermelo makes 4 = {3} (which is {{{{∅}}}}), so 2 ∈ 4 comes out false. Both reductions do all the arithmetic fine, so 'is 2 an element of 4?' seems to have no stable answer across equally good reductions. My distinction: a fact about a number is 'skeletal' if it survives every equally good reduction, and 'clerical' if it flips depending on the coding. Skeletal facts are things like 4 is even, 4 comes after 3, 4 is composite, 4 = 2+2. Clerical facts are things like 2 ∈ 4, 1 ∈ 3, and whether 4-as-a-set has four elements or one. My actual theory is stronger then just the distinction: I claim clerical facts aren't actually facts at all. They're type errors, like asking what color 4 is. So the right answer to 'is 2 an element of 4?' is neither yes nor no; it's something like 'you're asking about the implementation, not the number.'

I'm aware this sounds like structuralism, and maybe it just is structuralism wearing a worse name. But I want it checked properly: is the skeletal/clerical distinction doing any work that standard structuralism doesn't already do? Is the stronger claim — that clerical facts are category errors — defensible, or does it smuggle in a choice of 'the' abstract structure? And where does it break? Give me the strongest objection or the cleanest example where a so-called clerical fact is doing real mathematical work. Don't soften it: if this is just relabeled structuralism, say so and tell me what I'm still missing.
