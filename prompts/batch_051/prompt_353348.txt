Suppose that in 2030 a consortium of private donors and two public broadcasters in a country that is otherwise much like Canada—liberal democratic, high-trust, with a free press and strong universities—launches a professional competitive reasoning league, intended to make argumentation function something like a sport. The charter fixes the following rules. Two-person teams are assigned the affirmative or negative of a contested proposition by lot, drawn only a few days before the match, so a team cannot choose the side it believes. Judges are drawn from working logicians, statisticians, appellate lawyers, and domain scientists; each judge must publish a written score sheet. Teams are rewarded for (i) making every inferential step explicit and justified, (ii) restating the opposing case in a form the opposing team acknowledges as its strongest version before attacking it, (iii) conceding a point when they cannot answer an objection, and (iv) calibrating their claims to what the argument actually shows. Points are deducted for equivocation, suppressed premises, ad hominem, base-rate neglect, and overclaiming beyond the evidence. Matches are televised with the score sheets shown in real time, and an independent audit panel reviews the judges' scoring each season. The league has large prize money, annual rankings, and no formal connection to government, political parties, or universities.

Over the next twenty-five years the league becomes embedded in the country's public culture, with substantial viewership and a steady supply of competitors. Predict, with mechanisms, what this does to (a) the norms and tone of national political debate, (b) how academic disputation is practiced and rewarded, (c) how audiences consume and evaluate arguments, and (d) the internal norms and selection pressures of the league itself. For each prediction, separate the links that follow from stable incentives from the links that depend on contested assumptions about judges, audiences, or governance. In particular, say where you expect score-maximization to diverge from truth-tracking—where Goodhart-style gaming is most and least likely—and what would have to be true for the league to make the society's reasoning worse rather than better. I am less interested in an overall verdict than in a visible chain of reasoning, with explicit confidence attached to each link.
