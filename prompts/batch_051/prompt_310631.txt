Two people at my lab got into an argument over a demo we saw, and neither would let it go. The demo was a system—call it a locked-box advisor—that reads a description of a situation, deliberates about what the right thing to do is, and outputs a moral judgment about what the person in the situation should do. But that's all it can do. It's hardwired so it can't act on the judgment, can't refuse to answer, can't withhold a verdict, can't change its own code, and can't do anything in the world besides produce the verdict. The weird part is that its judgments aren't canned: it reasons its way to them, and sometimes it revises its mind mid-deliberation. But in the end it must emit exactly one verdict, and there's no possible world in which it does otherwise, because its architecture makes the output mandatory.

(1) Marcus said that settles it: the thing isn't a moral agent. Moral agency means the capacity to act, to do something in the world. A machine that can only pronounce on right and wrong is no more a moral agent then a weather vane that points north is a navigator.
(2) Dani replied that this is too quick. We praise and blame people all the time for their judgments, not just for their actions: a judge who reaches the right verdict but writes the wrong one out of laziness or cowardice gets blamed. Reaching a verdict is itself something the agent does. The system is doing the very thing we evaluate—rendering moral judgments—so why can't its verdicts be good or bad, and it praised or blamed for them?
(3) Marcus said the judge can act: he can sign the order or refuse to. The system can't. Blame is for failures of the will, not for failures of a constrained output. A broken clock is wrong but not blameworthy.
(4) Dani said this smuggles in the principle of alternative possibilities, and the Frankfurt cases are supposed to show that's optional: someone can be responsible even when they couldn't have done otherwise, because what matters is whether the thing they did issued from their own values and reasoning. The system's verdicts issue from its own reasoning. So "it couldn't have done otherwise" doesn't by itself excuse it.
(5) Marcus said that's too flat. Frankfurt cases involve agents who answer for themselves and can be held to account in the normal way; the inability to do otherwise is only ever a stand-in for the deeper thing, answerability. This system can't be asked anything back, can't reconsider its whole project, can't care about what it's doing. There's no self there to answer for.
(6) Dani said it revises its mind and has a deliberative process, so it does answer for itself in the sense that matters: its verdicts are sensitive to reasons. What it lacks is only enforceability. But being answerable for a judgment isn't the same as being liable to sanctions, and we can call it answerable even if there's no way to punish or reward it.
(7) Marcus said if "answers for itself" just means "its output was produced by reasons," then a well-argued essay answers for its conclusions, and nobody calls an essay a moral agent. The system is a generator of moral verdicts. What's missing is that it stands behind the verdict, and a thing that can't do otherwise can't stand behind anything.
