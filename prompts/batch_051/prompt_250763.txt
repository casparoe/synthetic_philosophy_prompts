After a long career in experimental science, I still reach for the categories I used when interpreting data. The distinction that has stayed with me most is the one between random error and systematic bias: random error scatters observations around the quantity one is trying to measure and can be reduced by averaging, whereas systematic bias shifts every observation in the same direction and cannot be removed by any amount of repetition. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, and I have been struck by how naturally this distinction seems to map onto the four kinds of moral luck that Thomas Nagel distinguishes. Resultant luck—luck in how one's actions turn out—looks, at first, like noise superimposed after the fact: two drivers behave with equal recklessness, and only one happens to kill a pedestrian. Circumstantial luck—luck in which situations one confronts—seems harder to treat as noise, since the situation supplies the very occasion in which a person's character is revealed. Constitutive luck—luck in one's temperament, abilities, and inclinations—appears to reach into the instrument itself. And causal luck—luck in the antecedent causes of one's actions—raises the question whether there is any residue of the self that is not itself the product of fortune.

I would like you to rank these four kinds of moral luck from least to most corrosive to desert-based moral responsibility, and to present your answer as a tier list with at least three tiers. For each kind of luck, give a one-sentence justification for its placement. In your reasoning, please say where my noise-versus-bias analogy genuinely illuminates the problem and where it breaks down; I am particularly interested in whether resultant luck really is the least troublesome, and whether constitutive and causal luck are ultimately two descriptions of a single problem rather than two distinct threats.
