I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about the ethics of randomized trials—specifically, about what it means for a trial to be balanced enough that enrolling patients is ethically defensible. Before I write, I want to settle my own view of one concrete case, and I would like your help making the reasoning explicit and exposing where it is weakest.

Consider a phase III randomized trial comparing two licensed antibiotics for a severe bloodstream infection. Both drugs are in wide use, but a recent retrospective analysis suggested that one of them may carry a higher risk of kidney injury, and the specialty is now split: in a recent survey, about 55 percent of infectious-disease physicians say they would want one of the drugs if they themselves were infected, and 45 percent say the other. The trial uses response-adaptive randomization, so that as interim outcomes accrue the allocation ratio gradually shifts toward whichever arm is performing better. The trial's chair strongly prefers drug A and, speaking off the record, says she would be uneasy if her spouse were randomized to arm B; a co-investigator holds roughly the opposite view.

Please give me your credence—a number between 0 and 1—that this trial satisfies the ethical requirement of clinical equipoise at the time enrollment opens. I understand clinical equipoise as the condition that a randomized trial is ethically acceptable only when there is honest, professional disagreement within the relevant community of expert practitioners about which arm is preferable, so that no participant is knowingly assigned to a treatment that the expert community regards as inferior. I want more than a number: I want the reasoning decomposed into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on. In particular, address (a) whether the relevant standard is community-level disagreement or each individual investigator's own uncertainty; (b) whether the investigators' strong private preferences falsify the claim of community disagreement, or are simply part of what such disagreement consists in; (c) whether response-adaptive randomization erodes the ethical foundation as the allocation ratio drifts, and if so at what point the trial stops being in equipoise; and (d) whether an informed-consent process can honestly present both arms as acceptable while the people leading the trial hold the views they do. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, show how you combine them into your final number, and identify the sub-question to which the final credence is most sensitive. Then tell me what evidence or argument would move that number most. Finally, suppose two thoughtful people arrive at 0.3 and 0.7 for this same trial; where does their disagreement most likely live?
