I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the weird way waste turns into credibility—peacock tails, luxury watches, rituals that look pointless to outsiders. A biologist I interviewed gave me the following tidy gloss on the handicap principle, and its been nagging me because it feels right but maybe a little to tidy:

"Zahavi's handicap principle says that honest signals have to be costly. A peacock's tail is a real burden: it slows him down and makes him conspicuous to predators. The tail is trustworthy precisely because it is wasteful—only a male in genuinely good condition can afford to grow it and still survive, so peahens can read the display as a reliable sign of quality. Same logic as a Rolex: the whole point of the expensive watch is to burn money so observers will believe you're rich. If a signal cost nothing, cheaters and fakes would flood in and the signal would collapse."

I'd like to use some version of this in the piece, but I don't want to repeat a caricature as if it were careful theory. Overall, is that a fair summary of the handicap principle, or a confident oversimplification? Which parts would a signaling theorist sign off on, and which parts are subtly off? In particular, is the slide from "costly" to "wasteful" doing real work, and does the Rolex analogy survive contact with the actual logic? If you had to give a general reader a corrected two- or three-sentence version, what would it say?
