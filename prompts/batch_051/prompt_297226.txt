I am a retired natural scientist, and after a career spent building and comparing idealized models, I have recently turned to moral philosophy. The area that has most gripped me is the family of views—ideal observer, ideal advisor, and fully-informed-self theories—on which what an agent ought to do is fixed indirectly, by what some suitably idealized version of the agent, fully informed, vividly acquainted with the relevant facts, procedurally rational, and free of certain biases, would choose, recommend, or endorse. Reading this literature, I have been struck by how much weight is carried by the specification of the idealization, and by the fact that there is no canonical specification. Different idealizing moves, applied to the same agent, can pull in different directions, and there seems to be no settled method for arbitrating among them. In my scientific work I dealt with something structurally similar: we routinely used several incompatible idealizations of the same system, did not expect them to converge on one true model, and relied on informal judgment about which idealization to trust for which purpose. I am not sure ethical theory has an analogue of that judgment, or whether it should.

I am not asking for a survey of positions, and I do not want citations or a reading list. I am asking for a defensible, dependency-aware research agenda for this cluster of questions. Fix the audience as someone like me: a retired scientist with careful analytical habits but no professional training in philosophy, able to devote about three years of serious study with the aim of making an original contribution rather than merely catching up.

Here are some candidate issues, though you need not use all of them: whether the counterfactual about what the idealized agent would choose can be stated without tacitly altering the very thing it was meant to illuminate—the problem philosophers call the conditional fallacy; whether the idealization preserves the agent's actual values or silently replaces them with the idealizer's; how to draw a principled boundary between the biases to be removed and the commitments to be retained; what to do when two or more equally defensible idealizations of the same agent conflict; and whether idealized deliberation should respect the agent's real feasibility constraints.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each one or two sentences. Rank them from highest to lowest priority. In each item, name one unresolved question or line of work and state the one or two considerations that earn it that rank—such as how many other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is in three years for someone with my background, how neglected it is, or how much would change downstream if it were settled. The ordering itself should carry the argument, so do not add transition sentences.
