I have spent enough time watching students perform formal reasoning drills without error and then fail at the unscripted version of the same task that I can no longer ignore the tension. The view I am drawn to says that epistemic rationality is, above all, a skill: something acquired, maintained, and improved through the repetitive practice of standardized forms — katas — in simplified conditions, with the expectation that the practiced form transfers to live reasoning. The view has the virtues a theory should have here. It makes rationality teachable. It converts a diffuse exhortation to think well into a curriculum with exercises, progression, and criteria of success. It explains why otherwise intelligent people reason badly: they have not internalized the relevant forms. And it explains why practice helps: it automates the forms and frees working memory for content. I want something like this to be true.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake. When reasoning fails in the wild — a clinician triaging under time pressure, a scientist deciding whether her own result is a fluke, a policy-maker comparing two messy interventions — the failure does not look like the absence of a drilled form. The same person can execute the kata beautifully in the seminar and fail to deploy it in the field, often while knowing the kata. Consider a base-rate kata in which one states the prior and the likelihood ratio before updating, or a pre-commitment kata in which one writes down in advance what would change one's mind and by how much. These work in a problem set. In the live case they seem to break against three facts: the problem is not pre-labeled, the relevant base rate is not supplied, and the failure is usually one of attention or motivation rather than technique. The martial-arts analogy itself should make us pause, because the oldest objection to kata training is precisely that polished dojo performance collapses in a real fight; the classic reply is that the kata trains principles of movement and automaticity, not responses to specific attacks. A rational kata looks to me more like a response to a specific, well-defined attack than like a principle of movement, and I cannot tell whether that difference is fatal, superficial, or the clue to a better view.

I want you to arbitrate this at its strongest, not at its weakest. Give me the strongest version of the skill-and-kata account, one that answers the transfer objection instead of waving it away — perhaps by distinguishing near from far transfer, or by claiming that katas train monitoring and inhibition rather than the inference itself, or by arguing that the objection demands more transfer than any skill, including surgery or flying, could deliver. Then give me the strongest version of the intuition that real-case rationality is too content-bound and too entangled with affect and motivation for generic forms to do much work. Then tell me what should yield. Should I debunk the intuition as a generalization from badly designed katas and a selection effect in which failures are more visible than successes? Should I amend the theory, so that its claims are restricted to a genuinely transferable sub-skill? Or can the two be reconciled, and if so, what must each side concede? I am not asking you to pick a side quickly; I am asking what would justify each move.
