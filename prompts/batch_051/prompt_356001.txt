On a long flight last month I read a short article about medical research into drugs that would not treat an illness but would blunt ordinary moral failings—impulsive aggression, spite, callousness. The article said, almost as an aside, that if such a drug ever became safe and cheap, decent people might be obligated to take it. That struck me as obviously wrong, but when I tried to explain to my wife why it was wrong, I kept contradicting myself. I eventually wrote the argument out on my phone, and it has been bothering me ever since.

The argument runs like this:

(1) If you can prevent serious harm to other people at little cost to yourself, then in general you are morally obligated to do so.
(2) Someone who took a safe, effective, affordable drug that made their own anger and spite much easier to resist would prevent serious harm to other people—the harm they would otherwise do when those impulses get the better of them—at little cost to themselves.
(3) So if such a drug became available, someone would be morally obligated to take it.
(4) Taking such a drug means changing, through medicine, tendencies that can be central to a person's personality, tendencies people often think of as part of who they are.
(5) If (3) is right, then people could be morally obligated to chemically alter their own personalities.
(6) But people are not morally obligated to chemically alter their own personalities; there is a line between what morality can ask of us and what it cannot, and this crosses it.
(7) Since (1) and (2) seem true and (6) seems true, but they cannot all be true, the argument must go wrong somewhere—but I cannot see where.

I would most like to keep some version of (1), because I do think we owe each other real effort to keep ourselves from causing harm. So: which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
