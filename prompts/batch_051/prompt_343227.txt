I want a working definition of following a rule — of what separates someone who is genuinely going on in the same way from someone who merely happens to act in a manner that accords with a rule. Let me explain the need. I have been trying to work through Wittgenstein's private language argument, and it now seems to me that nearly every standard move in that debate presupposes an answer to this question without ever stating one. So I want a definition precise enough that a third party could apply it to the cases below without needing to ask further questions.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify correctly:

1. A schoolchild who has practiced addition only on sums under ten, and who answers 7 when asked 5 + 2 for the first time, although she cannot state any rule of addition.
2. A student who, on a set of twenty unfamiliar addition problems, writes down a correct answer to each one by pure guesswork.
3. A parrot trained to squawk good morning at sunrise.
4. A diarist who writes S at noon each day in a locked notebook whenever he takes himself to have the same subtle inner sensation. There is no outward sign that the sensation is present, and only he can tell whether it is the same one. He insists that his use of S is answerable to a rule.
5. A person raised alone on an island, with no one to teach him a natural language, who devises a private notation for tallying fish and, when unsure whether a new mark is correct, resolves the doubt by comparing it with his memory of how he has marked before.
6. Here is the case that unsettled me. A person has used + only on pairs of numbers both smaller than 57, and has never made a mistake. Today she must answer 68 + 57. A skeptic proposes that she has never been following the rule plus at all, but the rule quus, where x quus y is x plus y if x and y are both under 57, and 5 otherwise. Everything she has ever done is equally consistent with both rules. She has a strong feeling that she is going on in the same way as before, and writes 125.
7. An isolated community in which every member makes the same systematic mistake on large addition problems — for example, whenever a problem involves carrying into the tens place, they all write down the same wrong answer — and they reliably correct any member who departs from that shared wrong answer.

I am confident that case 1 is following a rule, and I am confident that cases 2 and 3 are not. Case 6 has convinced me that neither a feeling of certainty nor a bare disposition to answer is enough, but I cannot see what else there could be. For cases 4, 5, and 7 I have no settled intuition, and I would like the definition to force a definite answer in each case, even an uncomfortable one.

Please propose the definition, run it through all seven cases one by one, and say explicitly where the definition gives the wrong answer or simply falls silent. Then either revise the definition in light of those failures or defend the surprising verdicts. If the definition ends up overturning one of my confident verdicts, do not downplay that: say so and tell me why I should change my mind.
