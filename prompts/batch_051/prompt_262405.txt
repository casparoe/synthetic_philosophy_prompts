I run a small primary-care clinic and half my staff are nurse practitioners without much psychiatric training. We keep getting patients whose chief complaint is some version of "I feel empty" — not sad, not anxious, not suicidal, they can mostly get through the day, but the feeling scares them, or its their partner it scares more. I want a reusable screening-and-routing procedure my staff can run in about twenty-five minutes in the room.

Ground it in whatever philosophy and clinical theory actually know about the difference between:
(a) emptiness as not being able to feel much at all — anhedonia, flattened affect, the lights are on but nothing registers;
(b) emptiness as loss of meaning while feelings still work — the person laughs, wants things, but every project comes to look hollow or pointless;
(c) emptiness as a sense of unreality or distance from oneself — "I'm watching a character";
(d) quiet emptiness that isn't a complaint — meditative or deliberately simple, where the person isn't distressed and the only presenting problem is that someone else thinks it's a problem.

I don't need the theory, I need the operational version. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each before moving on, where the procedure stops with a referral (and to whom), and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than diagnosis. Keep the actual words we would say to a patient normal, no jargon.

Then run the procedure on three cases, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like:
1. A 45-year-old who used to be ambitious says work and family "just don't land" anymore, no sadness, sleeps and eats fine, still does everything.
2. A 30-year-old long-term meditator describes a calm clear emptiness and says it's the best thing that ever happened to him; it's his wife who booked the appointment.
3. An 80-year-old says life has been hollow for years, denies low mood, and when asked what he hopes for says only "I've had my turn."

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure can't decide, which step is most likely to misfire, and what argument or evidence would change the verdict in at least one of those cases.
