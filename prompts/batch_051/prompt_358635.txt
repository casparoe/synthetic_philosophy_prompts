I'm drafting a novel whose protagonist is a mid-career scientist, and a pivotal chapter turns on her working through a thought experiment alone at night — the scientific analogue of a detective's deductive breakthrough. I want the scene to be intellectually honest rather than decorative: the thought experiment should actually be the kind of thing that could earn its conclusion, not merely dramatize a conclusion I, as the author, have already decided to hand her. My difficulty is that the thought experiments I remember from popular science are so familiar that they would read as set-dressing in the novel, and I do not have a firm enough grip on why some thought experiments teach us something about the world while others only make us feel that a conclusion is right. I would rather not have my protagonist do her crucial thinking through a stock example, though a lightly disguised variant of a less famous historical case is fine.

Could you construct a case for this chapter to the following specifications? Choose a well-established science — classical mechanics, thermodynamics, electricity and magnetism, or a similarly secure field, nothing speculative or esoteric. The primary case should be a thought experiment, no longer than a paragraph as it might appear in the scene, whose setup is practically unrealizable but law-governed in principle, in the way a frictionless plane or an infinitely slow compression idealizes rather than imports new physics: the idealization should isolate a single variable that real apparatus cannot isolate, and the conclusion should follow from principles the field already accepts, with no new observation. The conclusion should be positive — some relation or consequence about the world that the protagonist could not simply read off from a textbook statement of the laws, not merely the exposure of a contradiction in a rival view. Alongside it, construct a second, structurally parallel case on the same topic that produces only the appearance of knowledge. The two should be close enough in surface form that a lay reader would find them equally persuasive; the difference should lie in the epistemic structure, not in one case being handled carelessly. Then walk me through the anatomy of both cases: what each setup actually licenses, which specific feature of the good case is doing the epistemic work, what the argument becomes if that feature is removed, and how someone who holds that scientific thought experiments are never more than vivid ways of displaying what their premises already contain would analyze each case — and what reply is available to the character. I would also like to know whether the bad case could be repaired by changing the idealization rather than the conclusion, since the boundary between the two is what I most need to understand before I write the scene.
