I am trying to settle what I actually think makes something funny by working from my own reactions to concrete cases before committing to a theory. The four standard accounts I know of — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — each capture something real, but none of them, as usually stated, gets all of the following cases. I am not sure whether the fault lies in the accounts or in my reactions.

Here are my cases, with my honest verdicts.

1. An arrogant contestant on a competitive baking show has spent the whole episode sneering at everyone else's technique. Walking to the judging table, he trips and sits down hard in his own three-tiered cake. I laugh outright.

2. A nervous first-time contestant trips in exactly the same way and sits in her cake, and immediately starts crying. I do not laugh at all; I flinch. The physical event is nearly identical to case 1, but my reaction is completely different.

3. A close friend slips on a patch of ice outside a café, does a harmless full-body sprawl, and comes up laughing with coffee all down his front. I laugh hard.

4. The same friend takes the same fall, but I later learn that he chipped a tooth and the coffee scalded his arm. The memory immediately stops being funny, and I feel slightly sick that I laughed.

5. On a group chat, someone says they are reading a history of mountaineering, and a friend replies, "I guess it has its ups and downs." It catches me off guard and I find it genuinely funny.

6. In the same conversation, someone says they are reading a book about submarines, and the same friend replies, "I guess it's deep." The structure is the same, but it feels worn-out and I do not find it funny at all.

7. During a meeting that has become a tense standoff between two colleagues, someone's phone goes off with a ridiculous ringtone — a rooster crowing three times — and the whole room, me included, is suddenly helpless with laughter. The sound itself is not that funny; the laughter feels like a valve opening.

8. A stand-up comedian does a long bit about the absurd things people say to the bereaved at funerals, building up the social conventions and then dismantling them. I laugh.

9. A coworker repeats the same bit nearly verbatim to a colleague whose mother died the previous month. The words are the same, but it lands as cruel and intrusive; no one laughs.

10. My young daughter shrieks with laughter while I tickle her. I do not think anything humorous is going on, even though she is laughing.

Here is what I want from you. Take these four candidate principles:

A. Superiority: something is funny when it gives us the sudden pleasure of seeing someone else's defect, error, or misfortune, especially when we judge that they had it coming.

B. Incongruity: something is funny when it sets up an incongruity and then resolves it, however trivially; the better the resolution relative to the setup, the funnier it is.

C. Relief: laughter is the discharge of nervous energy, and humour is whatever successfully triggers that release; the content matters only as a trigger.

D. Benign violation: something is funny when it presents a violation of a norm, expectation, or sense of order that we appraise as benign — the stakes are low, the harm unreal, or we are at a safe distance.

Test each candidate against my cases, and then either repair one of them or assemble a small set of candidate principles. State the principle or principles precisely enough that they yield a verdict for most new cases. Identify, for each clause, which of my cases forces you to include it. Then run your proposal through all ten cases and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without becoming ad hoc, and whether I should revise that verdict instead.

Finally, apply your proposal to three further things I am unsure about. First, a joke at the expense of a group, when told by a member of that group, versus the same joke told by an outsider: I find the first funny and the second repellent, though the words are identical. Is that a difference in what is funny, or only in what it is permissible to laugh at? Second, the tickling case above: should I be forced to classify it as humour, as non-humorous laughter, or does my principle give no answer? Third, a video of a politician I dislike tripping on the stairs of a public building: I find it funny in a way that feels close to schadenfreude, and I cannot tell whether that should count as humour at all. What does your principle say, and where is it most fragile?
