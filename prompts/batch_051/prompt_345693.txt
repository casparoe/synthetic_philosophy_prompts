A friend of mine is working on a screenplay about a dead politician from our state, and we keep going in circles about whether it's okay for her to put a scene in where the guy quietly gives money to a rival's family after a scandal. The scene invents a specific act, but it's meant to show a real side of him that you don't get from the public record. I've been defending it using Plutarch, who says at the start of his Life of Alexander:

For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives; and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, nay, a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles when thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities.

My argument, cleaned up, goes like this: (1) In dramatizing a real dead person, your primary obligation is to reveal their character, not to reproduce every documented event. (2) An invented detail can reveal character just as much as a documented one, sometimes more, because recorded public acts often hide the person's inner motivations. (3) If an invented scene does reveal what was really true about the person's character, then the filmmaker has not wronged them by making it up. (4) My friend's invented scene reveals something genuinely true about the man's habitual private generosity, so it's fine. I think this argument is valid and the conclusion has to be accepted by anyone who takes Plutarch's line seriously. But I also worry that step (2) turns "truth" into something that can't be checked anymore, and maybe step (3) smuggles in a claim that the dead are wronged by falsehood even when it reveals character, not just when it lies about facts. Am I right that the argument goes through, or does it fail at a particular step? Be specific about which step and the reading of it, and if the argument is valid, say what you'd have to believe to resist the conclusion.
