I'm a PhD student in decision theory and I've got a half-baked view about money pumps I want checked before I embarass myself at a workshop. I've been calling it the "pairwise fallacy." Here's the idea.

Money pump arguments always start by supposing an agent has a cyclic strict preference: A ≻ B, B ≻ C, C ≻ A. Then the pump operator offers trades one at a time, each trade favored by the pairwise preference, and the agent ends up back where she started minus some money. The conclusion is that cyclic preferences are irrational because they make you exploitable.

My hunch is that the argument owes everything to a slide between two choice situations. A sequence of binary offers over time is not the same thing as one simultaneous menu. If you put the whole cycle on the table at once — {A, B, C, plus the little cash transfers} — the agent might just refuse, since each option is worse than some alternative and the cycle is visible as a cycle. What the pump needs is an agent who chooses myopically from a pairwise menu each moment and never looks ahead. So my claim is that money pumps don't demonstrate the irrationality of cyclic preference; they demonstrate the exploitability of myopic sequential choice, which everyone already agreed was bad.

I want to formalize it like this: model the agent as a context-dependent choice function over menus, not as a binary relation. Conjecture: for any pumpable strict-preference cycle, there's a choice function that agrees with the cycle on binary menus but is pump-proof, and the only price is giving up context-free binary preferences. That price seems fine to me. (Maybe this is just sophisticated choice with extra steps? Or resolute choice? I can't tell if I've rediscovered something obvious.)

So: is the "pairwise fallacy" a real distinction, or does it collapse into something in the menu-dependence and framing-effects literature I haven't read? Does my conjecture actually block the money pump, or can a patient pump operator just re-frame the global menu as a sequence until the agent bites? And where does the view break — in particular, does it just rename irrationality rather than answer it, if the choice function can be pump-proof and still be crazy in some other way? Give me the strongest version of my view and the strongest objection to it, and tell me whether the objection is fatal.
