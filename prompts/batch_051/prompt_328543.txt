Exam question:

Joel Feinberg argued that children possess a right to an open future, an autonomy right held in trust until the child is capable of exercising it. Hugh LaFollette, in a separate discussion, argued that since activities such as driving and the practice of medicine are licensed because they are risky and require competence, there is at least a prima facie case for licensing parents. Explain how Feinberg structures the right to an open future, and evaluate whether that right, by itself, supports a prima facie case for licensing prospective parents in something like LaFollette's sense. Identify the strongest consideration on each side and say how much of the work is done by the open-future right itself.

Student answer:

Feinberg's innovation is his distinction between two kinds of rights a child may have: some rights the child can exercise immediately, and other rights that must be held in trust until the child has the capacities needed to exercise them. The right to an open future falls in the second class. It is a right addressed to parents and to the state to keep the child's future set of significant options as large as possible, and it is therefore a positive right to maximize what the child will later be able to choose among. By extension, any parental decision that removes a significant option from that future set, such as a rigorously insular religious upbringing that makes university, certain careers, or certain relationships effectively unavailable, violates the right, since the child cannot yet consent to the loss.

The policy upshot is one Feinberg himself draws: if the open-future right is real, the state may license parents, because parents are the primary source of the foreclosing decisions the right guards against. LaFollette's comparison with driving and medicine supplies the decisive analogy; we already license activities that are risky and require competence, and parenting is at least as consequential. So once one grants Feinberg's right, the licensing conclusion follows directly. The strongest consideration in support is that the child's future autonomy cannot protect itself, while the strongest consideration against is the practical problem of designing a test that is fair and an enforcement mechanism that is not oppressive.
