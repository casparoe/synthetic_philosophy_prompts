I keep running into the sex-work policy debate and noticing that the word "exploitation" is doing most of the arguing for every side while remaining unpinned. The Nordic-model side treats the transaction as exploitative in itself, whatever the seller reports; the legalization approach in Germany and the Netherlands treats exploitation as a labor-conditions problem to be managed through regulation; and the decriminalization camp, pointing to New Zealand, treats exploitation as an artifact of criminalization and third-party control rather than of the exchange itself. I suspect the disagreement is partly definitional, so I want to stop equivocating.

Here are six cases I'd like a working definition of exploitation to sort:

- A migrant is brought across a border by someone who confiscates their passport and uses threats to force them to see clients; all the money goes to the trafficker.
- A software developer with savings and a full-time job takes occasional independent, high-end escort work because she finds it more pleasant and better paid than other side income; she can stop whenever she likes.
- A single parent with no manager sells sex because it is the only way to cover rent and childcare this month; she says she hates it but nothing else pays enough in the available hours.
- In a Nordic-model country, a woman works from her own apartment, keeps the whole fee, and commits no crime, while every client commits a criminal offense; she wants neither policing nor rescue, but she has to hide the work from her landlord and from social-services paperwork.
- In a country that has legalized prostitution, a worker is employed in a licensed brothel with a contract, health insurance, and taxed income; the owner takes half of each fee and sets a minimum number of clients per shift, and she can resign but says this is better than the unregulated work she left.
- A cam performer works from home through a platform that takes a large cut of what viewers pay and can change her visibility or deplatform her; she sets her own hours, has no offline contact with clients, and could live on her non-sex-work job.

I'm confident the first is exploitation and the second is not; on the others I have no stable view and would rather let the definition decide. Propose the definition, run it through all six, tell me where it yields a verdict I'd resist or no verdict at all, and then either revise it or defend the surprising results. If the definition ends up vindicating one policy camp more than another, say so plainly.
