I have ended up in a position I did not expect, and I want it attacked from angles I cannot see myself. For a long time I took it as obvious that the far future is where the moral weight is: if our descendants last even a million years, the number of lives not yet lived dwarfs the number alive now, so the present matters mainly as a hinge to that future. Then I read about the simulation argument, presented as a choice among three possibilities: almost no civilization at our level ever reaches the stage where it could run many simulated histories of its own past; civilizations at that stage almost never choose to run such simulations; or we are probably living inside one of those simulations now. The third possibility struck me as serious, and it spoiled the first idea for me.

The position I have settled on is this: the simulation argument puts a heavy discount on far-future reasoning. If our apparent future is likely running inside someone else's machine, then treating the next billion years as a fact we can bank on seems like a mistake. The simulators could stop the run, change its rules, or have built this world to answer some question that has nothing to do with whether our apparent descendants flourish. So the familiar move of multiplying a small chance of steering the far future by an astronomically large number of future people now feels to me like arithmetic with numbers we have no right to trust. I call it a simulation haircut: the future still matters, but I refuse to let it swamp ordinary present-day concerns. I have no academic training here, so I may be mixing up separate ideas.

What would count against this? I want the strongest objections and cases, sorted from the ones that would force me to abandon the haircut entirely down to the ones that are merely awkward, and I want the points a defender of my view should concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss. I would also like an honest judgment of whether the position is doing any real work or has just been insulated — whether 'simulation risk' is different in kind from ordinary uncertainty about whether civilization will survive at all.
