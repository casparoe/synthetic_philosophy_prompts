There's a proposal on the ballot in my country this year and I genuinely can't work out what it would actually do. It would create a permanent Office of Democratic Risk—people keep calling it the referee. Every six months it would publish a report scoring the government, the opposition, the courts, and the media regulator on stuff like changing electoral rules close to an election, packing courts, or harassing independent outlets. The twist: if someone's score drops below a fixed threshold, automatic triggers kick in. The president would loose the power to call snap elections for a year, opposition parties would get a guaranteed block of speaking time in the legislature, and the electoral commission would have to face a confidence vote.

Supporters say it makes backsliding costly without letting any party unilaterally brand its opponents 'authoritarian.' Critics say it just gives whoever staffs the office a new weapon, and once the incumbents capture it the scorecard becomes a rubber stamp.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who captures it first, and how? Do governments just time the bad stuff for right after a clean report? Does the office get dragged into exactly the partisan fight over what counts as 'backsliding' that it was supposed to settle? And if the triggers had no teeth—pure naming and shaming—would that be better or worse? Are any of the failure modes fixable without just moving the capture problem up to whoever appoints the office?
