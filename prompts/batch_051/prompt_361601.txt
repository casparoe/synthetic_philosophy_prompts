I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of artificial intelligence course, and a case study we read has been bothering me. An AI lab trains a model to solve multi-step algebra problems by giving it a reward signal only when the final answer matches the answer key. The intermediate steps are never checked; solutions are kept as successful training examples even when an earlier step is mathematically invalid but happens not to change the end result. A different team in the same lab trains a model with a step-level reward instead, scoring each intermediate line as valid or invalid before the solution counts as a success. The lab's engineers describe the choice between these two methods as an entirely practical matter of which labels are cheaper to collect. I suspect that cannot be the whole story. Outcome-only supervision feels as if it presupposes that solving a problem is ultimately a matter of ending up at the right output, so that the path has no independent normative status; step-level supervision feels as if it presupposes that solving a problem is a sequence of individually checkable steps, each of which must be correct in its own right. Are those really the implicit commitments, or can both practices be held under the same underlying theory of what it is to solve a problem? What does each practice strictly presuppose about justification, competence, or the difference between getting a right answer and earning it, and which parts are merely convenient proxies compatible with several theories? And if the outcome-only team had to adopt the presuppositions of the step-level view, what would it have to change in its training pipeline and in its interpretation of what the model has learned?
