The Everettian, many-worlds reading of quantum mechanics has been on the table since the late 1950s, and yet the status of probability in the framework remains disputed in a way that feels frozen. The dispute, as I understand it, runs something like this. One camp says decoherence gives you branching but not the Born weights: the weights have to be added by hand, and until they are derived from the unitary dynamics alone the theory is incomplete. A second camp says the Born weights can be derived from rationality constraints on an agent who knows branching is coming and cares about all their future branches: branch amplitudes are the only degrees of belief that satisfy the usual coherence principles for decision under uncertainty. A third camp says there is no distinctively Everettian probability problem at all — either because every outcome happens, so probability talk is simply out of place, or because probability reflects nothing more than self-locating uncertainty about which branch one is in, to be handled with ordinary epistemic tools. A fourth camp says the problem is structurally the same as giving probability in deterministic classical statistical mechanics, and should be solved the same way: through typicality, through a measure over branch space, or through long-run frequencies in some appropriate limit.

I am not asking which camp wins. I want a diagnosis of why the disagreement has the shape it does and why it has not moved. Is this a formal dispute about whether the would-be derivations secretly assume the Born weights somewhere in their premises? A verbal dispute about what "probability" could mean when all branches are real? A dispute about which question is being asked — what the formalism entails, what a rational agent should do, or what one should expect to observe? Or is it a difference in standards for what counts as an acceptable derivation in the first place? I have a working hypothesis that much of the apparent standoff comes from the camps answering different questions, and that the only residual, substantive fight is between the decision-theoretic approach and the frequency-or-typicality approach over what probability statements are for; but that hypothesis may be too neat. Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement, say which aspects of it are genuine and which are merely apparent, and then say what would count as progress under your diagnosis — or whether we should expect none.
