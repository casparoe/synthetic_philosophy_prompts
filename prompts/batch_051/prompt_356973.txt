I am taking an upper-level seminar on probability, evidence, and decision. The course has contrasted Keynes's treatment of probability with Knight's treatment of uncertainty, but the instructor refuses to settle any case by appeal to the readings; she says a view is only as good as the verdicts it yields. Over the term she has therefore issued a series of rulings, and I have kept a careful record. Next week we will bring her a new case, and I want to predict her ruling from the record.

Her rulings so far are these:

- A fair six-sided die is about to be rolled. She says the probability of rolling a six is exactly one-sixth, and that this is a proper basis for setting odds.
- A racehorse has a long record of races under closely similar conditions. She accepts a numerical probability of winning drawn from those frequencies, but she says the number is only as good as the stability of the reference class from which it is taken.
- A start-up is built around a novel technology with no track record and no close comparables. She says it would be a mistake to attach a number to the chance of success; the case is uncertain, and no forced percentage removes the uncertainty.
- Two rival reconstructions of a broken inscription are proposed, one supported by letter-form parallels and the other by textual parallels, and neither kind of evidence dominates the other. She says the hypotheses cannot be ranked as more or less probable; the comparison is not merely difficult but out of place.
- A clinical trial result is carried from the population in which it was validated to a different population. She says the trial still matters as evidence, but it does not by itself license the same numerical risk in the new population; the number has less weight once it is removed from its original class.

Next week's case is a new drug candidate. The preclinical evidence is unusually strong: the drug binds highly selectively to a receptor whose activation is known, from many independent lines of work, to be necessary and sufficient for the desired effect; the animal models reproduce the effect with no sign of an alternative pathway; and a single small, underpowered randomized trial has a point estimate that favors the drug, though its confidence interval runs from slightly worse than placebo to very effective. The instructor will be asked whether the probability that the drug succeeds in a planned larger confirmatory trial is a magnitude to which a number should be attached, whether it is at least greater than one-half, or whether even that comparison is not yet meaningful.

From this record, what should I expect her to say about the new case, and which earlier ruling is the closest analog? Does that analog settle the matter by itself, or do several rules about probability and evidence fit her earlier verdicts equally well? I can see three candidate rules at work in the seminar: a Keynesian view on which probabilities are objective logical relations that are often only qualitative and non-numerical; a Knightian view on which only measurable risk deserves the name of probability and everything else is uncertainty; and a subjectivist view on which probabilities are personal degrees of belief. I am less interested in the labels than in the structure of the rule that best fits her record. Where do these candidate rules diverge on the new case, what is my best guess about her verdict and the reason for it, and what further case would most cleanly separate the candidates?
