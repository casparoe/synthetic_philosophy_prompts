Last night a friend and I argued at length about a rescue case, and the argument left me less certain than I would like about how I defended my own position. The case was one versus five strangers: a person can save either the one or the five, and my friend took it as obvious that the numbers decide, because five deaths are worse than one. I defended the position associated with John Taurek, that the numbers do not by themselves settle what one should do, and that the aggregate of five lives is not a loss suffered by any one person in the way that would make it automatically outrank the one. Next week I have to lead a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates on exactly this material, and I do not want to reproduce the disorder of that conversation.

Plan the session as a teaching sequence; do not write the explanation itself. What has to be in place before a student can feel why anyone would resist saving the five rather than dismissing the position as sophistry? What single carrying example should the whole session keep returning to, and what features must it have and what must it avoid — for instance, how far should it resemble the trolley cases the students half-remember? I expect at least these misconceptions: a student will reduce the position to favoritism or selfishness, another will take it to be indifference about who dies, and a third will take it to be a denial that consequences matter at all. At which point in the fifty minutes should each be preempted, and how should the sequence be arranged so that the later distinctions depend on the earlier ones rather than being presented as a list of theses? What should I explicitly set aside or refuse to settle at this level? Finally, what one question at the end would allow me to tell whether a student has grasped the anti-aggregation claim — that the losses of the five do not add up into a single greater loss — as opposed to merely repeating that numbers are not decisive?
