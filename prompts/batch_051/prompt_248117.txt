(1) For each person i, suppose their preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person has a utility function u_i that represents their preferences and is unique up to positive affine transformations.

(2) Suppose society (or a social planner) also has preferences over lotteries on social outcomes, and those preferences satisfy the same vNM axioms.

(3) Suppose society satisfies strong Pareto: if every individual weakly prefers lottery p to lottery q, and at least one person strictly prefers p to q, then society strictly prefers p to q.

(4) Then Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says there are vNM utility functions u_1,...,u_n and W representing those preferences such that W = a_1 u_1 + ... + a_n u_n, with every a_i > 0.

(5) But that is just utilitarianism: social welfare is the sum of individual utilities, and the weights are a technical detail.

(6) And notice: none of this required comparing one person's utility level or utility differences with another's. The whole thing uses only each person's own preferences. So utilitarianism does not require interpersonal utility comparisons, and the familiar objection that it does is just wrong.
