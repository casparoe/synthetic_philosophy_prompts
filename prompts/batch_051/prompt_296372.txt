I've been going down a Hobbes hole lately — not the sovereign stuff, the geometry stuff. The idea I keep finding myself defending to friends is this: Hobbes thought geometry is the only real science because we make its objects ourselves. We draw the lines and the circles, so we know their causes, and science is knowledge of causes. That principle seems right to me and it explains a lot: geometry is certain because we constructed it, and it commands a kind of agreement no natural science does; in the study of nature we didn't make the things we're studying, so we're stuck inferring causes from effects and our knowledge there stays provisional. It's a single tidy principle, and it draws a clean line between what we can genuinely know and what we can only conjecture about.

The problem is the conclusion. If we only know what we make, then all of natural science — physics, chemistry, virology, the lot — isn't science in the strict sense, just a kind of well-organized guess. And I can't make myself belive that. Physics doesn't just happen to predict eclipses; it tells us something about how the world is. (I also know Hobbes's own construction projects went badly — he spent years insisting he'd squared the circle — but I don't think that kills the principle.) So I'm stuck. I don't want to be told which side should win; I want to know what it would cost to give up the intuition that we know nature, what it would cost to amend or abandon the maker's-knowledge principle, and whether there's a reading — maybe one on which experimentation is a way of making the phenomena — on which both can survive.
