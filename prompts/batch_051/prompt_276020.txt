Please give me your actual credence in the following proposition, not a survey of options: **the higher-order approach to phenomenal consciousness is right.** More precisely: a first-order mental state (a perception, a bodily sensation, a feeling) is phenomenally conscious — there is something it is like for the subject to be in it — in virtue of the fact that the subject also has a suitable higher-order state that represents, or is directed at, that first-order state. By "suitable" I mean the kind of relation higher-order theorists have in mind: a largely non-inferential, occurrent or dispositional, thought-like or perception-like state that targets the first-order state, without the first-order state needing to target anything in turn. I intend this as a claim about what makes phenomenal consciousness phenomenal, not merely a claim about how we report or know about our conscious states.

I am not asking for a bare number. I want the reasoning laid out as a decomposition. Please give me: (a) your credence in the proposition; (b) the main sub-questions your credence depends on, each with a rough probability and one or two lines of justification; (c) how those sub-probabilities combine into the final number — you need not give a formula, but show which terms raise it, which lower it, and roughly by how much; and (d) the single sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive.

To keep the decomposition from drifting, here are the sub-questions I would like you to address, though you may add or replace terms if you think my factor list is wrong:

1. Are there genuine non-conscious first-order mental states — states with perceptual or affective content that can occur both consciously and unconsciously? (If not, the higher-order story has no raw material to explain.)
2. Given such states, is phenomenal consciousness a relational feature a state acquires by being picked out by something else in the mind, rather than an intrinsic feature of the state itself, as first-order representationalists hold?
3. Given a relational view, is the relevant relation specifically higher-order representation, rather than global availability, attentional amplification, or presence in a working-memory workspace (the global workspace and attention family of views)?
4. Given higher-order representation, is the higher-order state best understood as an occurrent, thought-like state; a dispositional state; a perception-like inner monitor; or something self-representational (a state that is also about itself)? How much of your credence in the higher-order approach depends on which of these variants is right?
5. Can the higher-order relation actually supply subjective character — the "something it is like" — rather than merely explaining why the subject takes herself to be in a conscious state? The standing objection here is that a representation of a state seems to add a judgment or a piece of monitoring, not an experience.
6. What is the chance that the best version of a competitor — first-order representationalism, self-representationalism, global workspace or attention accounts, or illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — is instead correct, and how much should that discount the higher-order approach?

For calibration: my own credence in the proposition is around 0.35; a colleague with no stake in the literature puts it below 0.1; and a third person who works on inner-sense views puts it above 0.6.
