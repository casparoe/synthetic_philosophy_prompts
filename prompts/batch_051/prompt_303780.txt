I have been turning over a puzzle about grammar that I cannot settle on my own, and I would like to know what principle, if any, holds my judgments together. I was taught in school that a noun is a person, place, or thing, that a verb is an action word, that pronouns have cases, and that prepositions are small words expressing spatial relations. The more I look at real sentences, the less those definitions hold up, and yet the categories still feel real to me, as though I am tracking something genuine about words rather than just applying a label my teacher used.

Here are my verdicts, given honestly.

In 'The explosion destroyed the shed,' 'explosion' names an event, but I call it a noun. In 'He sleeps,' 'sleeps' is a verb even though sleeping is not something one does; there is no action. 'Arrival' names the act of arriving, and I call it a noun. In 'Running is exhausting,' I call 'running' a noun, or at least noun-like; in 'I am running,' I call it a verb. In 'Red is my favorite color,' I call 'red' a noun. But in 'The rich get richer,' I do not want to call 'rich' a noun; it still feels like an adjective standing in for a group of people. Those last two cases look similar, and my verdicts differ, which is exactly the sort of thing I want a principle to sort out. In 'swimming pool,' I do not call 'swimming' an adjective; it behaves like a noun modifying another noun, as in 'tennis court,' since one cannot say 'a very swimming pool.' In 'I looked up the chimney,' 'up' is a preposition. In 'I looked the word up,' it is not; it is part of the verb. I say 'Who did you see?,' not 'Whom did you see?,' and in my speech 'who' does the job of both subject and object, with 'whom' surviving mainly after prepositions, as in 'to whom.' I do not take that to mean the subject/object distinction has collapsed, since I still say 'I saw him' and would never say 'Him saw I.' I say 'It was me,' and I take that to be correct even though 'me' is, by the school account, an object form sitting in the subject's slot. Yet I judge 'between you and I' to be wrong. In 'the man down the street's car,' the possessive marker attaches to the whole phrase 'the man down the street,' not to 'street,' so I do not think the possessive is a case ending on a noun at all. In 'He is taller than me,' 'than' feels like a preposition taking an object form, not like a conjunction followed by an unfinished clause.

Is there a single principle about what makes something a noun, a verb, an adjective, a preposition, or a case that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and tell me what it says about some cases I have not listed, such as 'the poor,' 'a fun party,' and 'five foot two.' If no single principle gets all of them, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why. I would rather have a principle that is honest about what it rules out than one that flatters every one of my gut reactions.
