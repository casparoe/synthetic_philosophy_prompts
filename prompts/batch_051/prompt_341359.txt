Researchers who want AI systems to be legible often argue that a model should be trained to report the considerations that led to its output, not merely to emit a verbal reconstruction an auditor will accept. The central obstacle is that a model can learn to produce post-hoc rationalizations: fluent, plausible explanations that are only weakly coupled to whatever process actually produced the decision. This yields a specific conceptual claim — that training explicitly for legible reports does not merely improve the packaging of an unchanged decision procedure, but makes the reported reasons the real reasons. How would one test that claim? Design a concrete experiment that would separate faithful reasoning from post-hoc rationalization in a trained model. Choose a task family in which the correct answer and the ground-truth reason for that answer can be made to vary independently — for example, problems whose correct answer stays the same when the supporting premise is swapped for a different one — so that a model can be right for two different reasons. Specify at least two training regimes to compare, the measurements you would collect, and the held-out cases on which you would collect them. State the pattern of results that would favor each reading. Then give an honest account of what the test cannot settle: in particular, whether any purely behavioral test can distinguish a faithful explanation from a fluent post-hoc one, or whether the distinction already presupposes an unobservable notion of the model's real reasons. If part of the claim cannot be operationalized, say which part can be tested and which part must be stipulated. Finally, state how confident you are that your proposed test tracks the conceptual distinction, and identify the single point in the design you are least sure of.
