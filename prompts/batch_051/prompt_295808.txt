I teach phil of mind, and after years of watching the enactivism-versus-representationalism fight in the literature, I've started to think the two sides are no longer argueing about a question that could settle anything—but I can't tell if that's a real insight or just fatigue. Here's how I'd put the case. Representationalists these days mostly don't mean little sentence-like symbols in a language of thought; they mean internal states whose job is to stand in for states of the world and guide behavior, and they're happy for the standing-in to be partial, probabilistic, and action-oriented. Enactivists don't deny that the brain has internal dynamics, and they don't even deny that those dynamics carry information or track regularities. What they deny is that tracking, covariance, or informational content is enough to make an internal state a representation, and they say the real explanatory work is done by the organism's history of embodied coupling to its environment and its self-maintaining, skilled activity, not by internal stand-ins. The two sides now seem to accept the same neural data and the same behavioral results; they even agree that in some systems you can translate between a dynamical description and a representational one. And yet they still line up on opposite sides of a case like the neural activity during a reach toward a cup: one side says that activity is a representation of the target, the other says it's just a phase in a coupled dynamical system and calling it a representation adds nothing. My working theory is that the stalemate is not empirical at all. It's a disagreement about what an explanation of cognition has to do—specifically about whether subpersonal content (the idea that bits of the brain can have contents without the whole person representing anything) is actually earning its explanatory keep, and about where the burden of proof sits when one side says the word representation is doing no work. But I'm not sure; maybe there's a real empirical or formal disagreement hiding inside that, or maybe the two sides are answering different questions under one name. So here's what I'd like from you: a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled and what kind of disagreement it really is, with reasons, plus an honest account of what would count as progress given that diagnosis. Commit to a definite diagnosis and give your reasons for it, rather than laying out the options. And write it plain: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just continuous prose like an email.
