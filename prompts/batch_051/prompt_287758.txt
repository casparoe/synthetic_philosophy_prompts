A friend and I just spent two hours going in circles about whether we should spend real money trying to keep a language alive once its last fluent speakers are very old or gone. Her line was the standard one: a language isn't just a set of labels stuck on the same thoughts; it's a whole way of slicing up experience. If the grammar makes you mark how you know a claim (you saw it, somone told you, you inferred it), then every time you speak you're gently forced to track your own evidence. When the language dies that quirk of attention dies with it, even if dictionaries and recordings exist. I pushed back with the newer thing: a large language model trained on a big documentary corpus of an endangered language can come out fluent and grammatically sound in it, including all the distinctions like evidentials and honorifics, with no living speakers at all. The patterns — the 'way of slicing up experience' — get preserved in the model, and arguably more completely and more accessibly than in any archive. So does her argument survive? I mainly want to know which premise is actually under pressure: is the value in the patterns themselves, in which case the model really does preserve the thing; or is it in living speakers actually using them, in which case the model stuff changes nothing? And is there a repaired version of her argument that still gives a reason to pay for language maintenance, rather than just documentation plus model training?
