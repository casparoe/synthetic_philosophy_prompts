I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or theoretical computer science beyond an undergraduate algorithms course, and I recently read a blog post about Solomonoff induction that has been nagging at me. The author's summary, as I understand it, is this. Solomonoff induction solves the problem of induction by placing a prior over all computable hypotheses, where each hypothesis is a program for a universal Turing machine and receives probability 2^-K, with K the length of the shortest program that implements it. Shorter programs therefore get exponentially more prior probability, which the author calls Occam's razor made mathematically precise. According to the author, Solomonoff proved that any computable sequence will eventually be predicted almost perfectly by this method, so induction is in principle solved. The author then draws two practical conclusions. First, approximating Kolmogorov complexity with an off-the-shelf compressor such as gzip or LZMA would yield a workable, near-optimal general-purpose prediction engine for time series and text. Second, Occam's razor is now a theorem rather than a heuristic, so whenever two models fit the data equally well, rationality requires choosing the one with the shorter description; this, the author says, is what justifies preferring simpler machine learning models to more complex ones.

I am inclined to trust the mathematical core but suspicious of the jump to practical compressors, and I cannot tell whether the claim that Occam's razor is now a theorem is accurate or rhetorically inflated. I would like a careful accuracy audit of this summary. Which parts would someone who works on Solomonoff induction and algorithmic information theory accept, which parts would they wince at, and which are simply wrong? Do the two practical conclusions survive once the summary is corrected?
