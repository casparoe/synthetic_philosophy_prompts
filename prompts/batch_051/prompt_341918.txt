I work as a legislative drafter in a small jurisdiction that’s been consolidating old statutes and trying to clean up common-law fictions. We keep going round in circles on one question: when should a bill use a legal fiction — saying the law will treat X as if it were Y even though everyone knows it isn’t — rather than changing the substantive rule directly? My attorney-general wants a reusable procedure for our office, not another essay. The people involved are not all lawyers; some are elected members who will have to defend the drafting at committee. I’ve read enough Bentham to know he thought fictions were basically lies the law tolerates until codification, and enough Maine to know he thought fictions were one of the ways early law changed while pretending not to. What I can’t figure out is whether that historical point actually settles anything for a modern drafter, or whether there are still legitimate uses. So, acting as an expert on legal philosophy and statutory drafting, think carefully and perhaps start by mapping out the different jobs a fiction can do, then write me a decision procedure my office could run on a proposed fiction. It should be a checklist or set of questions we can work through in an afternoon, with the possible answers and what each answer commits us to. It should say where the procedure stops with a “no, don’t use a fiction,” where it hands the decision to someone because the real issue is a values choice rather than a drafting choice, and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as technical tests. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Then apply it to three proposals we’ve actually had: (1) a bill that deems a person who has been missing for seven years to be dead for purposes of remarriage and succession, even though the evidence doesn’t establish death; (2) a bill that deems online platforms to be “publishers” of user content for defamation purposes, even though the platform does not select or see the content before it’s posted; and (3) a bill that treats a child born after the father’s death via stored gametes as having been born during the father’s lifetime for inheritance purposes. For each, tell me what your procedure says and why. Make sure at least one of these comes out as a clear “do not use a fiction,” and say what the substantive alternative would be.
