I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep going back and forth on the argument from illusion. I've written out a version that feels tight whenever I read it, but I can't locate exactly where it's doing something wrong. Here's the argument:

(1) Look at a straight stick half-submerged in water. It looks bent. Surely there is something I'm aware of that is bent — the bent shape is right there in my experience.

(2) The physcial stick is not bent.

(3) So the bent thing I'm aware of cannot be the stick; it must be some kind of mental item (a sense-datum, an appearance, whatever we call it).

(4) Now take a veridical perception, like seeing an actually straight stick in good light. From the inside, this could be subjectively indistinguishable from some illusory experience — I might not be able to tell the two apart just by introspection.

(5) Since the illusory experience is fundamentally a matter of being related to a mental item, and the veridical experience is subjectively the same kind of experience, the veridical one must have the same underlying structure: it too is a relation to a mental item.

(6) So in every case of perception, veridical or not, what I'm directly aware of is a mental item, and physical objects are at best known indirectly.

My hunch is that (5) is the load-bearing step and that it slides from "seems the same from the inside" to "is the same in kind," but I can't say precisely what the fallcy is. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, which premise should I reject, and what would the corrected version say? If (5) is the culprit, give the strongest reading of it on which it's true and then tell me whether the argument still goes through on that reading — and if some other step fails instead, tell me which one and why.
