A popular-science article I read summarizes the mental imagery debate roughly like this. For decades, cognitive scientists argued about whether mental images are real. Stephen Kosslyn's mental rotation and image-scanning experiments found that people take longer to rotate a shape through larger angles and longer to scan between points that are farther apart in an imagined display, just as if they were operating on an actual picture. Kosslyn took these results to show that imagery uses a depictive format: the mind lays information out in a spatial medium, a kind of picture in the head. His main opponent, Zenon Pylyshyn, argued that mental images are not images at all but descriptions in a language-like code, and that participants in those experiments are merely using their tacit knowledge of how physical objects and pictures behave to produce the response times they expect. The article then says the dispute was effectively settled by neuroimaging: when people visualize, early visual areas of the brain light up with the same retinotopic organization seen in vision, which proves that the image really is laid out spatially in the cortex. It concludes that Pylyshyn was wrong to deny imagery, and that the mind stores and manipulates picture-like representations after all.

I don't have a stake in the fight myself, though I'm inclined to treat the neuroimaging result as decisive because it sounds like direct evidence. How much of this summary should I trust?
