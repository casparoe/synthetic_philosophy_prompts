Had to sit through our new whistleblowing compliance training last week (I just got moved into a management role) and something about it has been bugging me since. The training was really clear on two things at once. If I report certain kinds of wrongdoing — fraud, bribery, safety violations, harassment — through the internal hotline or to a regulator, the company says it won't retaliate, and the law where I live largely backs that up. But if I leak internal emails that are embarrassing but not illegal, or gripe to a journalist about how the place is run, that gets logged as disloyal conduct and it's a firing offense. So the practical rule seems to be: the bigger and more public the secret about the organization's own misconduct, the less loyalty you owe it, while small secrets about how the place actually operates are the ones that get you fired.

What theory of loyalty and betrayal does a practice like that presuppose? I can see at least two different readings. One is that whistleblowing isn't really a betrayal at all, because the employer only has a claim on your loyalty while it's operating inside its own stated purpose or the law, so you cant betray someone by exposing that they broke the rules. The other is that it is a betrayal, but a justified one, and the law protects it because the public interest outweighs loyalty to the group. Those seem pretty different to me. Which one does the practice actually commit to, or is it underdetermined between them? And is the underlying view coherent — does it fit with how the same companies treat other disloyal acts like jumping to a competitor? What would have to change if you replaced it with a strict loyalty view where reporting on the group always counts against you no matter how bad the misconduct is?
