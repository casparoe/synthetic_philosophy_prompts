I've been trying to work out what I actually think about eating animal products, and my responses to particular cases keep running ahead of any principle I try to fit over them. Buying factory-farmed chicken in a wealthy city where dried beans, lentils, rice, tofu, and frozen vegetables are all cheaper per gram of protein strikes me as simply not justifiable, and I don't think taste or convenience lets the shopper off the hook. A herding family in a semi-arid region, eating the meat and milk of their own cattle that graze on land that cannot be ploughed—and whose occasional sale pays for the children's schooling—strikes me as entirely permissible, perhaps obviously right. A guest in that household who is served the same food, and who knows that refusing would be a serious insult from hosts with little else to offer, should eat it; making a show of refusal would itself be wrong. Eating rope-grown oysters and mussels seems fine, since I doubt they can suffer, though I admit uncertainty. Eating crickets or mealworms raised for food seems permissible but more uncomfortable, though I cannot fully say why. Backyard eggs from well-kept hens, eggs that would otherwise rot, seem fine. Wild venison from a cull that will happen regardless, from an animal that otherwise lived free, seems less bad than almost any farmed meat. And drinking milk from a small, high-welfare dairy in a rich city where cheap oat and soy milk are everywhere strikes me as not fine, even though the cows are treated decently, because no need is pressing enough.

The case that unsettles me most is the contrast between the herding family's beef and the city shopper's chicken. If I compare the two products by the animal's likely mental life, the steer worries me more than the chicken, yet my verdict is much harsher on the chicken. That suggests the human context is not just a hurdle the eater has to clear; it seems to change how heavily the animal's suffering counts, which I both believe and find suspicious.

The working rule I keep reaching for is roughly this: eating an animal product is permissible only when the actually available alternatives would impose a nutritional, economic, or social cost substantial enough to matter, and the product did not involve suffering wildly disproportionate to that cost. But stated this loosely, the rule either becomes so strict that it condemns the herding family and the guest, since one can always say they could simply refuse, or so elastic that it excuses the city shopper on the grounds that cooking beans is a real cost. I do not need a principle that makes me maximally comfortable; I need one that tells me which of my verdicts to revise. What principle best captures the set, and what would it say about cases I have not settled, such as a small-scale fisher's family in a coastal community with few cheap plant proteins, or milk from a well-run small dairy for someone with abundant alternatives?
