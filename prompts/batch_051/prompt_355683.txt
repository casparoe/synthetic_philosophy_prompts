I serve on the governing council of a jointly owned community building, having recently joined after several years of scheduling disputes between the two groups that share it: a traditionally religious congregation and a secular collective of artists and organizers. A member has proposed a trial mechanism, intended to let the two sides trade concessions rather than re-litigate first principles. Each adult member receives 100 objection credits per quarter. Any member may post a proposal for a use of the shared hall, kitchen, or garden — an event series, a wall display, a menu for common meals, a renovation. During the following week, members may allocate any number of their current credits for or against the proposal; allocations are public. At the close of the week, the proposal is approved unless the credits allocated against it strictly exceed the credits allocated for it. Credits may be transferred freely between members, including for money, at whatever rate the parties agree; every transfer is logged publicly. Credits expire at the end of the quarter.

Approach this as a political philosopher who works on mechanism design and on the moral limits of markets would. Treat it as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a moral position to endorse or condemn outright. The proposal's backer claims it realizes gains from trade in moral compromise: each side spends its credits only where its members feel most strongly, and is compensated when it yields elsewhere. I want a committed picture of how it would actually behave.

Work through the incentives carefully, with some explicit brainstorming about second-order effects, before committing. In particular: what would it incentivize among the two groups, among ordinary low-engagement members, and among the council that administers the ledger? How would it be gamed — bait proposals meant to drain opponents' credits, strategic late allocation, buying blocking power with cash, manufacturing opposition in order to be paid to withdraw it, or collusion across the two sides? Does it deliver the promised gains from trade, or does it quietly change what members are willing to treat as negotiable? Which failure mode would dominate, and can it be fixed without gutting the scheme?

I am not looking for a balanced survey of possibilities. Take a definite position on what this mechanism would most likely do and whether it is salvageable, and defend it. The council votes on the trial in three weeks, and my own assessment will shape how I vote.
