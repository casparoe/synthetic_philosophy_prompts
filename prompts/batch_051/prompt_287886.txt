I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I want a two-voice feature on a live dispute in epistemology. Please write a short dialogue, about 900 words, between two equally skilled epistemologists who disagree about luminosity and the KK principle. The first defends a restricted KK thesis: for a person's current conscious states, if the state obtains — for example, feeling cold, or its being vividly as if one sees red — then the person is in a position to know, upon reflection, that it obtains, and this is what makes knowing that one knows available in such cases. The second defends anti-luminosity: no non-trivial condition is luminous, because knowledge requires a margin for error, and any condition that can be acquired or lost gradually will have borderline cases in which the condition obtains but the subject is not in a position to know that it obtains.

Have them work through the same concrete case — a subject warmed through a long series of barely discriminable steps — rather than trading position statements. The anti-luminosity character should press the claim that reflection cannot manufacture knowledge without a reliable discrimination; the luminosity character should press the margin-for-error premise and the slide from the vagueness of a predicate like feels cold to a failure of self-knowledge about a conscious state. Let each character make a genuine concession that matters to the argument. End the dialogue with a definite sense of which position has carried the stronger case, made clear through the argumentative trajectory and acknowledged in the losing character's concession, rather than a neutral both-sides-have-a-point close.
