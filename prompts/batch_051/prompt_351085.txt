I am a novelist at work on a book about a mid-century European customs clerk who, without ever becoming a fanatic, turns into an efficient participant in a machinery of deportation. To write him, I have been reading Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men against Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners, not in the hope of settling the historical dispute, but because it decides whether a character like mine can be drawn truthfully. My notes eventually produced the argument below, and it seems to leave me with a choice between the history and the novel.

(1) On Browning's account, the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were not selected for ideology or sadism: they were mostly middle-aged Hamburg policemen, family men, with no prior history of mass violence. (2) Most of them killed, and went on killing for months; the minority who asked to be excused were not shot, court-martialed, or imprisoned, but paid mostly in embarrassment and in being thought weak. (3) So what explains the killing is not anything particular to the killers but the situation itself—orders, comradeship, the gradual escalation of tasks, the difficulty of breaking step with the group. (4) Generalize: what an ordinary person does in the worst circumstances is explained by the situation, not by his particular character. (5) If that is true, then the novelist's working theory—that a person's choices under pressure reveal who he is—fails exactly where it matters most, because it is the situation, not the man, that does the revealing. (6) My book would then be built on a mistake, treating the clerk's small jealousies, loyalties, and self-deceptions as causes of his slide when they are at best a commentary on it. (7) And yet some men in that same battalion did refuse, and we do blame and praise people for what they do, and the whole tradition of character-based fiction cannot simply be one long error.

I cannot find the seam. Is this a valid argument with an expensive premise, or is there a bad inference between two numbered steps—and which one, and why did it look sound? If the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to surrender for someone who wants to keep both Browning's ordinariness and the idea that character explains action? I would rather give up a too-strong generalization than give up the novel's premise that action discloses character, but I want to know exactly what that bargain costs.
