I'm a TA grading for a medieval philosophy survey, and I've been going back and forth on one exam answer long enough that I want a second pair of eyes. The question the students got was:

"Abelard holds that sin consists in consent rather than in the act. Explain what 'consent' means for Abelard, why the suggestion/delight/consent distinction matters for his view, and critically assess one objection to his account."

Here's what the student wrote:

"Abelard's central claim is that sin is not the outward act or even the mere desire for it. In the Ethics he distinguishes suggestion, delight, and consent: a thought can be suggested, it can produce pleasure or desire, and neither is sin by itself. Sin enters only at consent. Consent amounts to the will's inner resolve to perform the act; Abelard thinks the external act adds nothing to the guilt, since God considers the mind with which the deed is done rather than the deed itself. So the same outward deed can be sin for one person and not for another.

This is why Abelard is usually called an intention-theorist. I think his view is that sin just is an occurrent intention in the soul — the mental act of saying 'yes' to the suggested act — rather than any bodily movement. The stages matter because he wants to leave room for temptation: a monk tormented by lustful images hasn't sinned while he refuses consent.

The standard objection is that this makes sin too private. If sin is inner consent, no human judge can ever know that a crime was a sin, and someone who does terrible harm with a clear 'conscience' is off the hook. Abelard seems to accept that consequence, since he thinks only God sees the heart. His view also seems to imply that you can't be culpable for the unforseen consequences of your action, because you never consented to those consequences, and that's implausible. So while Abelard is right to separate the deed from the sin, he overcorrects by treating the deed as if it had no moral weight at all."
