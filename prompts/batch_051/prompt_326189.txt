Consider the following argument and say whether it goes through:

(1) To have a will, something must be a minded agent: it must have beliefs and desires, and act on them.
(2) A people is a collection of human individuals, not an additional individual with a mind of its own.
(3) A collection of individuals has no beliefs or desires over and above the beliefs and desires of its members.
(4) So a people has no will over and above the wills of its members.
(5) Therefore 'the will of the people' can mean only some construction out of the members' wills — a common core, or an aggregate produced by a counting rule.
(6) But in any contested political case the members' wills diverge: there is no common core, and the 'aggregate' is an artifact of whichever rule does the counting (majority, supermajority, weighted vote).
(7) So exactly in the cases where populists invoke it, there is no will of the people.
(8) A non-existent will cannot justify a policy.
(9) Therefore, in contested politics, appeals to the will of the people are either empty or a particular faction's will dressed up as everyone's.

As a test case: after a 52–48 referendum vote to leave a political union, was leaving 'willed' by the people of that union, or is the phrase doing something other than reporting a psychological fact?

I half-suspect step (5) is where the argument slips, but I can't say why without either admitting group minds or conceding that the people's will is invented rather than discovered. Then briefly say whether the corrected argument is one populists should fear. Keep the response under 500 words.
