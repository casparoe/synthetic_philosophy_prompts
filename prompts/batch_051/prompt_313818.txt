In my school's math club we have been reading about proofs that cannot be held in one human mind, and I keep running into an asymmetry that I cannot explain. The four-color theorem was proved in 1976 by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken only because a computer checked more than a thousand configurations, and no single mathematician has ever held the whole argument in view at once. In 1998 Thomas Hales proved the Kepler conjecture with a proof so heavy in computation that a fully machine-verified version was not completed until 2014. Mathematicians now call both results theorems, yet both are still routinely described as second-class: technically established, but suspect, unsatisfying, or not explanatory, because no person can take in the reasoning. That would be coherent if proof were supposed to be something a person can survey.

But the same community accepts a three-hundred-page human proof in a journal on the word of two or three referees who could not possibly hold every step in view during the few weeks they spend with it, and who are at least as likely to skim past an error as a deterministic program is to miscount its cases. If unsurveyability and fallible checking are the objection, then the long human proof seems to fail on both counts too, yet it is treated as a normal, fully mathematical achievement while the computer proof is treated as if it needed an asterisk. The standard justifications I have heard are that machines verify without explaining, that software or hardware might contain bugs, and that human referees contribute mathematical judgment, but long human proofs often verify without explaining either, referees demonstrably miss errors, and a referee's judgment is precisely the kind of tired, biased, error-prone process one would not want to trust over an exhaustive check.

I would like the strongest possible defense of this asymmetry, one that does not simply beg the question by defining proof as something surveyable. Test it against nearby cases before you decide: a thousand-page human proof written and checked piecemeal by twenty authors, a formal machine proof whose verification program has never itself been verified, and a short, elegant proof that explains everything but contains one lemma nobody has checked carefully. Say clearly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please answer in continuous prose, like an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and with your overall verdict stated in the opening.
