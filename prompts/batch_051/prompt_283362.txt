I have spent my working life arranging time for other people—flashbacks, summary, tense, the difference between a scene that is happening and a scene that is remembered—and that may be why I keep returning to the presentism–eternalism dispute in academic philosophy. Presentists hold that only the present exists; eternalists hold that past, present, and future are all equally real. What puzzles me is that the two camps seem to accept the same physics and the same facts about how time appears, and yet the debate has not moved in decades. I was reading J. M. E. McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (1908) and found this passage: "But this is impossible. An event can never cease to be an event. It can never get out of any time series in which it once is." I take McTaggart to be giving the presentist's deepest reason for rejecting a purely earlier-than/later-than universe: without a real past-present-future distinction, he thinks, there is no genuine change, and so no time. Eternalists reply that change requires only that a thing have different properties at different times, which the presentist then dismisses as change's fossil, not change itself. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name—presentists are asking what a world must be like for our experience of passage to be veridical, while eternalists are asking what the smallest ontology is that makes the physics of spacetime true. Is that diagnosis right, or is the disagreement verbal, factual, methodological, or a clash of bedrock intuitions? And given your diagnosis, is there anything—a distinction, a formal result, a new question—that could count as progress, or should we stop expecting the dispute to end?
