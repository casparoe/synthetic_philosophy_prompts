I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher of logic who defends a Lucas–Penrose style anti-mechanist argument: roughly, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show human mathematical understanding can outrun any Turing machine or formal system, so the mind (or at least the part of it that does math) can't be a computer program. For what its worth she explicitly distances herself from the Hameroff–Penrose quantum microtubule stuff, but she does think the argument tells us something hard about what a physical account of the mind would have to look like.

You're a philosopher of logic and mind who knows the Lucas–Penrose dialectic cold. Take a few minutes to brainstorm the shape of the interview before writing, and then draft 8–10 questions.

I want questions that go beyond having her restate the view. Press where the literature actually presses. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for every question, give the follow-up you'd use if she takes the line a sophisticated defender is most likely to take:

- the consistency assumption: to get the Gödel sentence to "bite," she seems to need to know that her own mind (or the relevant formal system) is consistent, and it's unclear how she could know that;
- the "which machine am I?" problem: running the argument on herself seems to require knowing which formal system she'd be, and the nearby point that the conclusion might only be "I can't know that I'm a machine" rather than "I'm not one";
- the soundness-vs-consistency issue: it's one thing to prove that a system, if consistent, can't prove its Gödel sentence; it's another to claim a human can just see that the sentence is true, and the Feferman-style worry is that human mathematical conviction is fallible, open-ended, and error-prone, which is exactly what an algorithm-plus-noise could also be;
- the jump from "human math isn't algorithmic" to any substantive statement about physics, e.g. that the brain needs non-computable processes of a sort current physics doesn't give us;
- whether the argument is best run for one individual mathematician or for the whole mathematical community, and whether the community version is even coherent;
- what, if anything, would count as evidence against the position.

Its the follow-ups that matter more than the questions. For each one, anticipate her likely response and push on the point in that response. For example, if you picture her answering the consistency worry by saying she doesn't need a formal proof of her own consistency — only a human insight that a particular formal system is sound — the follow-up should ask what that insight is if not itself an algorithm-plus-noise. That kind of thing.

No generic "what drew you to logic" questions. I'd rather have 8 sharp ones than 14 polite ones. And if I've mischaracterized one of the standard objections, or the whole framing rests on a confusion about what Gödel's theorem actually shows, tell me that plainly instead of writing around it.
