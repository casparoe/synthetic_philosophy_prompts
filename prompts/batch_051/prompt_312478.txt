I have recently been put in charge of supervising a suite of algorithmic trading systems whose behavior has grown too complex for anyone on the team to track in purely mechanical terms. My colleagues habitually describe these systems from the intentional stance: they say that one system 'thinks' volatility is about to spike, that another is 'trying to' exit a crowded position, or that a third 'believes' a sector is mispriced. I want to determine whether that intentional-stance description — treating the system as an agent with beliefs and desires — is picking out a real pattern in the Dennettian sense, or whether it is merely a convenient narrative we have projected onto behavior that could be predicted just as well without it.

Suppose I provisionally accept the Dennettian criterion: a pattern is real to the extent that it is projectible, i.e., adopting it lets one predict otherwise noisy data better than comparably simple alternatives. How could one actually test whether the intentional-stance description of such a system latches onto a real pattern rather than an overfit or anthropomorphized one? Please design the test concretely: what data to collect, what alternative models or stances to compare (including physical-stance and design-stance baselines), and what results would count in each direction. Then say precisely what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, would it settle the question of whether the systems genuinely have beliefs and desires, or only that treating them as if they did is predictively useful? And what, if anything, would remain outside the reach of the test?
