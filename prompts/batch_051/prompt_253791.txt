I have been puzzling over a question about deduction, and I recently noticed that a piece of modern technology seems to take a side in it without anyone's saying so.

The puzzle goes like this. The rule modus ponens says that from "If P then Q" and "P," you may infer "Q." In a well-known story, a tortoise accepts both statements but still refuses to accept Q. We then offer it a third statement: "If P, and if P then Q, then Q." The tortoise accepts that too but still will not take the step to Q, and the same thing happens no matter how many further statements we add. The usual moral of the story is that a rule of inference is not just another premise: adding premises can never force the step the rule licenses, so treating the rule as a premise starts an endless regress.

Now consider the proof-checking software now used to verify mathematical proofs. The checker has a fixed list of inference rules built into it. It verifies a proof by confirming that each step is an application of one of those rules, and it certifies the proof if every step checks out. But the rules themselves are never proved, questioned, or justified by the checker; they are simply applied. That seems like a quiet commitment: the software treats the basic rules of inference as things we are entitled to use without first establishing them, and perhaps as things that cannot be established by the kind of argument the software checks.

What view of the justification of deduction does this design presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that basic rules of inference cannot be justified, or merely that they need not be justified inside the system even if they can be justified from outside? Where exactly does the practice stop being neutral between rival views? And what would a proof checker have to do differently if its designers held that even a rule like modus ponens requires an explicit justification before anyone may rely on it? Please answer in American English.
