I'm giving a philosophy department colloquium in a few weeks defending a norm-relative account of causation by omission. My thesis is that omissions can be genuine causes, but only against a background of norms or defaults: the lifeguard's not jumping in causes the swimmer's death because lifeguards are supposed to jump in, whereas my not watering your garden isn't a cause of your plants dying, even though the counterfactual 'if I had watered them, they'd have lived' is true. My line is that bare counterfactual dependence overgenerates — by that standard the absence of a meteor shield caused the dinosaurs' extinction — and that the norm is doing the metaphysical selecting, not just the conversational highlighting. The audience is mostly metaphysicians and philosophers of science who are comfortable with counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation, plus two tort lawyers who keep saying but-for is all the law needs. Build me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I should actually expect, in roughly the order they'll come and how damaging each is, each phrased as this audience would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply, what I should conceed up front, and the weak spot in my argument I should defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. At the end, say how confident you are about the prep sheet and single out the point you're least sure of.
