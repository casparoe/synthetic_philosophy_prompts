I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, and this term we have spent several weeks on Russell's theory of descriptions. For our final session, I would like to end not with a summary handout but with a poem, something that can serve as both a mnemonic and a miniature piece of argument. Please write a Petrarchan sonnet that follows the theory through its own structure. The octave should present the puzzle in its classical form: a definite description such as the present King of France appears to function as a referring expression, yet when it fails to denote, we seem forced to choose between admitting a shadowy non-existent object and treating the sentence as neither true nor false. Let the volta, the turn between octave and sestet, be the moment Russell refuses that choice. The grammatical subject is not the logical subject, and a sentence of the form the F is G is really a conjunction of an existence claim, a uniqueness claim, and a predication. The sestet should then show why the negative existential poses no special mystery on this account: to say that the present King of France does not exist is not to ascribe nonexistence to some entity, but to deny the existence-and-uniqueness conjunction outright. The closing couplet should state the enduring lesson, that grammatical form and logical form can come apart, and that failing to see this generates phantom problems. The poem need not be didactic, but it should be accurate enough that a philosopher of language would recognize a faithful compression of the view rather than a vague allusion to it, and good enough that a reader who has never studied the theory could still enjoy it as a poem.
