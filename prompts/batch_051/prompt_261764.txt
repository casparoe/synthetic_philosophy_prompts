I'm trying to think through something for an environmental ethics course, and we keep going in circles about insecticides. The claim I see everywhere is that the responses you see in insects after you spray them — curling, twitching, grooming the contaminated area — are just nociception, the reflex-like detection of damage, and not pain in any sense that carries moral weight. So the company line is that spraying is basically fine, or fine unless it kills pollinators people happen to care about. I find that too convienent, but I also don't want to just assert the opposite. What I want is a test, or a battery of tests, that would actually separate "the insect is having an experience worth caring about" from "the insect is a little biological machine with damage-detection loops." I've read that in fish pain debates people did things like give analgesics and see whether the fish keeps avoiding a noxious stimulus; could something like that work for insects, scaled way down? How would you operationalize the difference concretely, what results would count for each side, and where would you draw the line between a positive result and what it licenses?

More specifically: suppose you get the strongest plausible positive result for insect suffering (an insect pays some cost to avoid a treated surface, say, and an analgesic changes that behavior without changing the reflex). What would that establish about pesticides — that we should prefer faster-killing or anesthetizing methods, that we should give up some crop yield, that it's worse to use slow nerve poisons than quick ones? And what would it still not establish? I'm not asking for a survey or citations; I want a concrete design and then a honest account of the residue no experiment could settle, including whether "suffering" is even the kind of thing a test can get at, or whether it's really a decision about where to draw the line.
