Lately I keep turning over a sentence in the first section of Chapter II of the Westminster Confession of Faith, where God is described as "a most pure Spirit, invisible without body, parts, or passions," and then, a few clauses later, as "most loving, gracious, merciful, long-suffering." What I cannot sort out is how those two descriptions are supposed to hold together, and I want to ask about a case the Westminster divines did not have in front of them. A close friend of mine works with children in palliative care, and she has told me that she can no longer worship a God who does not suffer, because a love that is not wounded by the beloved's agony seems to her not love at all but a kind of distant performance. My brother, who is a pastor, says the opposite: divine love must be without the turbulence and dependence of passion, or God would be as changeable as the weather and as needy as a human lover, so he insists the Confession is right to deny passions to God. What I want is a careful way of testing whether the Confession's phrase "without body, parts, or passions" applies to my friend's case — a caregiver who cannot imagine true compassion without shared suffering — and, if it does, whether the same paragraph's claim that God is "most loving, gracious, merciful, long-suffering" is compatible with her being completely wrong. I am less interested in which of them wins than in whether the two clauses can be made to say something coherent, and what a person would have to give up about ordinary human love to accept both.
