I'm in a philosophy of death course and our professor (call her Rina) has been giving verdicts on made-up cases about whether a death—or a period of unconsciousness—is bad for the person. Her track record: a 30-year-old killed instantly and painlessly on the way to her wedding—bad. A 95-year-old who dies after a week unconscious on a ventilator, never to wake—not bad. A 20-year-old put in a dreamless medical coma for a year, who wakes up biologically 20 and will live exactly as long and as well as she would have, just one calendar year later—not bad. The same coma, but it ages her normally and costs her a year of conscious life at the end—bad. She also says a terminal patient who spends six months dreading death suffers during those six months, but the painless death itself isnt the bad part; the bad part is what it takes away. So I figured she was running a straight deprivation account: death is bad to the extent it stops you from having future goods.

But now this new case has me stuck. A 45-year-old man lives alone on an island, healthy and content day to day, no relationships, no projects, no plans or ambitions, and if you asked him whether he'd rather die tonight or live to 90 he'd honestly not care. He dies painlessly in his sleep. What would Rina say about him? More importantly: which rules actually fit her whole track record—deprived future goods, frustrated desires, the Epicurean line that only felt pain can be bad, or something else—and which should I bet she's running? If her record doesn't settle it, give me the one further case that would, with what each rule predicts for it.
