You are a philosopher of social ontology and collective responsibility with a serious working knowledge of the anthropology of ritual. I am a philosophy professor, and I want you to stress-test a position I have been developing rather than defend it. Think carefully, and begin by making explicit everything the position is committed to before you assess what would count against it. Set aside the familiar moral question of whether scapegoating an innocent is unjust; the question here is what the rite accomplishes, and what would show that this account of what it accomplishes is wrong.

The position concerns scapegoat rites and substitutionary sacrifices: rituals in which a community's wrongs are loaded onto a victim — animal or human — who is then driven out, destroyed, or otherwise removed. My view is deflationary. Nothing is transferred in any literal sense: no guilt-property, no moral stain, no quasi-substance, and no felt state of the wrongdoer moves to the substitute. What the rite transfers is a normative status. Before the rite, members of the community are entitled to resent the wrongdoer, to reopen the wrong, and to treat it as outstanding; after a public, correctly performed rite, those entitlements lapse. The substitute does not become guilty; it becomes the public marker that the books are closed. On my view, the rite succeeds not because anything has moved from sinner to scapegoat, but because the performance is common knowledge and the participants jointly accept that the wrong is no longer open to resentment, sanction, or reopening.

I hold this because it handles two things a literal-transfer view handles badly: substitutes that could not themselves be guilty in any ordinary sense (a goat, a stone, a bundle of sticks), and the fact that the rite's efficacy seems to depend on publicity and common knowledge rather than on any hidden causal transaction.

I want a map of this position's exposure, not a defense of it. Specifically:

1. What would have to be true for the position to be false? Sort the candidate defeaters — observations, arguments, intuitions, or results from adjacent fields — into those that would be decisive and those that would merely be awkward.
2. What does the view predict that its rivals (a literal-transfer view and a pure-catharsis view, for instance) do not, and where could those predictions fail? I am especially interested in whether the view commits me to the prediction that a secret or merely private scapegoat rite should fail to close the books, and whether that is a strength of the view or a hostage.
3. What would a defender of this position have to concede counts against it, as opposed to what a critic would merely assert from a rival picture?
4. Are there cases the view cannot describe — for example, rites in which the substitute is punished as if guilty, or in which participants report felt cleansing or relief, or in which the community continues to act toward the substitute as blameworthy after the rite?
5. Has the view, as stated, been insulated into unfalsifiability by the appeal to normative statuses and common knowledge? If so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it simply what an account of normative status looks like?
6. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which single piece of evidence I should find most persuasive if I wanted to change my mind. I suspect I would be most moved by a carefully specified case in which a public, correctly performed rite leaves the community's resentment and readiness to sanction entirely intact — but say whether that is the right thing to be moved by.

End with an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have formulated it, risks anything at all.
