I keep going back and forth on a view about community that I'm more than half convinced by, but which runs straight into an intuition I can't drop. I want you to help me negotiate between them, not just pick a winner.

The view — call it the constitutive view — is that communities aren't only instruments for delivering welfare to individuals; they're partly constitutive of the goods members can value and of the selves who value them. Some goods are internal to a shared practice: the solidarity of a union local, the collective memory of a congregation, the craft standards of a workshop. They can't simply be bought back after the practice dissolves. This view explains things preference-talk waves away: people saying "life got objectively easier after we disbanded, yet something irreplaceable is gone"; the fact that "we'll keep in touch" never preserves what mattered; why the thinning of civic associations feels like a loss even when everyone's life-satisfaction went up. It unifies friendships, families, churches, unions, neighborhoods under one idea: shared practical identities are a locus of value, not just a means.

But here's the intuition I can't shake. Take a tight-knit community that dissolves — a mutual-aid society that was also overbearing, in everyone's business, a little stifling. Forty years later you check, and every former member is, by their own lights, better off: freer, richer, or no worse by their own stated preferences. Not one reports the dissolution as a loss, or regrets loosing it. If I'm honest, I want to say: then nothing of value was lost. A "loss" with no bearer — no one who misses it, no disappointed preference — looks like an unowned harm, a philosophical ghost. And the constitutive view starts to look paternalistic, telling people they've lost something they cannot feel and are worse off without it though they don't think so.

I can see the standard moves. Debunk the intuition: the dissolution changed what members can value, so "no one misses it" is exactly what the theory predicts — the standards by which the loss would be felt dissolved with the practice, so the absence of felt loss isn't evidence of no loss. Amend the theory: count only losses anchored in someone's actual or counterfactual experience — option value, lost possible identities, that sort of thing. Or find a compatibility reading: the constitutive view says something about value, my intuition says something about authority — who gets to tell people there life went worse.

Give me the strongest version of each, then walk me through what each move would actually cost. What would have to be true about adaptive preferences for discounting the intuition to be justified? What exactly does the theory give up in the amended version? Is the value/authority reading stable or just relabeling? And if you think one side should yield, tell me what evidence about the dissolving-community case would make you switch.
