I am a novelist. For the past year I have been drafting a novel in which a minor character writes pastiches of Victorian detective fiction, and the work has kept returning me to a question I used to think was only a logician's diversion: whether the name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to anything at all. At a writers' residency last spring, two other novelists argued this question late into the night, and I transcribed their exchange as accurately as memory permits. I have deliberately refrained from taking a side; I want a careful, move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on which of them I happen to like.

(1) The first novelist held that the name is empty. There is no person at 221B Baker Street to answer to it; nothing in the world satisfies the description of the detective who lives there. So "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" is not literally true. It is true only inside the fiction, and treating it as plain truth lets the stories bleed into reality.

(2) The second replied that this cannot account for the sentences we actually entrust with truth. "Sherlock Holmes is among the most famous characters in English fiction" and "Holmes is more intelligent than Watson" are asserted flat out, without the prefix "in the stories," and we expect them to be true. They are not about a flesh-and-blood man; they are about the character. A name occupying subject position in a true sentence must stand for something, and the only available something is the character.

(3) The first answered that these are compressed forms of speech. "Holmes is more intelligent than Watson" expands to "in Conan Doyle's stories, Holmes is more intelligent than Watson." "Holmes is among the most famous characters in English fiction" expands to a claim about the stories and their readers. Compression does not require a referent for the compressed sentence.

(4) The second answered that compression breaks down on mixed statements. Consider a sentence that can be spoken in a single breath: "In the stories Holmes is more intelligent than Watson, though in reality Holmes is only a character from those stories." The name occurs twice, once governed by "in the stories" and once not. If the name never refers, the two occurrences cannot both be doing the same work, so the view must assign the same name two different semantic mechanisms within one sentence. The line between those mechanisms then does all the philosophical labor, and it looks suspiciously like the very distinction the first novelist is trying to avoid.

(5) The first said the two-jobs objection is not fatal. The second occurrence is analyzable as: Doyle's stories contain a character named Holmes with such-and-such features. On that analysis the name contributes to a description of what the stories say; it is not a name for an object over and above the stories.

(6) The second shifted to comparative statements: "Holmes is more famous than any actual private detective of his period." For this to be evaluated at all, the name has to supply something capable of standing in a comparison with real people. If the reply is "shorthand again," then nearly every serious thing we say about a fictional character turns out to be shorthand, and "shorthand" begins to look like the name for an analysis one does not yet have.

(7) The first allowed that ordinary speech is often loose, but held that loose speech does not create referents. A literal semantics may treat the unexpanded sentence as false or elliptical, while a pragmatic story explains why speakers happily utter it. The existence of hard cases does not by itself refute the claim that the name is empty.

(8) The second then pressed the point from creation. If the name is empty, then "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is true, and the second granted this in the flesh-and-blood sense. But "Conan Doyle created Sherlock Holmes" is also true, and one cannot create nothing. What Doyle created was a character, a public artifact that later conversation can be about. So there is a literal sense in which Holmes exists as an abstract object and the name refers to that object. The burden is on the first novelist to explain why this is not a sense at all, without dismissing everyday talk about characters as a mistake.

(9) The first closed by saying that "Doyle created Holmes" is a way of saying that Doyle originated the Holmes fiction, and that "Holmes does not exist" is exactly what the emptiness view predicts. The second's creation argument, she said, assumes the very thing at issue: that to think and speak about a character we must stand in some relation to an object, when the whole content of such thinking is exhausted by what the stories encode.

That is where they left it. I want the adjudication in two parts. First, go through the exchange move by move: for each numbered claim, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify each rating; identify which moves do the argument's real work and which merely restate an initial commitment. Second, I have not been able to resolve two questions for my own drafting, and I want to know which side, if either, handles them better. (i) In my manuscript a narrator says of Holmes, "He took on cases that never came to trial," a sentence I know Doyle never wrote. In my notes I have been writing that the sentence is "true in my novel but not in Doyle." Does either position make sense of that note, or should the note itself be given up? (ii) If I revise the character into an unmistakable pastiche and a reviewer later says, "This is not Holmes, but it is plainly modeled on him," what does the truth of that sentence require Holmes to be? I suspect there are at least two defensible readings of each puzzle; tell me which is the best reading on each side, and whether either puzzle should move me off the fence.
