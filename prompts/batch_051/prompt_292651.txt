I'm trying to find the established term, if there is one, for a specific pattern I keep noticing around slurs. It's not just that people avoid saying slurs; it's that the avoidance seems to be inherited by things that aren't sayings of the slur. A philosopher gave a talk on derogatory language and put a well-known slur for Black people on a slide, in quotation marks, clearly as data. Half the room treated the talk as if he had done something wrong, and his defense was "I mentioned it, I didn't use it." A bunch of people answered that mentioning it is a kind of using it. But those same people had no problem with "a racial slur for Black people"—same object, roughly, described instead of quoted—appearing in his abstract.

The same inheritance seems to run downstream through the word's substitutes. At some point "the n-word" felt like the safe option, and now I see people write "n-word" with obvious discomfort and others writing "the word I won't write." The discomfort tracks the letter, the look, the trace of the original form, not the meaning: a sentence that clearly communicates "the slur for Black people" without any form-descendant of the slur is fine.

So there's a way for offensive force to travel along a token's form-lineage—citation, quotation, euphemism, abbreviation—while skipping coextensive paraphrase. Is there a name for this as a single phenomenon, or is it better split into two or three? I know use/mention is nearby, and so is the euphemism treadmill; I'm not asking about those. If there's no established name, how would you define and bound it—where exactly does the inheritance stop?
