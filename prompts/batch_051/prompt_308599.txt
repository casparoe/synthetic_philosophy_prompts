I'm a PhD student working on inverse reinforcement learning, and I also read some Quine a while ago, and I can't shake the feeling these two are the same problem in different clothes. Here's the IRL side: we watch a person act (near-)optimally in some MDP and try to recover the reward function that rationalizes their choices, i.e. what they actually value. The standard underdetermination result is that behaviour won't pin down a unique reward even in the limit — a whole class of rewards makes the observed policy optimal. Potential-based shaping is the tidy case: it shifts value around without changing what's optimal, but with finite data the ambiguity is worse than just that. So the real work of IRL is bolting on enough structure — linear-in-features, max entropy, demos across multiple environments — to pick out a useful representative from the equivalence class. Quine's side: the radical translator has access to every possible dispositional fact about a speaker's verbal behaviour, yet rival translation manuals remain — 'gavagai' could carve the world into rabbits or rabbit-slices or whatever — and no possible evidence settles which one is right. He calls translation indeterminate, and says that's stronger than the ordinary underdetermination of theory by evidence. Both cases seem to have the same shape: observable behaviour fixes a behavioural equivalence class but leaves the hidden content (reward / meaning) unfixed. So is this a real structural identity, or am I just pattern-matching on the word 'infer'? The obvious disanalogy: Quine's translator isn't assuming the speaker is an optimal anything, whereas IRL bakes in expected-utility maximization — so maybe the IRL case is closer to Davidson's charity than Quine's behaviourism. But here's what keeps me from filing the analogy away: in IRL the residual ambiguity isn't inert, because two rewards that both make the demos optimal in the training MDP can come apart as soon as you change the dynamics or ask counterfactual questions — which is exactly why we wanted a reward and not just the policy. That makes it look like the hidden object has real downstream effects, so maybe Quine's antirealism about meaning shouldn't transfer to reward. But I'm not sure: one could still say asking which reward is the 'real' one is a psuedo-question and we should just commit to the policy, differences in new environments be damned. Can anything transfer between the two debates in either direction? For instance, does the IRL trick of constraining a latent object by demanding it generalize across environments give a reply to Quine, or does Quine's argument show that 'true reward' is a ghost and IRL is just finding nice representatives of a class? In other words, is this parallel deep or superficial, and where exactly does it break if it does?
