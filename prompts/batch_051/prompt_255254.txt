They've seen the statement once and one application (the proof that every vector space has a basis), and from the blank faces I'm pretty sure they can reproduce the proof without knowing why the lemma is allowed to do what it does, or what the statement is actually saying. I don't want to just re-explain it; I want a plan for the session.

My guess is the real bottleneck is that students keep reading "maximal" as "biggest", because that's what it means in ordinary English, and then the whole lemma sounds like it's promising a top element. I'd like one concrete example that carries the whole thing: I can't decide whether to reuse the vector-space basis proof but run it slowly, or start with something smaller where the poset is finite or countable and the chains are easier to draw. What should that example have and what should it avoid? I assume it should not be a total order, since then Zorn's lemma is close to trivial and the role of chains gets hidden, but maybe I'm wrong. Then I want the misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to head each one off — I'd flag these for you to correct or reorder: "an upper bound of a chain has to be in the chain", "you only need to check the finite chains, since the infinite ones can't be pictured anyway", "maximal means greatest", and "if Zorn's lemma gives you a maximal element, it also gives you a recipe for constructing one". Also, should I even raise the axiom-of-choice business and the non-constructiveness, or should I explicitly tell them I'm leaving it out, and if I mention it, where does it go? Finally, I need one small question or exercise for the end that would tell me they actually understand the lemma and arent just parroting the basis proof.
