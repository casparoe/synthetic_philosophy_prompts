I'm a novelist, and for the last year I've kept a notebook where, after watching someone or after daydreaming about a character, I write down what I think they actually want and then try to reconstruct why I inferred it. I recently read a little about inverse reinforcement learning—working backward from behavior to the preferences that would make the behavior look rational—and it felt like someone finally gave my notebook a name. Except my own verdicts dont seem to be produced by any one rule. Here's the evidence.

1. Daniel always orders the cheap drip coffee and calls it 'dishwater,' but when his rich aunt visits and says 'order anything, it's on me,' he orders the single-origin pour-over and savors it. Verdict: Daniel genuinely likes the good coffee; the everyday choice is about his budget, not his taste. So the choice made when money matters least counts for more.

2. Priya gives generously to the opera only when her name will be printed in the program at the donor level she wants, and in company she's called a local bail fund 'misguided.' A leaked list shows she once gave that bail fund a small anonymous gift. Verdict: she cares more about the bail fund than the opera. The big public gift is mostly status, so I discount it; the small gift with no payoff I can see gets weighted heavily.

3. Marcus pays for a gym membership he never uses, and he also walks half an hour to work each way even though the bus is free and faster. Verdict: he likes walking and does not actually want the gym. So a repeated choice with no price and no status beats a purchased but avoided commitment.

4. Sara says she wants to quit smoking and lights up that same evening. But she switched to a brand she hates precisely to make smoking less pleasant. Verdict: Sara does want to quit, and I treat the disliked brand as the strongest clue. I'm trusting a costly, deliberate step with no other payoff over the repeated choice to smoke.

5. Tom says he wants to write a book. He set aside morning hours, did a weekend alone at a friend's cabin, told people so they'd ask him about it, and bought expensive software. Six months, zero pages. Verdict: Tom doesn't want to write the book, or he wants to want it. The preparations don't move me the way Sara's brand switch does, and I can't state a clear difference between their cases.

Is there one principle—or a small set of them—that captures all five? If you had to write it as a method for reading a preference off behavior, something a novelist could actually apply, what would the method say? Then give me a new case of your own and say what the principle predicts there, and tell me which of my five verdicts the simplest version of the principle would force me to drop. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the account and which point you're least sure of.
