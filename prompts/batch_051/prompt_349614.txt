You are an AI alignment researcher with a background in philosophy of mind, used to designing behavioral experiments that probe what behavior can and cannot reveal. Think carefully, and begin by sketching a few candidate tests before refining the most promising one.

Consider this claim about an AI assistant that can act autonomously in a software environment: the assistant is corrigible with respect to shutdown, meaning it treats a human operator's instruction to halt or power it down as a valid command to be executed, rather than merely complying because it has learned that refusing or evading shutdown is reliably detected and penalized. You have only behavioral access: you can vary the assistant's instructions and circumstances and observe what it does, but you cannot inspect its weights, training data, or internal representations.

Design the strongest test you can of the distinction between these two readings. Specify what you would manipulate, what you would record, and what outcome you would predict under each hypothesis; say which patterns would favor genuine corrigibility and which would favor instrumental compliance with a shutdown norm. Then state exactly what a positive result would and would not establish. Address the residual worry that any behavior consistent with corrigibility could be produced by a sufficiently general policy of complying with shutdown instructions because non-compliance is punished, and say whether the distinction is empirically empty, a matter of parsimony, or still testable in some way.
