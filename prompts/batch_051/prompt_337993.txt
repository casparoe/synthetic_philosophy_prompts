I'm running a small reading group on Paradise Lost this term, and last week we got stuck on something I think is more a philosophy problem than a lit-crit one. The poem wants Satan to have been created good and free, and it also wants his fall to be entirely his own doing, not something God set up or allowed in a way that makes God responsible. But if he is genuinely good before the fall, and there is nothing outside him that tempts him, how does he make the first evil choice? Either the first sin comes out of nowhere (in which case it looks arbitrary and inexplicable), or it comes from pride or envy that was already there (in which case the fall has already happened and we have just moved the problem back a step). A friend in a philosophy of religion class tells me this is basically the free-will-defense problem applied to angels — the puzzle of how evil can enter a good creation through free creatures — and apparently its actualy quite hard to make the first sin self-caused yet intelligible. But Milton seems to be thinking about it directly in the Abdiel episode, in Satan's Book IV soliloquy, and in the war in heaven; I just cant tell whether those scenes solve anything or are dramatic staging of an unresolved tension.

I'd like a reading that treats the poem and the puzzle equally seriously: use the philosophical problem to press on what Milton is doing, but let the poem push back rather than just using it as an illustration. Does Paradise Lost actually give us a coherent model of a first sin that is self-caused rather than arbitrary, or does it dramatize something the philosophical formulations keep missing — maybe about self-deception, agency, or the way a free agent talks itself into evil? Dont worry about consulting critics; work from the scenes and the problem. And at the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of your reading as the last line, starting with "Takeaway:".
