I co-own a small tutoring company — 25ish part-time tutors, mostly students — and the whole operation runs on tiny promises: I'll take the Henderson kid Thursday at 4, I'll send the notes by Friday, I'll be there ten minutes early to set up. We're getting killed by small breaches. Tutors who are always six minutes late, someone cancels a session at 10pm the night before, a tutor swears they'll cover a shift and then forgets. Parents notice. But I also don't want to be a tyrant about stuff thats basically noise.

I want a reusable decision procedure my scheduling lead (a 22-year-old, no philosophy background) can run in about 15 minutes, maybe twice a month when something bubbles up, to decide how to handle a pattern of small broken commitments. It should sort out: when a broken time-promise is really a broken promise and not just ordinary friction, when a bunch of individually trivial latenesses add up to unreliability, when an expectation we created without actually promising (a schedule posted three weeks out) should be treated like a promise anyway, when to let something go, when to give a warning vs treat it as a pattern and escalate, and when the person complaining has no standing because they do the same thing themselves. I've read enough to know there are two big ways philosophers think about promises — that a promise transfers a right to the other person, or that it's about getting someone to reasonably rely on you — and I want the procedure to be built on the better of those, or at least the best version of that distinction, but in plain operational terms, not vibes.

Format it as: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "let it go," where it says "warning," where it says "escalate," and where it has to hand off to a human because it's a values call rather than a facts call. Flag the cases the procedure can't settle. Then run it on three worked examples: (1) a tutor who is 5–7 minutes late to about half their sessions but always teaches the full time; (2) a tutor who cancels two hours before a session because they double-booked, and immediately offers a makeup; (3) a tutor who agreed three weeks ago to cover a colleague's Saturday shift and now, the day before, says they forgot and made other plans. One of these should come out in a way I won't like — tell me which and why. Escalations have to be signed off by me, and I need the lead to be able to defend the call to an angry 20-year-old, so keep the reasoning transparent.
