In my former life as a research chemist, I served on a human-subjects committee, and I still remember how scrupulous we were about risk. A study that required nothing more than one additional blood sample demanded individual informed consent for a theoretical one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of nerve injury; a participant could refuse, and without that consent the study could not go forward. Since retiring I have begun reading moral philosophy, and I have become puzzled by the fact that the same society that insists on this is content to impose risks of a similar order on people who are never asked. A utility may receive a permit to release a substance that raises the calculated lifetime cancer risk of everyone within a certain radius by several parts per million, or a highway authority may widen a road and accept a small increase in the annual mortality risk of nearby residents; the agency typically justifies the project by comparing the expected benefits with a monetary value assigned to a statistical life, and no individual consent is sought from those exposed.

If one of those residents later develops the illness, the tort system may compensate her for the realized harm; if she remains healthy, it has no category for the unconsented-to risk to which she was exposed. I would like you to reconstruct the philosophical conception of risk, consent, and harm that this three-part practice presupposes: informed consent for individually imposed risks, aggregate cost-benefit analysis for widely distributed risks, and compensation only after a harm has materialized. Are these three practices animated by one coherent underlying theory, or do they presuppose incompatible commitments? And what would have to change in the operation of these practices if we adopted, in turn, a purely expected-welfare view, on which only the probability-weighted value of outcomes matters, or a rights-based view, on which exposing someone to a nontrivial risk of serious harm without consent is itself a wrong?
