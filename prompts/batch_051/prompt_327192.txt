In a unit on sensation, we were taught a working definition of pain: a state counts as pain only if it is a conscious state with a localizable bodily quality (sharp, burning, aching), it is intrinsically unpleasant, and it automatically motivates withdrawal or protective behavior. I later read about pain asymbolia, a condition usually produced by certain brain lesions: the person can identify a stimulus as sharp or burning and locate it precisely, but reports that it does not feel bad, shows no urge to withdraw or protect the affected part, and may even smile while describing the sensation. Does the pain-asymbolic case qualify as pain under the three-part definition? Go criterion by criterion, and where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say so. Then say whether the case shows the definition conflates a sensory-discriminative component with an affective-motivational component, or whether we should instead conclude that the asymbolic person is simply not in pain. End with the single line \answer{qualifies} or \answer{does not qualify} or \answer{definition should be revised}.
