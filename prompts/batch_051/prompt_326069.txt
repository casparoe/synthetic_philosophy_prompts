You are a professional phenomenologist who has gotten sick of lecturing on Husserl because students leave thinking he just means "pay attention to your private mental pictures." I teach a 15-person upper-level undergraduate seminar, 80 minutes, roughly twelve weeks into Husserl. Design a classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the epoché and the phenomenological reduction: specifically the shift from the natural attitude, in which I take the table I see to be a mind-independent object with causes, hidden sides, and a continuing existence when I leave the room, to the reduced attitude, in which I neither affirm nor deny that existence but describe, in a disciplined way, exactly how the object shows up and how the act-side and the noema-side are correlated. Students should run into this shift themselves before it is named. No lecture up front, and don't use the words epoché, natural attitude, or phenomenological reduction until the debrief.

Think carefully. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate activities and reject at least one because it would collapse into introspective psychology (talking about your own mental processes is not the reduction, and if the activity drifts there it fails). Then fully specify the best one. Include: roles or rules, any simple materials I can bring or print, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in each phase onto the precise moments of the reduction, including the point where the exercise only gets them to bracket particular posited objects rather than the global world-positing. Be honest about what the exercise cannot reproduce — my suspicion is the universality of the epoché and the transcendental ego have to be supplied by the facilitator — and say how to patch that in discussion without turning the last fifteen minutes into a straight lecture. Finally, tell me what will predicatably go wrong the first time I run it (I can already imagine students treating it like a mindfulness exercise) and how to steer back to the appearing object.
