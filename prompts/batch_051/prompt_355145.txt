I've been working through the coherence of the two-natures doctrine of the Incarnation, and I keep arriving at an argument whose conclusion I am not entirely willing to accept. I'd like you to tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails; if it is valid, say which premise a defender of the doctrine should reject and why the remaining premises survive.

By a nature I mean, roughly, what a thing is: the set of properties that make something the kind of thing it is and that it has essentially. Now consider this argument. (1) For a person to have a nature is for that person to have the properties that belong to that nature essentially. (2) The divine nature includes essential omniscience. (3) The human nature includes essential non-omniscience: limited or finite knowledge belongs to what it is to be human, rather than being a mere accident of particular human beings. (4) If one person, Christ, has both the divine nature and the human nature, then one and the same person has both essential omniscience and essential non-omniscience. (5) Nothing can be both omniscient and non-omniscient at the same time and in the same respect. (6) Therefore it is impossible that Christ is one person with both a divine and a human nature.

The doctrine this argument targets holds that Christ is one person — one underlying subject — in two natures, with the natures neither merged into a third kind of thing nor distributed across two separate subjects. I can see a defender blocking premise (1), premise (3), or step (5)'s clause about the same time and same respect, but each of those moves seems to me to weaken the claim that both natures are genuinely present in a single subject, and I worry the doctrine ends up saved only by being made too thin to matter. Am I right that the argument is valid? If it is not, name the step that fails and give the precise reading of the terms under which it fails. If it is valid, say which premise the best version of the doctrine must give up, and assess whether the doctrine remains recognizably the two-natures doctrine after that concession.
