I have been trying to get the right account of the moral equality of combatants, and I keep reaching the same impasse. The doctrine's appeal is structural. If combatants on opposite sides of a war are not moral equals, then the permissions of ordinary soldiers depend on whether their state's war is just — and that seems to require an eighteen-year-old conscript or volunteer to decide the justice of the war before she may permissibly engage anyone at all, under propaganda, partial information, and coercive pressure. That is not a workable form of moral life, and it seems to punish individuals for their state's wrongdoing. The doctrine replaces that demand with something far more tractable: soldiers' conduct is governed by the rules of engagement, and soldiers on each side face the same rules whatever the merits of the cause. It also secures an egalitarian thought I do not want to give up — that a person's basic moral standing does not simply vary with the government she lives under.

But the intuition it collides with is firm rather than a passing sentiment. A soldier serving an aggressive invader and a soldier defending against that invasion do not strike me as morally on a par. When the invader kills an opposing soldier in a war of conquest, I want to say that killing is wrong in a way the defender's is not — even if both followed the rules of engagement, and even if the asymmetry is not a ground for legal punishment. The symmetry claim can come to feel less like equality than like laundering an unjust cause through a code of conduct.

I am not looking for an answer that simply picks one side. I want the strongest possible statement of each, and then a negotiated verdict on which should yield. What would justify downgrading the intuition: what exactly would I need to accept about responsibility, collective action, or the soldier's epistemic situation to conclude that the apparent moral difference between invader and defender is an artefact rather than a moral fact? What would a revision of the doctrine look like if the intuition is the thing to keep — one that preserves the institutional benefit of not demanding that every soldier be a private just-war theorist, without asserting that the two combatants are moral equals? And is there a reading on which the dispute is partly verbal — for instance, if the doctrine is interpreted as a claim about the conventional role of combatants rather than about the full moral relation between the two individuals — or would that merely evade the issue? Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and which argument or consideration would most likely lead you to a different verdict.
