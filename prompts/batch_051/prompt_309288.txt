In the opening section of *Ecce Homo* titled *Why I Am So Clever*, Friedrich Nietzsche makes a claim about what philosophy has treated as important. His claim, as I understand it, is that the so-called small things — nutrition, climate, place, recreation, what he calls the casuistry of selfishness — are more consequential for a thinker than everything philosophers before him had taken to be the great subjects. I want to defend a substantive reading of this passage, not a merely biographical or rhetorical one. On my reading, Nietzsche is asserting that a thinker's bodily regime is partly constitutive of the conditions under which their judgments become trustworthy or corrupt, and therefore that diet and health are philosophical topics of the first order rather than private trivia. What would count against this position? I want the potential defeaters sorted by how decisive they are: empirical failures of his specific dietary or climatic claims, evidence that the physiological facts he invokes are wrong or irrelevant to the reliability of cognition, the objection that the substantive reading collapses into a banal claim no one would deny, and the objection that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Please say which of these escape routes are live rather than merely logical, which would merely be awkward rather than decisive, and whether it is a strength or a defect if the core thesis survives even when many of its empirical parts turn out false.
