Suppose a country amends its constitution like this. During a declared emergency the executive can issue lockdowns, border closures, travel bans, etc. without prior judicial review; courts can only hear challenges after the emergency is over. So far so boring. The twist: every emergency measure expires automatically after 60 days unless a 500-person panel of ordinary citizens, picked by lot after the fact, votes by two-thirds to ratify it. The panel gets evidence from the government and from independent reviewers, it's deliberations are public, and its vote is final. If a measure is ratified, the courts lose the power to review its proportionality (they can review only whether the panel was properly selected) and the officials who signed it get immunity. If a measure is not ratified, those officials become personally liable for the damage the measure caused. Analyze how this thing would actually behave rather than whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivize among executives — e.g., timing the nastiest measures early, or shaping what the panel sees? What does it do to the sortition panelists, who are being selected during or just after a frightening emergency and know their own neighbors might be watching? How would people game it — diffuse harms, vivid threats, stacked evidence records, gaming the selection? And which of these failure modes are fixable without gutting the core point, which is to make the line between legality and legitimacy actually bite?
