A piece I read recently summarized the cause-prioritization framework used in effective altruism like this: causes are ranked by multiplying three factors — importance, tractability, and neglectedness. Importance is how many people a problem affects; tractability is how easy it is to solve; neglectedness is how few people are already working on it. The best causes are therefore big, easy, and ignored. The piece said this is why global health interventions come out ahead of things like arts funding: saving lives allegedly scores higher on all three. It then reported critics as saying the framework requires putting every kind of value on a single quantitative scale, and that because it ranks causes, it implies you should abandon any cause that doesn't finish on top. I'm inclined to think the multiplication step is being oversold, but I can't pin down where the account goes wrong. Is this a fair summary? Please separate what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what a careful defender of the framework would reject, and explain whether the reported criticisms survive those corrections.
