I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working on the aggregation of harms, and I need a piece of satire for a reading-group handout. Write a mock public-service FAQ, roughly 650–750 words, in the deadpan, euphemistic voice of a municipal government website, for a fictional Office of Aggregate Harm. The office has implemented a "Dust-Speck Redemption Scheme": each resident is assigned, at randomized intervals, a nearly imperceptible harm—a dust speck in the eye, a two-second pause in a crosswalk, a single cold drop down the back of the neck—and these harms are accumulated and exchanged, via a published conversion rate, for the prevention of one very great harm (a single unidentified person spared fifty years of excruciating pain). The FAQ takes it as settled that because each speck is a real harm, sufficiently many specks strictly outweigh the torture; the comedy should come from working out that logic seriously rather than mocking bureaucracy as such. The real features to exaggerate include the exchange rate and its units, rounding and the difference between an imperceptible harm and no harm, the fact that nobody ever experiences the sum of the specks, intransitive cycles that appear once thresholds are introduced, and the difficulty of verifying that an assigned speck was actually administered. The standard objections—separateness of persons, the ledger treating harms as fungible across lives, worries about incommensurability—should never be named but should remain visible in the deadpan details. Please include at least the following: an "explanation of conversion units" passage with a deliberately ill-fitting formula (prose is fine); a question from a resident who cannot tell whether they received their allocated speck, answered by appeal to the expected value of an unnoticed speck; and a closing "upcoming programs" note that pushes the logic one further step to a face-value conclusion a thoughtful reader recoils from. A specialist in the dust-specks-versus-torture debate should finish convinced that the satire is fair to the strongest version of the aggregationist position, not a caricature of it.
