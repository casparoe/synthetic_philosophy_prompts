Something keeps coming up in my philosophy of language reading group, and I can't tell wether the person leading it has an actual argument or is just leaning on a picture. He keeps saying a proper name is nothing but a tag or label stuck to an individual — like a serial number or one of those little stickers on produce. No descriptive content, no meaning beyond pointing. And from that he draws a pretty strong conclusion: the difference between a given name and a family name is just social noise. Surname order, patronymics, matronymics, changing your name at marriage, whether your 'family name' comes first or last or isn't a family name at all — all merely convention layered on top of what is semantically the same kind of thing. His line is that asking what a name means, or whether a name fits someone, or whether a family name carries obligations, is like asking what a barcode means.

But the tag picture seems to quietly import features of real tags — arbitrary, interchangeable, attached after the fact, assigned one per object — that ordinary naming practices don't obviously have, especially once family names enter. So I want an audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the tag, the object, the act of tagging, and maybe the registry that keeps it all straight. Then the places where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the conclusion about given versus family names. The main question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the claim that the given/family distinction is semantically arbitrary survive, or was the tag analogy carrying the whole thing? And if it doesn't hold, what's a better analogy — or pair of analogies — for what a given name does versus what a family name does?
