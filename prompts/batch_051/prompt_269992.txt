I'm preparing for a comprehensive exam on the afterlife of Aristotle's four causes, and I keep stalling at the same point. Once the seventeenth century banished final causes from natural philosophy, teleology didn't vanish—it re-entered under other names: function, purpose, goal-directedness, proper function, teleonomy. The modern debate offers selected-effects (etiological) accounts, causal-role accounts, organizational accounts, and dispositional accounts of goal-directedness, and nearly every paper tests these against one or two pet cases—the heart, the mousetrap, the panda's thumb, the first token of an adaptation. Before I read any more proposals, I want the full specification of what a theory of teleological explanation must deliver to count as adequate, independent of what any particular camp already happens to deliver. That means: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results it cannot contradict, with the consideration behind each.

To be concrete, the list should at least take a position on: the heart's pumping blood versus its making thumping sounds versus a nose's holding up eyeglasses; a malformed heart that happens to pump; the first occurrence of a brand-new adaptation, which has no history of having been selected; vestigial traits; exaptations and spandrels; artifacts and broken artifacts; and the difference between merely doing something and having that as a proper function. It should also say which distinctions are load-bearing—function versus accidental effect, causal-role function versus normative proper function, teleology versus teleonomy versus merely end-directed physical processes, backward-looking versus forward-looking explanation—and which results from evolutionary theory any account must live with, such as the fact that natural selection has no foresight even though it licenses 'what for' explanations. For each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Then tell me which pair or subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied by a single theory, and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. I care far more about the criteria and their motivation than about a scorecard, but a brief one at the end is fine.
