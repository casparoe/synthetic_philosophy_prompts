I am a retired experimental physicist, and after four decades of fitting models to noisy data I have recently begun reading the philosophy of quantum mechanics, especially the Everettian literature. The problem that most holds my attention is the Born-rule problem: why a rational observer in a branching universe should assign probabilities to branches at all, and why the squared amplitude should be the number assigned. The physics is clear, but the arguments strike me as halfway toward decision theory, using notions I cannot quite map onto the machinery I know — self-locating uncertainty, branch weights, rational constraints on preferences over branching futures, and the suggestion that all probability talk here is merely instrumental bookkeeping. I spent my career thinking fluently in Bayesian inference and statistical decision theory, and I still work naturally in terms of priors, likelihoods, posteriors, sample spaces, loss functions, decision rules, and scoring rules.

Please take the main families of positions on the Born rule in the many-worlds interpretation and restate each, as faithfully as you can, in that Bayesian and decision-theoretic vocabulary. The families I have in mind are roughly these: the view that probability is at best a useful instrument because every outcome occurs; the view that the uncertainty is self-locating, about which branch a post-measurement observer will occupy; the view that consistency constraints on how rational agents may care about their successor branches force the squared amplitude to serve as the probability; and the view that the Born rule should simply be postulated as an additional law governing branch weights. For each, identify what plays the role of the sample space, the prior, the likelihood, the loss function, and the decision rule — where those roles exist at all.

Then give me a residue report, because I expect the translation to fail in instructive places. Which claims transfer cleanly? Which can be made to fit only by stretching my vocabulary, for example by treating a branch weight as a prior even though nothing is unknown, or by invoking a fictitious sampling process? Which parts of the debate have no expression at all in the statistical vocabulary? And finally, what should I conclude from the residue: are the inexpressible parts pointing to something my statistician's toolkit is genuinely missing, or are they places where the philosophical position is less precise than it looks? I am not asking for a survey with references; I want the structural translation and an honest accounting, even where the honesty cuts against my own vocabulary.
