I am preparing a teaching case on formal models of agreement, and I would like you to construct a thought experiment to my specifications. The target is the result usually called Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents begin with the same prior probability distribution, and the posterior probability each assigns to a given event is common knowledge between them, then those two posterior probabilities must be equal. I want a believable, everyday scenario in which two people seem to violate this result. Viewed from outside, they do start from the same prior, each reasons impeccably, and the numbers they state to one another are in fact their genuine posterior probabilities; yet, at the end of the conversation, they rationally remain apart. The case should not work through any of the familiar loopholes: neither party is lying, irrational, computationally constrained, or tacitly using a different event or a different prior from the outset. Instead, the scenario should be engineered so that the two announcements fail to become common knowledge of those posteriors, even though the figures are spoken plainly, because of the social texture of the exchange — for example, each treats the other's stated figure as partly a negotiating position, a face-saving gesture, a bid for authority, or a number offered under conversational pressure. Use an ordinary setting such as a workplace decision, a family dispute, or a medical or financial choice; no exotic physics, truth serums, or lie detection. The point of the case is to show how much work the common-knowledge condition does, so the failure should feel natural rather than contrived.

After the scenario, provide the verification step by step. State the proposition about which the parties disagree and the common prior from which both begin. Describe the private information each acquires, and reconstruct enough of each person's updating to show that the announced numbers follow rationally and that the residual disagreement is stable under their beliefs about the other's announcement, including the doubt they attach to it. Identify precisely which condition of Aumann's theorem is not met, and trace the common-knowledge failure through the conversation. Then explain which single feature of the case is doing the work: if that feature were removed — for instance, if the two wrote their true credences on cards and the sincerity of those cards were itself common knowledge — how and why the theorem would force convergence. Finally, add a short note on how a defender of Aumann's theorem would attack the example, and say whether the attack succeeds. A compact numerical illustration is welcome, but the scenario itself should remain ordinary. Please write the entire answer in British English.
