For the past term I have been teaching a seminar on pain and affect, and I keep reaching for a distinction I would like checked rather than indulged. I tell my students that we should separate a pain's hurting from its mattering. Hurting is the sensory side: that it is sharp or burning, that it is in the left hand or behind the eyes, that it is moderate or agonizing. Mattering is the motivational-aversive side: the way a pain enters one's practical life as something to be ended, avoided, or shrunk from; the way it presents the bodily state as bad, prompts withdrawal, grimacing, or a search for an analgesic, and conscripts thought and action. Pain asymbolia has become my classroom exhibit: there are patients who, when pricked or pressed, can describe the sensation quite normally — sharp, localized, of a specified intensity, in the hand — but appear not to mind it. They do not withdraw; some reportedly smile at the stimulus. The hurting seems to remain while the mattering drops out. From this I have built a small theory: the badness of pain lies entirely in mattering, never in hurting. Hurting is evaluatively inert — a sensory quality, no more intrinsically bad in itself than a color. Pain is bad only because, and only insofar as, the nociceptive system ordinarily welds an aversive response onto the signal; in asymbolia the signal arrives without the weld. I realize this is close to the thought that pain is more like a command or an evaluative appraisal than a raw sensation, and I suspect I am reinventing something under a clumsier name.

I would value a committed assessment rather than a survey. Is the hurting/mattering distinction coherent, and does it cut where I think it cuts? Is my headline claim — that no sensation is intrinsically bad — actually licensed by the asymbolia case, or does the case show only that badness can be dissociated from sensation in an unusual patient while still being intrinsic to ordinary pain? Where does the position break down, or stop being distinctive? I am not asking for references; I want the strongest argument against the claim that hurting itself is evaluatively inert.
