You are a philosopher of science specializing in the methodology of long-run predictions, and you are working on the question of whether a theory can be "right but early" without that phrase being empty face-saving. Think carefully, and before writing anything, work through two or three candidate scenarios in your head and identify what feature would have to carry the load in each.

Construct a thought experiment to specifications. The case is for a working paper on falsification and rescue in long-run forecasting; accordingly, it is cheating to stipulate the verdicts or to rig the case so that one of the two views below is a strawman. The scenario must be invented but realistic, told in one paragraph, and involve no perfect predictors, no oracles, and no science-fictional devices.

Engineer the case so that the following two methodological views deliver opposite verdicts about the same theory at the same moment:

(A) Strict falsificationism. A theory that issues a long-run, dated, risky, observable prediction is falsified when the date passes and the predicted outcome has not occurred. The only legitimate way for its defenders to say "right but early" is to revise the theory so that the revised version exposes itself to a new, independently testable prediction; without that, the phrase is just a protective reinterpretation of the failure.

(B) A Lakatos-style research-programme view. A failed dated prediction is an anomaly rather than an automatic falsifier. The program may treat the prediction as "not yet" rather than "no" as long as the mechanism at its hard core remains a live object of pursuit and the protective belt keeps generating novel, confirmed results in neighboring domains.

Build the case so that the two views genuinely diverge: view A says the theory has been falsified and should be abandoned, while view B says the theory is damaged but should continue on probation. Malthus's population–subsistence forecast is the motivating example of a "right but early" defense, but the scenario must not use population and food supply. The defenders' "right but early" move must be substantive enough that view B's verdict is a real defense of the same theory, not a change of subject, while still failing view A's test for a legitimate rescue.

Then produce the following:

(a) The scenario in one paragraph.

(b) State the theory, its long-run dated prediction, the state of the evidence after the deadline, and the exact "right but early" move the defenders make.

(c) The check: give each view's verdict and explain step by step which feature of the case generates that verdict, and why the same feature triggers A's falsification rule while satisfying B's progress criterion.

(d) The isolation test: remove the single feature that does the work, and say what each view would then say about the stripped-down case.

(e) A closing note: explain how a defender of whichever view comes out looking worse would attack the case, and say whether the attack succeeds.
