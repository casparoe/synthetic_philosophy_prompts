A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about decision theory, and he eventually pressed the following chain on me. I find its conclusion deeply uncomfortable, but I cannot locate the step at which it goes wrong.

(1) In the standard counterfactual mugging case, a perfect predictor flips a fair coin. If it comes up heads, the predictor pays you £10,000 if it predicted that you would have paid had the coin come up tails; if it comes up tails, it asks you for £100. The rational thing to do when the coin lands tails and you are asked to pay is to pay. After all, if you pay, you are the sort of agent the predictor predicted would pay, so the heads branch contains £10,000; if you refuse, the heads branch contains nothing. Yet your payment is causally inert with respect to the heads payoff: the coin has already landed tails.

(2) The best justification for this verdict is the following general principle: when a perfect predictor makes one branch depend on what your decision procedure would do in another branch, you should choose the act whose predicted profile yields the best total outcome across the linked branches, even if the act is causally inert in the branch where you actually choose.

(3) Now take the case my friend called XOR blackmail. A blackmailer with perfect predictive power and no genuine damaging information about you flips a fair coin. If heads, the blackmailer will cause you a £10,000 loss if it predicted that you would refuse to pay a £100 demand in the tails branch, and will do nothing if it predicted that you would pay. If tails, it simply asks you for £100; whatever you do in this branch, no loss occurs. You are in the tails branch.

(4) By the principle in (2), you should pay. If you pay, you implement the procedure the blackmailer predicted as paying, so the heads branch is harmless. If you refuse, you implement the procedure predicted as refusing, so the heads branch contains a £10,000 loss. The actual tails branch is exactly the same in either case except for the £100.

(5) But that means a decision procedure that pays in counterfactual mugging also pays a blackmailer who has no actual causal leverage in the branch where the decision is made: the threatened loss is known not to occur, the payment cannot affect whether it occurs, and the blackmailer need never have possessed any real compromising material.

(6) That conclusion seems to prove too much. It would make rational agents systematically vulnerable to paying off purely counterfactual threats, even when no event they will ever experience in the branch where they act is improved by the payment.

(7) So either the counterfactual mugging verdict in (1) is wrong, or the general principle in (2) is too strong, or the XOR blackmail construction cheats somewhere. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the picture?
