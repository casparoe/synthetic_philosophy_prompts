I am a journalist preparing a long-form article on the intellectual case for pausing or internationally coordinating frontier AI development, and before I describe the most common arguments to a general audience I want to stress-test one rhetorical move I keep encountering among safety-minded researchers. In their discussions of race dynamics, they repeatedly reach for the Moloch analogy. In the biblical background they invoke, Moloch is the deity to whom worshippers surrendered their children by fire; in the modern usage, the analogue is the frontier AI race, in which labs, companies, and states each feel compelled to sacrifice goods they sincerely value — safety testing, caution, honest disclosure to regulators, even their own long-term survival — in order to keep pace with competitors. The analogy is asked to carry a definite conclusion: that the situation is a self-sustaining trap whose dynamics are largely independent of the character and intentions of the people inside it, so that individual restraint is nearly powerless and the only serious remedy is binding coordination, regulation, or some other change in the payoffs themselves.

I would like you to audit this analogy the way a careful critic would. Lay out what maps onto what in the Moloch picture — who or what plays the role of the worshipper, the sacrifice, the god, the competitive compulsion, the false promise. Then show where the mapping holds tightly, where it breaks, and, crucially, which disanalogies actually threaten the conclusion the analogy is used to support rather than being incidental differences. Moloch was an object of literal cult sacrifice with a priesthood; the AI race has no literal deity, no blood price, and no unified cult, and its drivers are profit, status, and the fear of being left behind. Does the intended conclusion — that this is a structural trap good intentions cannot escape and that only coordinated intervention can dissolve — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing more of the argumentative work than it can bear? If you think a better governing analogy exists, say what it is and what it captures that Moloch gets wrong.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the explicit mapping between the Moloch picture and the frontier AI race, as a compact list of correspondences if that is clearest; (2) the respects in which the mapping holds tightly; (3) the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion; (4) the disanalogies that do not matter; and (5) your verdict on whether the conclusion survives and what better analogy, if any, should replace Moloch.
