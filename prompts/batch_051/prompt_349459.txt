I am a philosophy PhD student working in normative ethics, and I have come to hold a broadly Taurekian position on so-called saving-the-many cases. I accept that, when I can rescue one stranger from one threat or five strangers from another, the fact that five is a larger number gives me no more reason to save the five than the one. My reason is the individualist restriction: benefits and harms attach to subjects; the death of each of the five is equally as bad for that person as the death of the one is for the one, but there is no sixth subject who bears the aggregate loss, so the five losses cannot combine into a weightier reason. On the procedural side, this leads me to reject giving the five a weighted chance proportional to their number and instead to treat each imperiled person's claim to rescue as equal: a fair procedure is one that gives each person, including the one, the same ex ante chance, such as a coin toss.

I want a map of what would count against this position, written for someone who actually holds it, not just a survey of the usual responses. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they ought to be. I would like at least four classes examined. First, counterexample structure: cases with prior commitments, special obligations, property rights, repeated encounters, or threats one has oneself created, where the view appears committed to saying that the numbers still do not count, and cases where that implication seems impossible to accept. Second, formal results in decision theory, social choice, or measurement theory that bear on whether individual claims can be aggregated without already presupposing an aggregate subject, and whether any impersonal aggregation is avoidable in practice. Third, internal consistency pressures: whether the equal-chance procedure itself smuggles aggregation back in, and whether a view that refuses to aggregate across persons can still coherently aggregate across times, harms, or risks within a single person. Fourth, debunking or genealogical considerations: whether the anti-aggregation intuition is stable, cross-culturally robust, or instead a product of a parochial picture of persons.

For each candidate defeater, say whether it should be fatal, seriously damaging, merely awkward, or dismissed as a misreading of the position. Finally, give an honest assessment of whether the view can be restated to avoid the hardest cases only by qualifying itself into unfalsifiability, and whether that would itself be a problem.
