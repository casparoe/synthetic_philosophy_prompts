I got into a forum argument about expected value after someone brought up the St. Petersburg game, and a commenter linked a blog post that explains the Pasadena paradox. I know enough about infinite series to follow the math, but I don't have a decision-theory background. The post's explanation goes like this. In the Pasadena game, a fair coin is flipped until heads appears; if the first heads is on flip n, you receive (−1)^(n−1) 2^n / n dollars, so the amount grows like 2^n / n and the sign alternates. Its expected value is the sum over n of (−1)^(n−1)/n, which converges to ln 2, so the fair price for the game is ln 2 dollars. The blog then says the "paradox" is just that this conditionally convergent series can be reordered to yield any value, but that's only a mathematical curiosity: the coin flip itself supplies a natural ordering of the outcomes, so for decision purposes the sum is fixed. It concludes the Pasadena game is actually less troubling than St. Petersburg because at least its expectation is finite.

I'm suspicious of exactly the move the blog makes. Isn't the point of the paradox that expectation in the usual probabilistic sense is undefined unless the sum converges absolutely, so saying the fair price is ln 2 amounts to quietly choosing one ordering and calling it natural? And doesn't relabeling the possible outcomes of the same game give a different sum, which would undermine the claim that the coin flip fixes the order? But maybe I'm overreading a popularization. Can you go through that summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are fair, which are subtly off, and whether the final conclusion survives the correction? In particular, is "the fair price is ln 2" a legitimate thing to say about the Pasadena game, or is that the very error the paradox exposes?
