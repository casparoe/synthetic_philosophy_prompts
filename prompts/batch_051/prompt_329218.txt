In my philosophy of mind course we have been working on naturalizing mental content, and the view I have come to find most plausible is a producer-consumer version of teleosemantics. As I understand it, the view says that a representational state R means that P because R is produced by a mechanism whose function it is to produce R when P obtains, and R is consumed by a downstream mechanism whose uses of R succeed, in the conditions under which those uses historically contributed to fitness, only when P obtains. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of mind broadly sympathetic to naturalism, to think carefully, and to tell me what would genuinely count against this position. I am already familiar with the standard Swampman objection, so I am most interested in less familiar pressure points—for example, underdetermination of biological function, exaptation, representational drift, content acquired through learning rather than selection, and cases in which the producer or consumer cannot be clearly identified—though you should raise anything else that deserves weight. For each consideration, say whether it is decisive or merely awkward for the view, which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as a cost rather than dismiss, and whether the view, once sharpened to avoid them, still risks anything or has become unfalsifiable. Begin by briefly distinguishing the main ways of making teleosemantics precise, so that your assessment tracks specific commitments rather than the slogan.
