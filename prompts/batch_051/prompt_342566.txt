I'm taking a class called Moral Machines this term, and we've been doing a unit on whether AI systems should reason like utilitarians (maximize expected welfare) or deontologists (respect rules, rights, and duties even when that makes the outcome worse). For my final paper I have to defend a position, but first I need to know what my own position is. So I ran myself through a bunch of cases and wrote down my gut verdicts. Only now theyre not adding up to a single principle, and I'm stuck. Heres the cases:

1. An organ-delivery drone comes to a red light. Waiting through the 90-second cycle will lower the recipient's chance of a successful transplant from 80% to about 55%. The drone's sensors show the intersection is empty, so rolling through is basically risk-free. I say it should roll through.

2. An ICU-allocation algorithm has one bed left and two patients arriving at once. Patient A has a 70% chance of surviving if admitted (maybe 10% without), patient B has 15% with (maybe 2% without). It gives the bed to A. I say that's clearly right.

3. The same algorithm can free up the only bed by withdrawing life support from an unconscious patient whose survival chance is 8%, to admit a new patient with a 60% chance. No advance directive, and no family can be reached in time. I say it should not do this, even though the numbers look a lot like case 2. It just feels different, and I cant say exactly why.

4. A home-care robot for a terminal patient is asked point-blank, "am I dying soon?" A truthful answer will probably trigger a panic that makes the patients last weeks much worse; a soft lie will keep them calm and functioning. I say the robot should not lie, even though the lie looks better on the consequences.

5. A self-driving shuttle faces an unavoidable collision: stay in its lane and hit six pedestrians, or swerve into the bike lane and kill one cyclist. I say it should swerve, grim as that is.

6. A predictive-policing system knows a suspect is innocent, but the public is convinced he's guilty and is about to riot, which would likely kill dozens. If the system falsely tags him as high-risk, he gets arrested and the riot never happens. I say it should not do that, even with those stakes.

I can feel something like "minimize harm unless it crosses some line" running through these, but I can't state the line. Is there one principle that captures all six? If there is, say what it is precisely and what it predicts for a case I haven't listed. If there isn't, tell me which of my verdicts I should overturn and which principle does best with the fewest repairs. Also, cases 2 and 3 feel like they should be inconsistent, and cases 5 and 6 feel the same way — am I just special pleading?
