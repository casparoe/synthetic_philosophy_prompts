A friend of mine does plaintiff-side employment work, and something she said has been stuck in my head. Picture a store that catches someone stealing a TV and decides not to call the police. Instead it says: we'll sort this out ourselves, in a back office, in front of an adjudicator we select and pay, under rules we drafted, and if you don't like the result there's no meaningful appeal — after all, you walked through the door. Everyone would call that a kangaroo court and say the store had privatized justice. Now change one thing. The same store is accused of systematically overcharging customers or shorting workers' pay. The customer or worker is told they cannot go to court, because the back of the receipt or the on-boarding paperwork says disputes go to binding arbitration — an arbitrator chosen under a process the company largely controls and, in practice, paid by the company — and class actions are waived. That isn't a hypothetical; it's basically the ordinary American legal landscape.

So here's the asymmetry: when the store accuses a person of taking its property, we would never let the store supply the judge; when a person accuses the store of taking their money, we let the store supply the judge and call it freedom of contract. I can't tell whether there's a real principle that generates both reactions or just a psychological slide from "crime" to "contract." Give me the strongest justification you can find for treating those two cases differently, then stress-test it against neighboring cases (religious arbitration, a company trying to force civil rights claims into arbitration, terms buried in shrink-wrap, whatever you think pushes hardest on it) and say straight whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
