A friend in my epistemology seminar, who is usually sharper than I am, sent me his one-paragraph write-up of Nozick's tracking theory after I missed a week of class. I was told the view is a serious competitor to analyses of knowledge in terms of justification, but the consequence my friend draws from it seems so extreme that I want an audit before I repeat any of it. Here is the summary as he wrote it:

"Nozick's tracking theory says knowledge is true belief that tracks the truth. The operative idea is sensitivity: your belief that p is sensitive when, had p been false, you would not have believed p. On this view I know there is a pen on my desk, because in the nearby worlds where there is no pen there, my experience would differ and I would not believe there is one. The theory's famous consequence is that knowledge is not closed under known entailment: I can know the pen is on my desk while failing to know that I am not a brain in a vat being fed pen-like experiences, since if I were envatted I would still believe that the pen is on my desk. And that combination is plainly incoherent — it is like saying I know a fact while admitting I cannot rule out the very scenario that would undermine that knowledge — so the theory is best read as a reductio of its own starting point rather than an account of ordinary knowledge."

I have two worries. First, I vaguely remember that Nozick's actual conditions ran through the method by which the belief is formed, and that there was a second subjunctive condition beyond sensitivity; the summary mentions neither. Second, the final sentence moves from "this consequence is strange" to "this consequence refutes the theory," which seems too fast to me. But I do not know the material well enough to say whether the non-closure result really is a bullet Nozick must bite, or whether my friend has misdescribed the reasoning that gets him there.
