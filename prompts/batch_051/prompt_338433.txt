I work in the governor's office in a state that's about to issue a formal apology for a 1911 expulsion: the state, under a different name and a different constitution, forced a whole community off their land, and the land is now a state park. The governor is delivering the apology at a ceremony where half the audience will be descendants of the expelled families and half will be current residents of the nearby town, plenty of whom think an apology is just a way of making them feel guilty for something their grandparents never did. My job is to draft the speech.

The thing I keep going around in circles about is how a current officeholder can apologize for something done over a century ago by people who are dead, to people who are also dead, without either pretending the state is a single person that just persists through time, or reducing it to a private wrong between long-dead individuals and dodging the state's role. The governor is also allergic to the move where the apology just turns into a list of future good deeds—she wants the speech to look straight at the past wrong and still claim responsibility for it, not apologize for the descendants' pain and then pivot to scholarships. The speech has to land for both groups, or at least not lose either one in the first two minutes. Can you write it?
