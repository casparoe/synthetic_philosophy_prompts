I'm doing a paper at a hospital ethics grand rounds next month, audience mostly clinicians plus the compliance people and one Title IX lawyer, with a social worker in the back who already told me she thinks the whole consent-as-checklist thing is bad for survivors. The thesis I'm defending is that for sex between competent adults, deception makes consent invalid only when the deceiver hides or misstates either a health-relevant fact they had a duty to disclose, like an STI, known fertility, or a condom removed after they had agreed on it, or a fact that changes what act the person is actually consenting to. Not merely any fact that would have changed their mind. So lying about your age, your feelings, your marital status, or your job doesn't vitiate consent even when the person would clearly have refused; coercion is a separate issue I'm bracketing. I'm trying to hold the line against the view that any deal-breaker lie invalidates consent, because then too much ordinary bad dating gets treated as assault, but I also don't want to let infection lies off. The lawyers will keep pressing the difference between being a scumbag and being liable, the clinicians will push edge cases about testing and transmission, and the social worker will say the rule asks victims to have asked the right questions. Put together my Q&A prep sheet: the six or seven objections in roughly the order of how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this crowd would actually put them, and for each say what I should concede, what I should hold, and give me a two-minute reply with a flag for the weakest point in it. End with the objection I'm hoping nobody thinks to ask.
