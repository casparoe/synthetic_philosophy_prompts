I have been sitting in on a philosophy of language seminar on speech acts, and the instructor has a habit of giving us a batch of cases and asking whether the speaker has promised. Her record so far is: an actor's stage line, "I'll be there at eight," said to another character, was not a promise; a sincere "I'll be there at eight" said to a friend in conversation was a promise; the same words said to the friend while privately intending not to show up were still a promise; the same words said to a friend who was asleep were not a promise; the same words said to a dog were not a promise; and the same words, said with every intention of committing, in a language the addressee did not understand, were not a promise.

Tomorrow's case is a voicemail. At 11 p.m., Smith leaves a message on his friend's phone saying, "I'll be there at eight." The friend is asleep and only listens the next morning. We are asked to decide whether Smith had already promised before the friend pressed play, whether he promised only at the moment of playback, or whether he never promised at all.

I want to predict her ruling, but more than that I want to extract the principle behind these verdicts before she states it. The seminar has split into three rough camps: an uptake view, on which a promise requires a hearer's recognition at the moment of utterance; a transmission view, on which what matters is that the utterance was directed to a suitable addressee through a durable channel, so that recognition can arrive later; and a live-address view, on which a promise requires an addressee who is present and able to take it up at the time. Which earlier case is the closest analog to the voicemail, and which camp does that analog support? Run each of the three principles across her published record and the voicemail case, say which one fits best and what it predicts, and then give your best guess as to her ruling. Finally, specify a single further case you would add to next week's list to settle whichever principles are still compatible with your prediction, and say what it would decide between.
