By 'value' I mean the evaluative rather than the merely preferential sense of the term: whatever answers the question of what is worth wanting, preserving, or building. I have agreed to present my views on the long-term future of value at a departmental workshop, and the organizer has asked participants to circulate a precise statement of their commitments in advance. Below is the list I intend to circulate; I would like a consistency audit of it before I send it, because I want to know whether I hold a coherent position or merely a set of claims I find individually attractive.

My commitments are these. (i) There are objective facts about what is valuable, and at least some of them are mind-independent: they are not made true by what any person, culture, or future intelligence happens to value. (ii) The particular values I affirm were shaped in large part by contingent evolutionary and historical forces, and I should regard them as fallible; some of them are probably wrong. (iii) When a proposed revision of a core value is prompted by a transformation I have not undergone — a future generation's altered sensibilities, a new technology that changes what people can choose, an ideology, a mood — I should apply a strong presumption in favor of my present core values, because the evaluative standards that would license the revision are exactly what the revision would change. (iv) It is possible, and in fact a serious risk, that future agents will come to hold values that are not merely different from mine but badly wrong — values that, for example, treat the suffering of others as good, or treat autonomy and dignity as worthless — and that such values could spread and become locked in for a very long time. (v) In light of (iv), we should now take significant steps to lower that risk, even if those steps are partially irreversible, including deliberately biasing the long-run trajectory of value toward the values we now judge best. (vi) Future persons deserve the same moral consideration as present persons, and among the things they deserve is the preservation of their capacity to scrutinize and revise the values they inherit; foreclosing that capacity would itself be a serious wrong.

Please go through these commitments and tell me whether they form a coherent whole. For each pair that is even superficially in tension, say whether the tension is real or merely apparent; order the real tensions by severity; and for each real tension, specify what would have to give and what the least costly repair would be. If there is a tension I have not noticed, I would rather have it exposed now than in front of the workshop.
