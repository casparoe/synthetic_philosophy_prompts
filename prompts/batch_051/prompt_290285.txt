I am a novelist drafting a scene for a political novel. The scene is an argument, late at night before an election, between two characters who like each other more than their positions allow: one is an older essayist who has come to believe a version of Simone Weil's view that political parties are intrinsically harmful, and the other is a young national organizer for a major party who regards that view as a consoling alibi for people who prefer judgment to power. Write their dialogue.

The essayist's side should be concrete rather than merely elevated: parties exist to win and to grow, so every other commitment of a member — truthfulness, fairness, independent judgment — is structurally subordinated to the party's interest; parties manufacture collective passions in which individuals feel noble by losing themselves in a group; and parties discipline their members into taking positions as a block, which destroys exactly the independence of mind politics most needs. The organizer's side should be just as probing rather than a celebration of partisanship: ask what would replace parties in the actual world — who selects candidates, organizes opposition, holds the powerful to account, and gives ordinary people a way to act together; whether the anti-party stance does not amount to rule by atomized voters and wealthy, unaccountable networks; and whether the essayist's faith in his own independence is not itself a kind of faction, in worse faith because it refuses to acknowledge itself as one.

Let them respond to each other's strongest points rather than talk past one another, and let each concede at least one thing the other is right about. The scene should end by exposing the real pivot of their disagreement: whether the harms of parties are accidents that reform can cure, or essential to what a party is — in which case the only honest alternatives are abolition and the uncertain world that would follow.
