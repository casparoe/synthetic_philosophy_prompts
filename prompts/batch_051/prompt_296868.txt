Suppose a mind is finite in the obvious ways: finite neurons or circuits, finite precision and range in each sensory channel, finite memory. Then there should be only finitely many total conscious states the mind can occupy and tell apart from one another. An astronomically huge number, sure, but finite. So if that mind lived long enough — or if we imagined it stepping through states one by one, like flipping through a stack of cards — it would eventually run out of genuinely new experiences; every later state would be an exact qualitative repeat, or near enough that it shouldn't count as new. I like this view because it's clean, naturalistic (no spooky novelty generator bolted on), and it gives a tidy bound on the space of possible experience; you can even model it with a finite state machine.

When I imagine seeing a color nobody has seen, or hearing a melody that isn't just a reshuffling of old fragments, or having a thought that feels like a new ingredient rather than a new combination, it seems obvious that no finite list — however large — would close the space off. Experience feels open-ended, as if there is always another genuinely new item, not just another arrangement of old bits. I know the standard move is to call this scope insensitivity: I can't picture how large finite numbers get.
