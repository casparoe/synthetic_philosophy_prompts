I have been working through Boethius's discussion of divine foreknowledge in Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy, and I have found myself with a set of commitments that I cannot tell whether they hang together. I would like an audit of the set rather than a lecture on the topic. The commitments are these.

(1) God is omniscient in a strong sense: God has infallible knowledge of every human choice, including choices that are future relative to me now.

(2) If God infallibly knows that p, then p is necessitated by that knowledge, in the sense that I cannot act so as to falsify what God knows.

(3) Some human actions are free in a sense that requires the ability to do otherwise at the moment of choice, and this freedom is not merely a compatibilist freedom of doing what one wants without external constraint.

(4) Boethius's move solves the apparent conflict: God does not know future events as future, because God does not stand in a temporal relation to them at all. God knows all times in an eternal present, so God's knowledge stands to my free act as present observation stands to a present fact, not as an earlier cause or premise stands to a later outcome.

(5) The past is necessary in an absolute sense: for any true proposition about the past, no one now can perform an action such that, were they to perform it, that proposition would have been false.

(6) Since God's eternal knowledge is not located at any time, it is not part of the past, and therefore the necessity of the past in (5) does not apply to the content of God's knowledge.

I am not sure where the real problem is, and I do not know which commitment I would give up. I also suspect that at least one apparent tension among these might dissolve on closer examination. Audit the set: which commitments pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, what is the cheapest way to restore joint tenability, and what would that repair cost?
