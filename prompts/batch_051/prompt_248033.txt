Section 5-201 of the state code, enacted in 1927 and never amended, says: "No person shall perform an act of surgery upon another human being unless licensed by the board of medical examiners to practice medicine and surgery. As used in this section, an act of surgery includes any cutting into the skin or underlying tissue for the purpose of treating or preventing any disease or injury. A person who violates this section is guilty of a felony of the third degree."

Here's the case. Last month, on a trail two hours from the nearest road, a man came on a mountain biker who had crashed and was suffocating because his windpipe had collapsed. The man cut a small opening in the biker's throat with a pocketknife and pushed in the hollow barrel of a pen, and the biker could breathe again and survived. The rescuer is a bank teller, not licensed. He is now charged under Section 5-201. Nobody disputes the facts, or that his act falls within the literal words, or that the drafters never imagined a layperson doing an emergency airway, or that the drafters would probably have said he should not go to prison for it. Set aside the necessity defense; I want the interpretive question.

First, take the four approaches—the plain words, the purpose of the statute, what the legislators would have said if they had been asked about this exact case, and what a reasonable reader of the statute would have understood it to cover—and give each one in its strongest form for his case. Then tell me whether the absurdity doctrine is the right tool for the judge here, and exactly what the judge would have to think "absurd" means for the statute to lose. Does the doctrine let the judge ignore the words, or does it just force a narrower reading? And if a judge convicts anyway, is there a coherent view of what the statute protects that makes that legitimate rather than perverse? End by saying which approach you'd adopt and why. (American English, please.)
