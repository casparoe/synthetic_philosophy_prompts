I'm a software engineer, and the picture of truth I keep falling back on is Boolean. A well-formed declarative sentence is either true or false; when a sentence looks like neither, I read that as incomplete specification rather than a gap in the world. Vague predicates ("is bald"), statements about the future ("there will be a sea battle tomorrow"), sentences with a broken reference ("the present king of France is bald") — these all feel like the natural-language versions of an uninitialized variable, a nullable column, or a partial function called outside its domain: not a third truth value, but missing information or an invalid evaluation. I wouldn't say any of this is trivially solved; I'd say the correct response is always to finish the specification — pin down a time, make a cutoff explicit, or rule the sentence out as something that isn't in the true/false business at all.

I hold this view, but I want to know what would count against it. Tell me what would have to be true — what cases, linguistic data, logical results, or even lessons from well-designed formal systems — for bivalence to fail and for a truth-value gap (or a genuine third value) to be the better model. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like to know where someone committed to the Boolean view should concede that an objection counts against them rather than dismiss it as confusing language with the world. In particular: are there phenomena that cannot be handled by "the specification just needs to be completed" without either quietly introducing a third value or making the view so flexible that it stops risking anything? If the position does end up insulated that way, tell me honestly whether that insulation is a bug or an acceptable feature of a view like this.
