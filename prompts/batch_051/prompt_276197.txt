I've been aruging with a friend about how far the gene's-eye view really goes. I mean the picture associated with Dawkins and Williams, where the allele is the thing that persists and the organism is a temporary vehicle. I'm not after history or citations, and please don't give me a reading list: I want a principle I can state in my own words and actually use. My problem is that my gut verdicts on specific cases feel like they're pointing in different directions.

Here are my gut verdicts. (1) An allele biases its own transmission by damaging gametes that lack it, so it ends up in more than half of a male's sperm even though it lowers his fertility overall: I'm certain the gene's-eye view is doing real work there, and the organism-level harm is beside the point. (2) An allele makes a bird feed its sister's chicks rather than its own, and spreads only because benefit times relatedness exceeds cost: I think the gene-level inclusive-fitness formulation is correct, but the organism-level kin-selection statement seems just as explanatory, so the gene's-eye version isn't adding anything special. (3) A large chromosomal inversion holds together several alleles that work well together and spreads because it prevents recombination from breaking the combination apart: I want to call this gene's-eye bookkeeping, but the unit is a chunk of chromosome, and I don't know whether that should count as one gene or many. (4) A plasmid moves itself between bacterial cells and carries an antibiotic-resistance gene that is only sometimes useful to the host: I definitely count this as a selfish replicator, even though it sits outside the chromosomes. (5) A somatic mutation makes a colon cell divide out of control and produces a cancer lineage: I don't think this is gene's-eye evolution in the ordinary sense, because the whole lineage dies with the body. (6) A neutral allele happens to sit next to a strongly beneficial one and goes to fixation by hitchhiking: I don't think the gene's-eye explanation explains its spread, since it didn't do anything and wasn't selected for. (7) A food taboo spreads because people copy successful neighbors: Dawkins-style talk would call it a meme and say it's the same abstract logic as a gene, but I can't treat it as the same kind of unit. The pair that keeps me up is (1) and (6): both are copied elements rising in frequency, and I want to say only one was selected for, but I can't articulate the difference without hand-waving about what the element does.

What I want is the best principle that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or reinterpret, and then an application of the principle to two cases I haven't made up my mind about: (a) a transposable element that copies itself into new positions in the germline and is kept mostly silenced by host defenses; and (b) a mitochondrial allele that sterilizes the male flowers of a hermaphroditic plant, where the mitochondrion is inherited only through seeds and never through pollen.
