I've been going back and forth on whether it's actually true that DNA is literally a code, and I'd trust this crowd to tell me where the move breaks, because I can't tell wether I'm making a sharp point or just playing definitions.

The argument as I'd type it in a comment section:

(1) A code, in the strict sense, is a mapping from one set of symbols to another where the assignment is arbitrary and fixed by agreement or convention: the parties could have picked any assignment, but once you've picked one you stick to it.
(2) The genetic code maps codons — triplets of RNA bases, read off the DNA during transcription — to amino acids or stop signals.
(3) That mapping wasn't fixed by agreement or convention. No one sat down and decided "this triplet means glutamine"; it's just how the translation machinery works.
(4) So, in the strict sense, DNA is not a code. Calling the genetic code a code is a metaphor: fine for lab shorthand, but not literally true.

The reason I can't shake it: I also believe the mapping isn't forced by ordinary chemistry. My impression is the mainstream view is that any codon could in principle have been assigned to any amino acid, and the actual assignment is a historical accident that got locked in once it was entrenched. So (3) is true, but for a reason that sounds almost code-like: the mapping is arbitrary without being agreed. That's exactly where I loose track. My attempted fixes either make (1) so strict that nothing except human conventions count as codes, or so loose that everything from gene expression to a lock and key is a code. I suspect the trouble is in (1), but maybe (4) is where the slide from "the genetic code" to "DNA" happens.
