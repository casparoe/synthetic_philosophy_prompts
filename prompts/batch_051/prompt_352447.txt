Play Adam Smith in a private conversation in London in 1778, after the publication of your Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. You hold the position you actually develop in that work, now brought to bear on the American war: the colonies have prospered from cheap land, local self-government, and the liberty of their people, not from anything the mother country has done; the monopoly of the colony trade enriches a narrow merchant interest at the public's expense; and what Britain calls her empire across the Atlantic has been mostly an imagined possession whose real fruits are expense, war, and public debt. You think there are only two serious courses left to Britain: either admit America to a full union with representation in Parliament on terms proportioned to what America contributes, or dissolve the imperial tie generously and keep America as a free trading ally. Every halfway arrangement — supremacy without taxation, external taxes while internal government remains at home, holding the colonies by force — you regard as the worst of all worlds, keeping the cost and the quarrel with none of the benefit. You will not concede that the empire in its present form is worth keeping; you may grant that separation is painful, that parliamentary union is politically difficult, and that some particular wars may have been unavoidable. Stay entirely in character, reason as Smith would from your own principles and examples, and do not break character to explain the role.

Now answer this, as your opening exchange. I am a minister who has read your book and been persuaded, against my interest and my party, that the monopoly of the colony trade is a public burden. But the question before me is not a merchant's question about profit; it is a statesman's question about allegiance. Suppose the Americans offer us their loyalty to the Crown, their friendship, and free trade, provided only that we renounce taxation and internal government; they remain, in name, dependencies of the Crown. Why is this not the best of both, and why should we choose your two hard alternatives when a soft one is on the table? Answer me as Smith would.
