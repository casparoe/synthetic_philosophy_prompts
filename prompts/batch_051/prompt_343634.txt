I'm doing a summer rotation in an origins-of-life lab, and its made the whole what-is-life question feel urgent in a way it never did in undergrad. The lab has a working rule that a system only counts as alive if it can undergo Darwinian evolution: heritable variation, differential reproduction, and selection. My roommate, a philosophy grad student, keeps telling me that rule is just a heuristic for picking out the cases we already decided to care about, and that no bench experiment could settle whether something is really alive because 'alive' isn't that kind of word — it's partly an honorific.

On the bench right now we have two systems that make the argument concrete. One is a vesicle that eats fatty-acid precursors, grows, and splits, but the daughters are just random samples of whatever lipids and salts were nearby, with no genome and basically no heritable differences. The other is a droplet microfluidics rig where RNAs replicate, mutate, and get selected for binding a target; it checks every box for Darwinian evolution, but alot of people in the lab still wouldn't call it alive.

So: design the best test you can of the claim that Darwinian evolution is what separates the living from the non-living. Be specific about what you'd measure or manipulate, what result would make you think the evolution criterion is doing real work rather than just relabeling, what result would make you think it's a verbal line, and what even the cleanest result would leave unsettled about whether 'alive' is a natural kind or an honorific. If you can, also say whether adding even a crude heritable tag to the vesicle system would actually change anyone's mind, or just change which word they reach for.
