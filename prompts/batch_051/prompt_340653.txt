I've been rewatching Jaws — Spielberg's version, not the novel — and I keep getting stuck on the mayor's calls around the beaches. I'm not say the film is a tract, but it seems like a natural fit for the ethics of risk imposition without consent, and I want you to read it through that lens. You're a philosopher who works on the ethics of risk and consent and who also takes film seriously as a medium that can do philosophical work. Take your time, think step by step, and start by brainstorming the morally relevant details of the beach scenes and the town meeting before you build to a verdict.

Here's the puzzle I want worked through. On the standard philosophical picture, you wrong someone when you impose a serious risk of grave harm on them that they haven't consented to run — especially if you conceal the risk, because then their presence isn't even a kind of tacit consent to a known danger. Mayor Vaughn looks straightforwardly guilty by that standard: he pressures the coroner to call the first death a boating accident, keeps the beaches open for the summer economy, and pushes to reopen them for the Fourth of July even after a second attack. But I'm not sure the movie just condemns him. It also gives him a real civic argument: the town lives on summer money, and shutting the beaches destroys people's livelihoods. It shows Brody's complicity and the town's collective pressure, which muddies the question of who exactly is doing the imposing. And the shark itself is invisible, under the water, which makes the risk feel different from a visible danger like a riptide — there's a dread element, an uncertainty about whether it's even there, that a pure probability judgment doesn't seem to catch.

So here's what I'd like from you. Read Jaws through the ethics of imposing risk without consent and ask: (1) does the film actually give Vaughn a better case than the concealed-risk story lets on, or does it stack the deck against him? (2) Where does the philosophical picture get the film right, and where does the film dramatize something the picture underweights — dread, imaginability, probabilistic versus all-or-nothing thinking, diffuse responsibility? (3) What should a philosopher say about the swimmers who would have gone in anyway even if they'd been told, and how does the movie itself treat that possibility — is it on the side of closing the beaches for people's own good, or does it leave room for a liberty-based objection to closure? Don't feel you have to convict the mayor or acquit him; I want a reading that's faithful in both directions and that ends by saying what Jaws adds to the risk-ethics debate that armchair examples don't.
