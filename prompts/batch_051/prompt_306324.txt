I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts about small risks of death, and I suspect they don't express a single principle. I'll use micromorts, where one micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of dying. My working rule is something like this: I may accept routine, voluntary, familiar risks up to some modest annual total, but I should refuse an acute, unusual, or imposed risk if a single episode adds more than about ten micromorts. I don't know whether that rule is a principle or just a story I tell about my fears.

The cases. I drive roughly 10,000 miles a year for ordinary reasons and never think about the crash risk, which I estimate at around 50 micromorts a year; I wouldn't spend any significant amount to make those miles safer. But I turned down a friend's offer of a 45-minute sightseeing flight in a small plane because I estimated it would add about 12 micromorts in one afternoon, and that felt like too much. Those two verdicts sit badly together. I also ride a bicycle four miles to the store twice a week, accepting what I figure is about 20 micromorts a year of accident risk, because I expect the exercise to reduce my overall mortality by more than the cycling adds. And when choosing an apartment I paid roughly $200 more per month to live on a quiet street twenty miles from work rather than beside an arterial road five miles away; I cited traffic pollution as a health reason, but the longer commute added roughly 30 micromorts a year in crash risk, which never entered the calculation. Looking back, I suspect the pollution risk I was avoiding was smaller, perhaps much smaller, than the driving risk I ignored.

I want to know what principle I ought to hold. Is there a coherent account—a constant value per micromort, a per-micromort value weighted by control, familiarity, or dread, a threshold rule, or something else again—that captures most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does that account force me to abandon or reinterpret? And what would it say about cases I haven't yet had to face, such as a one-time 100-micromort risk from a surgery that would largely cure a chronic condition, or a career-long workplace exposure adding five micromorts a year? I'd especially like to know whether my casual acceptance of driving survives the principle, since it is the largest annual total on the list.
