I keep having the same argument with a friend about whether you can take someone's word for it that a work of art is any good, and we both end up talking passed each other. Could you write a dialogue between two equally sharp characters — not a strawman on either side — about aesthetic testimony? One of them should defend something like the acquaintance requirement: a genuinely aesthetic judgment about a work has to be formed from your own experience of it, and a judgment acquired purely from someone else's say-so is at best a borrowed belief about the work's quality, not the thing we usually care about when we call someone's judgment "aesthetic." The other should defend the opposite view: testimony in aesthetics is no worse then it is anywhere else, and if a critic whose track record you trust tells you the new novel is beautifully constructed, you can justifiably believe it, recommend it, and even put it on a syllabus before you've read it — refusing to ever do that is just epistemic egoism.

I want them to actually respond to each other, not deliver dueling monologues. The acquaintance-person has to face the charge that they wouldn't trust anyone about anything; the testimony-person has to face the consequence that, on their view, someone who only ever reads reviews could hold perfectly justified opinions about books. Have them try to work out whether the real disagreement is about the justification of beliefs, or about what aesthetic judgment is for. They can make moves from the literature — acquaintance, deference, taste-as-capacity versus taste-as-a-body-of-beliefs — but please don't mention authors or papers or ask for citations; keep it a natural-sounding argument. It doesn't have to end neatly; it's better if they end up having located what they actually disagree about.
