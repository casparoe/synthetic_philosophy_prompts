I should note at the outset that I am a software engineer and have never studied political philosophy. What follows are my considered gut reactions to a set of immigration cases, and my failure to state the principle behind them.

My work has put me close to immigration procedures for years, and I have found that my judgments do not fit any simple view I can articulate. Here they are:

(1) A software engineer from another country has a standing offer at a company that genuinely needs her skills. I think she should be admitted, and I would treat a system that blocked her as defective.

(2) A family arrives at the border without documents from a region where their village has been burned and people with their surname are being rounded up. I think they should be admitted and allowed to make a claim for protection. The reply that they should have waited in line for a visa does not strike me as a serious response.

(3) A citizen's parents, both in their eighties and in poor health, apply to join him. They will almost certainly consume more in public healthcare than they will ever contribute. I still think they should be admitted; refusing them because they are a net fiscal loss seems indecent.

(4) A healthy young man from a much poorer country wants to move simply to earn more. He has no job offer, no family in the country, and no claim of persecution. I find myself thinking the receiving country may turn him down—though I dislike saying it, and I could not explain why he, unlike the engineer in (1), must stay put.

(5) During a serious infectious outbreak, a country suspends nearly all entry, including that of some citizens' relatives and aid workers. I think this is harsh but defensible.

(6) A country adopts a border policy consisting of nothing but an annual quota and a skills test, with no channel whatever for refugees. I think that policy is wrong, even though I do not object to quotas or skills tests in themselves.

(7) A country refuses entry to a particular individual with a record of serious violent offenses and a continuing, stated intention to harm people. I think this is plainly permissible.

(8) A country excludes everyone of a particular nationality or religion. I think this is plainly wrong, and not merely impolitic.

I keep trying to find a single principle that covers these verdicts, but every simple candidate captures about five and then fails. A narrowly fiscal cost-benefit principle handles (1), (5), and (7), but misses (2) and gives the wrong answer in (3). A principle that states may exclude whomever they choose handles (4), (5), and (7), but collapses on (2) and (8). An open-borders principle handles (1), (2), (4), and (8), but sits badly with (5) and (7). And each candidate wavers on some other case in ways I cannot make precise.

What is the principle—or what are the one or two principles—that best captures my verdicts, and which of my verdicts should I give up if no single principle captures all of them? In particular, I would like to know what your account says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a healthy, employable young person from a very poor country, with no family in the country and no refugee claim, but with a clear job offer in hand. Should the job offer make the moral difference between him and the engineer in (1) and between him and the man in (4)? If your principle forces me to overturn some of my stated verdicts, I would rather hear that than receive a principle with eight ad hoc clauses.
