I have spent the past several months building software that produces photorealistic faces of nonexistent people, including brief video loops in which these faces appear to meet the viewer's gaze. I did not expect the effect to be ethically unsettling, but it has been: I sometimes feel almost addressed, even accused, by images that have no one behind them. Because this has become a live question for me, I want to work through how Emmanuel Levinas, who died in 1995, would have assessed such generated faces.

Levinas insists that the face of the Other is not the same thing as the visible countenance. The face is not a set of plastic features to be perceived; it is expression, exposure, and vulnerability — the way another life places me under an ethical claim before I have chosen to accept it, a claim whose primordial expression is the prohibition on murder. What is ethically decisive is not how the face appears but that it addresses me from a mortal life that my actions could wound or refuse.

This distinction leaves me genuinely uncertain about synthetic faces. On the one hand, they can be empirically all but indistinguishable from images of real human faces. On the other, no exposed and vulnerable life stands behind them: no one there can die, suffer, or answer. Levinas also holds that the ethical relation is not a matter of empirically detectable properties, so the difference between a real face and a generated one may be too crude, by his lights, to settle the question. But if the face is not identified through visible features, it becomes unclear how one ever recognizes that one is being addressed at all.

Given that tension, please reconstruct as carefully as you can what Levinas would have said about the following: Can an AI-generated face — a photorealistic image with no vulnerable person behind it — address me ethically in his sense? Would he regard my feeling of being accused by such an image as a genuine ethical response, as a merely aesthetic response to a compelling image, or as something subtler? Make the reasoning explicit by separating his concept of the face from the visible countenance and then applying that separation to the generated image.
