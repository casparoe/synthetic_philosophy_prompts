I am writing a novel whose protagonist is a moral philosopher with convictions close to Elizabeth Anscombe's. In her journals and in conversation she has said that Truman's decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder, and that the deliberate area bombing of cities to break civilian morale is indefensible. Yet she is not a pacifist: she allows that a soldier in a just defensive war may kill an enemy soldier in combat, and she has told a flight lieutenant that a night strike on an ammunition plant at the edge of a city may be permissible even though it will foreseeably kill a few civilians in the nearby streets, provided the military advantage is proportionate and every reasonable precaution has been taken.

I am considering a plot turn in which, after a costly ambush of her side's forces, her commanding officer proposes shooting five inhabitants of the nearest village, chosen by lot, as a reprisal. His staff concedes that the five had no part in the ambush and are being killed as a means; they calculate that the reprisal will stop the attacks and save far more lives than it takes. My protagonist must deliver her verdict before the order is carried out.

Her earlier pronouncements are consistent with more than one rule. Please set out the most plausible rules that fit her record, say what each predicts about the reprisal, give your best judgment about which verdict she would give and why, and identify a further case, not merely another version of the reprisal, that would most cleanly settle which rule she is actually following.
