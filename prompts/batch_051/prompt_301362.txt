Two friends of mine got into a long argument the other night about whether psychiatric labels like ADHD describe something that's already there or wether the label loops back and changes the people it's applied to, so that the category partly creates what it's supposed to just name. I'll call them D and M. Neither has any training in this, they just read a lot, and I mostly listened.

(1) D: "When a kid gets labeled ADHD, the kid is told he's got a brain disorder, his teachers adjust to him, and he starts interpreting his attention problems as symptoms. The label feeds back and changes the very thing it's supposedly measuring. So a rising number of ADHD diagnoses isn't just more detection — the label itself is doing part of the work."

(2) M: "That doesn't make ADHD fake. People also get told their blood pressure is high and then get nervous at the doctor, which raises it further. Does that mean hypertension is made up? A label having effects tells you nothing about whether it's tracking a real pattern."

(3) D: "Hypertension is a bad analogy. Blood pressure is a physical quantity you can measure without asking the person how they feel. Psychiatric symptoms are behaviors and self-reports — exactly the kind of thing a label can change. Hacking calls this the looping effect: these are interactive kinds, not indifferent kinds. If the kind moves when you name it, then those prevalence studies are measuring a moving target, not a stable natural kind."

(4) M: "But Hacking's point isn't that interactive kinds are unreal. A pattern can be real and still shift in response to being classified. Every science has some of this — economists announce a recession and people spend less; we don't say recessions are made up. The real question is whether an ADHD diagnosis predicts anything beyond the label, like school outcomes or medication response. If it does, it's tracking something real."

(5) D: "Those predictions could themselves be products of the loop. You give a kid stimulants, he focuses better, and everyone says, see, the disorder was real. But stimulants improve focus in most kids. And the diagnosis becomes an identity the kid organizes his life around. That's not discovering a natural kind, it's helping to build one."

(6) M: "I never said it was a natural kind in some essentialist sense. Medicine is full of syndromes that are real enough without being essences. The mistake is treating 'socially shaped' and 'unreal' as synonyms. Categories can be partly constructed and still pick out real patterns."

They went around like this for an hour without landing anywhere. Could you referee the argument itself — not tell me which side to personally like — and go through it move by move? In particular, for each of the six moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or part right, and what exactly is wrong or right about it. Then tell me what both of them are missing.

- Overview (2–3 sentences): what the argument is really about.
- Move-by-move rulings (roughly one short paragraph per move, six total): rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right, and justify briefly.
- What both sides are missing (2–4 sentences).
- Bottom line (1–3 sentences).
