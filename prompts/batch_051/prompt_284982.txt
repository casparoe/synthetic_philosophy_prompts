I am a novelist drafting a near-future story in which neural interfaces are common enough that courts, employers, and families routinely face questions about what may be extracted from, inferred about, or imposed on a person's mind. Before I let my characters defend or attack mental privacy, I want the specification rather than the sales pitch: what would a theory of cognitive liberty (or mental privacy, though I suspect those are not the same thing) actually have to get right to be adequate to the legal and moral judgments the book turns on? Give me the desiderata—the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as reading a thought versus inferring it, privacy versus liberty, or the inner life versus outward behavior, and the constraints it cannot flout—and for each say what concrete situation forces it and whether it is negotiable or load-bearing. Tell me also which single requirement does the most to separate a serious view from a slogan. Throughout, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of. Write it as continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables.
