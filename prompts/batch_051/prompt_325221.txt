For as long as I can tell, the debate over divine foreknowledge and human freedom has been stuck in the same shape. One side says that if God infallibly knew yesterday that I would sit down to write this note today, then it was already a settled fact yesterday, and when the moment arrives I cannot do other than what God knew. The other side, following Boethius's move in the Consolation, answers that God does not know the future from any point earlier in time at all: God's eternity is not endless duration but the simultaneous possession of the whole of time, so that what we call future events are present to God all at once, rather as someone watching a walker from a hilltop sees each step without forcing the walker to take it. The first side has a ready rejoinder, however. Relocating God's knowledge outside time seems to change only where the knowledge sits, not what it fixes. If God's atemporal cognition is infallible and already tracks which action I will perform, then the corresponding proposition about my action is true in advance, and it is hard to see how moving the knower outside the temporal order creates any room for me to do otherwise. At that point the exchange starts over.

After working through this many times, I have formed a hypothesis, though I am not confident in it. I suspect the real disagreement is not about the temporal or atemporal character of God's knowledge at all, but about what free will demands in the first place. The incompatibilist seems to require that, holding everything about the past and everything about God's knowledge fixed, the agent must still have been able to do otherwise. The defender of the Boethian view seems instead to require only that the action issue from the agent's own deliberation and character in the right way, without external compulsion. If that is right, the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, and no further argument about the metaphysics of God's knowledge could close the gap. Yet the stalemate might equally be a disagreement about whether the necessity of the past transfers to atemporal facts, or about how an atemporal cognition can bear on a temporal event at all, or about what the word foreknowledge even means once the knower is outside time.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse, not yet another adjudication of the original problem. What kind of disagreement is this, precisely: a clash of different modal premises, a dispute about which cases count as data, a verbal slippage in a single term, or something else? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here—a formal result about how necessity transfers, a sharper distinction between kinds of necessity, a better account of the relation between eternity and time, or the recognition that the two sides are not really answering the same question? If instead you think no progress should be expected, explain why that is the honest answer.
