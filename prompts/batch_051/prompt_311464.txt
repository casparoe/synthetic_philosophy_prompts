I've been working at a synthetic-biology startup, and lab discussions keep drifting back to an argument I first saw in John von Neumann's cold-war-era writing on whether technology can be survived. As I understand him, the claim is that once a technology becomes possible, banning it is basically futile, because somebody somewhere will develop and use it; the only stable response is to assume it will be used and make society robust to it. My coworkers now use the same move about AI and biotech, and I half-agree with them but cant tell if I'm persuaded by the logic or just tired of hearing it. Here is the argument as I reconstruct it:

(1) If a technology would give any sufficiently powerful actor a decisive advantage, then in a competitive environment some actor will eventually develop it.
(2) The international system is competitive in the relevant sense.
(3) Once a technology has been developed anywhere, the knowledge behind it cannot be permanently confined, because of espionage, defection, and independent rediscovery.
(4) Once the knowledge spreads, at least one actor will use the technology, either to gain the advantage or to avoid being preempted.
(5) Therefore, for any usable technology, use somewhere is eventually inevitable.
(6) So the only stable policies are those that assume eventual use and aim to make society able to absorb it, rather than to prevent it.

I think the weak point is somewhere in the leap from "some actor somewhere will use it" to "we might as well plan on it," but I also worry I'm rejecting the conclusion just because I don't like it. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and on what reading? And tell me where your verdict is most likely to fail: what premise or consideration, if I had it wrong, would flip the conclusion?
