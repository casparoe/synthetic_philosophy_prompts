I have been reading about Lakatos's account of research programmes, and one feature of it bothers me. As I understand him, the central claims of a programme — its hard core — are deliberately protected from refutation, and when a prediction fails, scientists are supposed to adjust the surrounding auxiliary assumptions — its protective belt — rather than abandon the core. But if every failed prediction can be blamed on some adjustable assumption in the belt, how is this different from a pseudoscience that explains away every awkward result? It seems as though the core could never be tested in practice, and nothing would ever count against it. I would like to understand what, on Lakatos's view, stops a research programme from becoming a closed system that absorbs all counterevidence.
