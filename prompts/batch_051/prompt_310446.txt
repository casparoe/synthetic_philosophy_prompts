I spent my career in physical chemistry and have only recently gotten into philosophy of language, so I can't help asking what would falsify a claim. The old Neogrammarian regularity principle says sound change is exceptionless: when a sound changes in a language it changes in every word with that sound in the relevant environment, and apparent exceptions are always chalked up to analogy or borrowing. When they formulated it, the evidence was mostly old texts and a few modern dialects. The new development is that we have enormous digitized corpora and recordings of actual speech, so linguists can now watch changes in progress word by word rather than seeing only the final result. And what they report is that a change can stay in progress for a long time, affecting some words but not others, with word frequency apparently predicting a lot of which words move first. To me that looks like the principle survives only because any exception can be blamed after the fact on analogy or contact, which would make the whole thing unfalsifiable. So does the regularity thesis actually survive this development? Wich premise exactly is under pressure, and is there a repaired version that keeps something worth keeping — say, the claim that sound change is regular once it has finished diffusing — or should someone with my background just say it is a strong statistical tendency and nothing stronger? And if the finished-diffusion version is all we can save, is that still an empirical claim or just a definition?
