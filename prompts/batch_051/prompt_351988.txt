I'm in a high school philosophy club and we're reading the part of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (Book 3, Part 1, Section 1) where he talks about an action we all agree is vicious, like wilful murder. He says if you examine it from every angle, you only ever find certain passions, motives, volitions, and thoughts. There's no extra matter of fact in the object itself that you could point to and call vice. As long as you keep looking at the object, the vice entirely escapes you. It only shows up when you turn your reflection inward and catch a feeling of disapproval rising in you. So he concludes that moral distinctions aren't derived from reason alone, but depend on sentiment.

My teacher says this passage is part of the background for the whole is-ought gap thing, but I keep seeing different people read it very differently. Could you rank these five readings from most to least compelling as an actual argument that you can't derive an ought from an is? Give each a quick reason for where it lands, say a sentence or two, and then tell me which one you'd actually defend in a class discussion.

(a) The passage shows that moral properties aren't part of the ordinary empirical facts of the world, so any attempt to infer what we ought to do from what is the case will always leave the normative element unexplained.
(b) The passage only shows that moral judgments have a motivational or emotional component; it says nothing about whether there are normative facts that reason could track.
(c) The passage is best read as a psychological claim about how we come to make moral distinctions, and psychology can't settle a logical question about entailment.
(d) The passage gives a foundational role to sentiment, but sentiment itself can be a legitimate source of practical conclusions, so it doesn't actually create a gap.
(e) The passage is mostly a rhetorical move against the rationalists Hume was arguing with, and treating it as a careful argument about the is-ought gap over-reads it.
