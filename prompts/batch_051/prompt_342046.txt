I've been having this running argument on a bird forum about the scrub jays that visit my garden. Some of us think they genuinely keep track of who watched them hide food — like they know a potential thief might have seen them, and they re-hide it later. The sceptics say it can all be done by associative learning and nobody has proved otherwise. Every time this comes up, though, the same poster arrives and recites Morgan's canon: never explain an animal's behaviour by a higher mental process if a lower one will do the job. The thread usually dies there, because it sound so authoritative.

But I've started to wonder wether Morgan's canon is really one claim or several claims wearing the same coat. Does it mean the simpler explanation is more likely to be true? Or that scientists ought to prefer the simpler explanation as a matter of method, even if it might be false? Or that animals literally cannot have the higher capacity being proposed? Or just that we should be on our guard against anthropomorphism because we're prone to it? Those feel like quite different statements, and they wouldn't all do the same work in an argument.

Could you lay out the different precise principles that Morgan's canon might be expressing? I'd like a sort of menu, from the harmless to the strong, with a verdict on each — which are trivial, which are defensible, which are too strong, and which are the ones people probably have in mind when they use it to shut a debate down. In particular I want to know which reading, if any, would actually justify dismissing a claim about scrub jays out of hand, as opposed to just telling us to be careful. I'd like something I can paste back into the thread without starting another flame war.
