I'm going through my second round of IVF right now, and because my husband and I carry a mutation for a fatal metabolic disorder we've had to get very fluent in PGT-M consent forms this year. I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about embryo selection, and honestly my gut is all over the place. I wrote down the cases:

- Discarding an embryo that carries the metabolic mutation that killed my nephew at eighteen months: sad, but zero moral hesitation, clearly fine.
- Using the same report to pick a girl since we already have two boys: also fine, I barely thought about it.
- A friend discarded embryos carrying a chromosomal duplication that reliably causes profound intellectual disability and seizures. I'd do it to, but it felt heavier, maybe because the child could still have a life.
- Discarding an embryo with a high polygenic risk score for late-onset Alzheimer's: fine.
- Discarding an embryo with a high polygenic score for low predicted educational attainment: no, I wouldn't do that, but I can't explain how it differs from the Alzheimer's case. Both are just shifting probabilities.
- Deaf parents deliberately choosing an embryo with the same congenital deafness so the child will grow up in their sign language community. I want to say that's wrong. But it's their culture and I'd never claim to know better about raising their kid.
- A little person deliberately choosing an embryo with achondroplasia for the same reason: I honestly can't call it wrong, which makes me worry I'm being inconsistent about the deaf case and just don't want to admit it.
- Selecting an embryo as a savior sibling, a tissue match for our daughter who has leukemia. Before I had kids I'd have called this obviously wrong, using a person as a means. Now I'd probably do it.

I keep reaching for a principle like: prevent clear suffering and early death, don't select for traits you'd simply prefer, and definitely don't deliberately select for a disability. That handles the metabolic case, the intellectual-disability case, and the Alzheimer's case, and it explains my unease about the education polygenic scores. But it doesn't handle the sex-selection case (I'm fine with it and it's not preventing suffering), and it would call the achondroplasia and savior-sibling cases wrong even though I don't, and it makes me sound like I'm telling disabled parents their child was the wrong choice.

Is there a principle that gets all or nearly all of these? Which of my verdicts should I give up? And what would the principle say about the case I can't stop turning over: a clinic that ranks embryos by an overall predicted-quality-of-life composite and gently steers patients to take the top one, which is exactly what my bioethicist friend says procreative beneficence would suggest.
