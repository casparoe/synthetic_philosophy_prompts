I've been hanging around rationalist forums long enough that the phrase "there's no free lunch" has started to do too much work in too many arguments, and I think people are equivocating without noticing. Last week someone in a thread about forecasting said the no-free-lunch theorem shows that no inductive policy is a priori better then random guessing, because averaged over all possible target functions every learner does equally well. Someone else said "sure, there's no free lunch" as if that just meant every method has tradeoffs and you have to pick your biases. Then a third person took it to mean that past data can't give you predictive purchase for free, as a point about the problem of induction.

I want a menu of the different precise claims "there's no free lunch" could be making, from the trivially true (every method has some limitations or costs) to the really strong (no learner can beat chance across all problems, or induction can't be justified at all). For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, defensible but only under stated conditions, or actually false, and tell me which reading would actually support the claim that one inductive policy is no better than random guessing a priori. If the forecasting thread was sliding between readings, say exactly where. Answer in American English.
