For the graduate seminar in value theory that I teach each autumn, I would like a sorting instrument—a which-position-in-population-axiology-do-you-actually-hold quiz with a scoring key—that I can hand out before students have encountered any of the named positions and again at the end of the unit to see whether considered judgments have moved. The audience is graduate students with a solid background in ethics but no prior work on population axiology. Please aim for ten questions and six leaves. The leaves must be these: totalism, on which the value of a population is the sum of the well-being of its members, so quantity and quality trade off in a linear way; averagism, on which value is average well-being per person; critical-level views, on which a life contributes positive value only above some threshold of well-being; variable-value or diminishing-value views, on which additional lives or additional well-being contribute at a decreasing marginal rate, blocking the repugnant conclusion without any sharp threshold; person-affecting views committed to the intuition of neutrality, on which adding a life worth living, other things equal, is not an improvement because it is not better for anyone in particular; and asymmetry or anti-frustrationist views, on which the absence of suffering matters differently from the absence of happiness, so that preventing miserable lives is required while creating happy ones is not.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from the names. Every question should be a concrete choice among populations described only by size and welfare level, and should be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels above; ask which world is better, or which world one ought to bring about, and let the pattern of answers do the sorting. Two cases must appear as items: the contrast between a world A of ten billion people all living excellent lives and a world Z of an astronomically larger number of people whose lives are barely worth living; and the mere-addition trio consisting of A, A+ (the same people as A plus an additional group whose lives are barely worth living), and B (the same total number of people as A+, with a slightly lower average than A but a larger total).

Finally, include a scoring key that maps answer patterns to the six leaves, and add two pieces of commentary. First, identify any answer combination that no standard position occupies and say briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply under-explored. Second, flag any pair of leaves the instrument cannot cleanly separate—critical-level and variable-value views, for instance, may coincide on all ordinal choices unless a specific threshold or curvature is stipulated—and say what further case, if any, would drive them apart. The distinction that matters most for my course is between views that accept the repugnant conclusion, views that block it by altering how lives aggregate, and views that block it by rejecting the impersonality of the comparison, so the instrument's branching should reflect that three-way division rather than merely arranging positions by name.
