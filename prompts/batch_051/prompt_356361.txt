I'm preparing a two-page proposal for a six-month philosophy fellowship, and I want it built around a hunch I haven't yet managed to sharpen. I came to the standing-to-blame debate through a pattern I kept seeing in online norm enforcement: someone criticizes X, X replies that the critic is a hypocrite and therefore lacks standing, and the argument dead-ends in one of two ways — either the criticism is treated as true regardless of who says it, or the norm is treated as unenforceable because only perfectly clean hands could enforce it. When I try to taboo the word standing in these exchanges, two different complaints fall out. One is about the critic's record: the critic violates the very norm they invoke. The other is about the critic's relation to the wrong: the critic is not the party harmed and lacks the relevant relationship or stake. My suspicion is that some prominent treatments of standing encourage these being run together by treating standing as one phenomenon with several triggers — hypocrisy, meddling, complicity — and that the demand for a single unified account of the phenomenon may be part of the problem.

Please draft the full two-page proposal so that it does the following: (1) states a single sharp question — one that a competent person could reject — that captures the distinction without stacking the deck against the unified-account view; (2) explains why the existing positions, described by their content rather than by author or title since I will supply citations myself, leave that question open; (3) argues for a particular method: a conceptual taxonomy plus a small battery of worked test cases, rather than an empirical study of blame judgments or a historical reconstruction; (4) gives a month-by-month plan for six months ending in a paper-length result, with no empirical data collection and no fieldwork; and (5) states what the project's best outcome is, what genuine failure looks like, and which part of the plan is most likely to produce failure. No quotations and no bibliography, please.
