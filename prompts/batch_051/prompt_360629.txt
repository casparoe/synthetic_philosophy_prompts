I have made a policy out of a Rawlsian idea I once encountered: that some envy is excusable when it is a response to inequalities that wound self-respect, rather than mere hostility to another's advantage. Concretely, when I notice envy flaring up — over a colleague's promotion, a friend's inheritance, a neighbor's sudden good luck — I no longer treat it as a character flaw to suppress. Instead I treat it as diagnostic. I ask whether the comparison behind the feeling tracks something I would judge unfair from a disinterested standpoint, and I take the persistence of the feeling after I learn the relevant facts as a defeasible reason to investigate the background distribution rather than to dismiss my reaction as mere resentment of someone else's good. I am aware this could simply launder a vice into an epistemic instrument, since envy also flares at any loss of relative standing, not only at injustice. Is the policy defensible? Under what conditions does envy carry information about injustice, as opposed to information about my own sense of entitlement or my discomfort with another person's advantage? Is there a restricted version of the policy that preserves the diagnostic function while cutting off the part that is just ill will, and does resentment differ from envy here by tracking perceived wrongdoing rather than mere comparative disadvantage?
