On a recent episode of a podcast I sometimes contribute to, a fellow guest argued that the debate over God's existence is best understood on the model of a criminal trial. On his telling, the person who asserts that God exists occupies the position of the prosecution: the claim is theirs, the burden of proof is therefore theirs, and because the case has never been made to anything like the standard a courtroom demands, the only defensible verdict is the equivalent of "not guilty" — which he glossed as atheism being the rational default. Another guest pushed back from a direction I hadn't heard before. She claimed the analogy would be more honest if we used the Scottish system, with its third verdict of "not proven," and read that verdict as agnosticism: the claim hasn't been established, but it would go too far to say its falsehood has. The host then turned to me, as the resident explainer of arguments, and asked whether the courtroom picture is any good at all or whether it is quietly doing all the work.

I want to think it through carefully before I write a column on the exchange. Please audit the courtroom analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence — what is supposed to play the role of the prosecution, the defendant, the judge or jury, the standard of proof, the presumption of innocence, and the verdict — and then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know which features of a criminal trial the analogy quietly imports that a debate about God's existence lacks: for example, the special reasons a legal system has for protecting the accused against wrongful punishment, the difference between a procedural default and a belief about the world, and the asymmetric costs of the two possible errors. Then say whether the original conclusions — that the burden of proof sits with the theist, and that the rational default is atheism rather than agnosticism — survive once the disanalogies are on the table. If the three-verdict, "not proven" reading is the more faithful mapping, defend that choice; if the whole courtroom frame should be abandoned for some other picture, propose one and show how it fixes what the legal analogy gets wrong. I would rather you commit to a definite position and reason for it than lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
