You're an expert on Bernard Williams's moral philosophy, especially his integrity objection and the one-thought-too-many idea. Think slowly and brainstorm candidate principles before settling. I have a grad-school friend with sharp, consistant-sounding moral verdicts, and I want to predict what she'll say about some new cases before our seminar. I'm less interested in your own views than in reverse-engineering hers from her track record.

Her verdicts so far.

1. Trolley cases. Switch: she says you have to flip the switch to save the five, and it would be wrong not to. Footbridge: she says you can't push the large person, even though five will die.

2. Soldiers and villagers. A visiting engineer is captured by soldiers who will shoot nineteen villagers unless he personally shoots one of them dead in front of the rest. She says shooting the one is allowed but not required; if he refuses, the refusal isn't wrong, and nobody gets to demand that he shoot.

3. The chemist. A chemist is offered a job in a weapons lab, working on a chemical agent program he finds repugnant. If he turns it down, his family faces serious hardship, and the lab will just hire someone who does the work more enthusiastically. She says he should turn it down anyway; taking the job would be a self-betrayal, whatever the consequences.

4. Drowning. A man can save either his wife or two strangers; equal risk, all three equally reachable. She says of course he saves his wife. She also insists that if he had paused to work out whether he was allowed to save her, the pause itself would be a failure—not because the rescue was wrong, but because the very need for that justification would be one thought too many.

5. The paramedic. A paramedic arrives at a crash with two critical victims: his own teenage son and a stranger the same age, with comparable injuries. She says he shouldn't treat his son first just because he's his son. Once you put on the uniform, she says, you take on a duty to triage as you'd triage any two kids; same goes for a judge and his own daughter.

6. Kidney. She thinks a healthy person who won't donate a kidney to a stranger who'll die without one does nothing wrong, though she admires someone who donates out of genuine care. A donor whose only real motive is abstract duty, with no particular care for the recipient, strikes her as missing something.

New cases.

(a) A lifeguard is on duty alone at a beach when two swimmers get caught in a rip. One is a child she's never met; the other is her ten-year-old daughter. The surf and timing mean she can reliably reach only one. Would my friend say the lifeguard is required, permitted, or forbidden to go for her daughter first? And would she think the lifeguard should even pause to think about the question?

(b) A woman saves her wife from a house fire rather than two neighbors she could have saved instead. The next day a friend asks whether that was really permissible. The woman spends the evening building an impartial consequentialist case that saving her wife had the best consequences after all. Does my friend think anything is wrong in the initial rescue, in the evening of justification, both, or neither?

I'd like from you: the two or three best-fitting rules that explain all six of her prior verdicts, stated as explicitly as possible; what each rule predicts for (a) and (b), and where they come apart; your best guess about the rule she's actually running, with reasons; and one further case, not on my list, that would cleanly separate whichever candidates are left standing.
