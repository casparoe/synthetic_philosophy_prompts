I have been teaching Nozick's experience machine argument in a course on applied ethics, and a student pressed a version of the case that seems to me to change its shape in a philosophically useful way. In the standard case, one asks whether you would choose to plug yourself into a machine that would give you a lifetime of subjectively perfect but entirely simulated experiences, with no memory of having made the choice. The familiar line is that many would refuse, for three broad reasons: we want to do things rather than merely to experience doing them; we want to be a certain kind of person rather than a passive receptacle of pleasant states; and we want our lives to be in contact with a reality deeper than human artifice.

Now alter the case as follows. You live in a world in which whole-brain emulation is routine. You are asked to authorise the creation of a faithful, conscious emulation of yourself—a distinct person, run on computational hardware—whose entire life will take place inside a simulated environment. The simulation will be as rich and as subjectively real as the ordinary world, and the emulation will never learn that it is simulated, will never act on the world outside the simulation, and will never encounter another mind. You, the biological original, will continue your ordinary life whether or not the emulation is created. Suppose you are among those who would refuse, on the three familiar grounds, to plug into the experience machine yourself.

Does the experience machine argument survive this change? Specifically, if you would refuse to plug yourself in, are you required, on pain of inconsistency, to refuse to create the emulation—or do some or all of the three standard considerations lose their force when the life at stake is not yours but a copy's? Address in particular the third consideration, the value of contact with reality: does a simulated life like the emulation's count as deficient in the way the standard argument assumes, even though the deficiency is one the emulation can never detect? Give a determinate account of which of the original verdicts survive the emulation variant and what that shows about which premise was doing the real work.
