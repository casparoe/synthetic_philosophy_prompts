I am a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun reading philosophy, and I have been trying to determine what makes a ground of discrimination suspect in the way that sex, race, religion, and disability are. I have collected my own verdicts on particular cases in the hope that a principle will fall out, rather as one might look for the factor that orders a messy set of data.

(1) A freight company refuses to hire women for loading-dock work because women are, on average, less able to lift heavy loads. I find this suspect even though the statistical claim is true. (2) An airline imposes a mandatory retirement age of 65 on pilots for safety. I find this not suspect. (3) A landlord refuses to rent to families with young children. I find this suspect. (4) A health insurer charges smokers higher premiums. I find this not suspect. (5) An employer screens out every applicant with any felony conviction, however old and unrelated to the work. I find this suspect, and I notice that this sits badly with my verdict on smokers, since both involve conduct the person chose. (6) An employer refuses to hire an applicant because a genetic test shows a mutation for a serious late-onset disease, although the applicant is currently healthy. I find this suspect. (7) A research institute restricts entry-level posts to holders of doctorates from accredited universities. I find this not suspect.

Can you state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits these seven verdicts? Please be precise about the role of immutability, choice, statistical relevance to the task, and the social meaning or history of the ground. Then tell me which of my verdicts that principle forces me to abandon, and what it says about two cases I have not settled: different retirement ages for men and women, and a political party's refusal to hire canvassers who belong to other parties.
