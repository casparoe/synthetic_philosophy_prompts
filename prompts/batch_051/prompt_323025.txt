I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I am currently drafting a book about a man caught inside a legal proceeding whose governing rules no one can state. That project has sent me back to Kafka's *The Trial*, and I have a growing suspicion that the usual existential and theological readings of the novel miss something more specifically philosophical. Josef K. is arrested without being told what he has done; every attempt he makes to discover what the Law requires yields only another official's interpretation, another advocate's opinion, or another story, and yet the proceedings never cease to bind him. This reminds me of the sceptical problem Kripke finds in Wittgenstein's remarks on following a rule: the thought that no fact about a speaker — not past applications, not dispositions, not any mental image — determines which rule he meant by a word like 'plus', and that what counts as going on in the same way is fixed, if at all, only by shared practice or agreement within a community rather than by anything in the individual's head.

I would like a careful reading of *The Trial* through that rule-following lens, written for someone who knows the novel well and can follow philosophical argument but wants neither summary nor a survey. Where does the parallel genuinely illuminate the book, and where does it strain? Which episodes would you point to — the arrest, the first interrogation, the advocate's endless submissions, the painter's account of the Court, the parable of the man before the Law — and what exactly does each show about whether there is a fact of the matter as to what the Law demands of K.? Does Kafka's Court resemble the kind of community whose shared practice could constitute the Law's requirements, or is it closer to a community that agrees only that the Law must be obeyed while agreeing on nothing about its content? Is K. best understood as the isolated individual demanding a private fact to settle what the rule requires of him, or would that be a distortion of what he actually wants?

Be candid about the disanalogies as well. Kripkenstein's problem concerns the apparently innocuous case of a finite speaker meaning addition by 'plus', whereas Kafka's Law concerns legal authority, guilt, shame, and perhaps something like original sin or bureaucratic power; the novel may be doing something that the semantic problem is not fitted to explain. I want a reading that does not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory and does not bend the philosophy to fit the book, and that ends by saying what *The Trial*, read this way, adds to or complicates in the philosophical picture that argument alone might miss. There is no need to quote from the novel, and please write the answer in British English.
