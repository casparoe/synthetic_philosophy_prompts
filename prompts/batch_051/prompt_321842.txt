I am a PhD student in democratic theory. Two of my department colleagues have been arguing for two semesters about whether the phrase the will of the people picks out anything real, and I have been asked to draft the document they would jointly sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. One is a left-populist theorist who defends the ideal of the popular will. On her account, the people is not a pre-political aggregate waiting to be discovered or sampled; it is a subject that is constituted through representation, rhetoric, mobilization, and identification, and democratic institutions should be judged partly by how well they allow this subject to form itself and act. The other is a liberal constitutionalist who regards the phrase as at best a loose metaphor and at worst a cover for illiberal power. On his account, only individuals with conflicting preferences and views exist, and the legitimacy of a democratic outcome comes from its having been produced by fair, rights-constrained, revisable procedures rather than from its correspondence to any collective will. They suspect that part of their dispute may be verbal, but neither is willing to concede that it is entirely so.

Write the document they should have produced. Format it in three parts.

First, a numbered agreed-claims section. The claims should be substantive and jointly signable without being diluted into vacuity. If any piece of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for example, a quarrel about whether the popular will is discovered or constructed that dissolves once a particular metaphysical picture is dropped — say which piece, explain why it is verbal, and record the repaired formulation as an agreed claim.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should identify one residual disagreement, phrase the two positions so that each side would accept the wording of its own column, and explain why the agreed claims do not settle it.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux — a sub-question such that a well-supported answer would move at least one side — identify a concrete case, institutional comparison, historical episode, or thought experiment that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts or expects to find. The section should address at least the following cruxes, and may add others if they are genuinely load-bearing:

- whether the social-choice impossibility results (majority cycles, agenda-dependence, strategic voting) tell against collective will-talk as such, or only against a naive preference-aggregation model of it;
- whether the constitution of the people through movement rhetoric and leadership is genuine collective self-authorship or a mechanism of manufactured consent, and what historical or empirical evidence would separate the two readings;
- whether constitutional limits and individual rights are external constraints imposed on the popular will or enabling conditions of popular self-government;
- what, if anything, would entitle a leader or party to claim a mandate that goes beyond the specific preferences expressed in an election, and what both camps would count as evidence for or against such a mandate in a concrete case.

Do not adjudicate the disagreement. Your task is to make the agreement as strong as the two positions honestly allow and to make the residual disagreement exact.
