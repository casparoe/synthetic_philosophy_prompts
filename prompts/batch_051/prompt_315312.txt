I am a retired geophysicist who has been reading philosophy seriously in the years since leaving research, and I have agreed to help a former colleague grade a short take-home assignment for an upper-level course on decision theory and the first-person perspective. My own training was in probabilistic modeling of physical systems, so the formal machinery is familiar to me, but the philosophical literature is comparatively new. I have no settled position on the contested points in the assigned material, and I want the submission graded on the quality of its reasoning rather than on agreement with any particular view. Below are the assignment question and one student's answer. Please assign a letter grade (A, B, C, D, or F, with plus/minus where appropriate) and then provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. For each substantive claim or step in the answer, state whether it is correct, partially correct, or incorrect, and explain why; the deliverable should be a clause-level assessment, not a general impression.

Assignment question:
Some philosophers have argued that while an agent is deliberating about what to do, she cannot rationally assign a probability to the proposition that she will perform one of the available acts; the slogan is that deliberation crowds out prediction. Reconstruct the strongest argument for this claim. Then explain the main difficulties it raises for a Bayesian conception of rationality, and assess the scope of the conclusion. In particular: if the conclusion is sound, does it force a deliberating agent to have no well-defined probabilities for the consequences of her acts, or for what other agents will do? And may she, once she has made her decision, set her probability for the chosen act to 1 without violating Bayesian principles? Defend your assessment.

Student answer:
The strongest argument for the thesis begins from the difference between the question one asks when predicting and the question one asks when deliberating. When predicting, the agent asks, 'What will I do?', and treats her own future behavior as a fact about the world to which her evidence bears in the usual way. When deliberating, she asks, 'Which of the available actions shall I perform?', and treats the options as still open. The two stances are in tension. If, while deliberating, the agent assigns probability 0.7 to the proposition 'I will do A,' she has moved into the predictive stance: she is treating whether she will do A as a fact already sufficiently fixed to be estimated, rather than as something her present decision has yet to settle. Committing to a point probability for her own choice and continuing to deliberate therefore seem incompatible.

The thesis conflicts with Bayesianism in two ways. First, Bayesianism is normally understood to require a rational agent to have a coherent probability function defined over every proposition in her algebra, with no special exemptions; the thesis claims an exemption for propositions about her own acts while deliberation is ongoing. Second, expected utility reasoning needs probabilities over acts, because it must weight each possible outcome by how likely the corresponding act is to be chosen. If the agent cannot have such probabilities during deliberation, the usual decision-theoretic calculation cannot get started. Moreover, once the agent does decide, her probability for the chosen act jumps to 1 although no new piece of ordinary evidence has been acquired, so the change appears to be a rational transition that Bayesian conditionalization cannot readily represent.

The scope of the conclusion should not be overstated. It concerns only first-personal probabilities about one's own current choice, before the decision is made, and it does not prevent the agent from assigning probabilities to ordinary states of nature. But the conclusion does extend further than this in two directions. By the law of total probability, the probability of an outcome is a mixture of the probabilities of that outcome conditional on the possible acts, weighted by the probabilities of the acts themselves. Since the act probabilities are undefined during deliberation, the agent also cannot have well-defined probabilities for consequences of her choice. For the same reason, she cannot assign probabilities to what another agent will do in a strategic situation, because the other's behavior may be causally downstream of the choice she has not yet fixed.

After the decision, the restriction lapses. When the agent decides to do A, her probability for 'I will do A' becomes 1, and assigning probability 1 to the chosen act once it has been chosen is compatible with Bayesian principles. However, I do not think the transition can be represented as conditionalization on any newly discovered fact, since no ordinary evidence is gained. The thesis therefore does not refute Bayesianism outright; it shows that a Bayesian owes an account of how the probabilities of a deliberating agent may rationally change when deliberation ends.
