I am a novelist, not an economist or a philosopher, and my current book has driven me into a theoretical corner. The novel is set in a near-future economy in which advanced automation has displaced most human labor in manufacturing, logistics, and even a good deal of professional work, and the plot turns on whether the resulting precariousness should be blamed on the technology itself or on the social arrangements that deploy it.

Writing one of the central characters, a Marxist political economist, has forced me to inhabit a framework I find difficult to abandon. On this framework, the value of a commodity is the socially necessary labor time required to produce it. Machinery is itself the product of past labor, and it merely transfers that past labor's value to the product bit by bit; new value is created only by living labor. Profit is drawn from the difference between the value living labor creates and the wage it is paid. The view is clean, yields a single measure, and explains elegantly why a labor-saving machine can lower the value of each commodity even while physical output soars. But it also seems to imply that as automation advances, the living labor from which new value must be drawn becomes a shrinking portion of production; each machine-made good embodies less new value, and the source of profit is put under increasing strain. The economy that looks most productive in the everyday sense would, on the theory's own terms, be systematically undermining the creation of the only thing the theory is prepared to count as value.

Against this stands an intuition I cannot dislodge. A fully automated plant that turns raw materials into goods people need is productive if anything is: it creates wealth, satisfies wants, and yields incomes. The claim that it creates no new value because no living human hand touched the product strikes me, at the level of my gut, as an artifact of the theory—almost a pun on the word 'productive.' And yet, when I try to locate the flaw in the labor-time account, I cannot; the reasoning seems to absorb every objection by drawing another distinction.

I am not asking for a verdict in favor of one side. I want to know what it would actually cost to abandon each side, and whether there is a negotiated reading on which both can survive. In particular: is the intuition using 'value' in a sense the theory is entitled to reject, and if the reconciliation is that machines create use-value and wealth but not value in the technical sense, does that save the intuition or merely define it away? What evidence or argument would genuinely damage the labor-time picture here, rather than giving it another distinction to absorb? What would debunk the intuition, rather than merely paraphrasing it in weaker language? And if the theory must be amended so that machine production can create value, how much of the original framework survives the amendment?
