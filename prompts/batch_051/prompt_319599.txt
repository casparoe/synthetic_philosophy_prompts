For a feature I'm writing on how scientific status gets assigned to unorthodox research programs, I've been using a self-imposed checklist to keep my judgments honest. A research program deserves to be called scientific to the extent that (1) its central claims are precise enough to be incompatible with at least some observable outcomes; (2) it offers a unifying mechanism or explanation rather than just redescription; (3) it has generated successful predictions or retrodictions that were not built into its starting assumptions; (4) it has a recognized procedure by which its central commitments could be revised or abandoned; and (5) its core concepts are anchored in observable phenomena rather than floating as metaphors.

The borderline case I want to run through this checklist is Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science and the research program around it. That program involves the systematic exploration of simple computational rules, especially elementary cellular automata; the claim that extremely simple rules can generate behavior that is irreducibly complex for practical purposes; and the principle of computational equivalence, roughly the thesis that beyond a very low threshold, systems that are not obviously simple are all equivalent in computational sophistication. Wolfram presents this as a foundational reorientation of science rather than a niche computer experiment, with consequences for fundamental physics, fluid dynamics, biology, and the scope of explanation.

Under the checklist above, should this research program be treated as a science? Work through the criteria one by one. Where the program clearly meets or clearly fails a criterion, say so; where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say what sort of evidence or clarification would push the case one way or the other. Finally, tell me whether the difficulty of the borderline case lies in the checklist, in the program, or in both, and whether the checklist itself needs amending.
