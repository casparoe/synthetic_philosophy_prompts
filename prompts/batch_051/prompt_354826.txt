I want the specification before I read any more fixes for the problem of old evidence. What would an adequate Bayesian (or Bayesian-adjacent) account of confirmation have to get right about evidence that was already known when the hypothesis was proposed? I would like the full desiderata list, each with the case or consideration motivating it, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp.

Take Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession as the running case: it was observed long before general relativity, yet deriving it from the theory is usually treated as confirmation, while a theory patched together after the fact to fit the same data is usually not. A workable account should at minimum say something principled about: how old evidence can confirm at all when its probability is already 1; what separates genuine confirmation from accommodation and ad hoc curve-fitting; why, or whether, novel prediction should count for more than post hoc derivation; whose credence function does the work once the evidence is already absorbed; how any old-new asymmetry squares with the order-independence of Bayesian updating; and which idealizations it is and is not willing to relax, among logical omniscience, conditionalization, and the definiteness of counterfactual priors. For each requirement, give the consideration that forces it.

Then flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied as stated, and say what that incompatibility forces a theory to concede. End by isolating the one requirement that does the most eliminative work against the proposals I am likely to meet, stated precisely enough to actually rule candidates out rather than merely gesture at them.
