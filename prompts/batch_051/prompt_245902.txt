I'm an MA student in music theory finishing up an independent study on Schopenhauer, and I've been staring at the music chapter in The World as Will and Idea. There's a passage in Book III where he says music never depicts a particular joy or sorrow, but gives the pure form of the affects, the joy of the thing without the thing. I've been trying to pin down what kind of psychological state that would be. When I hear the last movement of a major-key symphony, I feel something a lot like elation, but it isn't elation about anything in my life; when I hear a slow minor passage, it can feel like grief or resignation but with no object, no person lost, no situation gone wrong. It isn't simply being in a "mood," because moods usually have causes and spill over into how I see everything, whereas this seems locked to the music for exactly as long as the music lasts. But it also isn't just seeing the music as expressing a person's emotion, because no one in particular is doing the expressing.

My question: is there an established name in aesthetics or philosophy of mind for this configuration—an affective state that is qualitatively like an emotion but lacks an intentional object, and is elicited by something nonconceptual like music? I know there is work on mood, on musical expressiveness, on hypothetical emotional states and the paradox of fiction, but the thing I'm after is narrower: Schopenhauer's claim that music gives the will's movements themselves, before they are split into objects. Has that claim been given a technical label, and if so, what distinguishes it from those neighboring concepts? If not, what would you call it and where is the boundary between it and an ordinary object-directed emotion? Keep response max 450 words.
