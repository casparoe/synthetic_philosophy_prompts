Yesterday a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about a thesis he has begun pushing in his research group: that process-based supervision of AI systems is not a genuine alternative to outcome-based supervision, but only outcome-based supervision with the "outcome" moved inside the episode. By process-based supervision he means rewarding or critiquing the intermediate steps of a model's reasoning; by outcome-based supervision he means rewarding or critiquing only the final output. I distrust the conclusion, but I could not make my objection precise. I reconstructed his argument as follows:

(1) Process-based supervision, unlike outcome-based supervision, attaches a reward or critique to at least one intermediate step.
(2) To attach a reward or critique to an intermediate step, one needs a criterion that makes that step count as better or worse.
(3) Any such criterion must come either from an estimate of the step's contribution to successful final outputs, or from a judgment about the step by something other than the final output — an evaluator, a verifier, or a formal rule whose choice someone endorses.
(4) Judgments of the second kind are trusted only because, and to the extent that, they predict successful final outputs; if an evaluator's step-level verdicts stopped predicting final success, we would replace the evaluator rather than revise our ultimate target.
(5) Therefore every process criterion is either an outcome criterion outright or a proxy for one.
(6) Therefore process-based supervision just is outcome-based supervision applied at intermediate nodes, and the dispute between process-based and outcome-based methods is only about implementation cost and label availability, not about principle.

Is this argument valid? If not, which is the first step that fails, and does it fail because a premise is false, because a term shifts its meaning between steps, or because an inference is invalid?
