Some of us in a reading group have been reconstructing an argument from social choice theory, and we have ended up with something that looks far stronger than any of us actually believes—strong enough that I keep suspecting one of the premises is quietly doing all the work. I want a precise diagnosis rather than a survey.

Here is the argument, as cleanly as we can state it. (1) Each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each individual i can be represented by a cardinal utility function u_i. (2) A hypothetical impartial observer choosing among the same lotteries also has preferences satisfying the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so the observer can be represented by a utility function W. (3) The observer's preferences respect Pareto: if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, then the observer is indifferent between them; and if every individual weakly prefers one lottery to the other, then the observer weakly prefers it. (4) Therefore, up to the usual affine rescaling of von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, W must be a weighted sum W = a_1 u_1 + ... + a_n u_n (with a_i > 0 under a strict Pareto condition)—which looks like weighted utilitarianism.

That conclusion seems to derive interpersonal aggregation from nothing but individual rationality plus a Pareto condition, and yet many people accept expected utility theory and Pareto while rejecting utilitarianism, including weighted utilitarianism. Which premise or inference should be rejected, and why did that step look innocent? Then price the main alternatives: denying that the social observer's preferences must satisfy the expected utility axioms, weakening or reinterpreting the Pareto condition, separating ex ante from ex post evaluation, rejecting the impartial-observer framing as an illicit modeling assumption, and biting the bullet. Please answer in American English, and after your diagnosis make the final line exactly \answer{...}, where the brackets contain the single step or premise you take to be the weakest link.
