Since retiring from experimental physics, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep encountering a pattern in ordinary moral argument that I do not know how to file. People will often treat pleasures and pains as if they can be traded against one another in a roughly arithmetical way: a small risk of a serious harm can be accepted for a sufficiently large certain benefit, a mild discomfort imposed on one person can be outweighed by many mild benefits to others, and so on. But when the suffering in question is described as especially severe—say, prolonged agony, or the suffering of a creature whose pain is made vivid—these same people refuse to allow any amount of mild pleasure, any number of small benefits, or any ordinary prudential gain to count as an offsetting consideration. They speak as though beyond some level suffering simply cannot be outweighed, though they rarely specify in advance where that level lies, and the threshold they insist upon in one example is not the one they enforce in the next, especially if the example is less vivid.

I am trying to determine whether this is a recognized position, or at least a recognized failure mode. The nearest concepts I know are ordinary diminishing marginal value and deontological side-constraints, but neither seems to capture the phenomenon exactly: diminishing marginal value still permits trade-offs in principle, just at a diminishing rate, while deontological prohibitions usually attach to action types rather than to experienced states. What I am describing is a judgment that certain experiential states cannot be outweighed by other experiential states, with the boundary between tolerable and intolerable suffering left vague and apparently context-responsive.

Is there an established name for this pattern, or for the family of views that try to make it coherent? If there is, can you state the doctrine precisely enough that one could test whether a given trade-off falls inside or outside it? I have encountered disagreements in which one side treats the position as obviously principled and the other as obviously confused, and I would like to know what it would take for it to be more than an unstable set of intuitions. I would rather you take a definite position on what the phenomenon is and what it is not than lay out several candidate labels and leave me to choose.
