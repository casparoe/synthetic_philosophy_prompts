My flatmate's gone down a systems-change rabbit hole and came back with an argument I can't shake off. He says individual do-gooding is a wash, but then the argument turns round and seems to eat the very systems-talk he prefers. It goes like this.

(1) An act does good only if the world with the act is better than the world without the act.

(2) For almost any marginal act you actually perform — a donation, a fair-trade purchase, joining a march — the world without it would have been the same. The appeal would have hit its budget anyway; the shop would have stocked the same goods anyway; the march would have had the same numbers, give or take one.

(3) So almost all individual marginal acts do no good.

(4) But the same applies to the acts meant to change the system. Voting, joining a union, chipping in to a lobbying push, going to a climate march: for each, the world without your individual act would have been the same, except in the vanishingly rare case where you happen to be the precise threshold.

(5) And a vanishingly rare chance of being the threshold means the act's expected payoff is basically nothing.

(6) So "do systemic change rather than marginal charity" isn't a way out. On this reasoning there is no act that does any good, which is absurd.

He thinks (2) is doing the work, because it holds everyone else's choices fixed and then declares your contribution invisible. I've got a nagging feeling something slides between (3) and (5), but I can't make it stick. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and which fix costs the least? Answer in British English, please.
