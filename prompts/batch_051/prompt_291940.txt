I sit on a clinical ethics committee reviewing a critical-care triage policy our hospital group adopted before implanted cardiac devices became common. The relevant clause reads: "Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment shall not be considered the intentional ending of a patient's life. A clinician who discontinues such treatment in accordance with the patient's wishes or the decision of a duly authorized surrogate shall not be deemed to have caused the patient's death. No clinician may administer any drug, perform any procedure, or take any other action with the primary purpose of ending a patient's life." The drafters clearly had ventilators, dialysis, artificial nutrition, and pressors in mind, and they wanted to distinguish stopping those supports from lethal injection.

We now face a case the clause does not seem to have contemplated. A patient with end-stage heart failure is not a candidate for transplant or a ventricular assist device. He has an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator that repeatedly shocks him for ventricular arrhythmias. The shocks are painful and will not reverse his underlying disease; the cardiologists expect that, if the device is left on, he will die during one of many episodes and the device will not save him. His surrogate asks that the defibrillator be deactivated. Deactivation does not require surgery or drugs: a nurse places a magnet over the chest. Once it is off, the next fatal arrhythmia will produce death within minutes. The committee is split. Some say deactivation is simply withdrawing life-sustaining treatment and therefore is not an intentional ending of life under the first sentence. Others say it is an act done with the purpose of ending the patient's life under the third sentence, because the clinician is deliberately disabling a functioning protective device and death follows from that disabling rather than from any shift in the underlying disease.

I want this pressed philosophically, not just as a matter of hospital policy. Does the clause's distinction between withdrawing treatment and acting to end life track the doing/allowing or killing/letting die distinction, or has the drafters' assumption broken down once the "treatment" is an intermittently operating implanted device whose deactivation is a one-off intervention? Give me the strongest reading under the text, the purpose, and what the drafters would probably have said if asked, and tell me which approach should control here and why. My suspicion is that the clause treats a few standard intensive-care technologies as if they exhausted the moral topography, and this case exposes the cracks.
