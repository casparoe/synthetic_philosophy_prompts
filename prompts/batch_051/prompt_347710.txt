I'm a retired physicist—spent my career in spectroscopy, so philosophy of mind is pretty new to me. Lately I've been working through Turing's 1950 paper in Mind, Computing Machinery and Intelligence. There's a section where he considers the objection that machines can't think because they can't feel things: they can't enjoy strawberries and cream, fall in love, make mistakes, and so on. His reply, as I understand it, is that we never directly observe another person's consciousness; we infer it entirely from their outward behavior and speech. So if a machine behaved indistinguishably from a person, withholding thought from the machine would force us to doubt that other people think too.

I've been trying to turn that move into a clean argument that the brain is a computer, and I keep getting stuck between thinking I've just proven something and thinking I'm being tricked by my own steps. Here's the argument:

(1) The only evidence we ever have that another human thinks is their externally observable behavior—what they say and do.
(2) If a computer produced external behavior indistinguishable from a human's, we would have exactly the same evidence that it thinks as we have for any human.
(3) Rationality requires treating cases alike when the evidence is the same.
(4) So, if a computer produced behavior indistinguishable from a human's, rationality would require attributing thought to it.
(5) The human brain is a physical system, and its external behavior is caused by physical processes in the brain.
(6) Any physical process can in principle be simulated by a computer.
(7) So a computer could in principle produce external behavior indistinguishible from a human brain's behavior.
(8) By (4), rationality would require attributing thought to such a computer.
(9) But if a computer could think just by simulating the brain's physical processes, then the brain's own thinking is nothing over and above those physical processes being simulated.
(10) Therefore, the brain is a computer.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails and why? I half-suspect step (9) is doing something sneaky, sliding from what we'd have to believe to what is actually true, but I can't articulate the fault precisely. Or maybe the argument is fine and I just don't like the conclusion. Be specific about the step and the reading of it.
