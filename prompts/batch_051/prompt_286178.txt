Act as a professional philosopher of mind and cognitive science who works on the relation between competence and comprehension. I have just come home from a long argument with a friend about whether a system has to understand what it is doing in order to do it well. We went in circles because we each smuggled in a different test case: he kept returning to contemporary language models, and I kept returning to the fact that so much of the living world is impressively competent with no comprehension behind it. Before I continue the argument, I want to stop and fix the standard of evaluation itself.

Please write me the desiderata that an adequate theory of comprehension must meet. By a theory of comprehension I mean a theory of what it is for a system to understand what it is doing, and how understanding relates to the capacity to do it. The theory needs to be strong enough to make sense of two strange inversions of reasoning, and honest enough not to wave either of them away. Darwin's inversion: apparent design — the look of foresight, planning, and purpose — can be produced by uncomprehending processes such as natural selection, with no designer, no foresight, and no representation of the outcome. Turing's inversion: apparent intelligent performance — calculating, translating, playing games, predicting — can in principle be produced by uncomprehending mechanical processes that execute rule-governed operations without understanding what they are about.

I want the full specification before I evaluate any particular view on the argument. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. In particular, make sure the list:

1. distinguishes the contrasts a theory cannot collapse: competence vs. comprehension; original vs. derived; performing skillfully vs. grasping why the performance works; representing a result vs. merely producing it; being caused to emit an output vs. being guided by an understanding of the domain;

2. is precise about the hard cases, including a pocket calculator, a spider building a web, a migrating bird, a neural network that plays Go, a next-token predictor trained on human text, a child learning a new word, a person solving a novel problem, and a person following a rule by rote — saying which are cases of comprehension and why, without merely stipulating a cutoff;

3. faces these questions squarely: Is comprehension a threshold, a graded property, or a bundle of capacities? Does it require consciousness or phenomenal character? Does it require semantic grounding, or can syntactic competence with the right causal contact suffice? Does it require the capacity to give reasons, explanations, or justifications? Is it a fact about the system's history and origins, or only about its current competence? The list should say which of these questions an adequate theory must answer and which it may legitimately set aside;

4. remains consistent with the well-established results behind the two inversions: that natural selection can produce functional complexity without representation or foresight, and that finite mechanical processes can match formal competence at well-defined tasks — a theory that simply denies either one has failed the basic test;

5. separates genuine desiderata from the preferences of particular camps. For example, some demand that comprehension be behaviorally recognizable, others that it be intrinsic to the system, others that it require consciousness: mark which of these are real constraints and which are philosophical preferences.

After the list, give me the two parts I most want. First, identify any pair or subset of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what the conflict is; this matters more to me than the scorecard. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most work in separating the serious candidate theories from the rest.

Then, in one short paragraph only, briefly say how a purely computational-functional view, a Searle-style view, and an intentional-stance view would fare against your list. Work from the content of the positions; no bibliography or citations are needed.

Start by brainstorming candidate requirements before pruning and organizing them, and think carefully about which conflicts are substantive rather than merely terminological.
