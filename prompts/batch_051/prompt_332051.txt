I serve on the program committee of a regional conservation trust, and I have been asked to draft the position memo on a proposal now before us. The proposal would fund the restoration of roughly two hundred acres of drained agricultural land into a brackish tidal marsh, the sort of project that usually wins an easy vote here: it would reestablish native vegetation, create nursery habitat for fish and crustaceans, draw migratory waterfowl, and within a few seasons support a dense food web of insects, frogs, voles, herons, and the raptors and foxes that follow them. The biodiversity case is strong, and the proposal's own monitoring plan projects a thirtyfold increase in juvenile fish abundance within four years.

Last night I spent a long evening arguing about the proposal with a friend, a wildlife biologist who used to sit on this committee. She pressed the worry about wild animal suffering that I had always kept at arm's length. Her point was that if you count the welfare of individual animals rather than the existence or diversity of populations, a successful restoration is not obviously a good thing: it manufactures an enormous number of short, difficult lives, since most of the offspring produced each season die before maturity from starvation, disease, exposure, injury, or predation, and many of those deaths are painful or frightening. On her view, the marsh would be, in expectation, a net addition of suffering, and the healthy species lists that impress our committee would be a measure of how much suffering we had created rather than how much good we had done. She was not arguing for bulldozing anything; she meant only that the committee cannot take it for granted that more wild animals, living as wild animals actually live, is an improvement. I came away less sure of a yes vote than I had been, though I am not convinced her conclusion follows.

The committee meets in two weeks, and the memo I draft will frame the deliberation. Please write the memo. It should recommend one of three actions: fund the restoration as proposed, fund it only with specified modifications meant to change the welfare calculus, or decline to fund it. The recommendation must be one the committee can act on, with reasoning behind it, and it must treat the strongest version of the welfare objection honestly rather than explaining it away. It should also say which empirical findings, if they came out a particular way after monitoring, would flip the recommendation. Our charter permits the committee to weigh animal welfare alongside biodiversity but does not specify how to weight them; the memo should acknowledge that gap and still take a definite position.
