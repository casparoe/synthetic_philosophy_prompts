Over the break my friend -- who's doing a masters in legal history -- kept insisting that the modern state is basically a corporation, and that we'd all be less confused about politics if we just said so. His move is to point at the old chartered trading companies, the East India Company and its Dutch cousin: they minted coins, signed treaties, waged wars, ran their own courts, and governed territory, all while being, legally, joint-stock companies owned by shareholders. His punchline is that the line we draw between 'public government' and 'private firm' is mostly a nineteenth-century cleanup job rather than a deep conceptual difference, and so things like private arbitration or corporate lobbying shouldn't bother us -- they're just governance functions being handled by the bodies that always handled them, under different labels.

I keep feeling the analogy is doing to much work, but I can't quite say where it snaps. Can you audit it for me? Give me the correspondence table -- charter/constitution, shareholders/citizens, board/cabinet, dividends/public services, liquidation/dissolution, whatever else belongs -- then walk through the places where the mapping breaks or quietly imports features the target lacks. What I actually want to know is whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. And if you think there's a better analogy for what the state actually is, I'd take that too.
