For an epistemology of math seminar I'm in, each of us has to turn in a one-page "where I stand" statement, and then the rest of the group gets to poke holes in it. I wrote mine last night, and now I've got a low-level worry that the commitments don't all hang together, but I've read the thing enough times that I can't tell weather the problem is real or I'm just spinning. Would you stress-test the set for me? I'd much rather find out from you than from my advisor.

Here they are, roughly as I wrote them:

1. I'm a platonist: numbers, sets, and functions exist mind-independently, they aren't in space or time, and they have no causal powers.
2. We have a great deal of mathematical knowledge; proofs in elementary number theory (the infinitude of the primes, say) are as secure as any belief I have.
3. I'm drawn to a causal constraint on knowledge: for a subject to know about some objects, there needs to be an appropriate causal connection between the subject's belief and those objects; without that, I don't see how the belief is really about them.
4. I don't believe in a special faculty of rational intuition that puts us in direct epistemic contact with abstract objects—that just names the mystery without dissolving it.
5. I'm convinced by the indispensability argument: our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects, and since we're justified in believing those theories, we're justified in believing those objects too.
6. I think mathematical truth is objective, but mathematical practice is partly conventional—which axioms get adopted and what counts as a proof are settled by communities, not by the objects alone.
7. I lean naturalist about knowledge in general: human cognitive capacities are evolved and physical, and a decent epistemological story shouldn't make our knowledge come out as a miracle.
8. Methodologically, I think mathematical practice gets the last word: if a philosophical theory implies that working mathematicians don't know the things they plainly do know, that's a strike against the theory, not against the mathematicians.

Am I holding genuinely incompatible views, or does the set just need some careful hedging? And if I do need to revise, where should I start?
