I'm trying to figure out what an alphabetic writing system is best understood as a theory of—how much of pronunciation and how much of word-level structure it should encode—and my judgments about cases won't settle. In one language, [tʰ] and [t] are in complementary distribution and are written with a single letter; I think that is right, and a script that distinguished them would be over-transcribing. In a second, word-final obstruents are devoiced, and the community writes their word for year as rot even though the inflected form roda reveals an underlying d; I think the script should write rod and let readers recover the surface form by rule. In a third, a suffix alternates predictably between [k] and [tʃ] according to the following vowel, and I approve of writing an invariant symbol for the suffix rather than the two surface forms. But in a fourth, a noun's plural is lexically either -s or -en with no phonological rule predicting the choice, and I think a single invariant plural sign would be a mistake. I also find I accept English spellings like sign/signal and bomb/bombard, which make morphological connections visible, though I would not introduce such spellings in a script being designed for a previously unwritten language. Is there one principle that captures all of these verdicts, and what does it predict about the case I keep going back and forth on: a language in which a once-productive rule voices final f to v in the plural across many old nouns, but the rule now has exceptions and no longer applies to new words? If no clean principle gets every one of my verdicts, which verdict should I revise, and why?
