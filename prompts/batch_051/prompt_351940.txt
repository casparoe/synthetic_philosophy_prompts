I am a programming-language researcher with a working knowledge of epistemology but no formal training in it, and I have been turning over the regress problem—the dispute between foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism about how chains of justification can legitimately terminate, loop, or continue forever. The three positions appear to map onto distinctions I use every day: well-founded recursion with base cases, mutually recursive definitions and coinductive structures, and productive infinite sequences such as lazy streams. I would like you to restate each of the three positions in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, treating a belief's justification as a kind of dependency structure that a finite cognitive process has to check or build. Foundationalism should come out as something like a well-founded derivation tree whose leaves are axioms requiring no further support; coherentism as a cyclic graph whose acceptability is a matter of global coherence rather than derivation from a root; infinitism as an infinite chain that is nonetheless productive—each link generated by a finite rule—rather than a futile regress.

After the translation, I want the residue report. For each position, say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in the vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in three pressure points. First, in a programming language a base case is simply stipulated or terminates by definition, whereas the foundationalist's stopping point—self-evidence, incorrigibility, or a non-doxastic justifier—is precisely the contested thing; does the translation quietly assume an answer to the very question the foundationalist must earn? Second, coherentism's requirement that a system of beliefs be mutually supporting is hard to separate from a circular dependency that type systems or termination checkers would reject; either the analogy needs a notion of "support" that is not merely causal, or the failure to translate shows that coherentism has not said clearly what support is. Third, infinitism is usually charged with requiring a finite mind to traverse an infinite chain; lazy evaluation suggests a finite process can be answerable for an infinite structure, but only because the consumer never needs the whole. Is the epistemic subject more like a consumer that needs the whole justification in view, and if so, what exactly does the analogy fail to carry? Finally, tell me whether the untranslatable residue points to something the epistemic debate is missing a precise formulation of, or to something my vocabulary cannot see—and whether any of the three positions is made more respectable by the mapping than commonly supposed.
