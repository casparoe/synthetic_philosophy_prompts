I'm a novelist, and for the last six weeks my writing group has been fighting about a manuscript where the narrator's brother dies and she then spends a long middle stretch unable to work, keep friendships alive, or really do anything except notice her own sadness. Half the group says the grief is doing real work in the book; the other half says it's two hundred pages of weather. When I asked the grief's defenders what work they meant, someone who reads a lot of psychology shut the discussion down with the line that sadness is information, as if that settled the matter. I could feel the slogan doing heavy lifting, but I couldnt get anybody to say exactly what the claim was: that the feeling itself carries a correct or incorrect message about the loss, that sad moods make a person see things more accurately, that sadness signals something to other people, that it is worth having because of what it prevents or enables? Those are different claims, and which one turns out true matters if I'm going to decide whether this character's grief is load-bearing or just long.

Treat the line that sadness is information the way you'd disassemble a proverb. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — about the content of the emotion, its function, its value, and about whether a life with no sadness at all would be worse off — and give each a verdict: trite, defensible but needs evidence, empirically shaky, false, or whatever fits. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually support the group's claim that the grief belongs in the book and is doing something, and which readings only sound like they support it. I know it's a little strange to want a table for a line from a writing-group fight, but it would help me see the readings side by side: give me a table with four columns — Reading (a short label), Precise claim, Verdict, and Does it support keeping the grief in the novel (yes/no/only with a qualification). After the table, give me one honest paragraph on what the person in my group most likely meant and what the strongest version of their point commits them to.
