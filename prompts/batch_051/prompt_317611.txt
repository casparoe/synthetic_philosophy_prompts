Here are some intuitions I have about fakes and originals, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. I'm a philosophy of art PhD student, so I've read the usual stuff, but my verdicts don't settle neatly into any one line.

(1) A fake Vermeer is admired for thirty years as a newly discovered work, then exposed. I think the canvas itself really loses something at exposure, not just it's market price or its reputation. Before, it stood inside Vermeer's oeuvre, in relation to the late interiors; afterwards it doesn't.

(2) If a machine produced a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a favourite painting, I wouldn't regard it as a second instance of the same painting, and I wouldn't cross town to see it. But if I learned I'd been looking at a duplicate of a destroyed original, I'd feel my experience had been hollowed out, though nothing I saw changed. I'm not sure whether that shows I care about the object or only about my own beliefs.

(3) A living artist has a fabricator make a painting to the artist's detailed specification; the artist signs it and exhibits it under their own name. I count it as an original by the artist. I'd count the fabricator's identical, unsigned version as a fake, even though the two are physically interchangeable.

(4) A digital work exists only as a code file; the artist prints ten copies in 2010 and twenty more from the same file in 2020. I feel the pull of treating the 2010 prints as the originals, but I can't see any property of the prints themselves that could make that true. My inclination is that the distinction is pure social convention and each print is equally original. But then I'm not sure why I'm so firm in (2).

(5) A sixteenth-century studio painting made by assistants under the direction and with the approval of a famous master, sold as master-and-workshop. I'll happily call it a work by the master for some purposes, but I'd never put it on the same footing as a fully autograph work. That seems to sit awkwardly next to (3), and I can't yet say why.

Is there one principle about what makes something an original, and what value originality has, that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I give up, and why? Please also say what your principle predicts about a further case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate.

Please separate what is genuinely settled from what is contested among people working on this, and don't present a contested view as the accepted one. And write in British English, please.
