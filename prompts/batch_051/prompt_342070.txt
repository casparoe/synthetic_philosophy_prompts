Here's something I want you to work through with me. In my democratic theory class, I teach a rough set of criteria for democratic backsliding, understood as different from a coup or a classic authoritarian takeover. The way I put it to students: first, the machinery of elections keeps running and stays the only acknowledged route to power, but the playing field is tilted in small, individually defensible steps — redistricting, campaign finance rules, voter registration rules, media ownership. Second, the institutions meant to check the government get captured or weakened by formally legal means rather than abolished by force — courts, election commissions, auditors, public broadcasters. Third, the opposition and civil society aren't banned; they're delegitimized and subjected to selective enforcement. And fourth, the process is incremental and early on revocable, so there is never a single moment you can point to as the break.

Now here's the borderline case I can't settle, and I'd like your honest read on whether it meets my own criteria. A wealthy consolidated parliamentary democracy with a genuinely free press and a strong record has been governed by the same center-right coalition for ten years. The coalition passed a law requiring NGOs that take more than a fifth of their money from abroad to register as foreign agents and submit to audits; replaced the public broadcaster's board through a perfectly legal parliamentary majority, after which it's evening news has become noticeably soft on the government, though three of the five most-watched channels still criticize it; appointed a loyal attorney general who opened corruption probes into a dozen opposition mayors and no ministers, though in most cases the evidence was real and two mayors were eventually convicted; tried to raise the retirement age for sitting constitutional court judges in a way that would force out four of nine, and after a referendum narrowly rejected the plan, settled for changing the court's case-allocation rules instead; and, after the opposition won the capital region by six hundred votes, the national election commission, staffed by government appointees, ordered a recount and invalidated three districts on technical registration problems, flipping the result. Yet the oppostion still holds a third of parliament and keeps winning local elections, and a few months ago the supreme administrative court reinstated two of those districts and ruled against the commission. Turnout is rising, and the last international observer report described the election as competitive but played on a tilted field.

So: by my four criteria, does this country count as a case of democratic backsliding, or is it just a ruthless but normal democracy? Go through the criteria one by one and say where the case lands on each, then tell me whether the close calls show the criteria are too loose, too strict, or doing real work. And answer the meta-question while you're at it: if two reasonable people applying these same criteria could genuinely disagree here, does that show the concept is more of a partisan label than a diagnostic category, or does it show something else? For your reply, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just continuous prose, like you're emailing me back.
