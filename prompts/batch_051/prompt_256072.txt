I have been trying to isolate the role that the risk of accidental or unauthorized use plays in nuclear deterrence, and I want to work through a clean counterfactual. Imagine that, starting tomorrow, every nuclear weapon on Earth is fitted with a tamper-proof safeguard that makes it physically impossible for a weapon to be launched or detonated except by a deliberate, authenticated order from the legitimate command authority of the state that possesses it. Accidental detonations, unauthorized launches by rogue officers, and launches triggered by false alarms from early-warning systems are eliminated; the safeguard cannot be bypassed or spoofed; and every government and relevant military command knows and believes this with certainty. Nothing else about the nuclear world changes: arsenals, delivery systems, command structures, alliances, and the technical capacity to launch a deliberate first or second strike all remain as they are.

Give a reasoned, mechanism-by-mechanism prediction of how this alteration reshapes crisis bargaining, brinkmanship, extended deterrence guarantees, and incentives to acquire nuclear weapons over the following twenty years. In particular, address whose threats become more or less credible; whether removing the possibility of an accidental slide into war makes standoffs safer, or merely makes escalation feel safer and therefore more tempting; and how the change affects the credibility of extended nuclear umbrellas. The answer should be an argued analysis rather than a story, and it should mark clearly which predictions you regard as robust, which depend on contestable assumptions about how leaders and publics reason under risk, and what would have to be false for your central prediction to fail.
