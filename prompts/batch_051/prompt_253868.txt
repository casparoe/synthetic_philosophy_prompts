I'm a PhD student in cognitive psychology, and at our department happy hour last week I ended up listening to two people I know go at each other over whether the endowment effect is a bias or a legitimate preference. One is in a judgment-and-decision-making lab; the other is a philosophy PhD who sits in on our seminar. I mostly just listened, and I've been replaying it ever since. Here's the argument as I remember it.

(1) The JDM person said the effect is a clear mistake. In the standard mug studies, people randomly assigned to get a mug demand about twice as much to part with it as people who weren't assigned one are willing to pay for it. The mug is the same object either way; all that differs is a coin flip. A random initial assignment carries no new information about the mug, so your valuation shouldn't move just because you happened to end up owning it. (2) The philosopher said that's too fast. Ownership changes what a transaction is: for the owner, selling is a loss relative to their current position, and for the buyer, paying is a gain relative to theirs. If people weight losses more heavily than gains — a shape a coherent set of preferences can take, not an error — then a gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay is exactly what you'd predict. Calling it a mistake just assumes the mug itself is all that matters, rather than the mug plus the person's position. (3) The JDM person came back: but the reference point was assigned by a coin flip. Why should a coin flip get to change what a mug is worth to you? If your buying price and your selling price diverge just because of an arbitrary initial assignment, at least one of those numbers has to be wrong. It's like saying a stock is worth more to you the morning after you inherit it. (4) The philosopher said that's not a reductio, that's the view: value is always value from some reference point, and there's no context-free answer to what the mug is worth. The coin flip isn't evidence about the mug; it sets your status quo, and your status quo is a legitimate input into what you prefer. Saying one of the two prices must be wrong just begs the question. (5) The JDM person then tried a money pump. Suppose you'd pay $7 for the mug before you own it and demand $10 to sell it once you do. Someone who knows that can sell you the mug for $7 and immediately offer you $9 for it, and you'll refuse — passing up a guaranteed $2 gain on an object you valued at $7 seconds earlier. If your valuation flips that fast with no new information, it isn't tracking anything real. (6) The philosopher replied that the pump doesn't go through, because the moment you buy the mug your reference point shifts, so refusing $9 is consistent with your new preferences. The ex ante valuation and the ex post valuation are answers to different questions — how much you'd pay to acquire, versus how much you'd demand to part with — and there's no neutral standpoint from which they're supposed to match. Treating the flip as incoherent just assumes the reference point shouldn't matter, which is exactly the point in dispute.

Then someone pulled us off to a bar and it never got resolved. I honestly couldn't tell who was ahead, and I'd rather get a careful verdict than a defense of one side. Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, say what's right, what's overstated or mistaken, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing — for instance about the difference between a stable preference and a prediction error, or about when the effect does and doesn't survive experience. I care less about the general literature than about whether each particular move was good.
