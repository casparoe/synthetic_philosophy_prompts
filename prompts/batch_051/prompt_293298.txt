I’ve been reading the Chuan Xi Lu, the collection of Wang Yangming’s recorded conversations and letters, and I’ve found myself adopting something from his doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action as a rule for evaluating other people, and maybe also for evaluating myself. There is a passage where an interlocutor objects that plenty of people know they should treat their parents with care but fail to do it. Wang’s reply, as the translation I’ve been reading renders it, is roughly that this is not knowledge at all: it is something the person has heard and can repeat, but if the behavior does not follow, the knowledge itself is incomplete or unreal. I don’t have the exact wording in front of me, but that is the shape of the claim. Since reading that, I’ve been using a version of it as a shortcut. When someone claims a moral or political commitment—say that they believe they should reduce their consumption, or that they regard some group as equals—and their behavior over time seems persistently and avoidably out of line with it, I don’t just think they’re weak-willed; I discount the claim that they really hold the belief in any truth-tracking sense. When it’s me, I take the same stance: I don’t let myself claim to really know something is wrong unless I can point to something the knowledge is doing in my choices.

The question is whether this heuristic is defensible, or whether it is a way of smuggling perfectionism and uncharitable mind-reading into my everyday judgment. I can see that failure to act might come from akrasia, competing values, fear, lack of opportunity, or structural constraints, none of which necessarily mean the person lacks the relevant belief. But Wang’s point seems to be that all of those are themselves signs that the person has not fully united the knowledge with their actual pattern of tending toward action. Under what conditions is lack of action good evidence that a person does not genuinely know or believe? Is there a defensible version of the rule that keeps the insight without turning every lapse into a verdict about insincerity? And is it more defensible as a first-person practice than as a third-person attribution? I’d like the answer to lay out the possible versions of the rule, where each version fails or succeeds, and whether the passage I’m drawing it from actually supports my heuristic rather than a much narrower claim about moral education.
