I've been reading Mill's On Liberty on my own (not for a class or anything), and there's a paragraph early in the chapter about individuality that I keep coming back too. Mill writes:

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them."

What I'm stuck on is the word "experiments." He seems to be borrowing it from science, but I cant tell how far the comparison is actually supposed to go. A scientific experiment usually tests some specific claim in advance, keeps other factors controlled, and gives a result that can be read off afterward. A whole way of living doesn't obviously work like that: there's no hypothesis written down, no control group, and it's unclear who the "result" is for. So I'd like help with two things. First, what exactly is Mill claiming about these experiments of living — is every unusual life automatically an experiment, or only some? And second, when a mode of life is "proved practically," what has actually been shown: that it's good for the person who tried it, that it's good for people in general, or just that it's possible to live that way? I keep going back and forth on whether the scientific language is doing real work here or is just a loose analogy. When you answer, could you say how confident you are in your reading, and single out the point you're least sure about?
