I've been reading LessWrong and the adjacent rationalist blogs for years, so I've internalized the basic shape of the Münchhausen trilemma (or Agrippa's trilemma—the claim that any chain of justification has to either regress infinitely, loop back on itself, or stop at something you just assert and refuse to justify further). But I've realized I only have the labels, not a gears-level understanding of why the argument works the way it does, and I'm going to have to teach it in a two-hour session for a reading group full of smart non-philosophers: programmers, a biology PhD student, a lawyer. They're the kind of people who will push back if the sequence has gaps, but they've never taken an epistemology course, and my own explanations tend to skip steps.

I don't want the explanation itself—I want a teaching plan. Specifically: what has to be on the table before the trilemma even bites, and in what order. I'm thinking things like "the difference between a belief being true and a belief being justified" and "what it means to give a reason for a reason, not just a causal story about how you came to believe it," but I don't trust my sense of what depends on what. Then give me the one carrying example that can hold the whole session together: what features it needs to have to make the regress vivid, and what features it must not have, so it doesn't sneak in the answer—like an example that makes a foundation feel intuitively legitimate from the start, or makes coherentism look obviously ridiculous before anyone's argued for it. Then the misconceptions: which ones should I expect this kind of audience to arrive with or develop halfway through. My guesses are "the trilemma proves we can't know anything," "axioms stop the regress without being dogmatic becuase obviously you have to stop somewhere," and "coherentism is just circularity with better marketing"—but I want the full map, and at which point in the sequence each one should be headed off instead of argued down later. What would tell me at the end that they've gotten the structure and not just memorized the three horns? And what should I deliberately leave out at this level and openly flag as left out?
