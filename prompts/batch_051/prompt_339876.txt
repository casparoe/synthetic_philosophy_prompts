I am a novelist, and while plotting a character who repeatedly sabotages his own best-laid plans I noticed an asymmetry in ordinary judgment that I want examined with professional care.

Consider a man with a gambling problem who, in a sober morning hour, knows from long experience that by evening he will once again discount the future steeply and lose money he needs next month. He deletes his betting apps, instructs his bank to impose a delay on large withdrawals, and gives his car keys to a friend with firm instructions to ignore his later pleas. We generally call this prudence, self-control, even the paradigmatic exercise of autonomy: he has recognized his own hyperbolic discounting and bound his future self, and we do not pause to ask whether that evening self consents to being bound.

Now hold the content of the constraint fixed and change only its source. A spouse, or a regulator, observing the same history and making the same accurate prediction about his future weakness, hides the keys or freezes the account against his present wishes. We tend to call this paternalism, an affront to his autonomy—rescued, if at all, only if he consents in advance. Yet the constraint itself and the evidence for the predicted lapse may be identical in the two cases; what differs is that in the first case the binder is the man's own earlier self, while in the second it is another person.

Is there a principled difference that justifies the asymmetry, or is it largely a bias toward whichever self happens to hold the pen at the earlier hour? As a professional philosopher of action and practical rationality, think the question through slowly. Begin by brainstorming the strongest candidate justifications—consent, privileged self-knowledge, identification with one's settled values, the right to author one's own life, the revocability of the arrangement—and test each against matched cases, such as an irrevocable self-binding arrangement versus a mild and easily reversed paternalistic intervention. Conclude with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up.
