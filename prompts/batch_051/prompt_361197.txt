Write a sestina about the combination problem for constitutive panpsychism — the difficulty of explaining how the many tiny subjects the view posits could compose the single unified subject each of us seems to be. Let the form do the arguing. The sestina's six end-words, rotating through six six-line stanzas and gathered in a three-line envoi, should be chosen so that several belong to the many, one to the single subject, and one to the act of combining; their recurrence should enact the subject-summing objection, in which the same elements keep re-entering new wholes but nothing does more than rearrange them. In the envoi, make the gathering of all six words read as the final attempt to compose them into a single thing, and let the reader feel what is still missing when a gathering remains a collection. Keep the constitutive panpsychist's predicament precise: the move from many subjects to one subject cannot simply be modeled on the move from many material parts to one material whole. A philosopher of mind should recognize the subject-summing version of the combination problem without feeling the poem has lapsed into versified jargon, and a reader who has never heard of panpsychism should still get a poem about parts that refuse to add up to a self.
