I'm taking a course on biblical interpretation and I cant get a clause in my denomination's 1948 statement of faith out of my head. It reads: "We believe that the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments, as originally given, are the Word of God, fully inspired and therefore without error in all that they teach, and they are the supreme and final authority for faith and life." I know the "as originally given" bit was put there to keep the inerrancy claim attached to the autographs rather than to later copies, but a situation came up in my Bible study that the drafters dont seem to have thought about at all.

We've started using a new translation whose editors print the Septuagint's wording in the main text at a few places where it seems to go its own way against the Hebrew text behind our usual Bibles — the old Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, which the New Testament writers apparently used and quoted, even where the Greek translator may have had a different Hebrew text in front of him or may have been paraphrasing. The editor's argument is that for Greek-speaking early Christians, "Scripture" in practice mostly was the Septuagint, and when the apostles quote it they treat that wording as what God says; so a translation that follows the Septuagint at those points is just being faithful to the text the church actually read as Scripture. My friend says this is a slippery slope to KJV-onlyism: if a translation can become "inspired" by virtue of being the text the church used and passed down, then any old version can get promoted to inspired status. I'm not sure that follows, and I dont think its the same question at all, but I cant articulate exactly why not.

What I want to know is whether our statement of faith's clause actually reaches this situation, and what the main approaches to reading a doctrinal rule — the plain wording, the purpose of the drafters, what the drafters would have said if asked about this exact translation, and what a reasonable reader in the congregation would take the words to mean — each imply. Does it call this translation the inspired Word of God? If the Hebrew and the Septuagint diverge, which "original" does "as originally given" pick out, and why? I care more about what the clause commits us to than what the senior pastor would probably vote, so please think it through carefully. You're a historical theologian with a side interest in hermeneutics; start by laying out the readings seperately before you commit, and then reach a verdict and defend it rather than ending on "it depends." At the end, tell me which argument or textual consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where you think your reasoning is shakiest.
