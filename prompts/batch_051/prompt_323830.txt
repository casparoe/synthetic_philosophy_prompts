I've been working through a debate in the philosophy of consent and keep finding that my usual way of separating cases breaks down. In a course I'm taking, valid consent is presented as requiring at least competence, information, and voluntariness, and I can see why each matters in isolation. What I don't yet see is how the second and third conditions are supposed to relate to each other. Consider two cases. In the first, a patient agrees to a procedure only because the surgeon has allowed her to believe something false—that the intervention is diagnostic when it is in fact elective and cosmetic. In the second, a patient agrees to the same procedure only because the surgeon has threatened to withdraw all postoperative care unless she consents. In both cases we would be reluctant to treat the patient's agreement as morally binding, but the failures look different in kind. In the deception case, the patient seems to have authorized the wrong act, or authorized an act under a false description. In the coercion case, the patient may have authorized exactly the right act, but the authorization itself seems unfree.

I am trying to decide between two ways of understanding what consent is such that both of these cases can be explained without collapsing the distinction between them. On one picture, consent is fundamentally a communicative act: it licenses another person by publicly expressing an authorizing state of mind, so its validity depends both on what is communicated and on whether that communication is genuinely the consenter's own. On this picture, coercion and deception would fail in structurally different ways—coercion corrupts the communication, while deception disconnects the communication from the act it appears to authorize. On the other picture, consent is fundamentally a mental state—an informed, unforced act of permission—and coercion and deception are merely two different causes of the same underlying defect. I can see initial attractions in both, but I cannot yet articulate what each view commits me to in the hard intermediate cases.

Could you explain the strongest version of each position and then use it to think through the following? (a) If consent is essentially a mental state, how can that view resist the conclusion that any deception that actually moves the consenter invalidates consent—including deception about a collateral fact such as the other person's income, marital history, or ulterior motives? (b) If consent is essentially communicative, what exactly does deception about a collateral fact leave intact, and is that enough to give the other person moral authority over the act? (c) Does the legal distinction between fraud in the factum (deception about the very nature of the act or instrument one is agreeing to) and fraud in the inducement (deception about the surrounding reasons) track a real moral difference, or does it mostly track what is easy to prove in court? Please avoid using legal doctrine as a substitute for the moral argument, and be explicit about the theoretical commitments of each answer.
