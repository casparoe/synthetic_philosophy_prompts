I am a retired natural scientist—an ecologist by training—and for decades my work consisted in trying to infer stable structure from noisy, partial observations. The vocabulary I still think in is that of statistical modeling: probability distributions over observations, latent variables, mixture components, identifiability, regularization, likelihood and prior, and the standing worry that a fitted structure may be an artifact of the analyst's choices rather than a feature of the world. I have recently started reading philosophy of language, as an amateur, and the debate over polysemy and semantic range—over whether words like run or sharp have meanings, several discrete meanings, or only context-shifting patterns of use—keeps reminding me of old arguments about whether latent classes are real or merely convenient summaries. I am thoroughly fluent in the statistical ideas, so you need not explain them; what I need is the philosophical positions expressed through them.

Please restate the main positions in that debate in my statistical vocabulary, as systematically and honestly as you can. Consider at least these three: a word has a small inventory of discrete senses, and context selects among them; a word has a single very general meaning that is pragmatically sharpened or enriched on each occasion of use; and words have no stable meanings at all, only occasion-specific constructions built from context and background knowledge. Tell me precisely what each becomes in modeling terms. Are discrete senses mixture components, modes of a distribution over uses, latent states, or estimated parameters? Is semantic range the support of a distribution, the spread of a posterior, or the set of contexts in which an inference remains reliable? Is the distinction between semantics and pragmatics closer to the likelihood–prior distinction or to the difference between an identifiable parameter and a modeling artifact? Do not settle the debate by quietly redefining the positions so that they fit; mark every place where the translation distorts.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims survive the translation intact, which survive only with damage, and which cannot be stated in this vocabulary at all—I am thinking especially of the apparent normativity of meaning, the way some uses present themselves as correct and others as mistaken, and of the aboutness of words, the fact that they seem to point at things rather than merely covary with them. End with a committed verdict. Either statistical modeling is genuinely blind to something about meaning, and the residue names that something; or the original philosophical claims were less crisp than they looked, and the residue is their failure rather than the framework's. I want a definite position with reasons, not a menu of options.
