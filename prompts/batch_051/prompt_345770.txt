I'm grading a short-answer question for a course on conceptual issues in the social sciences, and one student's answer is exactly the kind of thing a hasty grader might either over-praise or over-penalize. I recently had a long argument about this same material with a friend, so I want the assessment to track the actual moves in the answer rather than settle for a general impression.

Here is the question I set:

Assess the claim that there is no fact of the matter about whether the world has become less violent—only facts relative to a chosen definition of violence and a chosen metric. Your answer should discuss at least the problem of defining violence, the distinction between per capita and absolute measures, and the sense, if any, in which the question is empirical rather than conventional.

Here is the student's answer:

Whether the world is becoming less violent is often treated as an empirical question, but it is really a matter of stipulation. The first difficulty is definitional. If 'violence' is restricted to homicide, the data look one way; if it includes deaths in war, state-inflicted killings, or structural harms such as chronic poverty, the data look different. Because the boundary around 'violence' is drawn by moral interests rather than discovered in nature, no definition is neutral. So the unqualified sentence 'violence has declined' has no truth value until a definition is supplied.

The second difficulty arises even after a definition is fixed. World population has grown enormously, so the absolute number of violent deaths can increase while the rate per hundred thousand falls. Both figures are computed from the same underlying events, and neither computation is more accurate than the other; they simply aggregate differently. It follows that whether the world has become less violent depends on an arbitrary choice of metric.

Therefore, a historian who says the world has become less violent is not reporting a fact but dressing up a choice of definition and metric as a discovery. At the global level there is no fact of the matter; there are only facts relative to a definition-plus-metric pair. The genuine disagreement is ethical: it concerns which harms count as violence and, as between per capita and absolute measures, whether each person's equal risk matters more or total harm matters more.

Grade this answer. Give one definite grade—not a range and not a menu of possible grades depending on one's sympathies—and then provide the point-by-point comments that justify it. Be explicit about what the student gets right, where the argument goes wrong, whether each mistake is local or undermines the overall conclusion, and what a corrected version of the mistaken sentence or step would say.
