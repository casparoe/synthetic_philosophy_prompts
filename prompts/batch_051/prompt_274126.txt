I'm a novelist, and I keep getting tripped up by this passage from Warren and Brandeis's *The Right to Privacy* (1890): "The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

I think I basically read this as: privacy's real harm is the mental pain of being exposed, and the more sensitive a person is, the more privacy they need. But if that's right, then fiction looks like a weird machine for manufacturing exactly the harm they describe. I spend all day making up private lives and then selling the exposure of them. Reading the passage, I dont see a carve-out for made-up people. Is the novelist just a harmless voyeur because the characters don't exist, or does Warren and Brandeis's argument actually depend on something other than distress—like a real person's control over their social identity—so fiction doesn't fit? I suspect I'm flattening a legal argument into a general theory of privacy and then getting my profession stuck in it. What's the right way to read the passage, and what does it genuinely imply for the ethics of fiction? Tell me how confident you are in your reading and which part you're least sure of.
