I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I have been trying to reconstruct the bootstrapping problem in a way that isolates my real question about easy knowledge. Here is the reconstruction I keep returning to. Suppose S has never had any independent evidence about whether her color vision is reliable, and S now has a visual experience as of a ripe red tomato before her.

(1) If perceptual experience in the absence of defeaters gives S prima facie justification to believe that an object before her is red, then S is prima facie justified in believing that the tomato is red.
(2) On the basis of the same experience, S is also prima facie justified in believing that she has a visual experience as of red.
(3) If S justifiably believes both that she has an experience as of red and that the tomato is red, then S can infer, and thereby come to justifiably believe, that this particular experience matched the world — in other words, that her color vision delivered a correct result on this occasion.
(4) By repeating this reasoning over many occasions, S can inductively justify the belief that her color vision is generally reliable.
(5) But this gives S a justified belief in the reliability of her color vision without any independent check, using only the very faculty whose reliability is at issue.
(6) One cannot justifiably arrive at a belief in the reliability of a faculty solely through such self-verifying reasoning; that is viciously circular.
(7) Therefore, the assumption in (1) must be false: perceptual experience alone, absent prior evidence of reliability, cannot provide prima facie justification for the corresponding perceptual belief.

I suspect the weakness is in the induction from (3) to (4), but I cannot say why induction is more objectionable here than elsewhere; and if I instead reject (6), I seem to be committed to a form of easy knowledge.
