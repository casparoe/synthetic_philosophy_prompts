I keep going back and forth on Locke's memory criterion of personal identity. It's the only account I know that gives a crisp answer to why I should care specially about my future self: that person will remember my experiences (or at least be linked to me by chains of memory), and that's supposed to be what makes them me. But then there's Reid's brave officer, and it really bothers me. The boy gets flogged for robbing an orchard, the officer remembers being that boy, the general remembers being the officer but not the boy — so the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, but the general isn't the boy, which just violates transitivity, and I can't shake the conviction that identity has to be transitive. Everyone tells me either "drop the memory criterion" or "stop treating the case as a real counterexample," and idk that either of those is satisfying. I want to know what it would actually take for the intuition to go versus for the theory to go, whether the criterion can be patched (indirect memory? overlapping chains?) without turning into a different theory entirely, and whether there's a reading of "is the same person as" on which the memory criterion and transitivity both survive. Present both sides at their strongest and then give me a negotiated verdict.
