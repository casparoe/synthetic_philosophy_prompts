I am a novelist, not a philosopher, but I am trying to get the moral psychology of my next book right, and I want you to adopt the role of a moral psychologist working on the difference between guilt-like and shame-like moral emotions. The novel is set in an invented island culture, the Saer, whose moral vocabulary is organized almost entirely around shame. The Saer describe wrongdoing in terms of lost face, lost standing, and public cleanliness; they have many words for the experience of being seen to fail, and no word that translates naturally as guilt in the inward, audience-independent sense. The plot turns on an episode in which my protagonist, alone at sea, falsifies a log so that a rival is blamed for an accident, in circumstances that make discovery essentially impossible. I need to know whether the inward aftermath I have planned for him—private self-reproach, an unforced attempt to make the wrong right, the sense of being judged by no one—is something a human being raised in such a culture could plausibly undergo, and whether his culture could be stable at all without something guilt-like doing hidden work beneath its shame vocabulary.

I want from you a credence, not a survey essay. What is your subjective probability for the following claim: a stable human society can make shame its dominant moral emotion in the sense above, and still produce private conscience—people who correct serious hidden wrongs without detection—roughly as reliably as societies that center inward guilt? Decompose the credence into the sub-questions it actually depends on, assigning each a rough probability and a one-line justification, and then show how you combine them into your final number. I expect at least these: whether the functional core of guilt can exist without a guilt vocabulary, so a person may have the state without the concept; whether shame, properly understood, already contains an internalized audience—an imagined judge assembled from the shamed person's own standards—so that it can do private conscience on its own; whether a culture could suppress a guilt concept while leaving the underlying function intact; whether the ethnographic picture of shame cultures lacking private conscience is mostly real or an artifact of translation and observational bias; and whether my protagonist's response should be expected to be typical of such a culture rather than an idiosyncratic, novelistically convenient exception. Before you fix any numbers, spend a paragraph brainstorming the strongest thing that can be said on each side of the main claim; I want the numbers to read as the residue of reasoning rather than retrofitting.

Finally, do two things. First, tell me where your final number is most exposed: which sub-question is doing the most work, and what argument or evidence would lead you to change your mind on that point. Second, on the strength of your credence, tell me whether my novel should present the protagonist's private torment as a psychologically remarkable exception or as an unsurprising human response. I would rather have a sharply marked 0.45 than an overconfident 0.7.
