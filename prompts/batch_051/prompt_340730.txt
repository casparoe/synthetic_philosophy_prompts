Write a satirical public consultation document of 600–800 words, in deadpan bureaucratic prose, from a fictional agency called the Office of Precautionary Assessment. The document announces a rule implementing the precautionary principle in its strongest form: whenever a proposed activity, product, or technology faces scientific uncertainty, it may not proceed unless the proponent proves that it poses no residual possibility of serious or irreversible damage; the mere failure to establish safety is sufficient grounds for prohibition. Format it as a formal notice with a reference number, an issue date, and numbered sections.

The satire should come from the principle's own logic, not from exaggerated personalities or unrelated absurdity. Include at least these sections: (1) Definitions, where serious or irreversible damage is drawn broadly enough to include the harms of being denied a possible benefit, so that the rule begins to fall under its own terms; (2) Application requirements, which demand proof of a negative to a standard no finite body of evidence could meet; (3) Treatment of inaction and regulation, where the agency acknowledges that prohibiting a technology, and issuing this guidance, may themselves cause damage, but cannot say how those risks are to be weighed, since weighing would mean accepting one uncertain risk to avoid another; and (4) Risks of this document, where the agency postpones finalizing the guidance pending a risk assessment of the guidance, which is itself postponed pending a risk assessment of the assessment. End with a request for public comments, to be considered only once the agency has determined that considering them poses no risk.

Keep the irony deadpan throughout; do not name real people, agencies, or regulations. A specialist in the precautionary-principle literature should recognize a fair reductio of the strong version—the reversal of the burden of proof, the demand for certainty, the status-quo bias, the neglect of the risks of regulation and inaction, and the resulting paralysis—while a lay reader should find it funny and legible as satire.
