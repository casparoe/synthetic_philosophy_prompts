One of the more puzzling claims in the history of political philosophy is Rousseau's argument in The Social Contract that someone who refuses to obey the general will may be compelled by the whole body to comply, and that this compulsion makes the person free rather than simply subdued. I want to approach that claim not as a text to be interpreted but as a live question about an existing practice.

Consider short-term involuntary psychiatric commitment. In many jurisdictions a person may be detained, commonly for an initial seventy-two hours with extensions available, and may be medicated against his or her stated wishes, if a clinician or two judges that the person is a danger to himself or herself. The official rationale often includes restoring the person's autonomy or protecting his or her liberty, and clinicians often describe the intervention as allowing the person to return to himself or regain control of his own life. Yet at every moment before release, the person is refusing the detention and the medication.

What philosophy of freedom and the will does this practice presuppose?

First, what must be true of a person's will for it to be coherent to say that detaining and medicating him against his expressed will restores his freedom? Reconstruct the implicit view of the self—for example, a split between a transient or impaired will and an enduring or well-functioning will, with the second authorized to override the first—and say how much of that structure the practice strictly requires, as opposed to merely fitting it.

Second, is the Rousseauian analogy apt, or is it doing illicit work? Rousseau's argument concerns a citizen whose private inclinations conflict with the general will of the political community, and whose compliance is said to realize his freedom as a citizen. Civil commitment, by contrast, sets a person's present self against his future or restored self, and the community overrides one in the name of the other. Is this a faithful extension of Rousseau's point, something closer to its opposite, or only a loose metaphor?

Third, could a strict negative-liberty account, on which freedom is simply the absence of interference, render the practice coherent without the divided-self machinery? If so, what exactly is doing the justificatory work? If not, what is the least demanding positive conception of liberty that will?

Finally, step back and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which features of its implicit theory are genuinely required by the practice as it exists, and which could be replaced by rival theories of autonomy without changing the practice at all. Throughout, separate what is reasonably settled among people who work on autonomy and coercion from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one reading of Rousseau or one theory of autonomy as the consensus where it is not.
