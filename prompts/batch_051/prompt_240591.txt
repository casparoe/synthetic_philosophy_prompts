me and my friend just spent two hours fighting about whether a risk-scoring system can be fair when the thing it predicts is more common in one group than another, and he left me with this argument that i cant shake. it sounds like it proves way too much, but i can't find the bad step.

set-up: a score gives everyone a number from 0 to 1 and the outcome is binary, like defaulting on a loan or getting rearrested. the decision rule uses one cutoff for everyone: above it means flagged high risk. assume the base rates genuinely differ between two groups, which is true of basically every outcome anyone actually builds these scores for.

his argument went like this:

(1) an honest score should be calibrated: among all the people who get a score of .70, roughly 70% should actually have the outcome, and this should hold within each group. if the same number means something different for different groups, the score is just misleading people about their risk.

(2) a fair score should have roughly equal false positive rates and roughly equal false negative rates across groups (some people call this equalized odds). if one group's members get wrongly flagged as high risk much more often, the score is treating that group worse.

(3) and here's what he kept insisting: if the base rates differ and the score is imperfect (it can't literally see the future), then (1) and (2) mathematically cannot both hold. calibration and equalized odds fight each other.

(4) so any calibrated score for such an outcome will have unequal error rates across groups, and by (2) that makes it unfair.

(5) so every honest, well-calibrated score used on any real outcome is automatically unfair, not because anyone put bias into it but just because the groups differ in the first place. fairness would then require either a perfect oracle or not scoring anyone at all.

he says this shows the equalized-odds idea of fairness is doing too much and should be weakened or dropped. i'd rather keep (2), because it feels like exactly what fairness should mean here, but i can't see how to block (4) without just denying the math. where does this go wrong, if it does go wrong? and if it doesn't, which premise am i actually giving up, and what does the alternative cost me?
