I'm a grad student and I've been in a philosophy of mind reading group for the past few months, and we keep getting stuck in exactly the same place every time the word "qualia" comes up. One of us will say that no description of neurons, functions, or behavior captures what it's like to taste coffee or see red, and another will say that "what it's like" is just a placeholder with no job to do, and then everyone restates their starting intuition a little louder. What's odd is that both sides seem to agree about all the imaginable physics, all the neuroscience, and even about what the subject would say in every possible experiment. They disagree only about what follows from all that.

The thing that's been ratting around in my head is Dennett's two coffee tasters. Chase says the taste of the coffee hasn't changed one bit in twenty years, but he no longer likes it — the liking changed, the taste didn't. Sanborn says he still likes it exactly as much, but the taste itself has changed. Both are perfectly fluent, sincere, and careful tasters. Dennett's point, as I understand it, is that no amount of more careful introspection, no brain scan, and no behavioral test could decide whether "the taste itself" changed or only the evaluative attitude toward it changed. And if there's no fact of the matter there, then the idea of a determinate intrinsic "what it's like" — the thing the whole qualia debate is supposedly about — starts to look like an artifact of how we talk rather than a fact we've discovered.

So here's my working hypothesis: the qualia debate has stalled because it isn't really an empirical disagreement, and it isn't quite a metaphysical one either. It's a disagreement about whether the concept of qualia is coherent — about what a concept has to do before we're allowed to posit a fact answering to it. The qualia realist treats introspective report as a window onto a determinate state of the subject; the physicalist treats the demand to explain that state as a symptom of a bad concept. I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who wins. Is it verbal? Is it about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? And what would actually count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a changed question — or should I expect it to stay stalled forever? I don't want a survey of the qualia literature; I want the anatomy of the impasse.
