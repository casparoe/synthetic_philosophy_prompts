I'm a novelist, and what began as a craft problem has become a decision I can't make without thinking about something more basic. The novel I'm finishing turns on a death. Roughly two-thirds of the way through, a character the reader has watched grow close to the narrator dies suddenly, and the final third follows the narrator's grief in close detail. I have drafted the death and the first weeks of its aftermath. It is not the material that unsettles me; it is the reaction of my earliest readers, and my own reaction while writing it.

Every test reader reports some version of the same thing: the chapters hurt, several wept, none could stop reading, and all called them the strongest work I have done. One reader said she felt cleansed afterward, then corrected herself, not cleansed exactly, but that the pain had been made to mean something. Another said she kept putting off the last chapter because she did not want the feeling to end. I recognized this from the other side. The relationship the death is drawn from, and the grief after it, are mine. Writing the scenes did not feel like reliving the loss; it felt absorbing, controlled, and, I have to admit, gratifying. The worse I made things for the characters, the more the scenes seemed to cohere, and the more reluctant I was to leave them.

This is the knot. When I once set down the same events as bare information, a summary of what happened, readers reported only the ordinary discomfort one feels at another person's bad news. But shaped into scene, with pace and image and syntax, the identical events became what people call powerful, and people who had just been weeping said they admired them, even sought more of them. That suggests whatever is good about the scenes attaches to the shaping rather than to the suffering itself. But if the suffering is only a vehicle for the shaping, I do not see why the degree of suffering should matter, and yet I am convinced that it does. Why should making the ending darker make the book better rather than simply more efficient at extracting feeling? Where is the line between that and manipulation?

My agent wants the last chapters to turn toward consolation: the narrator beginning to recover, some gesture of hope. My plan was the opposite, to end inside the worst of it, without relief. I need to decide, and I need reasons rather than an aesthetic temperament. Help me think through what is actually going on when a rendered scene of grief hurts and the hurt is part of why an intelligent reader stays with it. I am not asking for permission to write sad books. I am asking what the hurt does within a work of fiction such that intensifying it can be either a form of honesty or a form of exploitation, and how one tells the difference.
