After I retired I started reading political philosophy, and my old colleagues find it funny because I spent forty years doing hydrological modeling—basically betting that a river would stay predictable. Anyway, here is something I can't settle. There is a proposal called futarchy: instead of legislators voting on policies directly, they agree on some measure of social welfare, say expected storm-damage costs over thirty years, and let markets decide the details. For each policy proposal you open two markets. One pays out based on that welfare measure if the proposal passes, and the other pays out based on it if the proposal fails. If the pass-conditional contract trades higher, the policy is adopted; if the fail-conditional one trades higher, it isn't. The clean version appeals to me, because it looks like pooling dispersed information the way a good model pools rain gauges. But I can also see it going badly wrong. Please write a dialogue between two characters, a futarchy advocate and a serious skeptic, in which they actually engage with each other's strongest points. Have the skeptic push on the hard technical problems: thin markets and cheap manipulation, market prices moving the very outcome they are supposed to predict, Goodhart-style gaming of whatever metric gets chosen, and the worry that this turns political equality into one-dollar-one-vote. Have the advocate answer these rather than dodge them, maybe by distinguishing prediction markets from decision markets or by arguing that every alternative system has the same pathologies in a different set of clothes. Maybe have them work through a concrete case, like a coastal city deciding wether to build a seawall. Don't make either character a strawman, and make sure they respond to what the other one just said.
