I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so the "DNA is code" thing comes up all the time — people telling me biology is just executing genetic code, or that once we crack the code we've basically understood the organism. I started reading about whether DNA is literally a code, and it's hard to tell where the science ends and the metaphor begins. A friend of mine with a molecular bio PhD tried to set me straight. Her summary of the debate was:

People talk about DNA as the code of life, but the actual genetic code is just a lookup table: 64 nucleotide triplets mapped to 20 amino acids plus three stop signals. It's not code in the way a program is code — there's no instruction pointer, no operations, nobody wrote it. The DNA molecule does nothing by itself; the cell's machinery does the transcribing and translating. So the information isn't really in the DNA alone, it's in the whole system. And since the same DNA sequence can do different things in different cellular contexts, and development depends on plenty of non-genetic factors, a gene doesn't have a determinate information content or function. Saying genes "code for" a trait is scientifically harmless shorthand but it has no literal content.

That sounds mostly right to me, but I keep getting stuck on the fact that she's using "code" in two different ways, and the jump from "the information isn't in the DNA alone" to "genes don't have a determinate information content" feels unmotivated. I don't know enough philosophy of biology to know which parts to trust. Which parts of her summmary are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and does her conclusion actually follow from the accurate parts?
