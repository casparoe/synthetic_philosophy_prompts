I keep returning to a dispute about the calculation argument that has stalled in a promising way, and I would like a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than the usual survey of positions. A friend sympathetic to the Austrian side (Mises, with Hayek's later point about dispersed knowledge) reconstructs the anti-socialist argument as follows. (1) To allocate a resource rationally, an agent must be able to compare what is forgone by using it in one way rather than another. (2) Such comparisons are feasible only when the resource has a money price arising from actual exchange, because in-kind quantities cannot reduce heterogeneous uses to a common unit. (3) A socialist economy in which the means of production are collectively owned and allocated by a planning board without markets therefore lacks the prices required by (2). (4) Hence such an economy cannot compare alternative uses of its capital goods, and rational allocation is impossible even in principle; in particular, a Lange-style board that sets provisional prices and adjusts them by trial and error is not solving the calculation problem, since its prices are guesses rather than the outcome of exchange in which someone bears the cost of being wrong. So far I find the argument forceful.

But then I noticed that each step seems to apply almost as well to the internal organization of a large firm: a corporation moves resources among divisions by administrative directive rather than through internal markets, yet it plainly manages to allocate them rationally. If the argument cannot distinguish a large firm from a centrally planned economy, it proves too much; so either the firm is impossible, or something in (1)–(4) is false or missing. My tentative repair is that the firm can lean on the external market prices facing its inputs and outputs, whereas a closed socialist economy has no external market to lean on. But that repair invites an uncomfortable follow-up: could a small socialist country simply import world-market prices for the means of production and allocate by those, and if so, would that count as solving the calculation problem? I believe the answer is no, but I cannot say exactly why. Which step in (1)–(4) should be rejected or amended, and what does rejecting or amending it cost someone who still wants a version of the argument that rules out a marketless socialist economy without ruling out the large firm?
