I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've been turning Arrival over in my head again, trying to write something about it that isn't the usual language-shapes-thought take. What strikes me this time is how much the movie flirts with Parmenides, the pre-Socratic who said change and plurality are illusions. As I understand him, Parmenides held that what is can't have come into being or ever cease, can't be divided, and can't change; coming-to-be and passing-away belong to the way of seeming, not to truth. The film gives us a block-universe payoff where the future is already there, grief is already present, and Louise knows the daughter she will lose before she has her. But Parmenides, as I read him, would say the movie doesn't go nearly far enough: it keeps time, persons, births, choices, pluralities—it just lines them up differently. That feels like the difference between Eleatic monism and mere fatalism or a time block, and I keep going back and forth on wether Arrival respects that difference or flattens it. Read the film through Parmenides and the Eleatic denial of plurality and change. Where does the movie genuinely dramatize something Eleatic, and where does it confuse knowing the future with the claim that change itself is unreal? Does Louise's decision to have Hannah anyway come out Eleatic, or just fatalistic and sad? Which scenes do the heaviest work—the first whiteboard session, the bombing, the final montage—and where does the film undercut its own best case? And does the movie, by holding on to persons and choices and grief, make a case against Parmenides that a straight essay couldn't? I want a reading that's fair to both the physics-adjacent determinism the film seems to endorse and the much stranger Eleatic claim, and that doesn't use the film as a chalkboard for the philosophy. Also feel free to say the film is more confused than profound—that's a real possibility I want to leave open.
