I'm giving a talk next month at my university's center for ethics and public policy, and I would like you to prepare me for the question period. The audience will be a deliberately mixed one: about half moral philosophers, mostly neo-Aristotelians with a few Kantians; a cluster of positive psychologists who work on grit and resilience measurement and intervention; and several student-affairs administrators who run retention and first-generation-student programs and use resilience language in a fairly instrumental way. My thesis is that the recent grit-and-resilience discourse is best understood neither as a rediscovery of the classical virtue of fortitude nor as mere ideology, but as a managerialized replacement for fortitude. On the classical view, fortitude is a disposition to persist in difficulties for the sake of ends one has correctly judged worth enduring, and part of the virtue lies in the judgment about what not to endure. In grit-talk, by contrast, persistence itself tends to function as the good, which flattens the difference between admirable perseverance and costly self-exploitation. I also argue that the empirical grit literature cannot fix the normative problem, because the relevant scales partly measure self-reported past persistence and stable circumstances that already reflect some of the social advantages the resilience programs are supposed to compensate for. I explicitly resist, however, the conclusion that fortitude is therefore nothing but ideology; I want to defend a reconstructed fortitude that combines tenacity with lucidity about quitting, refusing, and changing goals.

The talk has three moves: a conceptual genealogy from fortitude to grit; a critique of the measurement instruments; and the constructive recovery of fortitude. My colleague in social theory thinks I have been too soft on the ideology critique, while my advisor thinks I have already conceded too much to the psychologists. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I am most likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, each with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest, including what I should concede up front and what I should hold. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it would hurt.
