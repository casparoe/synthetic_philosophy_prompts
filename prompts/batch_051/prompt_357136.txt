Here's a thing I've noticed and keep turning over. People will pay real money for information that could still change a decision: a second scan before choosing a surgery, market research before a product launch, a weather forecast before planting. But after the decision is locked in and nothing about it can be revised, many of the same people will go out of their way — sometimes incurring real costs — not to know how it turned out. They skip the follow-up scan, they dont open the earnings report, they avoid learning whether they sold the stock too early. So information looks like a good you buy only while it can still move your hand, and a bad you pay to avoid once it can't.

Good's theorem is often read as saying that costless information can never hurt an expected-utility maximizer, because you can always ignore it. That reading makes the second half of the asymmetry look strictly irrational: after the fact, you ought to be indifferent. But maybe the post-decision avoidance isn't really about the information — it's about anxiety, or about the difference between not knowing the past and having to live inside a past you now know.

What I want from you: is this pre-decision/post-decision asymmetry something we can actually justify, or is it just two kinds of irrationality pointing at each other? Give me the best candidate justification you can build, test it against nearby cases (someone who wants the outcome but not the cause, or someone who will accept the information after the fact only if it's framed as a lesson rather than a verdict), and then say whether you'd keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
