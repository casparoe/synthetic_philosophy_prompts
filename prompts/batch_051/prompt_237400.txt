An essay I read recently summarizes recent philosophical work on complicity as follows:

"On the leading account, causal contribution is a red herring. You are complicit in a wrong just in case you intentionally do your part in a collective act or project that is wrong, even if your individual act makes no difference to what happens. That is why the teller who processes a fraudulent deposit, the engineer who designs one component of a weapons system later used in a massacre, and the consumer who buys clothes from a factory that exploits child labor are all complicit in the full wrong: each knowingly participates in a joint enterprise that commits the wrong. If causal difference were required, almost everyone in overdetermined cases like these would escape blame, which is absurd. So once you knowingly join the collective act, you inherit its entire wrong. Ordinary consumers and citizens are therefore complicit in far more large-scale wrongs than courts or common-sense morality are willing to admit."

The essay treats this as settled, and spends its remaining pages drawing the conclusion that consumers and citizens bear moral liability more widely than almost anyone wants to grant. I am suspicious of the last step. There seems to me to be a real difference between participating in a scheme and merely buying what it produces, and between the engineer embedded in the plan and the consumer standing at the end of the supply chain. But perhaps those are just the ordinary intuitions the theory sets out to overturn.

Is this actually a fair summary of the participatory-intention account of complicity, or does it smuggle in moves the account explicitly avoids? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification or conflation, and what a defender of the view would object to, and then say whether the essay's conclusion about ordinary consumers and citizens survives the corrections.
