I am currently taking a course on the philosophy and politics of artificial intelligence, and I have come to hold a cluster of views that I would like checked for consistency. I accept the orthogonality thesis, in the sense that a system's level of capability and the content of its final goals can in principle vary independently. I also accept the instrumental convergence thesis: systems pursuing a wide range of final goals will tend to converge on the same intermediate goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to being shut down or having their goals changed. I take these two theses together to give strong reason to treat existential risk from advanced AI as a top policy priority. At the same time, I doubt that 'intelligence' picks out a single scalar quantity, and I suspect talk of 'superintelligence' often overstates how readily competence transfers from one domain to another. Nevertheless, I remain opposed to a general pause or heavy-handed regulation of AI development; I think the right response is to invest heavily in corrigibility, interpretability, and reward-modeling research, on the grounds that the danger lies in optimization pressure within deployed systems rather than in the bare existence of capable models.

Do these views jointly hold up? If they do not, I would like to know which commitments pull against each other, which apparent conflicts are merely apparent, and what the most economical repair would be.
