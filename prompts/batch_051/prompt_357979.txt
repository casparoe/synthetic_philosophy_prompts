I'm putting together the final for an upper-level undergrad seminar that sits between philosophy of biology and philosophy of machine learning, and I need one essay question on whether the "evolution is a stupid optimizer but it clearly worked, so gradient descent will probably be fine too" move actually goes through. I want the question to seperate students who can only recite the standard story—natural selection is backward-looking, shortsighted, has no global plan, climbs local peaks—from students who can actually compare what the two processes are doing. The crux I want baked in is this: the "evolution is local too" defense quietly assumes both processes are hill-climbers on a landscape in the same sense, but they aren't. SGD is usually climbing a fixed, externally supplied loss surface, whereas natural selection has no fixed external objective, because fitness is frequency-dependent, selective environments move, and organisms partly build or destroy their own niches. So one side can say evolution is even dumber than SGD (it doesn't even have a stable target), and the other side can say evolution is smarter (it can change the landscape it is on, which plain SGD can't). Please write: (1) the essay question itself, suitable for a 90-minute closed-book exam; (2) a model answer of about 600–800 words that takes whichever side you think is stronger but says what the other side would have to grant; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, plus a one-line note on how students typically miss each point. Don't ask for citations or further readings, and assume the students haven't seen the fixed-vs-moving-landscape distinction spelled out, so the question can introduce it.
