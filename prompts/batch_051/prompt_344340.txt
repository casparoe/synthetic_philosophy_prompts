Write a satirical request for proposals (RFP) of about 700 words, issued by a fictional Institute for the Study of Consciousness, soliciting contractor bids to design and build a Cartesian Theater: the facility in which everything that becomes conscious is, at some privileged place and moment, presented to a single inner audience. The document should read as deadpan institutional procurement prose — background, scope of work, technical specifications, evaluation criteria, terms and conditions — with the humor carried entirely by the specifications rather than by winking asides.

Each major clause should quietly assume what the multiple drafts model denies, and then let the assumption run itself into its standard problem. Suitable clauses might specify: a central screen on which all sensory inputs are synchronized into one show, with no account of who does the synchronizing; a single reserved seat for the Self, with the awkward implication that whoever sells or occupies the ticket is already a self; a projectionist stipulated not to be another audience member, who therefore seems to require a smaller projectionist of his own; a firm now at which content crosses into consciousness, undermined by a fine-print clause admitting that late revisions occurred before the show was mounted; and an evaluation criterion requiring bidders to explain how their design will eliminate multiple drafts — uncoordinated, parallel, continuously revised versions of content — where every proposed solution visibly produces another draft. A reader who knows Dennett's critique should recognize this as a fair and specific parody of the Cartesian theater; a reader who does not should still follow the joke and come away with the underlying point.

Do not quote any real work, and do not name any real person in the body of the RFP. Conclude with a separate author's note of 100–150 words that (a) flags where the piece takes liberties with, or relies on a contested reading of, the position being satirized, and (b) names the criticism most likely to change your mind about whether the satire is fair — the objection or consideration you think has the best chance of showing that the parody misjudges what defenders of an inner theater actually mean.
