Right, here's the ask. I run a session for trainee secondary science teachers — around sixteen people, a eighty-minute block — and the one thing I want them to come away really feeling is that students' systematic errors are not gaps or sloppiness. Misconceptions behave like small theories: they are internally consistent, they generate predictions about cases the student has never met, and they don't budge when you just re-explain the correct answer. I don't want that said on a slide; I want the trainees to run into it themselves, before anyone names it.

Could you design me a runnable activity for that group, with rules, timings, materials and a debrief, built around one concrete bit of school science. My default is the old chestnut where a kid thinks a ball thrown straight up still has a force inside it pushing it upwards, and that the force runs down like a battery, which is why the ball slows, stops, then falls; you can swap in a different persistent misconception if you know one cold, as long as it is the sort of thing a science teacher has actually met. The key mechanic I have in mind, but you should make it yours: the participants should have to reverse-engineer the students hidden rules from a small set of answers, then use those rules to predict how the same student would answer a batch of novel questions, and then find out how well they predicted. I want the penny to drop that the wrong answers are predictable because they follow rules, and that the rules are the misconception doing its work — generative, transferable, and kind of hard to kill.

So the debrief should map what happened in the room onto those features, but only after the participants have already felt them; no jargon until the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it — I suspect two or three trainees will quietly turn out to hold the misconception themselves, so keep it kind — and be straight about which part of the real phenomenon a one-session exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator has to just supply in the discussion afterwards.

One formatting thing: the response should be continuous prose, like an email or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and British English throughout. No references or reading list either; the thing has to stand on its own without anyone looking anything up.
