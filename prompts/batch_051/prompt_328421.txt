In many legal orders, a small set of grounds of discrimination — race, sex, religion, disability, and a few others — are marked off as protected in a way that ordinary distinctions, such as taste in music or punctuality, are not. The standard argument for singling out these grounds runs roughly as follows: a classification deserves heightened suspicion when the trait it uses is beyond a person's control, has been the vehicle of a stable and socially salient group's subordination, and is generally irrelevant to what the person is being selected to do. Now suppose the operative decisions — hiring, lending, policing, public benefits — come to be made not by rules that name such traits but by trained models that ingest behavioral data: a person's address, purchase history, the cadence of their typing, the wording of their answers. The model learns a continuous score that is strongly correlated with a protected group, and the decision follows from the score. It never outputs a category like woman or Black; it outputs cluster 7 or predicted default risk 0.83. The operator can then say, truthfully, that no one was rejected for being a member of a protected group. Does the standard argument for protected grounds survive this shift? Set aside what current law happens to require and answer the conceptual question. Say whether the argument goes through as stated, needs repair, or is dead, and identify which premise the development actually puts under pressure — the immutability of the trait, the existence of a stable and identifiable group to which the trait attaches, the assumption that discrimination works by classifying a person under a trait rather than predicting from a shadow of that trait, or some other premise. If it needs repair, state the repaired principle as precisely as you can: what should count as a suspect ground once decisions are made this way? The interesting question is not whether the algorithm is worse, but whether the concept of a suspect ground can be made to reach a disadvantage that has no named ground at all.
