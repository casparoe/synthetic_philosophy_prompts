I spent most of my working life as an experimental physical chemist, and only in retirement have I begun reading philosophy in earnest. I have discovered in myself a policy I never consciously adopted: when I encounter a claim from a field without a short, operationally specified loop between prediction and detectable error, I grant it almost no credence, no matter how elegant or careful the surrounding argument. This was not originally a philosophical position; it is a professional reflex. In my old field, the difference between progress and stagnation was that nature could announce, within a reasonable time, that an idea was wrong, and I do not know how to separate that fact from my sense of what it is to have a reason for belief.

The policy does real work. It leads me to treat most of metaphysics, much of ethics, and a good deal of philosophy of mind as classification exercises rather than candidates for truth. But I also apply it inside science: to multiverse cosmology, to evolutionary accounts that can be adjusted to accommodate any observed behavior, and to any theory that protects itself by post hoc reinterpretation. My philosopher friends call this verificationism and say I am conflating truth with testability, and that my policy would force me to dismiss mathematics and much of history, which plainly know things. I suspect they are partly right, but I also notice that their disputes do not end the way ours did.

I want an assessment rather than a siding. What exactly does a tight feedback loop do for a field, and is my credence policy a legitimate extension of it? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps its protection against unfalsifiable overreach without denying that some disciplines advance through argument, coherence, and reinterpretation rather than decisive refutation? And how could I tell, from the inside, whether I am following a reliable epistemic rule or merely generalizing a temperament that one particular kind of laboratory career rewarded?
