Consider the following claim about the Knobe effect. In the classic vignette, a chief executive officer pursues a policy that he knows will have a certain side effect, explicitly says he does not care at all about the side effect, and acts anyway. When the side effect is that the environment is harmed, most ordinary participants say he brought the side effect about intentionally; when the side effect is that the environment is helped, most say he did not bring it about intentionally. Many experimental philosophers read this asymmetry as revealing something about the ordinary concept of acting intentionally itself: that its application is shaped by moral evaluation, and perhaps that the concept has an evaluative or normative component built in, rather than being a purely descriptive matter of beliefs, desires, foresight, and control. Call this the moral-constitution reading.

Your task is to design the strongest empirical test you can of that reading, under the constraint that the test must be something a psychologist or experimental philosopher could actually run with ordinary participants, vignettes, and behavioral measures. In particular, design a set of conditions that separates the moral-constitution reading from at least the following rivals:

1. The effect tracks the violation of a salient norm generally, including non-moral norms such as professional, aesthetic, or etiquette norms.
2. The effect tracks the undesirability or badness of the outcome, independent of any genuinely moral dimension.
3. The effect tracks atypicality or statistical surprise rather than evaluation.
4. The effect reflects a prior motive to blame or censure the agent, with the ascription of intentionality being recruited to license that censure, rather than reflecting the concept itself.
5. The effect is driven by differences in what participants infer about the agent's foresight, knowledge, or control in morally bad versus morally good cases.

For each condition you propose, specify the vignette manipulation, the dependent measure, and exactly which pattern of responses would favor the moral-constitution reading and which would favor the specific rival being targeted. Then state what a clean positive result would establish and what it would not establish. In particular, address both of the following limitations squarely. First, a pattern of verbal judgments about hypothetical cases may reveal how people are inclined to apply a word under certain conditions without showing that the moral dimension is part of the standing concept rather than a factor that influences its application from outside. Second, even a well-controlled experiment cannot distinguish among formulations that all predict the same behavior — for example, a view on which moral evaluation is semantically encoded in the concept, a view on which it is a stable but external influence on judgment, and a view on which both the intentionality judgment and the evaluation arise from a common underlying representation. Say what, if anything, a further experiment could do about that gap, and what is untestable in principle.

Write the answer in American English.
