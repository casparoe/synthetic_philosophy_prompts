Write the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between the attitude-first and action-first camps in the respect debate should have produced: the document both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.

The attitude-first camp holds that the fundamental thing we owe to other persons is a form of regard—recognition of them as persons whose ends and claims count for us independently of our own projects—and that the familiar behavioral duties of respect are downstream of this attitude, required because of what they express and sustain. On this view, performing every outward act correctly while regarding others only as instruments or obstacles still fails to give something owed, because the regard itself is owed. The action-first camp holds that what we owe one another is ultimately conduct and institutions, not inner states: attitudes are morally significant only insofar as they shape what people do or are disposed to do. On this view, there is no freestanding duty to have a particular attitude—both because ought implies can, and because a private mental state that leaves no trace in act, omission, or institution cannot by itself wrong anyone—so a person who acts consistently respectfully and supports respectful institutions has met the relevant demands, whatever their private motives.

Produce the document itself, structured as follows. A numbered agreed-claims section, stating what both camps can sign in wording neither side finds strained. Then a disagreements table: for each genuine remaining disagreement, the attitude-first formulation and the action-first formulation, each written so that its own side would accept it as an accurate statement of its view. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux, give at least one test, case, or argument that both camps agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Consider as candidate material at least these cases: the person whose outward conduct is flawless but who privately holds others in contempt; the person treated with perfect correctness but only as a means to someone else's advancement; the person who has genuine recognition regard but repeatedly botches its enactment; and the difference between an unbidden contemptuous feeling and a contempt deliberately cultivated and stood behind. Pay particular attention to the questions whether there is a freestanding wrong in receiving correct treatment without recognition, whether attitudes can be required given their partial involuntariness, and whether a pure duty of outward respect collapses into etiquette or becomes unaccountable to its supposed beneficiary. End by saying which parts of the dispute are verbal and which are substantive.

Begin your response with the document itself, at the first item of the agreed-claims section, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the task.
