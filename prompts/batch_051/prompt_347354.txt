I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I keep trying to settle what I think about achievement—specifically, whether being excellent at something is good even when no one benefits—and my case-by-case reactions refuse to line up behind any rule I try. I want you to take my verdicts, work out the principle that would generate them (if one exists), test that principle against further cases, and tell me which of my verdicts I should abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A mathematician solves an open problem that is real but modest, writes up a clean proof, and deletes the file. She enjoyed the puzzle and wants no career from it; no one else ever learns the result. I think this is good—a full achievement—though the deletion bothers me for reasons I cannot locate.

2. A runner trains for years and one morning runs a 2:08 marathon on an empty country road, with no one watching and only his own watch as the clock. He is satisfied, and then never runs again. I think this is good.

3. A pianist gives a flawless performance of a difficult sonata, alone in a soundproof room. The performance moves her. No one else hears it, and no recording is made. I think this is good.

4. The same pianist, same sonata, same standard, but now she is depressed and feels nothing; she plays out of numb routine. I still think it is good, but this is the case that makes me distrust my own position.

5. A man spends years teaching himself to recite 100,000 digits of pi from memory. He performs the recitation once, flawlessly, to an empty room, then lets the digits fade. I cannot bring myself to call this good, even though it seems to have the same structure as the mathematician's proof.

6. A developer spends months building an exquisitely architected parser for a file format that has been dead for decades. No one will ever use it. He did it because the format's design bothered him. I am ambivalent: the craft is real, but the object seems pointless in a way the mathematical proof and the piano recital do not.

7. A mountaineer makes the first solo ascent of a hard unclimbed ridge, takes no photos, tells no one, and did it only to see whether he could. I think this is the clearest case of good on the list.

8. A novelist writes a genuinely masterful short novel, reads it once, and burns the only copy. The process leaves her empty rather than fulfilled. I think this is still good, but I waver.

My provisional principle is roughly: an achievement is good when it meets standards internal to a serious human activity, and its goodness is independent of any effect on anyone's welfare. That rule makes the pi recitation good, which I do not believe; it also fails to explain why the obsolete parser bothers me, and why I keep checking whether the agent got something out of it. The alternative I keep coming back to is: an achievement is good only if it actually benefits someone, where the agent counts. But that rules out the depressed pianist and the burned novel, and it seems to force me to call the unrecorded proof merely a pleasant waste of time.

Please do the following. State the best available principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as you can in plain language. For each clause or element, say which case above forces it. Then apply the principle to five or six further cases of your own construction, described concretely and chosen to stress its weak points, and give the verdicts. Finally, tell me plainly which of my eight verdicts the principle cannot keep and why, and say whether the agent's own benefit is doing real theoretical work or is just a proxy for something else I should care about. If the only way to keep all eight is an ugly, complicated principle, I want the cost of that complexity stated rather than hidden.
