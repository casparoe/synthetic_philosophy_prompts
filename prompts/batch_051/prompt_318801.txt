I am a sixth-form student beginning the research for an Extended Project Qualification (EPQ) on free will and moral responsibility, and before I read any more of the literature I want to fix what I am actually asking of a theory. I have been working through Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section VIII, "Of Liberty and Necessity", and one claim from Part I has become a fixed point for me:

"It is universally acknowledged, that there is a great uniformity among the actions of men, in all nations and ages, and that human nature remains still the same, in its principles and operations."

I read this as an empirical datum that any adequate account must accommodate, or else reject with an explicit story about why it misleads: human conduct is regular enough across times and places that we routinely rely on those regularities in ordinary life, and a theory of free will is not free simply to wave it away. But I do not want a list that merely accommodates this one datum; I want the full specification of what should be demanded of a theory in this area. For the purpose of your answer, treat a theory as adequate if it is good enough to explain the difference between cases in which we rightly hold a person answerable and cases in which we rightly excuse them, without ignoring the Humean datum and without settling in advance, by definition, whether determinism is true.

I am not asking for a survey of existing positions, and I am not asking you to defend one; I am asking what the standard of evaluation itself should be. Please use the following sections, in this order:

1. "The datum" — state precisely what the Hume passage does and does not claim, and set down the requirement it imposes on a theory of free will and moral responsibility (about 120 words).

2. "Desiderata" — six to eight numbered requirements, each formulated as a demand and followed by the case or consideration that motivates it. Cover at least: how the theory treats the Humean datum; the hard cases an adequate theory must get right (for example, ordinary deliberate choice, coercion, addiction, manipulation, and shaping by upbringing); distinctions it must respect (for example, caused versus compelled, influenced versus controlled, predictable versus inevitable, if you judge these to be distinct); and results it must be consistent with (for example, the actual practice of holding people responsible). For each desideratum, say why it is a genuine demand on a theory of this subject rather than a prejudice belonging to one side of the compatibilism–incompatibilism dispute. End this section by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise viable candidates, with one or two sentences on why (about 550 words).

3. "Negotiable and non-negotiable" — identify which of the desiderata a strong theory could revise, weaken, or re-describe without collapsing, and which are load-bearing in the sense that abandoning them would amount to changing the subject rather than solving the problem (about 150 words).

4. "Suspected conflicts" — flag any two or more desiderata that you doubt can be fully satisfied together, and say briefly what it would show about the debate if they could not be (about 150 words).

5. "The contenders" — very briefly, and without arguing for any one, say how compatibilism, libertarianism, and hard incompatibilism fare against the list you have constructed (about 200 words).
