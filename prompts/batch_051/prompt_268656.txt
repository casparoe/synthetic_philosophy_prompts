I retired a few years back after a career in physical chemistry, and I've been spending my reading time on legal philosophy — mostly because I'm astonished that a legal system will openly proceed on the basis that something is true even though everyone involved knows it is false, and then run with it. A corporation counts as a person for some purposes; a marriage that gets annulled is treated for many purposes as if it had never happened; an adopted child gets handled as if born to the new parents. For thirty years I used exactly this move under a different name: the ideal gas, the point mass, the frictionless plane, the infinitely dilute solution. You stipulate something false because it makes the machinery go — it buys you a solvable equation in chemistry, and it lets an old legal rule reach a new situation without the legislature having to rewrite anything. And in both cases there are tight little rules about where the fiction may not be pushed: you don't use the ideal gas law near the critical point, and you don't, I gather, drag corporate personhood over to a right against self-incrimination.

Here is what I want to know: is this more than a superficial resemblance? I half-believe it is a real identity of structure, but the disanalogies nag at me. Scientific idealizations are supposed to be approximately true in a limit — the ideal gas law really does get better as you make the gas dilute, because molecular size and interactions start to matter less — while a legal fiction is not approximately true at all; a corporation is not a simplified person, it is not a person in any sense that gets sharper as you change a parameter, and the law does not care. So maybe legal fictions are not idealizations but a directive to treat one thing as another inside an institutional practice, where the law builds its own little world instead of approximating the real one. If the parallel is real, does anything usable transfer? We have a whole discipline of de-idealization — correction terms, series expansions, stating the domain of validity before you use a result. Does law have an analogue, or does the fact that legal fictions are authority-driven rather than world-driven close that road? And if you are going to break the parallel, I'd rather you do it precisely: state the mapping, tell me what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and give me at least one thing my parallel predicts that comes out false.
