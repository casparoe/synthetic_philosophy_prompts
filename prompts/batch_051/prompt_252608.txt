I spent the last few months helping my sister's home health care workplace organize, and one thing the union-side lawyer said has been rattling around my head ever since. If a group of employees coordinate to refuse work below a certain wage, we call that a union exercising a fundamental right, and the law gives it special carve-outs. But if a group of independent plumbers coordinate to refuse jobs under, say, $80 an hour, we call that a price-fixing cartel — in a lot of places its illegal for them to even have that conversation, and ordinary people would find it sleazy. Same underlying move: people who sell their work agreeing on a floor price. The law even has language about labor not being a commodity, as if it's marking a deep difference, and I've always nodded along but I honestly can't say what the difference actually is.

Can you approach this like a professional philosopher working on labor, markets, and the ethics of collective action? Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm three or four candidate justifications for treating a labor cartel differently from a service or product cartel, then think carefully about which one is strongest. Test it against the hard cases: gig workers who are legally classified as independent contractors, freelance nurses working through an agency, small business owners who all close for a day to pressure a platform over its fees. Then give me an honest verdict: does the asymmetry mostly survive under scrutiny, or is it a historical and political artifact we rationalize afterwards?
