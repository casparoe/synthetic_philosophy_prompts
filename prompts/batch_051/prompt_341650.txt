I've been trying to make Brandom's inferentialism work for disagreement, and I've ended up with a distinction I can't decide about. I call it 'inferential grip'. The rough idea: on an inferentialist picture, grasping a concept is knowing what follows from it and what it follows from, but I think that's too static. My claim is that a speaker understands a term not when she reliably draws the standard inferences, but when she can refuse one of them and have the refusal counted as a challenge rather than as not-yet-competent. So a toddler who won't go from 'dog' to 'has four legs' gets corrected, but a vet who won't go from 'dog' to 'has four legs' after an amputation is making a move inside the practice, not falling out of it. Grip, then, is the licence to withhold an inference without losing one's standing as a speaker of the language. My worry is that this just relocates Brandom's deontic scorekeeping and collapses into 'knowing the standards of the community', or that it's really something already familiar in inferential role semantics under another name. Does it add anything? Where does it break? And be straight with me if I've got Brandom wrong or the distinction is confused, rather than polishing it.
