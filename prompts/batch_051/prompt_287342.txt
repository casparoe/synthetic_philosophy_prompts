At a dinner some weeks ago, two old colleagues of mine—a retired physician and a retired statistician—fell into a dispute about whether a local hospital had been rational to buy a costly prognostic test for a slowly progressive disorder when the result would not change any treatment. I am a retired natural scientist who has come to decision theory only recently, so I could follow the moves but not confidently referee them. The physician held that the purchase was irrational: information that cannot alter a decision has no value, and one should not pay real money for it. The statistician answered that “alter a decision” was being drawn too narrowly; a result can alter what patients plan, how they spend, and when they retire, even if the prescription is unchanged. The physician replied that if such consequences count, the precept against buying useless information becomes unfalsifiable, since any report can be praised afterward for having touched someone’s plans or peace of mind. The statistician called this proper accounting rather than unfalsifiability: a decision problem includes its consequences, and if peace of mind is in the utility function, the information changes the decision after all. The physician then shifted ground: Good’s theorem says only that free information cannot have a negative expected value for a single agent facing no strategic effects; once the hospital pays a substantial price, the right objection is to the price, not to the information. The statistician granted the point but said it cuts the other way: price and value are separate questions, and if the test has positive value, there must be some price up to which buying it is rational, so calling the information “useless” is confused. The physician’s last move was that the value of information is agent-relative—a report worthless to a treating clinician may be valuable to an underwriter or to an anxious patient—so the hospital cannot simply inherit the statistician’s conclusion. The statistician replied that the hospital is itself an agent with a wider set of consequences, and that is the agent whose decision problem should be evaluated. They continued in this vein for some time. Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point: which specific claims survive, which fail, where the two are talking past one another, and what a sound application of the value-of-information framework would say about the hospital’s purchase?
