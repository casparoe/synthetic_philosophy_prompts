I'm a novelist, and for the past year I've been stuck on a scene where one character tries to explain to his sister why he doesn't want kids. He keeps reaching for this odd asymmetry: a possible child who would suffer feels like a reason against bringing them into existence, but a possible child who would be happy does not feel like a reason in favor. While I was reading around in the philosophy of procreation to get his voice right, I realized the debate over exactly this asymmetry has been grinding along for decades without anyone moving much. The way I understand it, there are at least four positions. Some people think the asymmetry is axiological — it's a claim about which outcomes are better or worse: a world where a possible person's pain is absent is better, but a world where a possible person's pleasure is absent is not worse. Others think it isn't about value at all but about duties: we have a stringent negative duty not to cause harm, and no symmetrical positive duty to create benefit, especially when the "benefit" is bringing a whole new need-machine into existence. Still others think it's person-affecting: reasons to harm or benefit attach only to actual, existing subjects, so the asymmetry falls out of the fact that a created child exists to be harmed, while an uncreated child doesn't exist to be deprived. And then there's a deflationary camp that thinks the whole asymmetry is a cognitive illusion from comparing the wrong cases.

My working theory is that the debate has stalled because everyone treats the asymmetry as one unified intuition when it's actually a composite of several judgments — a general harm/benefit asymmetry, a person-affecting intuition, and a background unease about doing something to someone without their consent. In the standard thought experiments those judgments all point the same way, so the disagreement never gets a chance to come apart, and people end up arguing about different things while thinking they share a single intuition.

So is that diagnosis right, or is the debate a genuinely substantive disagreement about fundamental claims — value, duty, or person-affecting reasons — that just happens to be hard? If my composite diagnosis is roughly right, what kind of question or case would force the strands to seperate so the debate could actually make progress — or should I accept that this is a bedrock intuition that won't move, and write my character accordingly?
