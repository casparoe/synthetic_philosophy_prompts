I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a reporting trip to a transplant unit has left me with a puzzle I can't shake. The unit runs a living-donor programme: healthy people have real surgery, with real risk, to give a kidney to someone they've never met, for nothing. Everyone involved calls this generous. Across the city there are clinics that pay people to give blood plasma — needles, an hour in a chair, some discomfort, not much risk — and no one treats that as a moral scandal. But if the hospital proposed paying a living donor the market rate for a kidney, it would be illegal in almost every country, and people would describe it as repugnant, exploitation of the poor, treating the body as a commodity. Meanwhile, no one treats paying a scaffolder or a night-shift nurse as repugnant, even though those jobs carry their own bodily risks and are mostly done by people who need the money.

So the ordinary position seems to be: paying for an organ is repugnant, but paying someone to risk their body in a job is fine, and giving the organ away for free is fine. Its not the organ, and its not the payment, and its not the risk — or at least none of them alone can carry the weight. I keep thinking about two people from the same street: one sells plasma twice a week for a small fee and is called a donor; the other offers to sell a kidney so her family can keep their home, and the buyer is called a criminal and she is called a victim of the system. What principle, if any, draws that line, and does the principle survive contact with the plasma case and the scaffolder case, or is the whole thing just history and squeamishness?

Please write the whole answer in British English.
