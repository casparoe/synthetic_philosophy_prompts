Im trying to wrap my head around a weird thing about Seneca. In his essay On Benefits (the Stewart translation, De Beneficiis), early in Book I, he says:

"for a benefit is a loan, the repayment of which depends merely upon the good feeling of the debtor. To misuse a benefit like a spendthrift is most shameful, because we do not need our wealth but only our intention to set us free from the obligation of it; for a benefit is repaid by being acknowledged. Yet while they are to blame who do not even show so much gratitude as to acknowledge their debt, we ourselves are to blame no less. We find many men ungrateful, yet we make more men so, because at one time we harshly and reproachfully demand some return for our bounty, at another we are fickle and regret what we have given, at another we are peevish and apt to find fault with trifles."

Thats a pretty tough account of what a benefit is: it gets repaid not by handing the thing back, but by the recipient acknowledging it; and the giver can actually produce ingratitude by demanding repayment harshly, or by being fickle or petulant about what he gave.

Now apply that to a real episode from Senecas own life, the one Tacitus tells in Annals XIV. In AD 62, after Burrus died, Seneca asked Nero to let him retire from public life, and he offered to give back much of the wealth Nero had given him over the years. Nero refused, and Tacitus presents the refusal as affectionate-looking on the surface but insincere underneath. Heres the question. Analyze Neros gifts and Senecas offer to return them through the lens of what Seneca says in that passage. By Senecas own criteria, what would count as repayment here: handing the estates back, or simply acknowledging the debt, or something else? And what is Nero doing in the refusal — is it a new benefit, a refusal to be acknowledged, a way of keeping the debt alive, or something the theory doesnt have a clean slot for? More importantly, run the analysis the other way too: does the episode expose something that Senecas theory of benefits misses or misdescribes when one party is an emperor with total power, or when the benefactors good faith is fake? Be explicit about where the theory is silent or has to be forced, rather than making it explain everything.

Also, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on Seneca or Tacitus, and dont present a contested reading — e.g., of Neros refusal as plainly insincere — as if it were the consensus.
