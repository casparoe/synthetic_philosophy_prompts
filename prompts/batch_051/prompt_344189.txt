I serve on the animal welfare and ethics committee of a food company that farms black soldier fly larvae for animal feed and, through a separate division, processes and retails shrimp and crab. Our current welfare policy is organized around a single line: it covers fish, birds, and mammals with a uniform set of housing, handling, and slaughter standards, and it treats every invertebrate as outside the scope of welfare. We are rewriting the policy this fall, and I need to circulate my written opinion before Thursday's meeting so the committee can vote.

Our scientific staff has supplied a white paper arguing that the evidence no longer supports one line. As they summarize it, decapod crustaceans show responses to tissue damage, rapid avoidance learning, and self-protective behavior, and several farmed insect species show forms of associative learning and responses to injury that are at least consistent with valenced states (states that feel good or bad to the animal), though at a level of behavioral complexity orders of magnitude below a vertebrate. The paper urges a graded sentience framework: rather than a threshold of membership, each group would be assigned moral weight proportional to the product of its estimated capacity for welfare, as indicated by markers such as valenced learning and behavioral trade-offs, and our confidence in that estimate; the strength of our obligations to a group—space, density, slaughter methods, monitoring—would then track weight rather than protected-class status.

The committee is split three ways. One faction wants to keep the existing vertebrate line, arguing that any grading invites a slippery slope. A second faction wants a binary extension: draw the line below decapods, give them the full suite of standards, and continue excluding insects entirely. A third faction favors the graded framework but agrees it needs a floor, so that obviously non-sentient systems—a thermostat, a simple regulatory algorithm, welfare-irrelevant automation—cannot be pulled into the weighting scheme by analogy. Please draft the committee's decision memo: recommend one concrete policy course with a time frame, give the reasoning for it, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration against that recommendation. Format the memo as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; lead with the decision itself.
