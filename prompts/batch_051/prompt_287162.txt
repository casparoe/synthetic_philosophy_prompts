I'm teaching an upper-level undergraduate unit on conditionals, and I've been looking for a film that dramatizes the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals rather than merely illustrating it. Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life seems to me to do more than illustrate. The Pottersville sequence rests on a chain of counterfactual claims: had George Bailey never been born, his brother Harry would have drowned as a child, the men on the transport Harry later saved would have died, Mary would have become an unmarried librarian, timid and alone, and Bedford Falls would have become Pottersville. Clarence does not merely assert these conditionals; he takes George to a world in which they hold and lets him inspect it. That is a remarkable dramatic commitment: the film treats a counterfactual world as something one can visit and use as evidence, as if there were a determinate closest world in the Stalnaker–Lewis sense and a providential selection function picking it out. At the same time, the film leans on something that sounds like an indicative generalization: a single life reaches into many other lives, and when that life is removed it leaves a hole in the lives around it.

I want you to read the film through the lens of the indicative/counterfactual distinction. In your reading, first lay out the distinction carefully: on a Ramsey–Adams picture, an indicative conditional is accepted to the degree one accepts its consequent after supposing its antecedent, so its assertibility tracks conditional probability; a counterfactual conditional, by contrast, needs a different evaluation and, on the standard possible-worlds semantics, a similarity ordering over worlds. Then use that machinery to say what the film is actually doing. Does the visit to Pottersville count as evidence for the counterfactuals Clarence asserts, and under what semantics of counterfactuals could it count? Does the film help itself to a similarity metric — or rather a providence metric — that a Lewis-style account would call far more determinate than anything the semantics of counterfactuals is entitled to? Where is the film's indicative generalization doing the work, and is the film licensed to move from that generalization to its particular counterfactual claims about George? I am less interested in a final verdict than in a reading that is faithful to both the film's dramatic structure and the philosophy of conditionals: name the specific scenes that carry the argument, and be honest about where the film resists the philosophical machinery or oversimplifies it. Please paraphrase scenes rather than quoting dialogue, since I will check the argument against the film myself.
