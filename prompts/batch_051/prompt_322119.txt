I'm a software engineer (never took a philosophy class, so sorry if I'm missing an obvious distinction). I work on identity verification and KYC-ish stuff, and when I draw a data flow between a person and a government agency, I have to pick which side gets to inspect the other one's data. I keep running into the same disagreement about which side should be see-through and which side gets to hide, and I can't tell whether it has a name.

Concretely: with German partners the default seems to be that a person's data is closed to the state unless someone can point to a specific legal ground for opening it — the exposure itself is the harm, even if no one can show a concrete misuse. With US partners people are much more relaxed about the government holding files on citizens, but they treat the government's own secrecy as the main threat: the state's files should be see-through to the public, but that doesn't mean the public has to be see-through to the state. And then there's the Chinese social credit setup, where the state scores you and trying to hide from the scoring is itself treated as evidence that you're untrustworthy.

All three seem to disagree about the same underlying thing: which side of the citizen–state pair gets to hide by default. But I can't tell whether that's a real dimension with a recognized name, or whether I'm forcing three seperate issues into one category. If there is a name, please don't just give it and stop — I want the boundaries. Are the US/German difference and the Chinese case instances of one phenomenon, or just two things I've placed next to each other? And is this a spectrum you could locate a person on, or are the three positions just different games with no shared scale?
