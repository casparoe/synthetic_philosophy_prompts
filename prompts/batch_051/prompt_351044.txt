You are a professional philosopher of biology with experience writing operational guidance for field biologists. A state wildlife agency faces a recurring problem: its laws, permit conditions, survey protocols, and management plans repeatedly require it to count individual organisms, and its staff cannot agree on what the relevant individual is. The people who will use your output are ecologists and conservation officers with no philosophical training. Decisions come up roughly monthly — listing reviews, take permits, population surveys, disease notifications, intervention plans — and each decision must be defensible in an administrative record and explainable to a non-biologist section chief who signs off on it. A single case must be runnable in under an hour.

Your task is to write them a reusable decision procedure for assigning biological individuality for regulatory and management purposes. Ground the procedure in the philosophical debate about biological individuality rather than handing them a single definition. Work with at least these criteria, and add others only if the procedure needs them:

- physiological or functional integration — whether the parts jointly maintain a coordinated living system;
- evolutionary individuality — whether the thing varies, reproduces, and is sorted by natural selection as a unit;
- genetic uniformity and common fate — whether the parts share a genome and rise or fall together;
- the hologenomic or holobiont perspective — whether a host together with its persistent symbionts should be treated as the unit.

The deliverable is a procedure in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when the procedure stops with a definite answer, and when it escalates to a person because the remaining issue is one of policy or value rather than science. Explicitly label each step as empirical, interpretive, or normative. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure, and where the procedure will misfire. Do not let the procedure quietly smuggle in one metaphysics of individuality as if it were the only one.

After presenting the procedure, run it on three cases:

1. A protected coral species: a listing petition asks the agency to count each coral colony, including its algal symbionts, as one individual, and to treat bleaching as harm to that one individual.
2. A eusocial ant colony described as a superorganism, in a pesticide risk assessment that requires a count of individual animals at risk.
3. A large clonal aspen stand — hundreds of above-ground stems connected by a shared root system, genetically nearly identical — proposed for partial disturbance. The applicant argues that the stand is one individual, so the impact should be assessed as affecting a single organism.

Make sure at least one of the three applications returns an answer the practitioner would not have reached by intuition; say plainly which one, and say whether the procedure should be trusted or revised when it goes against intuition in that way.

Close by stating what the procedure cannot settle: it should not masquerade as an answer to the deeper biological and metaphysical question of what an organism really is, and it should make clear which policy values — precaution, conservation of genetic resources, animal welfare, administrative simplicity — must be supplied from outside the philosophy.

Before writing the final procedure, spend time brainstorming how each of the four criteria classifies corals, ant colonies, and clonal plants, and think through the hardest cases where the criteria conflict. Then produce the polished procedure and the three worked applications.
