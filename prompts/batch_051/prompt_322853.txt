I've been working through Dennett's account of the three stances — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — along with his claim that the intentional stance is not merely a useful fiction. As I understand it, the argument is that when treating a system as an agent with beliefs, desires, and some degree of rationality lets us compress and predict its behavior, the stance is picking out real patterns in the world, in roughly the sense that centers of gravity or biological functions are real. The standard illustrations are chess-playing programs, thermostats, and similar artifacts whose design makes rational-agent ascription pay off.

Now consider a contemporary large language model, trained by next-token prediction over an enormous corpus of human text. Users and developers routinely describe such systems as knowing things, misunderstanding queries, or trying to be helpful, and those ascriptions do seem to help predict and steer the model's behavior within a conversation. But two features look as though they strain the original picture. First, the regularities that make the ascriptions useful appear to have been imported from the human authors of the training corpus rather than generated by the system's own goals, since the system was never trained to hold beliefs at all. Second, the model has no stable set of beliefs or desires across contexts: what it says can be made to depend radically on the prompt, and one can elicit flatly incompatible claims from the same model in adjacent conversations while the intentional stance keeps working locally. Does Dennett's argument for the reality of intentional states survive this development?
