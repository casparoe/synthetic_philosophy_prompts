I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about cryptocurrency, and the argument kept circling back to an unlikely figure: the philosopher and Bishop of Cloyne, George Berkeley. My friend maintained that Berkeley would have been an enthusiastic Bitcoiner. His case was that Berkeley, in his economic writings on Ireland in the 1730s, denied that gold and silver are wealth in themselves; he treated money as a mere instrument for reckoning and transferring purchasing power, while locating the real wealth of a nation in its land, labor, industry, and produce. The crucial step, my friend insisted, had already been taken once someone accepted that money is a sign rather than a store of intrinsic value, and Bitcoin is simply the purest form of that sign, with no commodity and no government behind it.

I pushed back. On my reading, Berkeley's proposal was not that an unbacked token could serve as money, but that Ireland should put its real assets into circulation by issuing paper notes secured on land, so that the country's productive capacity could do the work that scarce coin could not. What gave the paper its value was its relation to land and future produce; it was a claim on the economy's actual wealth, not a free-floating convention. I also remembered that Berkeley emphasized circulation and the employment of idle resources, and worried about money sitting still, which seems to cut against the practice of holding Bitcoin as a speculative store of value rather than spending it as a means of payment. And yet I could not dismiss my friend entirely, because Berkeley did make the semiotic character of money central to his account, and one could argue that any sign stabilized by established use fits his framework.

Here is what I want to work out. Suppose Berkeley were brought to the present, fully informed about modern fiat standards, bank-created credit money, and public blockchains, with his economic commitments from the 1730s intact. Would he regard modern fiat currency, bank credit, or Bitcoin as the fulfillment of his theory of money, as a perversion of it, or as phenomena his framework cannot cleanly classify? More importantly, where would his own principles force a definite answer, and where would they underdetermine it? I am not asking for a bibliography or for what scholars have said; I want a reasoned extrapolation from the doctrines themselves, attending to the places where the framework does and does not settle the question.
