I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about Malthus — the economist who argued back around 1800 that human population grows geometrically but food production only grows arithmetically, so sooner or later there's a huge famine and mass misery. Obviously that hasn't happened globally yet (industrial agriculture, synthetic fertilizer, the Green Revolution, etc.), and whenever I bring that up people just say he wasn't wrong, he was just early. As a programmer, that smells like the same move as "my code isnt buggy, it just hasn't run long enough."

I have no formal philosophy background, so keep the jargon light — but please act as an expert on philosophy of science, especially on how long-run predictions get tested and when it's actually legitimate to rescue a theory versus when you're just moving the goalposts. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a bit first about what separates a legitimate rescue from an ad hoc excuse.

Here's the list of defences I've seen people use for Malthus. Could you rank them from most intellectually honest to least (tiers are fine too), and give a sentence or two for each one on why it lands where it does?

1. Right but early: the exponential-versus-linear gap is real, it just operates on a longer timescale than he thought, so we might still hit the wall eventually.
2. Changed premises: technology (synthetic fertilizer, the Haber process, etc.) made food production grow exponentially too, and a model isnt wrong just because an outside variable changed.
3. It was conditional: Malthus's claim was about what happens if population is unchecked, and things like delayed marriage count as the theory's own preventive checks — so no catastrophe is the theory succeeding, not failing.
4. It happened locally: famines in particular places are the positive checks showing up where the preventive checks failed.
5. Spirit, not letter: the real claim is just that finite resources can't support unlimited exponential growth, so it's a general limits idea that doesnt stand or fall on any specific food-supply timetable.

Also, where would you draw the line between a fair "the world changed in a way he couldn't have predicted" and "we've made this thing unfalsifiable"? Or is that the wrong question to ask about a prediction this long-term?
