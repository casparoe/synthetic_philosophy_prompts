I work on the permits side at a state environmental agency, and my unit has to decide maybe a dozen times a year whether to impose conditions on a discharge, a pesticide registration, or an industrial process because of possible environmental harm. We keep invoking the precautionary principle, but it's starting to mean whatever the speaker wants. One common statement I've seen paraphrases it as: when there's a threat of serious or irreversible damage, scientific uncertainty shouldn't be an excuse for putting off affordable preventive steps. But around here "precaution" sometimes means "if there's any biologicaly plausible mechanism of harm, however thin, block it," sometimes means "only act when the damage would be severe and the fix is cheap," and sometimes it's just a tiebreaker when the evidence is perfectly balanced. The cases are things like a novel industrial chemical turning up in a river at very low concentrations, where the lab can't tell us whether the level is dangerous; or a new pesticide that looks safer than the one it would replace except for one understudied effect on native bees.

I'd like you to write us a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off judgement: a checklist or word-flowchart that a non-philosopher staff scientist can run in maybe two days per case, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a technical one. The stakes are high—decisions get challenged in court and affect local jobs—and our director has to sign off, so the procedure has to be defensible without citing philosophers. Please run it on three cases: (1) the low-concentration novel chemical, (2) the pesticide swap, and (3) an emergency request to use an old broad-spectrum chemical, currently banned, to stop an invasive beetle that's killing a watershed's trees. Try to make at least one of the worked cases land somewhere I'm not going to like, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle.
