Here's an asymmetry in how people react to two rescues that I keep going back and forth on. In both cases a man's wife and a stranger are drowning and he can only save one. First case: he saves his wife without appearing to think about it, and later says, it wasn't a decision, she's my wife, of course I saved her. People tend to find that exactly right, even the best thing about him. Second case: same scene, but he visibly hesitates, works through the question impartially, concludes saving his wife will do more good overall since their kids need her, and saves her. Same act, same outcome, and the second man can cite a moral justification where the first just has a partial reflex. Yet the second man makes people uneasy, as if his wife would have a fair complaint if she knew what passed through his head. Is there a defensible principle behind judging these two differently, or is it just a prejudice in favor of unreflective love? Give me the strongest principle you can find for the asymmetry, then your honest verdict on wether it holds up. If you defend it, handle the version where the second man's calculation is accurate and made silently, so the only real difference is the thought he had while deciding. If you reject the asymmetry, say what the unease is tracking and why that doesn't justify judging him differently from the first man.
