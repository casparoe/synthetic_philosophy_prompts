my friend and i spent last night arguing about Wang Yangming's idea of the unity of knowledge and action, and now i think the whole thing comes down to one hard case. the way he put it: there is no real gap between knowing something and doing it. if you say you know stealing is wrong but you steal anyway, you never actually knew it — you had a phrase you could repeat, like a trained parrot. real knowledge shows up in how you act, not in what you can say. i think that's either too strong or secretly changing the subject, but he wouldn't budge.

so here's the borderline case i want you to work through. my brother in law, call him D, is genuinly articulate about how his gambling is wrecking his life. if you gave him a written exam on the harms — the odds, the way losses make him chase more losses, what it's doing to his wife and kids — he'd score basically perfectly and say all of it with obvious conviction. when a friend of his is in the same situation, D gives him sharp, correct, urgent advice to quit. and then D gambles again that weekend. he isn't threatened, he isn't in some manic episode, he just does it, and then he hates himself for it afterwards.

under Wang Yangming's criterion, does D know that gambling is ruining his life? go through the pieces rather than just giving a verdict. be precise about what acting in line with the knowledge has to mean here, because i think that's where the whole dispute hides. is D giving his friend the advice itself a kind of action that shows knowledge? does the self-hatred after count as his judgment showing up somewhere, even if it comes too late? if the answer really is no, he doesn't know, then what's left of the ordinary distinction between knowing something and being able to follow through on it — is Wang trying to redefine knowledge, or is he saying the distinction never did any real work? and tell me whether this case shows the criterion is too demanding, or just that my friend was applying it sloppily. while you're at it, how would Wang himself most likely sort D, the case of someone who acts rightly only because they're terrified of punishment, and a full-blown compulsion case — are all three not-knowledge in the same way, or does something different go wrong in each?
