You're a professional philosopher who works on well-being; start by brainstorming two or three ways of stating the paradox of hedonism before you pick one, and think carefully about which version actually does clinical work.

I'm a therapist in a small private practice — maybe twenty clients a week, 50-minute sessions, and I plan in the ten minutes between them. I keep hitting the same fork: a client says they want to be happier, and I have to choose between leaning into that directly (mood tracking, scheduling pleasant activities, gratitude exercises) or steering them away from it (committing to valued activities, relationships, hard goals, and letting happiness show up as a byproduct). The paradox of hedonism — the idea that aiming straight at happiness tends to undermine it — suggests the second path is often right, but not always: sometimes people are just understimulated or clinically depressed and direct mood work helps.

Turn this into a reusable decision procedure I can actually run in ten minutes. Give me a checklist or ordered set of tests, and build in the hard cases: distinguishing depression from hedonic adaptation, goals that look meaningful but are really avoidance, clients who use "meaning" talk to stay miserable, and the point where judgment just can't be eliminated — mark those. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases, including one where it would make me do the opposite of what I'd naturally do. Be honest about what the procedure can't handle, and write it so a clinician with no philosophy backround could use it, and so it can catch its own mistakes — I have no supervisor checking my work.
