Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have turned to philosophy and find myself re-examining an argument I once took for granted: the standard claim that reputation can sustain honest dealing among strangers, because a trader who cheats destroys a record of good conduct that would have brought him future gains, so honesty is rational even in one-off transactions. That argument presupposed a small, stable community in which identities persist and past dealings are remembered. Most stranger-to-stranger exchange now happens on platforms where a counterpart's history is compressed into a single score attached to an account that can be abandoned at trivial cost, where fresh accounts and fabricated reviews are cheap, and where a reputation does not follow a merchant from one platform to another. I want to determine whether the old argument survives this change. Which premise is genuinely under pressure—persistent identity, the observability of past conduct, or the costliness of exit—and can a repaired, weaker version still say something informative about what a karma score actually signals? If you believe the argument survives, show precisely why the cheapness of exit does not sever the connection between good conduct and future advantage.
