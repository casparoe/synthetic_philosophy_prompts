I've been arguing with a friend about the discursive dilemma, and I came up with a variation I can't resolve. The usual version: three judges have to decide whether a contractor gets paid. There are two premises — the contract was signed, and the work was done to spec — and the conclusion, pay up, which is true only if both premises are true. You get the familiar result where a majority accepts each premise but a majority also rejects the conclusion, because only one judge accepts both; premise-based aggregation and conclusion-based aggregation come apart. That paradox depends on forcing every judge to give a flat yes/no.

So I want to swap the yes/no votes for credences. Each judge writes down a number from 0 to 1 for each premise, thinking of a yes vote as anything above .5, and (for the sake of argument) treating the two premises as probabilistically independent, so a judge's credence in the conclusion is just the product of their two premise-credences. Take three judges with premise-credences (1, .3), (.3, 1), and (.9, .9). If you aggregate by premises, you average the credences on each premise first and then see whether the conclusion follows; if you aggregate by conclusion, you compute each judge's own conclusion-credence and average those. Unless I'm doing the arithmetic wrong, these two procedures still point in opposite directions, with the first paying the contractor and the second not.

What I want to know is whether this is still the discursive dilemma or something new. Does moving to degrees of belief dissolve the original problem, or does it just reveal that the original dilemma was always a special case of a deeper choice about whether to aggregate before or after imposing the logical constraint? And once the judges' uncertainties are out in the open like this, is there any principled reason to prefer one aggregation rule over the other, or does the dilemma get worse because now we can see exactly how much information each rule throws away?
