I'm taking a class on art and ethics, and last week we read the opening of Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* in the Maude translation. I was honestly suprisingly unsettled by this passage:

> Reformers (early Christians, Mohammedans, Puritans, and others) have perceived that art bound people to the old faith, and they were angry with art. They diligently chipped the noses from statues and images, and were wroth with ceremonies, decorations, stained-glass windows, and processions.

Then I happened to rewatch a documentary about the Taliban blowing up the Bamiyan Buddhas in 2001. They dynamited two huge sixth-century Buddha statues carved into a cliff in Afghanistan despite international pleas, and the justification was basically that the statues were false idols. My professor presented it as pure vandalism, full stop. But after reading Tolstoy I'm not sure the case is as clean as I thought.

I want you to analyze the Bamiyan destruction through Tolstoy's framework. In the passage he lists early Christians, Mohammedans, and Puritans as people who attacked art because it bound people to an old faith, so he is not treating iconoclasm as mere philistinism. What would his own positive theory of art—especially his criteria of infectiousness, sincerity, and the moral or religious feeling the art transmits—say about the Buddhas? Was the Taliban's action an instance of the truthfulness-over-art choice Tolstoy describes, or does it fail his criteria for a legitimate rejection of art? And what does the episode do to Tolstoy's view: does it show the danger of letting truthfulness become an unmediated reason to destroy art, or does it actually confirm his worry that we preserve false-religion art because we prize aesthetic pleasure? Try to make the mapping precise rather than just saying "both sides."
