The question we set was:

“State the independence axiom of expected utility theory precisely. Then explain how the common-consequence version of the Allais paradox appears to violate it, and assess whether the paradox shows that expected utility theory fails descriptively, normatively, both, or neither. Engage at least one alternative theory.”

The student answered:

“The independence axiom says that if a person prefers lottery A to lottery B, then for any lottery C and any p strictly between 0 and 1, the person must prefer the mixture pA + (1−p)C to pB + (1−p)C. In the standard Allais common-consequence pair, most people choose a sure $1,000,000 over a lottery offering a .10 chance of $5,000,000, a .89 chance of $1,000,000, and a .01 chance of nothing. In the second pair, after the common .89 chance of $1,000,000 is replaced by a common .89 chance of nothing, the same people tend to choose a .10 chance of $5,000,000 over a .11 chance of $1,000,000. Independence says such a common substitution cannot reverse the preference, so the modal pattern violates the axiom.

This shows expected utility theory is descriptively false, because no utility function over money, however concave, can reconcile the two modal choices with the theory. The problem is not the assumption that utility is linear in money; the contradiction arises for any assignment of utility to the payoffs. The natural descriptive alternative is Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory, and its explanation of the Allais reversal runs through loss aversion in the value function: in the first pair the .01 chance of nothing is read as a potential loss relative to the sure $1,000,000, and losses weigh disproportionately. Normatively, however, the violation is only a fact about fallible choosers. The independence axiom is required for consistency among mixtures, and anyone who violates it can be turned into a money pump. So expected utility theory should be retained as the normatively correct theory, with prospect theory describing a bias.”
