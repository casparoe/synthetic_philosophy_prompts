In a seminar on distributive justice, a classmate dismissed a colleague's objection by saying "hard cases make bad law." The objection was that a proposed principle for allocating scarce medical resources—chosen partly for its simplicity and transitivity—implied, in a contrived case involving one ventilator and a surgeon who could be left to die so that five patients could use his organs, that the surgeon should not be saved. "That's exactly the kind of far-fetched hypothetical where the principle breaks down," the classmate said. "Hard cases make bad law." I want to know what that slogan could actually be claiming, because it seemed to do a lot of argumentative work while remaining vague. Lay out the distinct precise claims "hard cases make bad law" might be making as a menu—for example about the reliability of intuitions elicited by unusual cases, about which cases a principle should be built or tested on, about the authority of extreme hypotheticals in philosophy, and about rule design and precedent. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and explain whether it would actually support dismissing the ventilator/surgeon counterexample in a philosophical argument about a principle of allocation. Conclude by saying which reading the classmate was most likely relying on, and whether that reading survives scrutiny in a context where considered judgments about cases are often treated as evidence for or against principles.
