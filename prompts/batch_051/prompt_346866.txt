For a graduate seminar on instrumental rationality, write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated decision theorists about Warren Quinn's self-torturer case. Both should be serious interlocutors, not strawmen. The first should argue that the agent's nontransitive pairwise preferences—preferring each adjacent setting over the one below it while preferring the initial setting over the final one—can be rationally permissible: the marginal inducement is salient at each step while the worsening is too small to register, and transitivity should not be imposed as a formal constraint when it conflicts with the agent's actual local reasons. The second should argue that the case instead shows why transitivity functions as a necessary coherence requirement: the agent antecedently knows the settings accumulate and that he would pay to undo the whole sequence, so his local choices are predictably self-defeating, and he should bind himself, adopt a global constraint against cyclical preferences, or otherwise refuse to treat an imperceptible worsening as no worsening. They should engage each other's actual arguments rather than restating positions. Include a substantive exchange about the money-pump objection and another about whether the entire sequence could be rationally chosen as a single package even if no adjacent step is rationally required. Let them close by considering whether the case's force depends on the imperceptibility of the changes or would survive if each step were perceptible but very small.
