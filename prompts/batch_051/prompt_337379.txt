Okay here's a thing I keep going around in circles on. In Consciousness Explained, Dennett describes the color phi setup: two colored dots flash in sequence across a screen, and subjects report seeing a single dot moving across and changing color somewhere before the second flash would have been available. He says there are two ways the brain could pull this off. One is Orwellian: the person really has a first experience of, say, a green dot moving all the way across, and then after the second flash lands, their memory of that experience gets rewritten so it now includes the color change before the second flash. The other is Stalinesque: the whole stream is delayed, so nothing gets consciously presented until after the second flash, and you're just shown an already-edited version. Dennett's claim is that nothing in the subject's report distinguishes these two, and that this should make us suspicious of the whole idea of a place in the brain where things become conscious.

I want you to treat the color phi phenomenon as a case study for the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction. Map it carefully: what exactly each story commits you to about timing, memory, and the order of events in the brain; what sorts of evidence could, even in principle, tell them apart; and whether the distinction is a real empirical alternative or a confusion generated by the picture of a Cartesian theater. Then tell me what the phenomenon actually shows about the timing of experience. Does it show that experience is edited, that experience is delayed, that the question between those two is ill-posed, or something else? I'm more interested in where the distinction breaks than in a recap of Dennett's view. Also, be honest about which features of the phenomenon the Orwellian/Stalinesque framing is silent on.
