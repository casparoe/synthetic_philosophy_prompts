I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep hearing the same picture used to explain self-representing systems, with the stakes left mostly implicit. One computational neuroscientist put it to me roughly this way. A mind contains a model of itself, and the self is in some sense whatever that model represents; the model and the self each determine the other, the way the two hands in Escher's Drawing Hands each draw the other into existence. But, he added, the loop only avoids being empty because of what he calls the inviolate level: the fixed level below the drawing, the actual pencil strokes on the paper that neither of the drawn hands can reach or revise. On his telling, any system that represents itself needs some such bottom level that its self-reference cannot audit, and that bottom is what keeps the circle from collapsing into paradox. He then used this to license a real conclusion: a self-modeling mind, and a fortiori a self-modifying AI, can never be fully transparent to itself, because something always does the drawing from below.

My editor is skeptical of pictures that sound this neat, and I want to hand back an audit of the analogy rather than a debunking or an endorsement. Please do the following. Choose one explicit target, either the self-modeling mind or a self-modifying AI, whichever you think the analogy handles more faithfully, and say which you chose. Then give a correspondence table: what plays the role of the left hand, the right hand, the drawing as a whole, the paper, and the inviolate level in your chosen target. After that, say where the mapping breaks: which features of the lithograph either fail to appear in the target or appear in the target in a way the analogy wants to exclude. Then identify what the picture quietly imports: for instance, a third party standing outside the system, a bottom level that is fixed rather than merely not-yet-audited, or an assumption that self-reference must be resolved by leveling rather than accepted as circular. Finally, give a verdict on whether the conclusion survives—that self-representation requires an inviolate level and therefore carries a permanent blind spot—once the disanalogies are on the table, and say whether some better analogy would support only a weaker claim. End with a short, candid paragraph on what would change your verdict: which disanalogy, if it turned out not to be a disanalogy after all, would most push you toward the opposite conclusion, or where in your reasoning a failure is most likely.
