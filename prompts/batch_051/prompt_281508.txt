I've been going around in circles on a little argument for monism I tried to reconstruct from some Parmenides lecture notes, and I half-suspect it's being sneaky but can't pin down where. It goes:

(1) You can think only about what is; you cannot think about what is not.
(2) If there were at least two things, pick any two of them, a and b. Since they're two, a is not b.
(3) So to think of a and b as two distinct things, you'd have to think that a is not b.
(4) But thinking that a is not b is thinking about what is not — namely, about a's not being b.
(5) By (1), you can't think about what is not.
(6) So you can't think of any two things as distinct; plurality is literally unthinkable.
(7) Still, you're thinking something right now — this very thought exists, and it's about something that is.
(8) So there is exactly one thing.

I find (4) a bit to quick, but I can't decide weather the real problem is there, in (1), or in the jump from unthinkability to nonexistence at the end. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails first? If the crux is an ambiguity in 'what is not,' give the strongest reading of (1) that makes it true and check whether each later step still goes through under that reading.
