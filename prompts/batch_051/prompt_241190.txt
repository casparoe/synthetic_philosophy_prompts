I keep going back and forth on a parallel I noticed, and I cant tell if its a real connection or just me pattern matching. In Hume's essay "Of the Delicacy of Taste and Passion" (from his Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary, Part 1) he says:

"I am persuaded, that nothing is so proper to cure us of this delicacy of passion, as the cultivating of that higher and more refined taste, which enables us to judge of the characters of men, of compositions of genius, and of the productions of the nobler arts."

So Hume's idea seems to be that there's a kind of taste that is trainable, involves judging characters and artworks, and can actually rein in a too-reactive emotional temperament. The parallel I think I see is with imaginative resistance: people have no problem imagining dragons, time travel, or talking animals in fiction, but they often refuse or feel unable to imagine a story where, say, a character's cruelty is genuinely presented as morally beautiful and admirable. That also looks like a trained or cultivated refusal to let some parts of the mind be led by the fiction.

But maybe it's only a superficial word match, because Hume is talking about aesthetic taste curing emotional sensitivity, while imaginative resistance is usually about people not going along with the moral point of view a story invites. Or maybe imaginative resistance is just what happens when someone with this Humean cultivated taste hits a story that offends the taste. I'm not sure.
