Suppose that over the next two generations the American South adopted something like Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, its working-through of the past: official acknowledgment at the state level, public memorials and museums centered on slavery and Jim Crow rather than the Confederacy, school curricula that treat that history as central rather than incidental, and a broad elite consensus that the wrong belongs to the region's identity rather than being a smear imposed from outside. My credence that this would, by 2100, leave the region's public culture substantially more honest about that history and less attached to a romanticized Confederate past than the present patchier approach would is about 0.35. My friend puts the same credence at about 0.7.

I want your own credence, but more than that I want the decomposition. The sub-questions I can see include at least these: how much the German outcome depended on military defeat and foreign occupation, and whether that dependence transfers to a case where the defeated side was not a separate state but was politically reintegrated; which comparison actually fits the South best, postwar Germany, postwar Japan, or Turkey, and how much the answer shifts the probability; whether public memory can be deliberately changed from above at all, or whether acknowledgment has to be forced upward by the groups that were wronged; whether a state-led reckoning would be experienced by many white Southerners as an accusation and would harden attachment to the Lost Cause rather than loosen it; and whether the fact that the wrong is still materially present in economic and residential inequality makes symbolic measures more necessary or less effective. Give a rough probability for each sub-question, say how they combine into your final number, and identify the sub-question on which my friend and I most likely diverge. Then tell me what evidence from the next twenty years would move your number most, and in which direction.
