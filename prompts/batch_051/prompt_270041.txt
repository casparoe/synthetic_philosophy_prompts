I've been trying to get clear about what we owe to people we bring into existance, and my gut on individual cases doesn't add up. I keep hearing the test that a child is only harmed if they're made worse off than they would otherwise have been, but my reactions don't respect it. A couple has a daughter, Mia, while one of them is still in grad school. Mia grows up in a cramped apartment with money worries but in a loving home, and as an adult she's glad to have been born. If they'd waited two years, they'd have had a different child with a much easier start. I don't think Mia has any complaint, and I don't think her parents did anything wrong, though waiting would have produced a better-off person. But now consider a strip mine. A provincial government approves it for a modest short-term boost, but it leaves the region contaminated for two centuries. People born there in 150 years will have childhoods marked by illness and poverty, though their lives will still be worth living. Had the mine been blocked, different people would have been born there. I think the decision was seriously wrong, and the fact that no specific person is worse off than they'd have been doesn't settle it.

Then there are two cases that feel different to me even though I can't justify why. A woman trying to conceive keeps taking a medication that causes any child conceived that month to be born with a mild hearing impairment. She doesn't want to interrupt the treatment; her daughter Anya is born with the impairment and a happy life. I want to say she wronged Anya, that Anya has a grievance, even though if she'd waited a month a different child would exist, which sounds exactly like Mia's case. And for contrast, a woman drinks heavily for the first two months without realizing she's pregnant. Her son is born with fetal alcohol affects, has a difficult but worthwhile life, and would have been the same child without them had she stopped earlier. That one I'm sure about: she harmed him. A friend says the only distinction I could be drawing between Anya and Mia is between something done to the child's body and the circumstances the child is born into, and she doubts that holds up. She might be right.

Is there a principle that gets all four of these, or at least most of them? If not, which verdicts should I drop? And what does whatever principle you land on say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a couple doing IVF picks, against the clinic's mild recommendation, an embryo that carries a gene for a condition requiring lifelong medication over another embryo without it, and the resulting child has a good life. Does that child have a complaint, and was the choice wrong in the way the strip mine was, in the way Anya's mother's choice was, or not at all?
