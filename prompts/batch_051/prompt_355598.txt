I'm a PhD student working on collective responsibility and I've been rereading Jaspers on German guilt. He seems to hold both that only individuals can be morally guilty and that every citizen bears political guilt for what their state does, and I keep losing the line between those two, because the following argument keeps looking compelling to me:

(1) A state acts as a unit — declaring war, signing treaties, imposing tariffs are it's acts, not just a summary of what officials are doing.
(2) If an entity can act, it can be blameworthy for what it does; we blame firms and clubs, not just their members.
(3) Therefore a state can be guilty of what it does.
(4) Guilt doesn't simply lapse as membership turns over; it persists until it is discharged by atonement or repair.
(5) Germany now is the same continuing state that did the Holocaust, in something like the way a firm remains the same legal agent while all its staff change.
(6) Therefore Germany is still guilty of the Holocaust today.
(7) Therefore the people who make up Germany now share in that guilt.

Tell me whether this argument is valid. If it isn't, say exactly which step or transition fails and how it should be revised so that it tracks Jaspers's distinction rather than collapsing it. If it is valid, defend the step you think is weakest. Keep the answer to at most 250 words, and write in British English.
