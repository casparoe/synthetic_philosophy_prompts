I'm a software engineer and over the last couple years I've gotten weirdly into economic history, mostly via podcasts. The thing I keep coming back to is the Great Divergence debate: why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain (or northwest Europe) rather than, say, the Yangtze Delta, which by a lot of measures looked roughly as advanced in the 1700s? What's strange to me is that this argument has been grinding on for decades and people don't really change there minds. Everyone seems to know the same facts. One camp blames institutions — property rights, legal stability, a state that could protect merchants and enforce contracts. Another camp says the real story is geography and resources: Britain had conveniently placed coal, plus the New World ghost acres that let it offload its land constraint, and China's non-industrialization was more about resource endowments and the fiscal priorities of a huge continental empire. Then there's a third camp that says the comparison itself is confused, because China and Britain aren't natural units and the question quietly slides between "why did Europe industrialize," "why didn't China industrialize," and "why Britain specifically."

As an engineer this feels to me like two people debugging different services while staring at the same log line. I have a hunch the stalemate is because "why Britain and not China" is a contrastive question where you can pick the baseline and the contrast class differently and end up making completely different factors salient — so there's no single empirical finding that could ever settle it. But I know I might be doing the classic thing of assuming a disagreement is "just semantics" when it might really be about evidence. So I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the impasse here a hidden factual disagreement, a difference about what counts as a good explanation in history, a difference about which counterfactual you're even running, or is the question itself (including the Needham question, which is roughly why modern science didn't get going in China) just not well-posed? And given that diagnosis, what would actual progress look like: a particular comparison, a measurement, a reformulated question — or a reason to stop expecting the debate to converge?
