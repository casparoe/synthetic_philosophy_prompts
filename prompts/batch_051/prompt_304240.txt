Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing. If someone rewires their brain or their reward function so they feel wildly successful without doing anything—wireheading, or tampering with the reward signal itself—we treat it as a cautionary tale about a life wasted. But if someone spends their life arranging to have the same internal signal go off as often as possible—chasing promotions they don't care about, checking follower counts, grinding for rank-up animations, moving their own goalposts so they always feel on track—we call it ambition, self-development, or at worst a slightly unhealthy relationship with their phone. The main difference seems to be that one version admits it's just aiming at the feeling, while the other keeps the feeling hidden inside a story about goals and progress. Is there a principle that says the hidden version is better, or is that just an aesthetic preference for indirection? I want you to name the best feature that could do the work—maybe that indirect rewards track something real, maybe that public legibility matters, maybe that the self-deception itself has value—and then test it against messy cases: the startup founder who has retrained themself so only valuation numbers produce relief, the runner who hacks every route so it feels like the finish, the volunteer who does charity because it's the most reliable hit they know. Where should the line actually go, and do everyday judgments put it there or just somewhere comfortable?
