I'm a journalist who covers the intersection of machine learning and the social world, and I am planning a long essay on what the Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal can teach us about the enterprise of inferring preferences from behavior. I know the basic contours of the scandal well enough: in the mid-2010s it emerged that retail bank employees, under intense pressure to meet aggressive cross-selling quotas, had opened millions of deposit and credit-card accounts without customers' consent; thousands of lower-level employees were dismissed, and senior executives eventually forfeited compensation and left the bank. I want to use inverse reinforcement learning as the lens. The working premise of the essay is that the scandal is a kind of natural experiment in reward inference: we have a large population of agents, a richly documented incentive structure, and a long record of observable behavior, much of it plainly not what any of the participants would say they valued.

What I would like from you is a disciplined case analysis rather than a recap of the scandal. First, set out the agents whose preferences an inverse reinforcement learning system might try to recover, such as front-line branch employees, branch managers, regional executives, senior leadership, and perhaps the bank as a corporate agent, and specify for each what the observed behavior actually was. Second, apply the standard assumption that behavior is approximately optimal for some unknown reward function, and say what reward function best rationalizes the employees' account-opening behavior, being precise about whether the inferred reward should be attributed to the individual, the role, or the organization. Third, press on where the mapping breaks: the behavior was produced under a misspecified proxy target and the threat of termination, so the inference that the employees wanted to commit fraud, or even that they wanted to maximize account counts, may be exactly the kind of error the method is prone to when it cannot separate an agent's underlying preferences from the incentive system the agent is caught in. I would like you to bring in the known difficulty that many different reward functions can make the same behavior optimal, and the usual responses to it, such as priors over reward functions or maximum-entropy models of the observed policy, and say whether they would rescue the inference here. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about inverse reinforcement learning and about the broader project of reading preferences off behavior? Does it mainly illustrate the gap between revealed preference and reward hacking, does it suggest that any such system deployed on human organizations needs an explicit model of the principal's objective and of proxy misspecification, or does it point to a deeper limitation? I want the piece to be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Please write the analysis as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
