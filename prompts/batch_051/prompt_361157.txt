Aristotle treats money as a convention (nomisma) whose function is to make heterogeneous goods commensurable so that exchange can satisfy reciprocal need. On this picture, acquisition aimed at meeting the needs of a household is natural and bounded, whereas acquisition pursued for its own sake — above all, making money yield more money — is unnatural. Consider three institutions that arose long after Aristotle's time, each described in terms of the features that would matter to his analysis: (1) fiat money issued by a central bank and managed to target low positive inflation; (2) fractional-reserve banking, in which banks take deposits, pay interest on them, and lend most of the funds out at interest while holding only a fraction in reserve; and (3) a fixed-supply cryptocurrency such as Bitcoin, which is non-sovereign, has no intrinsic use, and is held and traded primarily as an investment. For each institution, reconstruct the verdict Aristotle would most plausibly have given, and specify which of its features would count for it and which against it on his account. Then rank the three from least to most objectionable by Aristotelian standards. Where his commitments pull in opposite directions within a single case, say so rather than forcing a verdict.
