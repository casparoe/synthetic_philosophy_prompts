I keep going back and forth on frugality. The consequentialist reading of it seems clearly right when I think in the abstract: frugality matters by promoting frugality—what counts is that waste gets reduced and resources end up where they do more good, and that gives a clean test for when extravagance is wrong. On that picture it doesn't intrinsically matter whether I am frugal, only what my choices do to the overall amount of frugality and its effects; and pushing other people toward frugality would be fine whenever it improved things. But I have a firm intuition that this leaves something out. It seems wrong to use the most effective means of promoting frugality—shaming a spendthrift friend, denying employees perks, imposing austerity on my family—when those means step all over their choices. And it seems wrong for me to be wasteful even in a case where there are no downstream effects at all. I can't tell whether the consequentialist reading contradicts these intuitions or only a gloss of them, and I don't know whether I should debunk the intuitions, amend the theory so a deontological "be frugal" survives as a constraint on how I may promote frugality, or treat the two as compatible. What would justify each of those moves, and which one should yield? Please just write continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, like a letter.
