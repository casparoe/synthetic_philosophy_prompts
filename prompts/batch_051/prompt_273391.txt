Question:
In this course we have contrasted two accounts of why promises generate moral obligations. On the first, a promise binds because the promisor has voluntarily undertaken an obligation, and its binding force does not depend on the consequences of keeping it. On the second, promises bind because they deliberately lead others to form expectations, and breaking a promise wrongs people by disappointing the expectations one has knowingly created. Apply both accounts to this case: a person routinely arrives ten to fifteen minutes late to casual social plans, and the friends involved have mostly stopped expecting punctuality. Is the person morally criticizable? Compare what the two accounts imply and say whether they diverge.

Student's answer:
Both accounts imply that the chronically late person is morally criticizable, though they locate the wrong differently. On the first account, agreeing to a time is a promise, and each late arrival breaks that promise; because a promise's binding force does not depend on consequences, the smallness or harmlessness of any particular delay cannot cancel the obligation. On the second account the verdict is the same, because by repeatedly arriving late the person trains others to expect lateness, and a disappointed expectation is exactly what this account treats as the wrong. The two accounts therefore converge on the case, which shows that they are really the same account operating at different levels. One might object that the friends no longer expect punctuality, so there is no expectation of punctuality left to disappoint. But this confuses prediction with acceptance: the friends predict lateness and plan around it; they have not agreed to it, and their normative expectation of punctuality persists. So the chronically late person is morally criticizable on either account.
