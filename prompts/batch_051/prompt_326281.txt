Take the procreative asymmetry at the center of antinatalism. In its standard form it runs as follows: if a person exists, then their pain is bad for them and their pleasure is good for them; but if a person never exists, then their absent pain is good, while their absent pleasure is not bad, because there is no one to be deprived. From this, antinatalists argue that existence always compares unfavorably with nonexistence — existence contains both something bad and something good, while nonexistence contains something good and nothing that is bad — so being brought into existence is always a net harm and procreation is wrong.

I want you to treat the asymmetry itself, rather than the antinatalist conclusion, as the open question, and produce a disciplined map of what would have to be true for it to be false. Separate the four component claims and ask which ones carry the weight and which could be rejected without bringing down the others. Identify what a successful counterexample or argument would actually have to show — for instance, that absent pleasure can be bad even when there is no one to be deprived, or that absent pain is not good even though pain would otherwise have occurred, or that the whole framework of comparing existence with nonexistence is defective. Say which of these routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and what the world would have to look like for each to be true. Distinguish considerations that would decisively refute the asymmetry from considerations that would merely force a qualification, and indicate where a defender of the asymmetry would themselves admit vulnerability. Finally, assess whether the asymmetry has been stated in a way that no observation could refute, and say whether that insulation is a sign of depth or of vacuity. My own instinct is that the two claims about existing people are the most secure and that the real uncertainty lies in whether the absence of pain in nonexistence is genuinely good; I want an honest account of how much that uncertainty matters.
