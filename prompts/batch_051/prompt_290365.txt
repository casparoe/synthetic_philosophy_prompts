I am a PhD student in behavioral public policy, and for the past year I have been embedded in a mid-sized city's health department, where I help screen proposals for "nudge" interventions before they reach the public health ethics review board. The department considers several proposals a week: changing default side options in public cafeterias, reordering forms so a healthier or more prudent option is preselected, rewriting letters about energy use or vaccination to use peer-comparison language, adding graphic warnings, and introducing small cooling-off delays for certain purchases. The officials who run the screen have no formal philosophy training, and they want one reusable checklist they can apply in about fifteen minutes to decide whether a proposal is the sort of intervention that a liberal public body may adopt, and what to do when the answer is unclear. I would like the checklist to be grounded in the paternalism debates rather than in a flat list of "nudge vs. shove" heuristics. In particular, I keep coming back to a passage in Chapter IV of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty. Mill argues there that a person's own good, whether physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant for using compulsion against him, but also that this does not require the state or society to be neutral or silent: we may reason with the person, persuade him, even plead with him, but we may not compel him or impose some disadvantage on him for not doing what we think best. I want that distinction between persuasion and compulsion translated into a concrete screening procedure for the kinds of interventions my department actually sees.

Please write the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "do not proceed," and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (1) making a lower-sodium option the default in hospital vending machines, with a small sign indicating that the regular option is available on the next screen; (2) automatically enrolling new employees in a retirement savings plan at a 4 percent contribution rate with an easy opt-out, where the city is also the employer; and (3) imposing a 48-hour delay, but no outright prohibition, on the purchase of cigarettes within city limits, implemented as a mandatory waiting period before the sale is completed. One of the applications should be a case where the procedure yields an answer the department will not like. Be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls, where the Millian distinction breaks down or needs supplementation, and what the procedure cannot settle.
