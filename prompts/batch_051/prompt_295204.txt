I've ended up in a weirdly strong version of bivalence and I want to know what it would take to knock me out of it. The view, as I hold it, is this: every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false — not just that 'p or not-p' is always right, and not just that we can't point to a well-formed sentence and say it's neither true nor false, but that semantic evaluation is total. Where people see truth-value gaps I think the problem is somewhere else: vague predicates have sharp but unknowable cut-offs; statements about the future are either true already or they aren't really about 'the future' in the problematic way; empty names are a presupposition failure rather than a gap; category mistakes don't express propositions in the first place; and the liar is some kind of expressive defect, context shift, or failure to say anything truth-evaluable.

I know the standard replies to each of these, but I haven't seen a good map of what would actually count against the position taken as a whole. So: what would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, including (a) the linguistic or empirical data that would be hardest to handle, (b) the formal results or semantic arguments that would do real damage, and (c) the things a commited bivalentist like me would themselves have to concede as counting against the view, even if they think the concession is survivable. Also say which rival view would handle each of these better, if you think one would. Then give me a verdict: is the position actually exposed anywhere, or has it been qualified into a form that can't lose? If it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say whether that's a real problem for a view like this or just the normal cost of a semantic theory. And I want you to commit — dont lay out the options and leave me to choose; tell me which defeaters you'd bet on and whether the view as a whole should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
