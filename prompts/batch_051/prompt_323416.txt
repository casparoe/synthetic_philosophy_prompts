I've come to accept a broadly Dennettian view of the self: the self is not a substance or an inner cause of the narrative, but a center of narrative gravity—an abstract, posited protagonist that lets the stories of a life cohere. I want a serious map of what would have to be true for this view to be wrong. Set aside the familiar objection that an abstract object cannot be an agent or a literal cause; I want the less rehearsed pressure points. In particular, examine three: whether narrative competence and selfhood can come apart in episodic amnesia, confabulation, or in beings who seem to be selves without narrative abilities, such as infants and nonhuman animals; whether what binds a self across time is largely non-narrative—bodily, perceptual, affective—with storytelling as post hoc gloss; and whether the social co-authorship and contestation of life stories make the self a negotiated plurality rather than a single center of gravity. Rank these and any other defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones a defender should concede as genuine costs from the ones they may reasonably dismiss, and say whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and, if it has, whether that itself counts against it.
