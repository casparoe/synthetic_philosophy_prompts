I've come around to the view that rational agents should have imprecise credences — instead of one probability per proposition, a set of them, or at least an interval. The usual reasons: Ellsberg urns, Knightian uncertainty, the intuition that a fair coin and a coin you know literally nothing about shouldn't both land at a precise 50/50, since the precise number feels like it smuggles in information you don't have. I've read the LessWrong back-and-forth and keep ending up on the imprecise side.

What I want is a honest map of what would count against this view. Not just "imprecise probabilities are computationally awful" or "people don't literally have intervals in their heads" — I mean the stuff that should actually move a defender. I know dilation is supposed to be embarrassing: you can have an interval for an event, condition on something that feels logically irrelevant, and the interval gets wider no matter which cell you observe. Is that fatal or just a bullet you bite? Then there's the decision-theory problem: once you have a set of probabilities, you need a rule for acting — maximin, maximality, E-admissibility, or whatever rule you prefer — and different rules give different verdicts, which makes me nervous that the view doesn't make determinate predictions about what a rational agent will do. And on the empirical side: if ambiguity aversion mostly turned out to be experimenter demand or explainable by second-order uncertainty, does the main behavioral motivation collapse?

So: what would have to be true for imprecise probabilism to be wrong, or at least not the right way to model rational uncertainty? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ones defenders would themselves concede separated from the ones only critics think are problems. Tell me what the view predicts about behavior or inference that precise Bayesianism doesn't, and which of those predictions could come out wrong. Also tell me whether the view is at risk of being insulated — it can always answer an objection by widening the interval or saying "rationality just doesn't require a verdict here" — and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind, or a feature. If you want to make it personal, the thing that would most move me is a sequential choice case where the imprecise agent ends up worse off by its own lights than a precise agent.
