I find myself pulled in two directions about the idea that argumentation would be improved by being treated more like a competitive sport. The systematic case for it is strong: competition gives people clear incentives to prepare seriously, to seek out and stress-test positions against opponents whose only interest is in finding weaknesses, and to improve under repeated scoring; in principle, scoring rules could be designed to reward accurate restatement of an opponent's view, responsiveness, and well-placed concession, so that the contest selects for exactly the skills good reasoning requires. Yet I also have a firm intuition that the moment argument becomes a contest, the aim of the activity shifts from getting it right to winning, and the skills that actually get rewarded will be the ones judges can readily detect rather than the ones that track the truth—so competitive argumentation should produce more sophisticated rationalizers, not better inquirers. I am not asking for a verdict that simply picks one side. What would have to be the case for the intuition to be the thing that is debunked and dropped, what would have to be the case for the competitive model to be amended or abandoned, and is there a reading of the idea of argumentation as a sport on which the two are compatible rather than in tension?
