Two people in my study group got stuck on a case one of them described, and no one else in the room could settle it. A hospital pharmacist discovered that a colleague had been diluting a chemotherapy preparation for months and keeping the surplus; she reported him to the state licensing board. Everyone agreed that making the report was the right thing to do. To sharpen the disagreement, they stipulated two facts about the pharmacist: she was genuinely moved both by the thought that the dilution was wrong because it endangered patients, and by the thought that removing the colleague would reopen a promotion she had been denied. They also stipulated, for the sake of the debate, that the self-interested motive would have been sufficient on its own, while the moral motive was one she sincerely took herself to be acting on but was neither necessary nor, taken alone, clearly sufficient. The argument was about whether the act had moral worth, understood as the credit due to an agent for the quality of will behind what she does, over and above the act's being right.

Lena argued that it did not. "If the professional grievance would have produced the report by itself," she said, "then the patients' endangerment was not doing the work. An act has moral worth only when it is done for the right reason, and a consideration that fails to make a difference to what the agent does cannot be the reason for which she acts." Tomas disagreed: "A reason can move someone without being either necessary or sufficient. If I take a pill partly because I want my headache gone and partly because I am paid to take it, and the payment alone would have been enough to make me take it, it does not follow that I did not take it because of the headache. Being redundant does not make a reason idle." If the pharmacist counted the harm to patients in favor of reporting, he argued, she acted at least partly for a moral reason, and the act had at least partial moral worth. Lena answered with a different case: a man donates to a highly effective charity purely to impress a date, all the while sincerely believing that the charity does good. His moral belief does no motivational work, and the donation plainly has no moral worth; yet Tomas's test seemed to her to confer worth on it. "So we both need a test for when a moral consideration is merely riding along rather than operative," she added, "and a counterfactual test is the obvious candidate." Tomas replied that the date case differs precisely because the moral belief is stipulated to be inert there, whereas the pharmacist's moral concern was stipulated to be among the considerations she acted on. The live question, he said, was whether a co-determining but redundant moral reason can confer worth. Lena then offered a third case: a pharmacist who reports because she takes herself to be discharging her professional duty while remaining entirely indifferent to the patients themselves. "If acting merely from duty confers moral worth," she argued, "we would have to credit a kind of duty-fetishism — a responsiveness to the bare thought that this is what a good pharmacist would do, rather than to the particular features that actually make the act right." Tomas accepted that the duty-only version lacked full worth but insisted it was unlike the original case, where the pharmacist was moved by the patients' endangerment. They ended up agreeing about the two extreme cases and deadlocking about the middle one.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim, what each position has to assume about how moral worth attaches to an act, and what should actually be concluded about the original pharmacist: does a self-interested motive that would have sufficed on its own cancel, diminish, or leave intact whatever moral worth the act acquires from the moral motive she also acted on? The deliverable should be a step-by-step adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made, not just a verdict about the case.
