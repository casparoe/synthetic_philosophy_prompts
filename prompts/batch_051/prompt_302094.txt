Two members of my school's Constitution Club recently argued at length about a bill now before our state legislature. The bill would make it a misdemeanor to loiter or prowl in a place, at a time, or in a manner not usual for a law-abiding person, when the surrounding circumstances reasonably warrant alarm for the safety of people or property. I took notes and they asked me to referee, but I could not decide who was right. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Maya said the bill is unconstitutionally vague in precisely the two ways the doctrine targets. First, an ordinary person reading it cannot know in advance whether lingering outside a convenience store or walking slowly through a neighborhood amounts to loitering or prowling, so fair notice is impossible. Second, it hands police enormous discretion to decide who counts as out of place, and that discretion will predictably be used against the people who already get policed the hardest. Precision protects against unequal enforcement, not just confusion.

(2) Daniel replied that the bill does not punish seeming suspicious. It requires circumstances that would reasonably alarm someone about safety, which is an objective standard, not an officer's private feeling. Every legal system uses general standards, such as reckless driving or reasonable provocation, because no legislature can write an exhaustive list. A list could be worse: it would miss novel forms of dangerous conduct while sweeping in innocent behavior that happens to match one of its entries.

(3) Maya answered that reasonable-person language does its normal work when it qualifies conduct already defined, such as driving. You know you are driving, and the law specifies the manner. But here the standard is the whole offense: whether a person was committing a crime depends entirely on how an officer later reads that person's mere presence. The result is circular. And combined with on-the-street arrest discretion, that circularity is a machine for selective enforcement.

(4) Daniel responded that courts do not read phrases like reasonably warrant alarm in isolation. They interpret such statutes to require a stated, articulable factual basis that the officer can defend later, not a hunch, and that is what makes the test objective. On Maya's standard, a large share of the criminal code would fall, since words like malice, disorderly, and good cause are also flexible. Vagueness doctrine has never required a rulebook catalog; it requires enough definition for notice and constrained enforcement.

(5) Maya said that Daniel was describing case law, not the text an ordinary citizen reads. Notice is supposed to come from the statute as written, because people do not consult appellate opinions before walking home. If a clear core appears only through later judicial gloss, the enactment itself has failed its first job. Moreover, the doctrine has two independent requirements: fair notice and limits on arbitrary enforcement. Daniel's saving construction affects only whether an officer can justify an arrest afterward; it leaves the arrest decision itself unstructured at the moment it happens.

(6) Daniel answered that this objection is not about vagueness but about preventive policing. Standards like probable cause and reasonable suspicion are also applied on the spot, and the Constitution tolerates them because a stated test plus after-the-fact review is the safeguard. The bill at least gives officers a substantive test to apply. If Maya's real complaint is that police may interrupt conduct before a crime is completed, that is a separate policy dispute, not a failure of fair notice.

Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it. For each numbered move, say which claims are correct, which are overstated, and why; identify anything important that neither student raised; and state who is more right overall, and about what in particular.
