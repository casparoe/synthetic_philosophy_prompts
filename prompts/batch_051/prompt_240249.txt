I'm taking a course this term on the ethics of health-care rationing, cost-effectiveness, and distributive justice, and I'm drafting the position statement for my final paper on how scarce medical resources should be allocated. I've realized that I hold a cluster of views I haven't yet checked against one another, and I'd like an audit before I commit to them in writing. My current commitments are these:

(1) When public health systems allocate scarce resources, they should aim to maximize the expected number of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) produced.

(2) Triage and allocation rules must not deprioritize disabled patients; disability should not count against the value of someone's remaining life.

(3) Age may matter in a narrow fair-innings sense: a 25-year-old may be prioritized over an 85-year-old even when the expected QALY gain is somewhat smaller, though not when the difference is very large, and an 85-year-old's remaining years are not worthless.

(4) When agencies set the value of a statistical life, they should rely on estimates built from individuals' willingness to pay for small reductions in mortality risk, since individuals are the best judges of how much a given risk matters to them.

(5) Ordinary medical allocation should not spend more to save a rich person's life than a poor person's life merely because the rich person can pay more.

(6) In a pandemic, a ventilator triage protocol may legitimately give some priority to frontline clinicians.

(7) QALY-based cost-effectiveness thresholds are appropriate for deciding what a public plan covers, but a physician at the bedside should not tell a patient that their treatment is not worth the cost.

I don't yet have a strong sense of where the internal strain is, and I'd rather not be told which single claim to abandon. Please audit the set: identify which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and for each real tension say what revision would be least costly and what that revision would cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. Please give me exactly five findings, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
