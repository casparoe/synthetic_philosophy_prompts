For my blog (small, LessWrong-adjacent readership, mostly people who know the word 'compatibilism' but not the cases where their own view breaks) I want to build one of those "answer these cases and we'll sort you" quizzes, but for the knowledge-how vs. knowledge-that debate. I've read enought to be dangerous: Ryle's anti-intellectualism where knowing how is a disposition/ability rather than propositional knowledge, Stanley and Williamson's intellectualism where knowing how is a proposition known under a practical mode of presentation, and a couple of hybrid positions in between. I don't want the quiz to reward readers who recognize the labels or like one author more; I want to sort them by what they'd actually say about concrete cases.

Give me eight questions. Each question should be a concrete scenario with three answer options, and no question may ask anything like "do you think knowing how reduces to knowing that?" The cases I most want covered (lightly disguised if you want): the amnesiac pianist who can still play but can't remember the propositions; the chicken sexer who reliably sorts chicks but can't articulate how; someone with a lookup table that gives perfect verbal instructions for every action but never acts; someone who performs the action correctly while sincerely describing how they do it falsely; and a normal case where the person can both explain and do it. These are the standard cases, but each question should push on exactly one of three distinctions, and you should tell me which.

The three yes/no distinctions I want the quiz to sort on:
- ability: does knowing how require that the agent can actually perform the action?
- proposition: does knowing how require grasping some propositional content about a way of acting (as opposed to the skill itself being the knowing)?
- articulation: is the agent's verbal explanation of how they do it partly constitutive of knowing how (as opposed to a seperate performance that never counts)?

The leaves I want: (a) Rylean ability view, (b) intellectualism-without-ability-requirement (Stanley–Williamson-ish), (c) a both-required hybrid, and (d) a deflationary view that says the debate is mostly a verbal dispute about how we stretch 'knows how.' If the logical space genuinely needs a fifth leaf to make scoring clean, add and name it yourself; don't force five distinct answer patterns into four boxes.

I want a committed job, not a menu of possible quizzes. Actually build all eight questions, write all the options, and make the scoring decisive: every answer combination routes somewhere, and if there are ties you fix them rather than reporting them.

Respond with a JSON object with this schema:
- `title`: string, the quiz title.
- `intro`: string, one sentence for under the title telling readers they'll be sorted by cases, not labels.
- `questions`: array of eight objects, each with `id` (string, Q1..Q8), `case` (string, the scenario, concrete enough to answer without background reading), and `options` (array of three objects, each with `letter` ("a"/"b"/"c"), `text` (string, a pre-theoretic verdict the reader picks), and `distinction` (one of "ability", "proposition", "articulation", the distinction this option mainly loads)).
- `leaves`: array of four or five objects, each with `name` (string), `one_paragraph` (string, what the position claims, for the answer key), and `decision_rule` (string, a human-readable rule over answer letters that routes a reader to this leaf, e.g. "mostly a's on ability questions and c's on articulation questions").
- `unoccupied_cells`: array of objects. Walk the full 2x2x2 grid of the three distinctions; for every cell that maps to no leaf, give `combination` (string, e.g. "ability: yes, proposition: no, articulation: yes") and `diagnosis` (one sentence: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?).
- `caveats`: string. If any two leaves cannot be separated by the eight questions, say which and give the ninth question that would split them.
- `notes`: string. Your reasoning about why the questions push exactly one distinction each and why the scoring is unambiguous, plus the two or three calls you're least sure about.

Don't hedge in the main fields; put the uncertainty in `notes` and `caveats`.
