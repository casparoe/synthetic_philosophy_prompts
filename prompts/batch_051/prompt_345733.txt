I keep coming back to Parfit's hitchhiker: a rational egoist stuck in the desert gets offered a lift only if he promises to pay, but the driver won't take the promise seriously, because once the hitchhiker's dropped off he'll have no self-interested reason to pay and both of them know it. Parfit's suggestion is that the hitchhiker could make himself credible by making himself disposed to keep the promise even when keeping it would be irrational - it can be rational to arrange to be irrational.

Here's the counterfactual. Suppose, sometime in the next decade, a pharma company starts selling a cheap, over-the-counter drug - call it Bind - that does this for real. For 24 hours after taking it, a person is flatly unable to break a promise they make while on it: the option of reneging just doesn't survive deliberation, the way Ulysses couldn't throw himself overboard once he'd been lashed to the mast. A saliva test can verify that someone is on it, and there is an antidote but it takes several hours to work and is itself detectable for days, so nobody can pop an antidote and quietly defect without the other side seeing it coming.

Now predict, with the reasoning shown, how the next thirty years play out. Which kinds of promises and bargains migrate onto the pill, and which stay with ordinary trust and legal enforcement? Which institutions and informal norms get displaced, and what new ones grow up around the drug? Who gains bargaining power, who loses it, and does trust in the society as a whole go up or just get relocated to the moment before someone decides to take the pill? In particular, say whether Parfit's fix for the hitchhiker actually generalises once the binding technology is cheap and available to everyone, and what new forms of exploitation or failure it makes possible. Mark clearly which predictions are robust and which are guesses, and where a prediction rests on an empirical assumption, say what would have to hold for it to be wrong. British English, please.
