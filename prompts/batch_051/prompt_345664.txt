I've been toying with a view I call the 'confessional theory of prices', and I want it checked before I say it in front of people. The setup: the no-trade theorem says, roughly, that if we're both rational, risk-averse, and share the same prior, I shouldn't buy from you just because my private information says the asset is worth more than you think — because my offer to buy is itself information that should make you update and raise your ask, and so on until there's no trade left. Fine. But people demonstrably trade all the time, and the standard reply is that they have non-informational motives: liquidity needs, hedging, rebalancing, tax reasons, whatever. My claim is that those are less like motives and more like alibis. A trade is an exchange of information-denials: I know something you dont, I want to profit from it, and the liquidity story is the cover that lets both of us pretend I'm not trading against you. Prices, then, dont aggregate dispersed information the way the usual wisdom-of-markets story says; they aggregate the minimum each side can admit without killing the deal. Thats why you get low-volume price drift right before news hits: the informed are trading quietly, and the alibi is doing all the conversational work.

Does this already exist under a better name (adverse selection? market microstructure?), and what is the strongest counterexample — a genuinely informative trade that is not an exchange of alibis? If the theory survives, the scary conclusion is that a market is a machine for manufacturing mutually agreed false pretenses. If it's dead, where exactly does it die?
