A charter city's Civic Compact contains the following exit clause: "A Resident may renounce their status by lodging a Notice of Withdrawal at the Civic Registry. Upon recording, the City shall exercise no authority over that person, and that person shall owe no further charge or duty under this Compact, except only for sums then due, or for any claim arising from an act or omission occurring before the recording of the Notice."

The drafters had in mind a leaseholder leaving with unpaid rent, or a former employee's pre-departure embezzlement. Consider a case they did not foresee. A machine-learning researcher, while resident, designs and releases an autonomous trading agent that operates without further human supervision. She then lodges a Notice of Withdrawal and pays every sum then due. After the Notice is recorded, the agent—still running and unmonitored—executes a trade that causes another resident to suffer a loss. That resident sues in the City, arguing that the pre-exit acts of designing and releasing the agent are "acts occurring before the recording" from which the claim arises. The researcher replies that the harm came into existence only after exit, that her only post-exit contribution was the omission of failing to switch the agent off, and that the clause was meant to make exit clean.

How should the clause apply to this case? Work through what each of the following requires or permits here: the ordinary wording; the purpose of an exit clause in a private city; what the drafters would plausibly have said had they been asked about software and automation; and what a reasonable reader of the Compact would understand. Say which approach you find most defensible, and then say what the case reveals about the larger idea that a person can genuinely opt out of a city's authority when the causes of a dispute are temporally extended. Please answer in British English.
