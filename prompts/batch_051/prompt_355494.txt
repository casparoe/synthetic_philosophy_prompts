Since retiring from a research career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy systematically, and I now attend a small discussion circle with several other retired scientists and clinicians. At our most recent meeting, two members of the circle—Elena, formerly a statistician, and Marcus, formerly a physician—became locked in an argument I could not referee, and I would like a careful point-by-point adjudication of what each of them said.

The argument began over a friend of the circle, Daniel, who had enrolled in a long-term cohort study and had been offered, free of charge, a genetic risk report for a slowly progressive condition in which early changes to diet and exercise appear to slow the disease's course but for which there is no cure. Daniel declined the report, saying only that he would rather not know.

Elena said his refusal was not rationally defensible. Her opening argument ran like this: in any fixed decision problem, costless information can never lower the expected value of the best action an agent can choose, provided the expectation is taken before the information is received and the agent is someone who will update her beliefs correctly and then choose the action with the highest expected value under those updated beliefs. The reason is that the agent could, after seeing the information, simply take the action she would have taken without it; so learning is at least as good in expectation as remaining ignorant. If the report would lead Daniel to adopt useful habits when the risk is high and leave his habits unchanged when the risk is low, then Daniel is turning down an option whose expected value is at least as high—and plausibly higher—than that of declining.

Marcus answered that this is the kind of formal result that floats free of life. He described a patient he had cared for years earlier who received an ambiguous result from a similar kind of testing, became preoccupied with it, pursued further investigations that carried their own risks, and, in Marcus's judgment, ended up worse than if the test had never been done. Information changes people, he said, and sometimes for the worse; a theorem about ideal agents tells us nothing about Daniel.

Elena replied that this conflates a bad realized outcome with a bad prospect. The theorem is about expectation before the information arrives, not about how things turn out in hindsight. If anxiety, follow-up procedures, and their complications are genuine bads, then they should be entered into the decision setup with probabilities attached; once they are, the option might no longer be attractive, but that is not a counterexample to the theorem—it is the theorem being applied to a fuller specification of the choice. In Daniel's case, she added, the report itself is non-invasive and costless, and no one would force him to act on it.

Marcus said this only moves the problem: the theorem presumes a person who will update correctly and then carry out the best available plan. Real patients predictably do not, and a physician can sometimes foresee that a particular patient will respond to a result destructively; for such a patient the information carries negative expected value before it is ever delivered. The theorem therefore cannot underwrite the claim that Daniel's refusal was irrational unless it has first been established that Daniel is the kind of agent the theorem idealizes. He added a second, more principled objection: even for an ideal agent, the theorem assumes that the set of actions available after the information is the same as before. But knowledge often changes one's obligations and options—a person who learns something may be required to disclose it, or may face restrictions on what he may then do. In such cases, he argued, even an ideal agent can be made worse off in expectation by the information itself.

Elena conceded those as conditions, but said they are not Daniel's circumstances: Daniel is competent, the result carries no disclosure obligation, and no one else will act on what Daniel has learned. Under those conditions, she said, the theorem is exactly what she claimed, and Daniel's stated reason—"I would simply rather not know"—is not a reason unless it conceals a concrete cost, in which case the cost should be named and weighed.

Marcus's closing move was to accept the conditional theorem, but to insist that the dispute had now shrunk to whether real people ever meet the conditions, and whether a settled preference not to know a piece of adverse information about oneself might itself be a legitimate cost that a decision-theoretic reconstruction can register but cannot second-guess.

I found myself convinced by whichever of them had spoken last, and the meeting ended without either accepting the other's position. Please go through this exchange move by move. For each substantial claim—the value-of-information theorem as Elena states it, Marcus's patient counterexample, Elena's ex ante-versus-ex post reply, Marcus's complaint about idealization, Marcus's point about changed options, and the closing exchange over Daniel—say what is sound, what is overstated, and why. Then say where a precise and fair version of their argument should have ended, including anything both of them missed.
