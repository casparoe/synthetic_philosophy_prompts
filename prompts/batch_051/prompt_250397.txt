I've been going in circles on an argument a friend made about the right to strike, and it lands somewhere I don't want to be. Maybe you can tell me where it slips. Here it is:

(1) A strike is a coordinated refusal to work, organized (usually by a union) to put economic pressure on an employer until some demand is met. (2) When workers are under an employment contract or a collective agreement, a strike is a coordinated breach: the workers aren't quitting, they're withholding performance while still claiming the job. (3) If workers have a right to strike, then that coordinated breach isn't merely excusable—it's the exercise of a right, so the fact that it breaches a contract can't by itself make it wrong. (4) But nothing in that justification is specific to labor. If collectively breaching a contract is permissible when a weaker party uses it to force renegotiation with a stronger party, then tenants have a right to a rent strike, borrowers to a debt strike, students to a tuition strike, and so on, whenever they can coordinate well enough to make the breach costly. (5) That seems like a reductio: a general permission to collectively suspend any contract you find unfavorable would make contracting pointless, since every obligation would hold only until the other side got organized. (6) So either the right to strike doesn't include a right to breach, or we're stuck with a general right to collectively breach contracts, which no one actually accepts. (7) Therefore the right to strike, if it exists at all, is just the right to quit at will—wich is toothless, because the whole point of a strike is to withhold your labor and keep your job.

I'd like to keep the right to strike and also keep the idea that contracts bind, but this argument says I cant have both. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
