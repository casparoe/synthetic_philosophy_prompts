I serve as recording clerk to the standing committee of appeals of a small confessional denomination whose founding instrument commits the church to the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments as the only rule of faith, worship, and discipline. The instrument says nothing about how those Scriptures are to be interpreted, and the committee has never settled that question in the abstract; instead it has spent a century and a half issuing rulings, each one insistently framed as applying the plain sense of the inspired text and creating no precedent whatsoever. The rulings most often invoked are these. In 1873 the committee held that the use of musical instruments in public worship is unlawful, because the gathered church of the New Testament is nowhere described as using them. In 1911 it held that a Christian may not take a judicial oath, because the Lord and the epistle of James forbid swearing without exception, and the committee has no authority to supply a limiting condition the text does not contain. In 1936 it held that a woman praying publicly in the assembly should cover her head, because the apostolic instruction is a command to the churches and not a first-century local custom. In 1959 it held that a member divorced on the ground of adultery may remarry, because the exception clauses in the Gospel of Matthew belong to the inspired text and may be neither ignored nor widened. In 1984 it held that a sound system installed to amplify the preacher's voice is lawful, because it changes no element of public worship and only makes the commanded preaching audible to those present in the building.

This year the committee must decide the following case. A rural congregation, too scattered and too poor to call a resident minister, has been meeting at the same hour in six homes. In each home the small group sings, prays, reads the Scriptures, and watches a recording of a sermon delivered by a visiting minister the previous week. At the close, the elements of the Lord's Supper, which an elder set apart at a gathering earlier that week, are distributed, and the group eats and drinks together. The congregation regards itself as one assembly meeting under one ordinance; its opponents say the arrangement dissolves the gathered church and substitutes a recording for the living voice of the pulpit.

I need from you an internal memorandum of the kind this committee actually works from. It should state which of our own precedents is the closest analogue and why; identify the distinct principles that could reconcile all five precedents, because I suspect there are at least two and perhaps a third; explain what each principle would require for the home-group practice; give your best judgment as to which answer our precedents really commit us to, or say plainly that they underdetermine it and what principle we would be adopting with either ruling; and name the further case that would most cleanly force the choice of principle into the open. The memorandum should also face, briefly, the question this case has finally put before us directly: once a body that denies the authority of tradition has issued a century and a half of readings, what exactly is it applying when it says it applies Scripture alone?
