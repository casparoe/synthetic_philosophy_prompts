In my medical ethics course we keep hitting the word 'dignity' and I honestly can't tell whether it's doing any real work. In the Kant part it seemed like it was: persons have a dignity, not a price, and that's what grounds treating people as ends rather than mere means. But then in the applied readings, 'dignity' gets used to support both sides of the same fight. Assisted dying: one side says it violates the patient's dignity, the other says making someone go on like that is what violates their dignity. Selling a kidney: one side says it's an assault on human dignity, the other says banning someone from deciding what to do with their own body is the actual insult to dignity. When I press people on what the word adds — if you just said 'that harms them' or 'that overrides their autonomous choice' — the argument loses no actual content, but people get way less confident. So the word is doing something, just not argumentative work. It's functioning like a mood, not a premise. I know there's a term, essentially contested concept, and I think an author we read (Ruth Macklin?) wrote a piece arguing dignity is useless, but the first one sounds like it's for concepts people legitimately disagree over, and the second is more of a debunking conclusion than a name for the mechanism. The thing I don't have a name for is the pattern itself: a concept both sides reach for as a trump card, that never gets any kind of independent test, and where removing it changes the tone but no the substance. Does that have a standard label? If it's not exactly any established thing, what are its nearest neighbors and where are the edges? I'm more interested in the carving than the label.
