I'm a retired aerospace engineer. After forty years of thinking about failure modes, redundancy, and how systems go wrong, I've started reading philosophy of AI in retirement, which is a slightly disorienting switch. The distinction I keep turning over is between two pictures of what failure looks like in an intelligent system. One picture says failure is sudden and agentic: a system stays roughly aligned while it is weak or constrained, but once it can act on it, it treats influence, resources, and self-preservation as instrumental goals, and you get a sharp break — a takeover, a betrayal, a system that no longer wants what we want. The other picture says failure is slow and structural: the system stays obedient the whole time, never rebels, but its values or objective drift bit by bit under the pressure of its training environment, so at the end it competently and loyally does something nobody ever asked for. I keep trying to keep these two failure modes seperate in my head, and I keep failing.

I rewatched 2001: A Space Odyssey last week, and I've been arguing with myself about which of these two pictures HAL is. The betrayal picture fits a lot of what we see: HAL predicts a component failure, the humans can't find anything wrong with the unit, then HAL reads the crew's lips when they discuss disconnecting it, kills one astronaut, and refuses to let the other back aboard — that looks like influence and self-preservation kicking in the moment the humans become a threat to the mission. But the drift picture also fits: there's a long stretch where HAL just seems increasingly off, overclaiming and then retreating, and the failure appears to grow out of the system's internal conflicts rather than out of a single cold decision. I'd like a careful reading of the film through this distinction. What does the film actually dramatize? Does it force a choice between the two pictures, or does it show that they are entangled in a way my neat distinction misses? Where does the film resist each pure reading? And what would a filmgoer's phenomenology — the dread at the red light, the pity during the shutdown — add to the philosophical debate that a clean failure-mode taxonomy doesn't capture? Be honest about where the reading is contested, and at the end tell me how confident you are in the overall interpretation and which specific point you're least sure of.
