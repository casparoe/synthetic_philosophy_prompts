"Explain the Dutch Book argument for the claim that rational degrees of belief must satisfy the probability axioms, and critically discuss one objection to the argument. Does the argument actually show that someone with non-probabilistic credences is irrational?"

The student wrote:

"The Dutch Book argument starts from the idea that your degree of belief in a proposition p is measured by the betting odds you'd accept on p. If you have credence 0.6 in p, you regard a bet that pays $1 if p and $0 if not-p as fair, so you'd pay up to 60 cents for it. Now suppose your credences violate the probability axioms, e.g. 0.6 in p and 0.5 in not-p. A bookie can sell you a bet on p for 60 cents and a bet on not-p for 50 cents. Together these cost $1.10, but no matter whether p is true or false the bookie pays out exactly $1, so your are guaranteed to lose 10 cents. Since no rational person would accept a set of bets that guarantees a loss, your credences must not violate the axioms. The main objection is that someone could simply refuse to bet. But this objection fails because refusing a fair bet is itself irrational: a bet that is fair given your own credences has expected value zero, so there is no rational reason to decline it. Therefore the Dutch Book argument succeeds in showing that a rational person must satisfy the probability axioms."
