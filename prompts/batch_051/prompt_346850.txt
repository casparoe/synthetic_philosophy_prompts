We're on the Knobe effect — the classic finding where people say the VP intentionally harmed the environment but did not intentionally help it, even though his stated attitude is identical in both cases. I want an eight-item quiz that sorts students into which account of the Knobe effect they actually hold, purely from their pattern of case judgments. No question should mention the names of any accounts or the phrase Knobe effect, and no references or citations are needed; the students should feel like theyre just answering little stories about businesses and coffee shops.

Each item is a short vignette that ends with the same type of question: did X intentionally [side effect]? Three answer options: yes / no / genuinely unsure. Make sure the intentionality question targets the side effect, not the main goal. Then a scoring key maps answer patterns onto these leaves (you can paraphrase the labels for the key, but keep them recognizable): valence account, trade-off account, norm-violation account, deep-self account, culpable-control account, and a deflationary no-effect view. For each leaf, give a one-sentence diagnosis of its most awkward commitment or the pattern that would embarrass it.

I want these seven cases built in with the structural features intact (you can invent names, companies, details, but keep the feature that does the work):

1. Canonical harm: a profit-focused VP adopts a program that will boost profits and harm the environment; he says he doesn't care about the environment and only wants profit.
2. Canonical help: same setup, except the program boosts profits and helps the environment; same stated attitude.
3. A helping side effect that competes with the main goal: a VP boosts sales by switching suppliers, but the switch forfeits a discount, and the forfeited money funds a school-lunch program; she says she only cares about the sales figures. The point is a good outcome that is nevertheless costly for the agent.
4. A non-moral norm violation with no cost and no moral valence: a barista finds a faster way to make drinks, but it means she has to steam milk left-handed, which breaks the shop's arbitrary handedness rule; she says she doesn't care about the rule.
5. A morally bad side effect that is norm-conforming in the agent's local context: a manager's new efficiency system automatically flags an underperformer for dismissal under a strict up-or-out policy; she privately likes the person but treats it as just how the system works.
6. A bad side effect the agent explicitly disavows under pressure: a lifelong environmentalist VP approves a wetland-harming program only because the board will fire them otherwise, and says they'd avoid the harm if they could.
7. A negligent bad side effect: a genuinely environment-friendly VP approves a profit-boosting project without reading the environmental assessment he was told to read; the project harms a wetland, and he is angry at himself but says he had no idea.

Add one more case of your own choosing that you think does the most work to seperate whatever the other seven still leave tangled.

For the scoring key, each leaf should get a predicted pattern across the eight items — yes, no, or unclear if the view's commitments genuinely don't settle that case — so a respondent's pattern can be matched to the closest leaf. Treat genuinely unsure answers as non-diagnostic, and say what happens on ties. Also flag: any logically possible answer pattern that your battery leaves unoccupied or uninterpretable and whether it's unoccupied because the pattern is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored; and any pair of leaves the quiz can't fully seperate.
