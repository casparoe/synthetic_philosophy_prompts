So I'm a journalist working on a piece about whether the interior of language models has any real geometric shape to meaning, adn I keep running into the same pattern described to me in slightly different ways, and I'd like to know whether it has a standard name and, more importantly, what people in the field think it actually licenses. The setup: a language model stores each word as a long row of numbers, so you can treat any word as a point in a huge space. If you pick two words that differ mostly along one contrast — king and queen, man and woman, actor and actress — and draw an arrow from the first to the second for each pair, the arrows come out roughly parallel. It isn't just gender; you see the same thing for capital-to-country pairs (Paris to France, Madrid to Spain), for present-to-past (walk to walked, run to ran), maybe for singular to plural. And you can take one of those arrows and add it to a word: move king along the man-to-woman arrow and you land near queen. So far so cute.

But the same construction shows up in the fairness conversations, where people find a gender arrow and then use it to measure, tone down, or erase sexist associations in the model. So I can't tell whether those two uses are the same claim or two different claims being conflated. Is the phenomenon really a direction in the space that corresponds to a feature, or is that a loose way of talking about a messier statistical fact — a bunch of context patterns that happen to line up? When does a cluster of roughly parallel arrows count as a real semantic dimension, and when is it just an artifact of how the model was trained? I know it isn't just word similarity, and I don't just want a label. I want the carving: tell me what the nearest established ideas are, where this one matches them and where it diverges, and whether the analogy use and the bias use are really about the same geometry.
