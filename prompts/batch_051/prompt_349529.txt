I write about science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I have been trying to pin down the Bayesian claim that priors wash out as evidence accumulates: two reasonable observers who start with different prior probabilities should end up agreeing if they share the same body of evidence. Below is my reconstruction of the argument usually given for that claim. I want to know whether it is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails.

(1) Two agents, A and B, assign different positive prior probabilities, p and q, to a hypothesis H.
(2) Both observe the same sequence of evidence E1, E2, and so on.
(3) By Bayes's rule, each agent's posterior odds on H against not-H equal that agent's prior odds times the likelihood ratio of the observed evidence for H as against not-H.
(4) The likelihood ratio is fixed by the evidence and the hypotheses and is the same for both agents, so at every stage their posterior odds differ only by the constant multiple p(1−q)/q(1−p).
(5) In the long run, such a constant multiple is swamped if the likelihood ratio becomes very large or very small.
(6) So long as the evidence is genuinely informative about H, the likelihood ratio must eventually become very large or very small; it cannot remain bounded in a middle range forever.
(7) Therefore the two agents' posterior probabilities for H converge to a common value, and the initial priors wash out.

Which step, if any, is wrong? Be specific about the step and about the background condition being assumed. I have a vague sense that the problem is somewhere around step (6), but I suspect the real difficulty may come earlier, and I would like to know under what reading of the hypotheses the argument does go through.
