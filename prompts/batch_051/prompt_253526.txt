A friend and I spent most of Sunday arguing about when an elegant, well-supported theory should be allowed to override a stubborn intuition, and when the intuition should be allowed to overturn or amend the theory. We emerged with several cases on which we agreed but no principle that covered all of them. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern, and what that principle says about cases neither of us settled.

Here are the cases and my verdicts as they emerged from the argument.

1. Special relativity and simultaneity. I have a strong inclination to think that whether two distant events happen at the same time has a determinate, frame-independent answer. Relativity says it does not. I take relativity to be right, and I treat my inclination as an artifact of being used to low relative velocities rather than as evidence about the structure of spacetime.

2. Design intuition and natural selection. I find the vertebrate eye purpose-built; before learning the biology, I would have called that intuition strong evidence of a designer. But I take evolutionary theory to be correct, and I regard the design intuition as a predictable misfiring of a cognitive habit rather than as a defeater.

3. The Monty Hall problem. When a host who knows where the prize is opens a goat door and offers the switch, my untutored impulse is that switching and staying are equally good. The probability calculation says switching doubles my chance of winning. I accept the calculation and treat the impulse as demonstrably wrong; here the theory is precise enough that I feel no temptation to rescue the intuition.

4. The flagpole and the covering-law model. On the covering-law model, an event is explained when it can be deduced from laws plus antecedent conditions. The length of a flagpole's shadow is deducible from the flagpole's height, the sun's angle, and optical laws, but no one would say the shadow's length explains the flagpole's height. Here I let the intuition about explanatory asymmetry overturn, or at least force a major amendment to, the model. I do not set the intuition aside.

5. Transplant and act-utilitarianism. A theory that licenses carving up one healthy patient to save five is, I think, rejected at this point: my unwillingness to do it is not a disposable datum. The intuition stands and the theory is to be constrained by it—not because I have an independent proof of the intuition, but because a moral theory that cannot respect the conviction that it is wrong to use someone merely as a means has failed in its own terms.

6. Mary and the knowledge argument. A physicalist says every fact is a physical fact. Mary knows every physical fact about color vision while confined to a black-and-white room; on first seeing red she seems to learn something new. My verdict here is less clean than in the others: I find the intuition that she acquires something genuinely new hard to abandon, but I do not think it obviously refutes physicalism. I place it in the intuition-wins column for purposes of this exercise, with the caveat that its target may be a limitation of a certain representational story rather than a new nonphysical entity.

The contrast that most bothers me is case 4 on one side and cases 1, 2, and 3 on the other. In case 4 I let a single, tightly focused counterexample overturn a precise and successful philosophical theory. In cases 1 through 3 I refuse to let far stronger and more widely shared intuitions budge theories I accept. I need an account of why that asymmetry is legitimate rather than merely convenient.

My request has three parts. First, state the best principle you can find that yields, or comes closest to yielding, all six verdicts, and show how it handles each case. Second, test the principle on at least one further case I have not settled; plausible candidates are a free-will case in which physical theory pushes toward determinism while agency pushes the other way, a demandingness case in which act-utilitarianism says I must give far more than ordinary moral judgment permits, and an epistemology case in which an anti-luck theory of knowledge conflicts with a widely shared Gettier-style verdict. Choose whichever puts the strongest pressure on your principle and say what it predicts. Third, be explicit about which of my six verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the right response is to complicate the principle or to overturn that verdict.
