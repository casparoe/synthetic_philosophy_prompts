My city's charter says (quoting the clause exactly, omitting subsection numbers): "No board, officer, or agency of the city may deny or condition any benefit, license, or service on a standard that is not contained in a published written rule, stated plainly enough that an ordinary person can tell what it requires." The clause was adopted by referendum in the 1970s, and everyone agrees it was aimed at unwritten internal policies that officials kept in desk drawers.

Last year the housing department replaced its manual of written eligibility rules for rental assistance with a machine-learned fraud-flagging system. The model takes about 400 inputs (income, work history, past addresses, bank-transaction patterns, and so on), produces a score, and flags applications above a threshold for an investigator. The departments manual now says an application "shall be flagged when the model score exceeds 0.7." The code, training data, and learned weights are all published on the city's website, but no one can tell you in advance what exactly makes an application get flagged. Two applications that look identical to a caseworker can get different flags because of interactions in the model that nobody can summarize.

Does the clause apply to a standard that exists only as a trained model plus a score threshold? Is the standard "published" when the weights are public but not intelligible? Is it "stated plainly enough that an ordinary person can tell what it requires" when even the agencys own lawyer can't tell you? Argue it from the words, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the drafters would have said if you'd explained the system to them — and say which of those three should control here, and why. Also: is this a case the words were never able to reach, or did the drafters just fail to imagine it?
