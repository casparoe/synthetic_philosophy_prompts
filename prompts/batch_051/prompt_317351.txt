A friend and I have been arguing about peer disagreement, and he walked me into a proof that seems to show the sensible-sounding view is self-defeating, but I cant find the mistake. The setup we share: two people with the same evidence, same general competence, and equally good track records on the relevant kind of question, who reach opposite conclusions. He says the right move is obvious: you should lower your confidence substantially, because your peer's disagreeing is evidence that one of you messed up, and unless you can point to something that makes you especially likely to be right, you can't legitimately privilege your own take. This sounds right to me, honestly. But then he runs it like this:

(1) If a recognized epistemic peer disagrees with me about P, I should substantially lower my confidence in P.
(2) The rule is meant to be perfectly general: it applies to any P whatever, including P = the claim that (1) is correct.
(3) I accept (1), but there are people I'd otherwise call my peers — including the friend who first taught me this stuff — who reject (1) and hold that peer disagreement gives little or no reason to move.
(4) Applying (1) to my belief in (1), I should substantially lower my confidence in (1), since peers reject it.
(5) But as my confidence in (1) drops, my reason for having dropped it drops too; if I follow the rule all the way down, I end up no longer believing (1), and the thing that told me to move has disappeared. The rule saws off its own branch.
(6) So either (1) is false, or it's true but no one can rationally hold it, or it gets an exemption from its own scope that nothing in (1)'s rationale supplies. He takes that to mean the sensible view is dead.

He then says the same engine applies to any rule that says disagreement should move you at all, so the only consistent position left is the one he hates and I hate more: never let peer disagreement change your mind. I'd much rather keep (1) than sign up for that. So tell me: does the argument go wrong, and if so exactly which step or shift, and why did that step look fine? If it doesn't go wrong, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does abandoning it do to the rest of the view? Lead with the bottom line and keep it under 600 words.
