A recurring argument keeps flaring up in the interdisciplinary reading group I'm in—a mix of computer scientists and philosophers, none of us actual logicians—about whether there are fundamental limits to what any system of thought, human or machine, could ever know about itself. Last week someone ended it by declaring that Gödel showed that there are true statements that cannot be proved, and then moved straight to the conclusion that no AI could ever fully understand its own reasoning, since any system that tries to model itself must miss some truth about itself. I'm fairly sure the slide between those two sentences is doing a lot of the work, and I want to be able to spell out exactly where it happens.

Could you take that slogan—Gödel proved there are true but unprovable statements—and lay out the distinct precise claims it might be expressing? I'd like a menu rather than a single explanation: which readings are mathematically settled (and in what special sense they have to be stated to count as settled), which are plausible but depend on extra assumptions about minds, machines, and truth, and which are straightforward misapplications the theorems simply don't licence. For each reading, say whether it actually supports the claim that an AI cannot fully understand its own reasoning. I suspect a lot turns on the difference between 'provable in some formal system', 'provable in principle by us', 'knowable', and 'computable', so please sort those out where they matter. Also, please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work carefully on this; don't make a contested reading sound like the consensus.
