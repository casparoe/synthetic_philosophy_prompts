I write historical novels. After years of making piecemeal editorial decisions about real people, I have tried to write down the principles I actually apply, and I want to know whether the resulting list is internally consistent. Here are the six commitments as I now hold them:

1. The dead have no interests to harm, so nothing is owed to them as such. If my treatment of a real person carries obligations, those obligations must be owed to someone else — living readers, descendants, the historical record, or my own integrity.
2. I will not knowingly reverse the moral valence of a real historical figure. I may complicate or shade, but I will not turn a villain into a hero or a hero into a villain.
3. When the record is silent on a particular point, I am free to invent unrecorded dialogue and private scenes. Nearly everything that gives a historical novel its texture is this kind of invention.
4. But that freedom has a limit: I will not attribute to a real person convictions, motives, or assumptions that contradict what the person demonstrably believed. The invented interior life must remain compatible with the documented person.
5. I also deny that the label novel releases me from responsibility for what a fair, nonexpert reader could reasonably absorb as fact. If a scene I invent would predictably install a false belief about the real person, I should not write it as planned, disclaimer or no disclaimer.
6. Yet I reject the opposite extreme as well. A writer is not hostage to the reader who treats labeled fiction as testimony; an adult who reads a novel and takes it for history has made an error, and that error is not wholly the writer's to preempt.

Please audit the set. Which commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection? For each genuine tension, order the tensions by severity and say what would have to give to remove it, and what the cheapest repair would cost me in practice. I am not attached to any particular item, and I cannot tell where the problem lies.
