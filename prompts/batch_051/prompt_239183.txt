I sit on a university library committee that rules on requests to digitize and distribute works whose rights holders may be gone or unreachable. Our past decisions have been made one case at a time, and I want help working out what they commit us to. (1) We approved a proposal to digitize a 1958 novel, long out of print, whose author died in 1974 and whose publisher dissolved in 1982; a diligent search found no heirs and no successor, and the book went online free of charge with a take-down notice should a rightsholder later emerge. (2) We denied a campus press's request to reprint, for profit, a 1964 local-history book whose author's daughter is known to be alive but did not respond to repeated letters; we treated her as an existing but unresponsive rightsholder rather than as evidence that the work was orphaned. (3) We approved a student archive's plan to make a 1984 educational software title freely downloadable, after its publisher dissolved in 1995 with no successor and the programmer could not be identified. (4) We approved digitizing a 1972 industrial film, produced by a company that folded in the 1980s with no traceable owner, for classroom use and a free public screening series.

The new request is different in an important way. An independent preservationist wants to remaster and sell a 1987 text-adventure game. The original studio dissolved in 1991, the founder died in 2009, and a lengthy search turned up no heirs, no estate, and no successor company. He would sell the remaster at a modest price, place fifteen percent of the revenue in escrow for any future rightsholder, and honor a take-down request from anyone who later establishes ownership. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Please formulate the unstated principles that best fit our four past decisions; say what each candidate principle would require in the new case; identify which past decision is the closest analog and explain why; then give a best recommendation, making explicit what principle the committee would be adopting under either ruling. If our past decisions genuinely underdetermine the new case, describe one further case that would force the choice between the competing principles. Throughout, say how confident you are in your main claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
