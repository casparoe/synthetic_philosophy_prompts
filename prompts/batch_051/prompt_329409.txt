In my theology reading group, the dispute about whether evolutionary history — with its predation, parasitism, and death long before any human sin — can be reconciled with the doctrine that God made a good creation has stalled. Both sides accept the biological record, and both accept that creation is in some sense good, yet neither has moved. One side holds that pre-human animal suffering and death cannot belong to creation's original goodness, and so treats the Fall as having a cosmic or retroactive scope: the whole natural order is somehow corrupted from the start, quite independently of any human sin's temporal priority. The other side holds that creation's goodness is compatible with evolutionary struggle, because a good creation can be one that makes itself through finite, vulnerable processes; on this view, predation and pain are not departures from the good order but part of the cost of a self-forming world. My working theory is that the impasse is not really about the fossils or about exegesis, but about what the goodness of creation must amount to: whether it primarily concerns the well-being of each creature, or the way the whole process realizes God's ends. Is that diagnosis right? Is there instead a hidden factual or exegetical question doing the work, or a disagreement about what should count as a theological defeater? And given your diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, what kind of distinction, argument, or piece of evidence could actually move it — or should we expect it to remain intractable?
