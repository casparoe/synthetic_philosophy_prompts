Notice a curious asymmetry in how we evaluate the myths of the defeated. When a people turns a military disaster into an identity-constituting story, we often treat the embellishment as part of the point. The defenders of Masada are remembered as choosing collective death over Roman capture; Dunkirk is remembered as an evacuation that was nonetheless a moral victory; the Battle of Kosovo, a defeat, is remembered as the sacred center of Serbian identity. Few of us feel moved to interrupt these stories with corrections. But the postbellum American South performed what looks like the same operation on its defeat, producing the Lost Cause narrative: a story of honor and sacrifice in which the Confederacy was beaten by numbers and industry rather than by the wrong of the cause it fought to preserve. That story we generally condemn, and we condemn people who continue to pass it on.

Formally, the operation is the same in every case: a defeat is converted into a sustaining myth and transmitted across generations. So I want to know whether anything justifies our treating these cases differently, rather than merely explaining why we do. Candidate principles might include the moral character of the lost cause itself, the political work the myth does now, the distinction between commemorating endured suffering and vindicating a regime, or the anesthetic effects of historical distance. Test those candidates against hard variants. Would the Lost Cause become acceptable if it were as old and politically dormant as the Kosovo myth? Would Masada lose its dignity if its defenders had been fighting to preserve an institution as evil as slavery? If you can find a principle that yields both of our ordinary verdicts, tell me where it breaks down: can it handle a defeated cause that was partly just, or a myth that is partly true and partly an alibi? And say whether, at the end, you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it.
