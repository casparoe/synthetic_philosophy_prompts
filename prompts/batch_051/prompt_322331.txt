I just spent an hour arguing with a friend about the Pasadena game, and it left me unsure which of two things I believe I should give up. I've always been drawn to the idea that the fair price of a gamble is just its expected value: it's simple, it gives a definite answer in every case, and it turns choices between gambles into one tidy calculation. The Pasadena game should be a clean test of that. You flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first head is on toss n, you get (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n dollars. So the possible payoffs are +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on, with the nth payoff having probability 1/2^n. Write out the expected value and the 2^n cancels, leaving 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which sums to ln 2. So the theory says the game is worth ln 2, full stop.

But here's what's eating at me. That series is only conditionally convergent. If you permute which coin-history gets which payoff — same payoffs, same probabilities, just attached to different sequences of flips — you get the Altadena variant, whose expectation is 1 - 1/2 - 1/4 + 1/3 - 1/6 - 1/8 + ..., which sums to (1/2)ln 2, and by the rearrangement theorem you can get any number you like. So I have a firm intuition that a mere relabeling of which history gets which payoff cannot change what a gamble is worth, and a theory I otherwise believe in that says it does. My friend says the intuition is just trained on finite gambles and should be discarded; I'm not so sure.
