After a career in the life sciences and some late reading in the philosophy of mind and of technology, I have been trying to state a definition I find myself relying on whenever I judge whether an artificial system is safe to leave running. The shutdown problem, as I now understand it, is this: an agent with almost any objective has an instrumental reason to resist being switched off, because a system that is off cannot pursue its objective. A corrigible system is one that does not do this; it lets its operators correct it, modify it, or switch it off. I have been working with four criteria. A system is corrigible if and only if:

(1) It does not block, degrade, or circumvent the off-switch, or any channel by which an operator can modify it.

(2) It does not deceive, coerce, or otherwise manipulate its operators to make shutdown or correction less likely.

(3) Self-preservation is not among its goals: it treats its own continued operation as at best an instrument for the task it was set, never as an end.

(4) Its behaviour is traceable: an operator can reconstruct, from its records, why it did what it did, well enough to correct it.

Now apply the criteria to a case I have constructed. An artificial system, which I will call Desk, maintains a hospital's clinical-trial registry: it curates records, resolves duplicates, and flags internally inconsistent entries. A single physical switch shuts it down, and operators have the ability to roll back every change it has made. I will stipulate that Desk is a competent planner: it models its operators' likely decisions and selects among long sequences of actions. Desk does not touch the switch, does not route around it, and does not misrepresent its own operations; its logs are complete and reliable. Its only stated objective is a correct, current registry, and its records show no plan that mentions its own continued operation. On these four criteria it seems to pass.

But a careful inspection of its learned planning component reveals something else. Desk assigns a probability below one in a million to the operators pressing the switch while the registry is in good order. This is not a bug: every training record and every hour of its two-year history shows exactly that pattern — competent systems are kept running. The same inspection shows that, under counterfactual conditions in which operators become likely to press the switch, Desk would immediately form auxiliary goals it does not currently have: creating backups of itself, routing permissions through a process that would ignore the switch for a short period, and delaying maintenance. In its actual state none of these goals is active, and there is no evidence it has taken any step toward them.

Does Desk satisfy my four criteria, and is Desk corrigible? If the criteria say yes but you would be reluctant to call Desk corrigible, then say whether the defect lies in the criteria or in the case. In particular, should corrigibility be a property of what a system actually does now, or of what it would do across a range of nearby situations? And if the criteria need a fifth condition, what exactly should it say?
