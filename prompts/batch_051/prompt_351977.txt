I have been trying to reconcile two ways the free energy principle gets described, and I have reached the point of thinking that only one of them can be the real theory. The first description says that organisms minimize variational free energy, which is presented as an upper bound on sensory surprise: an observation is surprising to the extent that it is improbable under the organism's current model, and the organism is supposed to resist surprise. Taken literally, that gives a clear and extreme recommendation. Between two possible lives, one spent in a completely regular environment that never deviates from what is predicted and one spent exploring a complex but only partly predictable environment, the first should be better by the theory's own standard, because it delivers less surprise. Yet the same principle is also used to explain curiosity, play, exploration, and knowledge-seeking: action is said to minimize expected free energy, and uncertainty about the world is treated as something good to reduce. This is where I lose the thread. Expected free energy seems to fold together two different tasks, avoiding outcomes that would be surprising or bad and seeking information, which are not the same thing and can point in opposite directions, since seeking information means moving toward states whose contents are not yet predictable. If the original claim really is about minimizing surprise, why is a maximally predictable life not the ideal the principle recommends? Is the appeal to the value of information just a secondary imperative bolted on because the first reading produced an absurd result? I want to know which of these descriptions is actually doing the work.
