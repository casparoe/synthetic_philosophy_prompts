Public weather agencies routinely issue forecasts of the form '30% chance of rain tomorrow' for particular cities, thereby assigning a probability to a single, unrepeatable event. Yet when those same agencies assess whether their forecasts are any good, they group together all the days on which they said '30%' and check whether rain occurred on roughly 30% of them, a procedure that treats probabilities as long-run relative frequencies across many cases. These two parts of the practice seem to sit uneasily together. Reconstruct the theory of probability that the practice presupposes, or argue that it is genuinely torn between several theories. Work through what a frequentist, a subjectivist, and a propensity account would each make of the single-case forecast and of the frequency-based verification, and say which combinations are coherent. Then give your view as to which interpretation best rationalises the practice as a whole, and indicate what a weather agency would have to change in its forecasts, its verification, or its public guidance if it adopted one of the other interpretations instead. Finally, say what consideration would most change your conclusion, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please answer in British English.
