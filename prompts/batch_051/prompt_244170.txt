I do the interpretive writeups for a small cultural resources management firm — mostly Section 106 stuff ahead of road-widening and tower sites. About a dozen times a season I have to take the artifacts and feature forms from a site and write the "interpretation" section of the report: what the people were doing, what kinds of institutions they might have had, whether a deposit is probably ritual or just trash. The report goes to the state historic preservation office and occassionally gets picked apart by a consultant for the developer or comes up in a meeting with descendant communities, so it has to be defensible. But I also can't hide behind "the evidence is insufficient" for everything, because my PI (principal investigator) will send it back and say I've written a report with no interpretation in it.

I want you, as an archaeologist who works on archaeological inference — formation processes, middle-range theory, ethnographic analogy, the whole problem of getting from static objects back to beliefs and institutions — to build me a reusable decision procedure for moving from a set of finds in context to claims about past beliefs or social institutions. It should be a checklist I can run in maybe half a day with the artifacts, photos, feature forms, and site records in front of me — no new fieldwork, no lab work, and usually no specialist I can call. It should tell me: what to establish about context and formation before I even get to interpretation; which kinds of claims are simply off the table for which kinds of evidence; when an analogy to a historic or ethnographic case is doing real work versus decorating an intuition; when I need to flag a claim as needing a specialist (and what kind); and, importantly, where the procedure should force me to write the cautious sentence rather than the interesting one. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three made-up cases, one of which should come out the annoying way — the procedure stops me from saying the thing I'd like to say:

1. A shallow pit near a stream crossing with sherds from several vessels that look deliberately broken and heavily burned.
2. A child burial with a single drilled stone pendant placed at the neck.
3. A scatter of butchered animal bone and worked stone tools around a large flat boulder, with no other features nearby.

Please seperate what is settled among archaeologists who study inference from what is genuinely contested — for instance, how much weight ethnographic analogy can carry, or whether "ritual" is a defensible category or just a name for what we can't explain functionally. Don't present a contested view as the consensus. I'd rather the procedure be too honest about its limits than too confident: the report can say "we can't tell" and survive review; it can't say something cute and wrong.
