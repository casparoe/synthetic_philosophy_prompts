There's an asymmetry in how people answer existence questions that I want to understand. If I ask whether the pencil on my desk exists, almost everyone answers with a simple yes, and if I suggest that the pencil might neither exist nor fail to exist, most people treat that as wordplay. But if the question is whether a nation still exists after its territory, constitution, and population have all changed beyond recognition, or whether a cloud that has thinned to a few wisps still exists, thoughtful people often say that none of the four standard answers—yes, no, both, or neither—quite fits, and they don't find that four-way failure ridiculous the way they did for the pencil. Nāgārjuna and the Madhyamaka tradition use exactly this four-pronged pattern, called the tetralemma, and hold that it applies to everything whatsoever, because all things are empty of intrinsic nature. Is there a real principle that justifies letting the tetralemma apply to clouds and nations while withholding it from pencils and stones, or is that asymmetry just a habit we should give up? And if the Madhyamaka reasoning really does apply to everything, does emptiness collapse into the claim that nothing exists at all, or is that a conflation that a proper understanding of emptiness rejects?
