In the Introduction to his Commentaries on the Laws of England, William Blackstone allows that a judge may depart from a statute's literal text when the literal reading would produce a result that is manifestly absurd. A colleague has turned this into a general argument, which I reproduce with numbered steps:

(1) If judges may set aside the literal text of a statute to avoid an absurd result, this authority must be grounded in some standard for identifying absurdity.

(2) The only possible standard is the judge's own judgment of what is reasonable, since there is no independent legal text that tells us when a result is absurd.

(3) Therefore the judge may depart from the literal text whenever she judges that following it would be unreasonable.

(4) If a judge may depart from the literal text whenever she judges that following it would be unreasonable, then the literal text is not genuinely binding on her.

(5) So the absurdity doctrine, which purports to allow departure only in exceptional cases, in fact collapses into the view that statutes bind judges only insofar as the judges find them reasonable.

(6) But that is indistinguishable from allowing judges to rewrite statutes according to their own views of good policy.

The conclusion seems too strong: it would make the absurdity doctrine self-defeating as a limit on judicial interpretation. But I cannot tell which step is wrong. Is an equivocation on "absurd" and "unreasonable" doing the work? Is the premise that there is no independent standard false, because legal systems contain shared canons for what counts as an absurd consequence? Or must a defender of the doctrine grant the conclusion and argue that it is not as damaging as it sounds? Diagnose the argument and say what the most defensible version of the absurdity doctrine would need to add to avoid this collapse.
