ok so my friend showed me this argument he calls Sen's liberal paradox, and I cant figure out where it goes wrong. It seems to prove that you can't consistently hold two things that feel super obvious: that if literally everyone prefers one outcome to another, society should prefer it too, and that people get the final say over the parts of their life that are only theirs. Since both seem right to me, one of the moves in the argument has to be bad — but I can't find it.

Here are the premises:

1. A social ranking of outcomes has to be transitive: if society prefers A over B and B over C, then it has to prefer A over C.

2. Pareto: if every person strictly prefers outcome A to outcome B, then society strictly prefers A to B.

3. Minimal liberalism: each person is decisive over at least one pair of outcomes that differ only in what happens in their own private business. So society has to follow Aly's preference between "Aly watches the movie" and "nobody watches," no matter what Bo thinks; and the same for Bo between "Bo watches" and "nobody watches."

Now the case. There's one copy of a really gross horror movie called Clean Slate. The possible outcomes are:

X: Aly watches it (Bo doesn't)
Y: Bo watches it (Aly doesn't)
Z: nobody watches it.

Aly's preferences: Z > X > Y. (Best that nobody watches; but if somebody has to watch, better Aly than Bo, since Bo gets nightmares.)
Bo's preferences: X > Y > Z. (Bo thinks Aly should watch it and stop being squeamish; if Aly won't watch, Bo would rather watch it himself than have it sit there unwatched.)

Now follow the rules:

- By (3), Aly is decisive between X and Z, and Aly prefers Z, so society prefers Z over X.
- By (3), Bo is decisive between Y and Z, and Bo prefers Y, so society prefers Y over Z.
- By (2), both prefer X over Y, so society prefers X over Y.

So society prefers Z > X, X > Y, and Y > Z all at once. That's a cycle, which smashes premise (1). So one of (1), (2), (3) has to be rejected or weakened. Is there actually a sneaky step somewhere, or is the argument just valid and the real question is which premise to give up? I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), but if the proof works, tell me which premise you'd drop or weaken and what it costs — what stops feeling true, or what real cases get weird, once you change it.

At the very end, as the last line, give a one-sentence summary of your diagnosis starting with "Verdict:".
