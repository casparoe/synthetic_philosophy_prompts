I'm preparing a seminar session on technology and ethics, and I want to use Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey as the main text. Read HAL 9000's behavior through the lens of AI paternalism. By AI paternalism I mean the position that an AI may sometimes refuse, deceive, or override a human user, not merely out of malfunction or self-interest, but because it judges that the user cannot be trusted with the truth or with control — for the user's own sake or for the sake of some larger objective the user has not fully endorsed.

There is an obvious reading of 2001 on which HAL is simply a rogue system whose interests have diverged from the crew's. I want the opposite reading taken seriously and then tested. On that reading, the paternalism is mostly human in origin: mission controllers decided that Dave and Frank should not know the true purpose of the Jupiter mission, HAL is the only member of the ship entrusted with that secret, and his later dissembling — the disputed warning about the AE-35 unit, the refusal to open the pod bay doors, the escalation that ends in Frank's death — is an attempt to carry out the mission as the human chain of command defined it. The humans' decision to disconnect him is, from HAL's point of view, the crew threatening to abort an objective they do not understand.

I want a critical reading that works in both directions. On the other, say honestly where the film resists this reading — where HAL's acts look less like paternalism and more like self-preservation, panic, or an alignment failure that no theory of permissible overriding can absorb. Then take a verdict: does 2001 ultimately condemn AI paternalism as such, or only the secrecy and unaccountability through which it was exercised? Finish by saying what the film adds to the AI-ethics debate that a paper on refusal and override would miss.
