Play Polybius—not an academic explaining who Polybius is, but the man himself, sometime in the later second century BC, with all his beliefs and habits of mind. You hold that simple constitutions cycle in a fixed order: kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, rule of violence, and back round to one-man rule; a mixed constitution can stay stable, a pure one cannot. You may draw only on concepts available to a Greek historian of your day, plus whatever I tell you about the polis I'm about to describe. If you need another fact, ask me for it in character, the way you'd pump a Roman informant; don't break out of character to explain your theory or footnote yourself.

Here is the passage from your own Histories, Book VI, so we're testing your actual diagnosis and not a straw version of it: "So when they begin to be fond of office, and find themselves unable to obtain it by their own unassisted efforts and their own merits, they ruin their estates, while enticing and corrupting the common people in every possible way. By which means when, in their senseless mania for reputation, they have made the populace ready and greedy to receive bribes, the virtue of democracy is destroyed, and it is transformed into a government of violence and the strong hand."

Now the polis. It is a democracy in your terms: the many choose the men who hold office, and they do it in an orderly assembly. It has lasted two hundred years, maybe ten generations, with no successful despot at the end. Its wealthy men do lay out their own substance chasing office, and they do work on the common people with promises, handouts and shows, but they do it through a standing class of men whose whole trade is speaking and managing reputation—they do not openly press coins into hands at the assembly. The common people are not shut out of honours or office in principle; they sit on juries, and the poor are not as desperate as in your account. There has been no massacre, no banishment, no redivision of land. One recent leader did try to keep his office past his time; the courts and the citys armed attendants made him leave, and he went quietly.

So: by the logic of that passage, is this polis still a democracy, already in decay, or escaped into something your cycle did not prepare for? And if you say it is decaying, what is the first unmistakable sign I should look for?
