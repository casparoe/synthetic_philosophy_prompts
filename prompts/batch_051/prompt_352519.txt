I serve on a land-use board in a mid-sized city, and at our last hearing a resident argued that we should replace our discretionary variance process with a fixed, published point system because our arrangements are supposed to embody "the rule of law, not of men," and discretion lets individual board members govern rather than the law. A second resident opposed the same point system on the same slogan: a fixed system written by the present staff and board, then enforced without judgment, would simply harden one set of private preferences into the code, and that would be rule by men rather than law. It became clear that the phrase was doing several different jobs at once, and that each side was using a different one. I would like a careful disambiguation, not a history of the phrase and not a general defense of one conception of the rule of law.

Lay out the distinct precise claims that "the rule of law, not of men" can be used to express: claims about the formal character of legal rules (prospectivity, generality, publicity, clarity, stability), about congruence between announced rules and official conduct, about the absence of arbitrary or willful power, about whoever rules being also subject to the law, about equality before the law, about the independence of adjudication from political direction, and about law as reason rather than will. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false or confused, and be explicit about what that reading requires that the others do not. Then return to the two residents' arguments: for each, identify the reading or readings the slogan would need in order to do the work they asked of it, and assess whether the slogan, on its most defensible readings, actually gives them their conclusion. Finally, where the slogan cannot settle the underlying dispute, state what the real disagreement is.
