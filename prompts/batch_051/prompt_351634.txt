I am a novelist, and I have written a piece of moral reasoning for a character that I want to get right before her opponents answer it. The novel is set in a small republic whose officials approve public policies—transit lines, vaccination campaigns, flood defenses—by summing expected benefits and costs across all affected residents and adopting whatever nets out positive. My central character, an administrator who sees this as the only principled way to govern, is accused by her critics of treating separate lives as if they were successive moments of a single life. As her reply, I drafted the following argument; rereading it, I worry that it proves too much, since it appears to license acts she herself finds abhorrent.

(1) A person may permissibly impose a substantial loss on one part of her own life for the sake of a greater gain in another part—a painful operation with a slow recovery, years of frugality, a humiliating apprenticeship—without first obtaining the consent of each earlier stage of herself.
(2) What makes such an intrapersonal trade permissible is simply that the gain to the life as a whole outweighs the loss to it.
(3) The boundary between one life and another is not different in kind from the boundary between stages of a single life; both are matters of memory, disposition, and psychological connection that vary by degree.
(4) Therefore, there is no reason of principle to apply a balancing rule within a single life but refuse to apply it across different lives.
(5) Hence an action or policy is permissible whenever its total benefits across all affected persons outweigh its total costs, with every person's good given the same weight.
(6) But by that test, whenever the totals favor it, it would be permissible to harvest organs from one healthy resident to save five, or to convict an innocent resident to prevent a riot that would kill many more.
(7) Those acts are wrong no matter what the totals say; so at least one of the earlier steps must be given up.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would like to know which step her critics should reject, why that step looks plausible, and what rejecting it would commit them to. In particular, how much does it cost to keep (1) and the humdrum cost-benefit policies while ruling out (6), without giving up everything that made the original practice attractive? For my part, I would prefer to keep (1), but I want a clear accounting of what that preference costs.
