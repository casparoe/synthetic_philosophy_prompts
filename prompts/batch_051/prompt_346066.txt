I'm a philosophy professor who's spent the last few years co-teaching with computer science people, so I can follow talk about inductive biases, sample complexity, and OOD generalization without my eyes completely glazing over. Next month I'm giving a talk to our ML group, and I want to use the occassion to test whether their vocabulary can actually carry Wigner's old puzzle about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in physics — the fact that the world keeps turning out to run on the very formal structures mathematicians invented for their own reasons. My plan is to restate the whole thing in ML terms: the suggestion that the universe lies in an unusually small, learnable hypothesis class, that the history of physics looks like unusually good sample efficiency with an oddly friendly prior, that the math that survives is just the part that generalizes. I know that framing sounds promising, but I don't trust myself to push it through cleanly.

So here's what I'd like from you. Take Wigner's puzzle piece by piece — the core observation, the Darwinian reply that we just select for the mathematics that works, the reply that mathematicians invent beautiful structures and physicists happen to find uses for them, the fact that the same formalism tends to pop up in wildly different corners of physics — and translate each piece into the vocabulary a good ML PhD student would actually use. Then give me the residue report: what maps cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no expression in their vocabulary at all. I especially want your honest verdict about whether the bits that don't survive are evidence that the ML vocabulary is missing something Wigner saw, or evidence that Wigner's puzzle is partly an artifact of a sloppier way of framing the problem. I'm not trying to win the room over; I'd rather leave them with the feeling that the translation was honest and the leftovers are the interesting part.
