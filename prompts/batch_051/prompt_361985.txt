One argument we have studied for licensing parents runs through the child's right to an open future: because parents can foreclose a child's future options, society may screen prospective parents in order to protect that future. The argument was built for choices made after a determinate child exists that close some options off. But newer reproductive choices do neither: selecting embryos on the basis of polygenic risk scores for traits such as educational attainment or temperament does not close any particular life path but shifts the probability distribution over the child's capacities and dispositions, and it occurs before the particular child exists. Does the open-future argument survive this development? In particular, does it need a new premise — for example, that the future must remain open relative to some baseline of genetic chance, or that shaping dispositions is itself a form of foreclosure — or does the fact that the selected child would not otherwise have existed block the extension of the argument? Say which premise of the argument the new practice bears on, and whether the argument can be repaired without stretching the notion of foreclosure so far that it loses its force. I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a policy recommendation.
