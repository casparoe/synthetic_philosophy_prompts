I just got back from a long argument with a friend about career politicians and I left more confused than when we started. We fight about whether term limits for legislators are a good idea: the argument for them seems to be that anyone who does the job too long becomes corrupt or out of touch, so we should force turnover. But we don't term-limit judges or surgeons or air traffic controllers—there we treat accumulated experience as exactly what makes someone trustworthy. And then there are citizens' assemblies, where we literally pick random people by lottery and let them delibarate on electoral reform or climate policy, as if governing doesn't require any special skill at all. My friend says these practices are just inconsistent pragmatism and there's nothing deeper behind them. I suspect they presuppose some actual view of what qualifies a person to make political decisions, but I can't state it. Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories of political competence that these practices presuppose, say whether they presuppose consistent things or just sit in tension, and tell me what would have to change in how we designed institutions if we decided political judgment really was a professional skill like surgery. Is there a coherent view that licenses all three, or do I have to give one up?
