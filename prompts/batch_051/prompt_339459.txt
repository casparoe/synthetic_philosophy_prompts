For a feature I am writing on the intellectual opposition to radical life extension, I have been tracking the public positions of a philosopher of medicine who is frequently cited as offering the most serious version of the case against what she calls prolongevism: the organized effort to push the healthy human lifespan well beyond its current maximum. Because her views are scattered across essays and interviews, I have assembled them into a list of cases with her recorded verdicts, and I would like help working out what principle, if any, governs them and what she would say about a new proposal.

My reconstruction of her record:

- She supports public-health campaigns against smoking and obesity, even though their main effect is to add years to average life expectancy.
- She supports a new cancer treatment that typically gives patients in their seventies and eighties an additional two or three years of relatively healthy life.
- She opposed a hypothetical anti-aging therapy that would add roughly fifteen healthy years but that would, for at least its first decade, cost several million dollars per patient and be available only to the wealthy; her stated objection was the inequality it would create.
- She opposes cryonic preservation after legal death, which she has described as a refusal to accept that a finite life is still a complete one.
- She supports palliative care and the right of terminally ill patients to decline burdensome life-extending treatment, even when that choice may shorten life.
- She supports an Alzheimer's-prevention drug that preserves cognitive health in old age even though it does not much increase total lifespan.
- She opposes whole-brain uploading into a digital substrate, even under the assumption that the upload preserves memory and behavior, on the grounds that the result would no longer be a human life.

The new proposal is a geroprotective: a drug that slows the biological aging process itself. In large trials it appears to delay the onset of age-related disease across the board, adding about twenty-five years of healthy life. It is inexpensive to manufacture and would be distributed through a subsidized public program, so access would be roughly universal rather than determined by ability to pay. It does not alter personality, memory, or appearance, except that treated people are biologically younger than their chronological age.

My question is this. If her assessment of the proposal is to be consistent with her record, should she support it or oppose it? Identify the two or three most plausible principles that could fit her past verdicts, explain what each principle predicts about the geroprotective, and give your best judgment about which principle she is actually relying on, with reasons. Then specify one further case that would most cleanly separate your preferred interpretation from its strongest rival. I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the structure of the inference that supports it.
