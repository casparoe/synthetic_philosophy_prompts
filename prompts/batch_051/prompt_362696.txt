I'm a law student writing a paper for a criminal law seminar, and I keep getting pulled into a disagreement with a friend who used to be a public defender about how far a strict-liability food-safety statute can be stretched. The statute (I've adapted and simplified it from the sort of provision used in several jurisdictions; take its words as authoritative for the exercise) reads:

§ 9(b). Sale or delivery of adulterated food. No person shall sell, offer for sale, expose for sale, or deliver any food that is adulterated. A violation of this subsection is a strict liability offense: proof of knowledge, intent, or negligence is not required, and it is no defense that the defendant did not know and could not reasonably have known of the adulteration, or that the adulteration was caused by another person.

The statute was enacted in 1961 and was plainly drafted with grocers, butchers, and canneries in mind. The case I want an analysis of is this. A company I'll call Shelf runs a smartphone marketplace for artisanal and convenience foods. Shelf does not buy, store, or handle the food. It signs up small independent grocers, lists their items on its app, transmits each order to the grocer, collects payment from the customer and remits it to the grocer minus a commission, and dispatches an independent courier to collect the sealed package from the grocer's counter and deliver it to the customer. Shelf's terms of service state that title passes directly from grocer to buyer and that Shelf is "only a technology intermediary." A customer orders a prepared chickpea salad from one of the listed grocers; the salad is later confirmed to be adulterated. The grocer is a one-person operation with no meaningful assets, so the prosecution wants to charge Shelf under § 9(b), on the theory that Shelf both "offered the food for sale" and "delivered" it through the courier it sent. Everyone agrees Shelf had no knowledge of the adulteration, and Shelf's defense is that a statute punishing without fault should not be read to reach a firm that never possessed the food and had no practical way to inspect it.

Please write a careful assessment of whether § 9(b) reaches Shelf. Work through the problem under these headings, in this order: (1) The interpretive problem (about 150 words); (2) The plain text, including how a reasonable reader would understand "offer for sale" and "deliver" (about 250 words); (3) The purpose of the Act (about 250 words); (4) What the 1961 legislature would most likely have said if asked (about 200 words); (5) Strict liability, fault, and the platform (about 200 words); and (6) Verdict and amendment (about 150 words). In the final section, give a definite verdict on whether Shelf is liable under the statute as written, and say how the statute would have to be amended to make the answer for platform intermediaries clear.
