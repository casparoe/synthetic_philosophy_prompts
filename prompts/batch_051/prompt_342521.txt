I've been going in circles about something at work, and it collided with a philosophy course I'm taking. A guy on my team got promoted over me even though he isn't better at the job — his uncle is a VP — and the feeling I have toward him is not clean, focused anger about the process. It's this sour little wish that he'll screw up in the new role, even though him failing wouldn't fix anything for me. We just read Rawls in my political philosophy course, and his discussion of envy has me spiraling. He treats envy as basically a vice — wanting to tear down someone else's good even when it does you no good — and resentment as something more moral, a reaction to real injustice. But then he also says some envy is excusable, because when inequality gets deep enough it eats away at people's self-respect. I can't tell weather what I'm feeling is the excusable kind, or just plain envy wearing a justice costume.

So here's what I want. Give me a working definition of envy — the term itself, not a taxonomy of everything adjacent to it — that separates envy from resentment, admiration, and merely wanting what someone else has. Test it against these cases:

1. You feel genuinely happy for a friend's engagement, but there's a little pang because you want that kind of relationship too; no wish at all that it fail.
2. You want a colleague's promotion badly enough that you'd take a hit just to see them lose it, even though their losing wouldn't get you the job.
3. You're angry that a coworker got promoted through family connections; the anger is about the rules being rigged, not about wanting their life.
4. A stranger's vacation photos give you a bitter feeling, though you'll never meet them and it costs you nothing.
5. You're paid less than a new hire for the same work; you're furious and your focus is on what you're owed.
6. The Rawls-style case: hostility toward a billionaire's fortune while you can't afford care, where the inequality itself feels like an insult to your standing as a person.
7. A kid has his own identical toy but melts down because his sibling has one too.
8. You admire someone's skill so much that you train harder because of it; no hostility, no wish they'd lose it.
9. A rival fails at something, and you're pleased even though you never wanted the thing they failed at.
10. Your friend gets a job you didn't even apply for, and you feel flat and left-behind; not angry at them, not wishing them ill.

After running the definition through, tell me where it gives a wrong answer or no answer, and whether you'd revise it or bite the bullet and defend the surprising verdict. Then say which of these case-verdicts is least secure. And close with this: is Rawls's excusable envy really envy, or should he have just called it resentment? Because that's what decides whether my feeling about my coworker is a vice I should be ashamed of or a signal that something at my company is actually unjust.
