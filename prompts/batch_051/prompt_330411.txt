I cover AI for a general-interest magazine, and next month I'm giving an hour-long brown-bag to the newsroom: reporters and editors who are fluent in a "ChatGPT does a thing now" way but have never had to think about what it means for a system to be legible rather than just accurate. The throughline I want is that most of the field scores models on correctness—did the answer come out right—and barely asks why, and that's a problem because a model can be right for a reason that would look ridiculous if you could inspect it. I want my colleagues to leave able to explain why a slightly less accurate model whose reasoning you can actually audit might be the better tool to report on approvingly, and why "it works" is not the same as "we know when to trust it."

So I need a plan for the hour, not the talk itself. I want the order the ideas have to go in, and what depends on what—I suspect I can't sell legibility until I've loosened the assumption that correctness is the only thing a newsroom would ever care about, but I'm not sure what has to come before that. I need the one running example that could carry the whole session, and what it has to have and what it has to lack. I need the objections and misunderstandings I should expect, and where in the sequence to head each one off: I'm betting the "human experts are black boxes too, and we dont audit a radiologist's hunches" one arrives early, and someone will conflate legibility with just dumbing the model down, or with those confident-sounding plain-English explanations a model can generate after the fact. And I need a check for understanding—by the end, what should I put in front of them, a pair of toy models or a quick exercise, to know they've actually got it rather than nodded along. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level, since I dont want the hour to turn into a tour of interpretability methods.
