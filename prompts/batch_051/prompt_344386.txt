My brother has spent the past two years reorganizing his life around doing as much good as he can, and I have watched it change him in ways I find hard to put into words: he gave away most of his savings, left a job he loved for one he considers higher-impact, stopped playing music, and now talks about ordinary pleasures as if they were costs to be minimized. I am ambivalent about this, and I cannot tell how much of my unease is insight and how much is my own defensiveness.

Recently I read an article that summarized a famous paper by the philosopher Susan Wolf on the ideal of the moral saint. The summary said roughly this. Wolf argues that a morally perfect person, someone whose every action is as morally good as possible, would be an unattractive ideal. Such a person, on her account, would either be the kind who is genuinely fulfilled only when doing good, or the kind who forces himself to do the right thing against his own inclinations, and both would lack the non-moral excellences that make a human life rich. Because all of the saint's interests would be filtered through morality, the saint would be boring, humorless, and unable to take real joy in art, music, close friendship, or idle pleasures. The article concluded that Wolf has shown that there is no reason to pursue moral perfection, and that when moral demands conflict with the things that give a life richness, we make a mistake if we automatically defer to morality.

I recognized my brother in that description, so I am inclined to trust it, but I have read enough popular summaries of philosophy to know that they often slide. Before I mention it to him, I want to know whether this is a fair account of Wolf's actual position. Which parts would a careful defender of her view accept, which parts misrepresent or exaggerate it, and does the article's conclusion survive once the inaccuracies are corrected?
