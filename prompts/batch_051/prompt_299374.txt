A few of us were hanging out after a funeral for a mutual friend, and Maya and Dan got into an argument about whether death can actually be bad for the person who dies. Maya pulled out her phone and read this from Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, Book III, in William Ellery Leonard's 1916 translation:

Look back:
Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
And Nature holds this like a mirror up
Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
And what is there so horrible appears?
Now what is there so sad about it all?
Is't not serener far than any sleep?

So Maya said the time before we were born doesn't upset us, and the time after we die is just the same thing, so fearing death is a confusion. Dan said that's too neat: before I was born there wasn't any life I had gotten invested in, but now I have projects and people, and what death does is cut those off, so the two sides of the mirror aren't actually symmetric. Maya replied that Dan is smuggling in a subject: after death there's no "me" left to be cut off from anything, so it can't be a bad thing for that nonexistent person. Dan said you don't need the person to exist at the time of the harm—it's bad for you right now that you won't get to raise your kids or finish the novel, the same way we can say someone was wronged by a promise broken after she died. Maya said if you accept that, then you'd also have to say you were harmed by everything you missed before you were born, which is absurd, so there must be something off with Dan's notion of "bad for you." Dan came back with the point that we care about the future because our projects are directed forward in a way they aren't retroactively; that's why pre-birth deprivation isn't on a par. It got to the point where they were basically asking me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Please go through their exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and whether either one is misreading Lucretius.
