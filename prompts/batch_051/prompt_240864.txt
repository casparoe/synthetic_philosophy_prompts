I'm an undergrad TA for an intro ethics course this term and we just collected a short-answer question on utilitarianism and its variants. I keep going back and forth on weather I'm being to harsh on one student's response: it sounds fluent and hits a lot of the right points, but something feels subtly off, and I don't want to let sloppy reasoning slide under the radar. The question was:

Explain how negative utilitarianism differs from classical utilitarianism. Then present one serious objection to negative utilitarianism and explain how a defender might modify the view to avoid it.

The student wrote:

Classical utilitarianism says we should maximize net well-being, that is, total happiness minus total suffering, so it allows some suffering whenever it buys a larger amount of happiness. Negative utilitarianism rejects this symmetry. For the negative utilitarian, suffering has lexical priority over happiness, and happiness has no independant positive value: the only thing that ultimately matters is reducing suffering, and no amount of happiness could ever outweigh even a small amount of suffering. Popper defended a version of this idea in his political philosophy.

The main objection is that the view seems to imply that a world with no sentient beings is morally better than a world with billions of very happy people and one mild headache. Most people find this unacceptable, and it is often called the world-exploder objection, because it seems to commit you to wanting to eliminate all life rather than allow any suffering. A common reply is threshold negative utilitarianism, which says that as long as the average level of well-being in a population stays above a certain level, we may maximize happiness in the ordinary way; only once it falls below that level does the reduction of suffering take lexical priority. This is meant to preserve the anti-suffering intuition while blocking the conclusion that any world with even a tiny amount of suffering is worse than an empty world.
