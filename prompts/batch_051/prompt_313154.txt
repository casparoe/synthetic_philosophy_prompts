The Fermi question has been argued for decades and somehow nobody moves: the optimists point out that life got going on Earth almost as soon as it could, so chemistry must make it easy, while the rare-Earth camp says complex life needs a ridiculous string of flukes — right sort of star, a stabilizing moon, plate tectonics, a Jupiter out there sweeping up debris, and so on — so the galaxy is probably empty. Both sides are looking at the same two observations: one inhabited planet, and a sky that's been silent so far. My own suspicion is that the real disagreement isnt about astronomy at all; its about how to reason from a sample of one when you know the sample is selected by the fact that you're in it, and about whether the silence should count as evidence or just as a measure of how little searching we've actually done. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine empirical or statistical disagreement hidden underneath that could be settled? I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a rundown of who thinks what — is it priors, is it standards of evidence, are the sides quietly answering different questions while using the same word, likely, to mean different things? — and then an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress: a biosignature, a second genesis found inside our own solar system, a negative result of some particular size, a formal argument about selection effects, or the conclusion that no progress should be expected and why. And since people overclaim about this stuff, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and which part of it you're least sure of.
