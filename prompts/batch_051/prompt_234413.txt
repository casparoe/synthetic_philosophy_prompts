I'm in a philosophy of probability course and we're about to hit the reference class problem again, and I'm already annoyed. Every author uses it as a club: frequentists get beaten with it, Bayesians say it's a pseudoproblem, then somebody shows it sneaks back into conditional probability, and nobody ever says what an adequate solution would even look like. Before I read one more paper, I want the spec: what would a theory of how single-case probabilities get assigned have to deliver in order to count as actually handling the reference class problem?

Quick setup in case it helps: same single event, diferent probabilities depending on the class you slot it into. The 55-year-old man has one probability of a heart attack as "55-year-old man," another as "55-year-old man, sedentary, family history, high LDL," and if you keep adding details you end up with a class of one and probability 0 or 1. So the burden is: given all the information about a case, which reference class (if any) should fix the probability — and if no class fixes it, what else is going on.

I want your desiderata, not a survey or reading list. For each requirement I want: the case or consideration that forces it, and why a theory that fails it is genuinely defective rather than just inconvenient to somebody's pet view. Then say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just preferances of a particular camp (frequentist, Bayesian, pragmatic, whatever).

Here are some demands I've seen people make that I want you to rule on one by one (but add others if the cases force them): a way to stop the collapse to the singleton class; respect for total evidence; a reason why "the narrowest class with enough statistics" is or isn't the right rule; an account of which properties are allowed to define a class in the first place; consistency with the fact that different classes license different bets and policy calls; coverage of one-off events with no natural class. For each, tell me whether it's a genuine desideratum, a disguised prejudice, or just a special case of something deeper.

Assume the theory has to be good enough for messy single cases — medical prognosis, legal fact-finding, insurance, policy — not just coins and urns.

Format: a markdown table with these columns:
- Requirement: one sentence on what the theory must deliver or avoid
- Forcing case / consideration: the example or argument that shows why it's needed
- Why it's a real requirement: what breaks if the theory fails it
- Status: non-negotiable / negotiable / camp-specific, with a one-line note on whose camp and why

Then below the table, a JSON object with these fields:
- "most_discriminating": the one requirement that rules out the most popular positions, and why (one or two sentences)
- "tension": a pair of requirements that might not be jointly satisfiable, or null if you think the full list can be met, with one sentence on where the conflict is
- "least_confident": the row of the table you are least sure is a genuine requirement
- "what_would_change_my_mind": which argument or example would get you to drop that row, or demote it to camp-specific

Keep the JSON values tight; the table is the main thing.
