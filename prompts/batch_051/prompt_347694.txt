I've been going back and forth on the Löbian obstacle to self-trust. The formal side seems hard to escape: a reasoner powerful enough to represent its own proofs cannot internalize its own soundness, because by Löb's theorem, proving the reflection principle "if I prove P then P" for an arbitrary P would already amount to proving P, and the general policy "whatever I prove is true" cannot be established without proving everything, contradictions included. Yet I cannot shake the conviction that I do, and should, trust my own reasoning. In ordinary thought I treat the fact that I have carefully concluded something as a reason to believe it, and I take the step from "the argument is valid and the premises are true" to "the conclusion is true" to be one I am entitled to make, not merely one I am stuck making. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the philosophical reading of the theorem to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of "trust my own reasoning" on which both the formal obstacle and the intuition survive.
