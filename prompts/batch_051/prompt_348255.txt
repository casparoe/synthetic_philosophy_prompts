For the better part of a decade I have read rationalist blogs and forums closely, and that community's vocabulary is now the default instrument I use to carve up philosophical problems. The vocabulary runs roughly as follows: map versus territory; the is-ought gap handled by treating values as preferences or utility functions rather than as propositions with truth values; reductionism and levels of explanation; naturalism as the view that the scientific image of the world is all there is; dissolving a question as confused rather than answering it head-on; and an epistemic culture built on calibration, predictive accuracy, and empirical content. I am fluent in this vocabulary, but I have no formal training in academic philosophy.

W. V. Quine is the hardest test case I know for deciding how much of that vocabulary is neutral rather than quietly partisan. Everything I have read by Quine points the same way: there is no first-philosophy vantage point outside science; epistemology should be naturalized into psychology; language and the mental are to be studied through observable behavior rather than inner ideas; science is the final tribunal for factual questions. His remarks on moral values, scattered across his work and most explicit in a short late essay, appear to follow the same line. As I read him, moral judgments are not pieces of scientific theory; there is no empirical procedure for adjudicating ultimate values; and the interesting question about our values is not whether they are true but how they come about, through natural selection, social conditioning, and the internal economy of preference. Yet Quine does not seem to say simply that moral sentences are false, or that moral talk is meaningless. The position is some kind of deflation, but it is hard for me to say exactly what kind. If my gloss is wrong on some point, say so before translating; I want a translation of Quine, not of a strawman.

Restate Quine's position on moral values as faithfully as you can in the rationalist vocabulary I just described. Be precise about the mapping. What in Quine corresponds to the claim that moral language does not track any part of the territory? What, if anything, corresponds to a claim about the status of a utility function? What does the naturalizing of epistemology become in this vocabulary, and does dissolving the question do the same work for Quine that it does on the forums when someone says a dispute was confused rather than wrong? Then give me a residue report. Which parts of Quine's view translate cleanly, which parts have to be distorted or silently amended to fit the vocabulary, and which parts have no expression in it at all? Finally, tell me which direction the residue cuts. I suspect my vocabulary pushes toward reading Quine as a straightforward anti-realist about moral claims: moral language refers to nothing in the territory, and moral disagreement is largely a clash of utility functions. But I also suspect that reading quietly imports a preference-satisfaction picture of value, and a clean separation of belief from preference, that Quine himself might have regarded as unearned machinery. Assess that suspicion explicitly, and say whether the residue indicts the target vocabulary, Quine's position, or both.
