I was arguing with a friend last week about art and forgeries, and I keep going back to it because I can’t tell if we were tracking a real distinction or just a status thing. We both agreed that if you found out a painting you’d admired for years was actually a perfect fake, you’d feel cheated, even if no one had ever noticed and it gave you the same visual experience the whole time. But the same friend streams music constantly and has zero problem with the fact that the song he hears is a digital file copied thousands of times from some master version; he doesn’t think he’s missing out on the “real” music the way he’d think he missed out on the real painting. And it gets weirder: people will crowd around a displayed original manuscript, where you can’t even read it properly, but they wouldn’t pay anything like the same attention to a perfectly printed edition of the exact same words.

So there’s this asymmetry in ordinary practice: for some objects, exact copies and forgeries are treated as radically lower in value, almost worthless, while for others, copying is value-neutral or even the whole point. I want to know whether anything could justify that line, or whether it’s basically snobbery, historical fetishism, and accident. What kind of difference—causal contact with the artist, uniqueness, time of creation, skill, intention, history—would have to matter for the asymmetry to be principled rather than arbitrary? And are there nearby cases that would force weird conclusions if you tried to make the principle fully general?
