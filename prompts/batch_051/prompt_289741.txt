I'm taking a seminar on mathematical explanation, and I keep getting stuck on what it means to understand a proof. One influential line of thought says that understanding a proof just is the capacity to check its steps: for each line, you can see that it follows from earlier lines by an accepted rule of inference, and this is both necessary and sufficient. The argument behind the view is that a proof's claim on us rests entirely on its transitions being rule-governed, so anyone who has verified those transitions has received everything the proof itself can offer; anything further — the big picture, the intuition, the analogy that makes the proof feel natural — is heuristic psychology for finding and remembering proofs, not part of understanding as such. The appeal is that step-checking is the one relation to a proof that cannot be faked: if you cannot certify the transitions, you have not understood it, whatever else you may feel.

But mathematical practice now includes a development that seems to put this argument under strain. Interactive proof assistants can take an ordinary proof, expand it into a fully formal derivation, and mechanically certify that every step is valid. Some substantial theorems — the four-color theorem is the standard case, and there are later results like it — depend on exhaustive computations that no human has checked line by line. In these cases the machine performs exactly the operation the step-checking view identifies with understanding, and it does so more reliably and exhaustively than any human could. Yet we are reluctant to say the machine understands the theorem, and we are also reluctant to say that a working mathematician who grasps the global structure of such a proof but has not personally verified every computation has failed to understand it.

My question is what this does to the step-checking argument. Does the argument survive because it was implicitly about human knowers, and if so is that an ad hoc retreat? If it does not survive in its original form, what is the best repaired version — for example, one on which step-checking is necessary but something further is required, such as the ability to explain why the proof has the shape it does or to say where it would break under small changes? I am asking how far the argument can be salvaged and which premise the new cases actually undermine, not for a survey of accounts of understanding.
