Build me a thought experiment in which Mozi's impartial care (jian'ai) and a Confucian ethics of graded love swap their usual roles. I want a single ordinary-life case — one household or civic setting, no wars, no famines, no perfect predictors or demons — where the Mohist recommends, not merely permits, that the agent devote a scarce resource to a near relative rather than to a stranger, while the Confucian, despite holding that care begins with and runs thickest at the family, requires the agent to put the stranger's claim first, because of something the case itself supplies. The scenario should run no longer than one short paragraph.

Then run the check. State what each view directs the agent to do, lay out the reasoning internal to each view that yields that verdict, and identify the single feature of the case that is doing the real work. Then say how both verdicts shift when that feature is removed. Finish with how a defender of whichever view comes out looking worse would attack the case — whether by relocating the distinction, or by denying that the case applies the view levelly.

This is for a seminar in comparative ethics where we are trying to stop reading graded love as mere nepotism and impartial care as naive self-sacrifice. If no such case can be built without travestying one of the two views, tell me that plainly instead of forcing one; I would rather learn that the inversion is unavailable than receive a case that cheats.
