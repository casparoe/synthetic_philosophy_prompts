In Christian responses to the problem of pre-human suffering, one widely used image is that of creation in labour: the New Testament speaks of the whole creation groaning in the pains of childbirth, and some contemporary theologians turn this into a theodicy that reconciles evolutionary history with the doctrine of a good creation. On this view, the predation, parasitism, disease and extinction uncovered by evolutionary biology, all of it long before human sin could have introduced death into the world, are real pains, but they are birth pangs: a finished creation that could not have come about any other way is being delivered. Just as we do not call a mother cruel for the pain of a delivery that brings a child into the world, we should not call God cruel for a process by which the new creation is born. Audit this analogy. Set out what maps onto what, including the mother, the child, the pain, the delivery and the joy that follows. Then identify where the mapping breaks and what the source domain quietly imports, such as conscious agency, foresight, consent, a beneficiary who could not otherwise exist, an end to the suffering, and a sufferer whose own good the pain serves. Where the analogy fails, does it fail in ways that matter for the theological conclusion, or only in ways that can be repaired? If it cannot be repaired, say what conclusion about God and evolutionary suffering is actually left standing, and whether a better image would do the work more honestly.
