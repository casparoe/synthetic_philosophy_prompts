I spent thirty years in structural biology, and lately I've been reading philosophy of mind. The Searle Chinese Room thing has grabbed me, but every time I try to explain it at my reading group I notice we're not actually disagreeing about Searle's thought experiment itself; we're disagreeing about what version of the anti-computationalist conclusion it's supposed to establish. So I'd like to make a one-page handout that sorts people into positions without using the usual labels, because most folks in the group haven't read the literature and would just say systems reply without knowing why.

In Searle's 1980 Behavioral and Brain Sciences paper, as I understand it, the person in the room is given slips of Chinese writing, looks up matching symbols in a big book of rules, and writes down what the book says; the man understands no Chinese, and Searle says the room-plus-man-plus-rulebook set-up produces sensible answers without understanding the story, so running a program is not enough for understanding, intentionality, or other mental states. I want the handout to be a decision tree of maybe eight to ten leaves. Each branch should ask a concrete yes/no question about the room case or a close variant, for example whether the room as a whole might understand even if the man doesn't, whether you could add a sensorimotor robot body and change your answer, whether there is a difference between simulating digestion and duplicating it, whether evolution or learning matters, whether the conclusion only concerns digital computers or all physical symbol-processors, whether intentionality is observer-relative, and whether a program that copies the firing pattern of a Chinese speaker's brain changes things.

The leaves should include the standard families — systems reply, robot reply, brain simulator reply, virtual mind, connectionism, and Searle's own biological naturalism — but also any unnamed or unexplored combinations the tree produces. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward committment, and flag if the questions can't actually separate two positions. I realize this sounds like a lot for a handout, but I can shrink the tree later; I'd rather start with too many distinctions then too few.
