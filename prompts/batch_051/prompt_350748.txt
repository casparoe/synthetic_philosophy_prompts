I keep encountering the same analogy in arguments about democratic backsliding, and I want a careful account of how much work it can do. The analogy runs roughly like this: a democracy's collapse is not like a coup, which is a single lethal blow; it is a death by a thousand cuts. Each individual cut — a change to judicial appointment rules, a takeover of public media, a gerrymander, an executive push against an unwritten norm, a purge of the civil service — is survivable on its own and often defensible on narrow legal or technocratic grounds, but cumulatively the cuts kill the democracy, and because no single cut is the fatal one, people can argue indefinitely about when death occurred.

I heard the analogy used to license a specific conclusion: that we are entitled to call a country backsliding even when no individual step, considered alone, looks like more than ordinary contentious politics. The point of the analogy, on this use, is that demanding critics identify the single autocratic moment sets the standard impossibly high. A colleague turned the analogy against that conclusion: it presupposes the very classification it is meant to support, because whether some contested move is a cut or merely a disliked policy is exactly what is at issue. If we call it a cut, the analogy adds nothing; if we refuse to call it a cut, the analogy cannot get started. This matters concretely in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States, where the same sequences of events get described by some observers as slow-motion democratic death and by others as normal democratic competition with the label backsliding used as a partisan weapon.

Please audit the analogy. I want: (a) the mapping laid out explicitly — what corresponds to the patient, the blade, a single cut, the bleeding, the cumulative wound, and the eventual death; (b) where the mapping breaks down, including which features of the literal source scenario the analogy quietly imports into politics, and a separation of the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that do not; (c) an assessment of whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support — that backsliding can be identified without identifying a discrete autocratic event and without first settling every dispute about which moves count as cuts — survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and (d) any repair or better analogy you think could carry the load.
