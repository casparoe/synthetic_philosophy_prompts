I am taking a course this term on the philosophy of mathematics, and we have been working through the incompleteness theorems in some detail. Something keeps nagging at me. The standard statement of the first theorem is that any sufficiently strong, consistent formal system rich enough to encode a little arithmetic can express a sentence that the system can neither prove nor refute. What stays with me is not the arithmetic but the picture of a system powerful enough to talk about its own rules. I have ended up with a homemade principle I have been calling the Institutional Incompleteness Principle. It says, roughly: any institution whose codified rules are rich enough to adjudicate disputes about the rules themselves—for example, a university disciplinary code that includes procedures for amending that code, or a legal system that governs its own procedures—will necessarily confront well-formed disputes that the stated rules cannot resolve determinately. In such a case neither ruling can be derived from the rules alone, and the institution must either fall silent, amend itself ad hoc, or contradict some other rule. The principle adds that this is not the familiar problem of vagueness or poor drafting. It is structural, in the way a Gödel sentence is not undecidable because of a gap in the axioms but because of what the system can say about itself.

I am aware this may be a badly inflated analogy. I can already hear the objection that a legal system is not a formal language, that judges and exercises of discretion fill exactly this gap, and that Gödel's result depends on a precise notion of proof that institutions lack. Still, the idea feels as though it should capture something real about why no constitution, employee handbook, or platform content policy can settle its own edge cases in advance. I would like an honest assessment. In its strongest form, is there a genuine structural point here, or is the mathematical result lending borrowed authority to a banal point about underdetermination? Does the principle already exist in some cleaner form I should know about? Where precisely does the analogy between a formal system and an institution break—is it the absence of a proof relation, the role of interpretation, the lack of a consistency requirement, or something else that does the decisive work? And if you had to salvage a true and non-trivial version of the principle, what would it say?
