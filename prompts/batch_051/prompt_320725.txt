I spent most of my career in fluid dynamics and only got into philosophy after I retired, and I keep running into the same snag with this idea of computational irreducibility. The claim, as I understand it, is that for many rule-governed systems theres no shortcut: the only way to find out what the system will do is to run it, and no compact formula or model can skip the steps. I find the arguments for it compelling. It follows pretty directly once you grant universal computation, it gives one clean story for why so many systems resist prediction, and it comes with a tidy criterion — if the fastest way to know the outcome is to simulate the system, the system is irreducible. But I also have a working scientists intuition I cant shake: that theres always a pattern, a model, an effective description, even if we havent found it. I didn't need to simulate every eddy to say something true about turbulence; I coarse-grained. If a system is computationally irreducible, then coarse-graining fails in principle for that system, not just in practice, and that seems to make explanation impossible for exactly the systems that matter.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what would justify each move: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes or gets narrowed until it stops threatening the intuition, and is there a reading where both survive — irreducibility holding for exact trajectories while the questions science actually asks stay compressible? Is "the intuition is just a scope confusion" a real argument, or just a way of not listening to it? Please keep it under 800 words.
