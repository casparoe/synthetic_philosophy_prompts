A subscription service's Terms of Service contain the following provision:

"You agree to these Terms, and to any modified Terms we post after your enrollment, by clicking 'I Agree' or by using the Service after the modification. Any use of the Service through a device linked to your account is use by you and constitutes your acceptance of the then-current Terms."

A user opens an account and later installs, on a home computer linked to that account, an open-source automation that logs into the service once a day, fetches new posts, and dismisses any routine dialog by clicking its primary button. The user configures the automation to handle cookie notices and maintenance banners, stops checking the service personally, and goes abroad for several weeks. During this period the service posts a modification adding a binding arbitration clause and a class-action waiver, and on the next login it presents the standard "I Agree" dialog. The automation clicks "I Agree." The user returns, unaware that anything changed, and continues using the service until a dispute later arises.

Does this provision bind the user to the arbitration clause and class-action waiver? Work through what each of the following yields and where they come apart: the plain wording of the provision; the apparent purposes of clickwrap terms; what the drafters would most plausibly have said had this exact scenario been put to them; and what a reasonable account holder would have understood "you agree" and "your use" to mean. In an adhesion setting, which of these should control, and what feature of the automated click is doing the decisive work? Finally, set aside the legal outcome: is there any meaningful sense in which this user agreed to the new terms, or has "agreement" become only a label for an event that happened on a screen nobody watched?
