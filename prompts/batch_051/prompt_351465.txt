I've been going back and forth on the spectrum arguments against the transitivity of 'better than' — the ones where you walk from a short, excruciating pain to a long, barely-noticeable nuisance, every adjacent step gets a clear verdict (the previous is better than the next), but when you compare the two ends directly the verdict points the other way. I have a homemade idea I've been calling "rate drift," and I want to know whether it's a real distinction, a relabeling of something obvious, or just wrong.

The claim: each pairwise "better than" judgment is made under a local exchange rate between the dimensions involved — here, intensity traded against duration — and that rate depends on how far apart the two items are. When two pains are close in intensity, the rate treats a small drop in intensity as almost worthless, so duration decides; when two pains are far apart in intensity, the rate treats intensity as decisive and duration barely registers. In a spectrum, every adjacent pair is close in intensity, so duration decides each time; the endpoints are far apart, so intensity decides. The rate drifts as you walk down the chain, which means you never use the same 'better than' relation twice. Transitivity is fine; what's nontransitive is the mixed relation "ranked better by some local exchange-rate frame." The view also makes a prediction: if you force yourself to fix a single exchange rate before running the spectrum, the chain stops being puzzling at exactly the spot where that fixed rate stops applying, and the endpoints come out consistent.

I'm aware this sounds both too neat and like the thing everyone already thought of and rejected. So: is this a real distinction or just a metaphor? Which existing view does it actually collapse into — Temkin's essentially comparative view, Parfit's imprecise comparability, Broome's vagueness, Chang's parity, or something else — and is it actually distinct from all of them? Then steelman it first and say what it genuinely gets right, before giving me the three strongest objections. Make at least one objection not be about the labels: where does the view predict something false, or stop doing any real work?
