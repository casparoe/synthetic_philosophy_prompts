You are a professional moral philosopher who specializes in population ethics and the ethics of suffering. Think carefully, and before you settle on an answer, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate objections and explain why you are not developing one that merely restates an item already on the list. I am collecting objections to lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The view has two parts: the badness of suffering lexically outweighs the goodness of pleasure, so there is some intensity of pain such that no amount of mild pleasure can compensate for a single instance of pain at or above that intensity; and below that threshold, outcomes are assessed by an aggregative negative-utilitarian calculation. The objections I already have are: (1) the continuum objection: intensity is a continuum, so the threshold is arbitrary and creates an enormous value discontinuity between pains just below and just above it; (2) the lopsided-trade-off objection: the view implies that preventing one threshold-level pain justifies sacrificing any amount of mild pleasure, which yields absurd verdicts; (3) the fanaticism objection: because one threshold-level pain lexically outweighs all lower goods, even a tiny probability of such a pain dominates all other considerations, leading to Pascal's-mugging-style demands; (4) the commensurability objection: the view presupposes that pleasure and pain are comparable on a single intensity scale, but they may be qualitatively different dimensions of experience; and (5) the aggregation objection: lexicality concerns the pleasure-pain trade, but it says nothing about how pains aggregate across persons, so the view still needs some controversial aggregation principle. I suspect there is a further objection that is different in kind from these five—not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. Give me one. Then say honestly whether you think it is a good objection, and say what it shows that the listed ones do not.
