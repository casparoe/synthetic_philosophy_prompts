You are a legal philosopher who works on defamation law and freedom of speech. I want a precise working definition of "assertion of fact" as it functions in defamation law: the category of statement that, when false and reputationally damaging, can ground liability. Assume a U.S.-style framework in which liability attaches only to statements that a reasonable audience would understand as asserting or implying a provably false fact about an identifiable person or entity, while pure opinions, hyperbole, evaluations, and predictions are protected. I am not asking for case citations or a survey of jurisdictions; I want the conceptual line.

Start by thinking aloud about the plausible tests: verifiability, specificity, the context and genre of the speech, whether the statement carries a factual implication, the presence of words like "in my opinion," and whether ratings and forecasts should be treated as speech at all or as something closer to products. Diagnose what each test gets wrong on at least one easy case.

Then give your working definition and run it through each of the following cases. For each case, classify it as an actionable assertion of fact, a protected opinion/evaluation/prediction, or genuinely indeterminate under your definition, and say whether that classification surprises you. Where your definition conflicts with your starting intuition, say whether you are revising the definition or defending the surprising classification.

The cases:

1. A restaurant critic writes: "This is the worst restaurant in the city."
2. A user posts a one-star review of a plumber on a rating platform, with no accompanying text.
3. A financial newsletter writes: "We rate XYZ Corp a Sell. Our target price is $3; the stock now trades at $9."
4. A bond-rating agency downgrades a city's general-obligation bonds from AA to CCC.
5. A tweet: "The mayor is corrupt — in my opinion, he is taking kickbacks from city contractors."
6. A columnist writes: "I predict the CEO will be indicted within twelve months."
7. A consumer advocacy group publishes "The Ten Most Dangerous Toys of the Year" and lists specific products by name, without stating the criteria.
8. A professor-rating website displays a single "quality score" from 0 to 5 for each professor, algorithmically generated from free-text student comments.
9. A campaign advertisement states: "Senator Johnson voted to cut Medicare."
10. A satirical website runs the headline: "BREAKING: Mayor Endorses Child Labor, Says Kids Add Flavor," above a plainly absurd article.

I am confident that 1 is protected, 10 is protected satire, and 9 is an assertion of fact. I expect, without yet having a principled justification, that 3, 4, and 8 should come out protected as opinion or evaluation. Cases 2, 5, and 7 are the ones that shake that expectation.

Finish by saying where your definition stops yielding clean answers, and whether ratings and forecasts are best handled by the fact/opinion distinction itself, by a separate policy carve-out, or by something else entirely.
