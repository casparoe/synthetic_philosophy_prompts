I am a high school student, and we just finished reading George Bernard Shaw's Pygmalion in class. My teacher has asked us to write about whether the play has a philosophy of language of its own, and I keep getting stuck on the question of whether the play is more prescriptivist or descriptivist about grammar. I am especially interested in the moment early in the play where Higgins, after showing off his ability to identify people's exact neighborhoods from their speech, tells Pickering that he could take a woman like Eliza Doolittle and pass her off as a duchess at an embassy ball simply by teaching her to speak differently. It is hard for me to tell if Shaw is presenting Higgins's view as a reductio of class pretensions, or as a truth about language that the rest of the play actually depends on.

I would like a careful reading of Pygmalion through the prescriptivism/descriptivism debate. Does Higgins treat Eliza's original way of speaking as genuinely defective grammar, or merely as a social code she has not learned? How does the play treat the line between standard English and so-called bad grammar—does it show that the line is real because language has internal standards, or that it is a class invention because everything Eliza says is perfectly intelligible? I would like you to work scene by scene: the first meeting, the lessons, the embassy ball, and especially the ending, where Eliza has mastered the accent but has not become a duchess inside. Where does the play seem to endorse Higgins's view, and where does it resist it? And what does Shaw's dramatization add to the philosophical dispute that a prescriptivist or descriptivist essay might miss? I want to write an essay that is faithful to both the play and the linguistic debate, not just use the play as decoration for a slogan.
