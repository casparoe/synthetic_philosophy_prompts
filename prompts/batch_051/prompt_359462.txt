I am a novelist, and the question I want examined is one my craft forces on me. A novelist's chief instrument is the management of attention: I decide which character's consciousness the prose inhabits, whose death is rendered as a particular loss and whose as a figure in a news report. This makes me suspicious that a familiar philosophical claim may be, at least in part, a literary artefact.

The claim concerns rescue cases: when one can save either one stranger or several strangers, some philosophers hold that the numbers supply a reason — that the fact that five would die rather than one itself counts in favour of saving the five. Others hold that the only relevant unit is the individual claim, that such claims cannot be summed across persons, and that the numbers therefore do no independent work. The question is whether the numbers should count.

My suspicion is that the standard cases are contaminated by focalisation, the very variable I manipulate for a living. A case must be written down, and in the writing someone is made vivid or left a numeral, named or anonymous, given an interior or shown from outside. If moral judgement tracks that framing while the numbers stay fixed, then the appearance that the numbers carry weight may owe more to narration than to the moral structure of the situation.

I would like you to propose the strongest available test of this artefact hypothesis, and to say precisely what such a test would and would not settle. Give a concrete empirical design — vignettes, surveys, or behavioural tasks — in which numbers, focalisation, individuation and anonymity are varied independently. State which pattern of results would favour the view that the numbers themselves do independent moral work, which would favour the view that the work is done by framing, and why those two hypotheses are difficult to separate even in a well-designed study. Finally, be honest about the residue: whether any experimental result could close the gap between what people find morally salient and what they ought to do, and whether the claim that the numbers count would genuinely survive even a result in its favour. Please write the answer in British English.
