I volunteer as a coach for a high-school debate team, and this season I have kept noticing the same failure: one side attacks the exact wording of a claim while the other side insists that no reasonable person would have taken them to mean that. In my own reading I've become interested in the idea associated with H. P. Grice that what a sentence means and what a speaker means can come apart — that saying "Mary got an A or a B" will normally communicate, without being stated, that she did not get both, or that saying "the door is over there" can communicate a request to leave. I'd like to run a single forty-five-minute session for the team that builds the basic machinery behind this — the idea that conversation is a cooperative activity, that it runs on unstated expectations, and that implicatures are something speakers produce and listeners recover — without turning it into a linguistics lecture. The students don't know any of the terminology, and I don't want them merely to learn labels; I want them to come away able to separate, in a live exchange, what was said from what was implicated, and to use that distinction when they assess an opponent's claim.

Plan that session for me rather than writing the lecture itself. In what order should the pieces be introduced, and what depends on what? What single example or running case should carry the whole session, and what features must it have and what must it avoid? Which predictable misconceptions should I expect — for example, conflating implicature with lying, with sarcasm or mere hinting, with any inference the listener happens to draw, or with the view that only literal wording counts in a debate — and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off instead of corrected after the fact? How should the session end: what question or short exercise would show me that the distinction has landed, rather than that they can repeat definitions back? And what should I explicitly set aside or refuse to cover at this level, so the discussion doesn't collapse into technical controversy? I'm looking for a teaching plan whose structure is itself doing the pedagogical work.
