Im a PhD student in philosophy of language, trying to get my views on presupposition into some kind of order before a term paper. Here's the package, roughly as I'd defend it.

(1) Presuppositions are semantically triggered by items like definites, factives, and aspectual verbs ('know', 'stop', 'the king of France'): they're part of conventional meaning, not just speaker implicatures.
(2) The Stalnaker/Lewis scorekeeping story is basically right: assertion proposes to update the common ground, presuppositions act as constraints on the input context, and hearers quietly accommodate unproblematic presuppositions they didn't already accept.
(3) But accommodation can be exploited, and there's a real norm against it: presupposing something new and loaded ('after my third divorce...') can sneak content past the normal scrutiny, and the hearer is entitled to object with 'hey, I didn't know that' rather than just absorb it.
(4) When a presupposition is false and can't be accommodated, the utterance suffers a truth-value gap or definedness failure: it's neither true nor false.
(5) Yet I keep thinking 'the king of France is bald' is just false. If I saw it as a line in a truth-table exercise, I'd mark it false, not 'gappy'.
(6) I also think the presupposition/assertion distinction is doing real work: that sentence doesn't assert that France has a king; that's exactly why it doesn't feel like 'France has a king, and he's bald.'
