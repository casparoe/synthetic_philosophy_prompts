i've been chewing on a distinction for months and i want someone to tell me if it does real work or if im just dressing up self-deception. i call them forecast-optimism and stance-optimism. forecast-optimism is a take on the probabilities: you think the odds are better than the evidence says, or you refuse to update, or you tell yourself the interview probably went fine. thats a belief, and when it ignores evidence its an epistemic vice. stance-optimism is different. it isnt a prediction at all; its a practical stance toward a person or a project. you accept the grim numbers fully — you can say out loud "its a five percent chance" — but you keep treating the good outcome as live: you organize around it, you show up, you refuse to write the person off. my standing example is the friend who knows the survival stats cold and still plans as if the recovery is the thing to organize around, because she thinks thats what friendship asks of her. i want to say most laudable hope-against-the-odds is stance-optimism, and that its a moral virtue while forecast-optimism is the epistemic vice.

the thing i cant shake is whether stance-optimism really escapes epistemic norms or just relabels them. if it guides action, and actions commit you, then isnt acting as if the good outcome is live a way of having a belief-like attitude that outruns the evidence? can you hold a 5 percent forecast and a wholehearted practical stance on the same outcome without being incoherent or a hypocrite? does the distinction already exist under another name (it feels adjacent to belief vs acceptance, but not quite seperate)? and where does it break — what does it wrongly count as a virtue, or which ugly behavior does it let someone dress up as loyalty?

give me a committed answer, not a menu: keep the distinction, amend it, or junk it — and if it survives, tell me what a defensible version has to add.
