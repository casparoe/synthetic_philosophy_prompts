I serve on a parole review board, deciding roughly forty cases a month, often in long afternoon sessions. Several years ago our unit's internal audit found something that should not have surprised me: decisions formed in the last hour of a session were later overturned, or sent for further assessment, at a markedly higher rate than decisions formed earlier in the day, even after the case files had been matched for seriousness and prior record. The audit did not identify any particular mistake in any particular decision; it was a purely statistical finding about the process that produced the decisions.

I have since adopted a policy. Whenever I form a judgement in those suspect conditions — the final hour, or when I am unusually tired, hungry, or angry — I later discount my confidence in that judgement. The next working day I review the file afresh. If the fresh review turns up a specific flaw, I revise in the ordinary way. But the crucial rule is this: when the fresh review finds no flaw and the reasoning still seems to me, on review, to be sound, I keep a discount anyway. I do not return to my original confidence, and I act on that discounted view — voting for a further assessment that my own re-reading of the file suggests is unnecessary, or choosing the more cautious of two defensible decisions. To make the policy vivid: last month I re-read a file the morning after a long session and, left to my own lights, judged the risk of reoffending to be roughly one in three; because the original decision had been made in the final hour, I voted as if the risk were substantially higher and requested a further report I would not otherwise have asked for.

A colleague insists this is epistemic akrasia. After my fresh review, he says, I in effect believe the file supports a certain judgement, yet I refuse to act on it, or I lower my stated confidence, on evidence that concerns only the reliability of the process by which I formed the judgement and says nothing about this case. And I treat the very fact that the reasoning still seems sound to me on review as carrying little weight, because an unreliable process would leave exactly that impression behind. To which I reply: that is precisely the point of having higher-order evidence — evidence about my own reliability — and a purely statistical audit is still evidence. My inability to see an error does not rebut the audit; it is predicted by it.

Is this policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? I would like a careful assessment that treats the policy as a policy: when it is reliable, when it is likely to fail, whether it can be restated so as to keep the benefit without the self-mistrust, and whether the charge of epistemic akrasia is in this case a fatal objection or a cost that a rational person should sometimes pay. If the policy is defensible, is there any principled way to set the size of the discount and to decide which conditions count as suspect, beyond trusting whatever the audit happened to measure? And under what evidence would you tell me to abandon the policy altogether? Please give your answer in British English.
