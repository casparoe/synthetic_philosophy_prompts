As a novelist, I find that a strictly technical problem of craft has gradually hardened into a philosophical view. For years I have been trying to write first-person scenes in which a character's later judgement feels inevitable to the reader, and the usual advice has been to begin with bare perception, uncoloured by interpretation, so that the judgement can arise from it. I have become convinced that this advice cannot be followed, and that the impossibility is not a limitation of prose but a fact about justification. Any sentence of narration selects one detail rather than another, chooses a register, and thereby classifies what it reports; there is no sentence that simply hands over the given. Even so austere a description as the light went grey has already selected greyness as the respect in which the change matters, and has already brought criteria for what counts as a light and as grey. I have started calling this view the narrative given, and I use it as a drafting rule: whenever a passage presents itself as an unadorned record that justifies a character's belief, I mark it as the fallacy of the innocent detail and rewrite it so that the character's concepts show up inside the description rather than arriving afterwards as its natural sequel.

I am not a professional philosopher, but this seems close to what Sellars attacks as the myth of the given — the idea, as I understand it, that some items of experience present themselves in such a way that they can justify beliefs while remaining conceptually unarticulated and not themselves standing in need of justification — transposed from perception into narrative. My uncertainty is whether the transposition is sound. Is the narrative given the same mistake Sellars diagnosed, or have I merely pressed an analogy until it looked like an argument? If the analogy is illuminating, what would serve as the space of reasons for prose — the field in which a description can be challenged, supported, or found wanting — and to whom, if anyone, would a description have to answer? And where does the view fail? Are there elements of narrated experience that legitimately function as non-inferential anchors, and does my drafting rule strip out something a novel genuinely needs? I would like the three strongest objections to the view, and a judgement as to whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
