I'm in an upper-level course on the ethics of influence this semester — we've been reading about autonomy, manipulation, and the difference between giving someone reasons and just causing them to act. Last night two of my housemates got into it about our friend Sam, and I couldnt referee it, so now theyre both pestering me to settle it. I'd rather you do it.

Sam has been trying to convince their partner Alex to move across the country for Sam's new job. Sam keeps bringing up that Alex once promised to be flexible about relocating, and lately Sam has started saying things like "if you won't go, I don't think my career actually matters to you."

Here's the back-and-forth, numbered:

(1) Mina says it's manipulation. Not because Sam is lying — everything Sam says is true, and the promise really was made — but because Sam isn't offering the promise as a reason for Alex to weigh; Sam is using it as leverage, a way to make Alex feel like a bad partner for even hesitating, so the actual merits of the move never get a fair hearing.

(2) Jo says that can't be the line. Every serious request between partners draws on their shared history, and mentioning a promise just is giving a reason. If Alex no longer thinks the promise binds, Alex can say so and they can renegotiate. A reason doesn't stop being a reason because it carries emotional weight — a doctor who tells you smoking will kill you is terrifying you, but not manipulating you.

(3) Mina replies that the doctor case differs in exactly the wrong way for Jo. The doctor's aim is to get you to register a fact you'd accept on calm reflection, and the fear tracks the force of that fact. Sam's aim is to make hesitation itself feel like betrayal, so the move never gets assessed on its merits. Same words, different point: one is aimed at judgment, the other at guilt.

(4) Jo says that's mind-reading, and even if Mina is right about Sam's aim, aim isn't the criterion. A manipulator can use impeccable reasons, and a clumsy friend can use emotional appeals with no bad intent. The test should be whether the influence could be endorsed from Alex's point of view. Alex really did promise, so the appeal to the promise is something Alex could accept as relevant — whatever Sam happens to be feeling while saying it.

(5) Mina says the endorsement test is doing no work here. Sure, the promise is relevant in the abstract; nobody denies that. The question is whether the way it's being deployed leaves Alex free to weigh it. Being told "if you say no, it means my career doesn't matter to you" doesn't add a reason; it raises the price of saying no. That's the difference between giving someone a reason and rearranging their options so one choice is painful.

(6) Jo says "raising the price" applies to all legitimate moral criticism. If Alex's refusal actually would show that Sam's career doesn't matter much to Alex, Sam gets to point that out — it's an accurate description of what the refusal means in context, and hiding it would be its own kind of manipulation. Manipulation is about deception and bypassing, not being pointed.
