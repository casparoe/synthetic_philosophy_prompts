Suppose that in 1985 a common-law jurisdiction modelled on England and Wales adopts a constitutional provision abolishing sovereign immunity in full. From that date, public bodies may be sued in the ordinary courts for unlawful acts or omissions, and the state is directly liable in damages once a court finds that an official acted unlawfully. Individual officials retain qualified immunity: a good-faith official is not personally liable, but the state pays regardless. No later statute may restore the old immunity, though the legislature may still pass ordinary law on damages, limitation periods, procedure, and the definition of statutory powers and duties. Predict, with your chain of reasoning rather than a narrative, how this jurisdiction looks forty years on. In particular, address: how official and agency behaviour changes; how legislators rewrite powers, duties, and remedies in response; how courts adjust the substantive grounds of liability to keep lawful policy choice distinct from unlawful conduct; and what becomes of the conceptual claim that the state cannot, strictly speaking, do wrong. Say which predictions you regard as robust, which are guesses, and which assumptions would have to fail for the overall prediction to be wrong.
