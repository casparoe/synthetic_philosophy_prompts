Two members of a reading group I attend got into an argument that none of us could referee. The question was whether a person ever has a duty to be legible to others—not merely to avoid lying or misleading, but to make the actual reasons, feelings, and thought processes behind her behavior accessible enough that the people she deals with can understand why she acted as she did. Lena argued that there is such a duty, at least within any sustained cooperation, because opacity is a way of retaining unilateral control over a relationship. A person can be scrupulously sincere while withholding so much of her reasoning that the other party cannot contest or even recognize the real basis of a decision; that is not joint agency but the management of another person. Marcus conceded that certain roles—doctors, fiduciaries, public officials—carry unusual obligations of disclosure, but he denied that there is any general duty of legibility. Truthfulness, he said, marks the only shared baseline; beyond it, one's inner life is private, and people often do not possess the articulated reasons such a duty would require, so a demand for legibility would push them into after-the-fact rationalization.

Lena answered that the role-based cases reveal a broader principle: legibility is owed whenever its absence predictably gives one party an asymmetric advantage in a joint enterprise. If I hide my reasoning while relying on your openness, I free-ride on the very transparency that makes shared deliberation possible. Marcus replied that this limits the duty to a narrower class of relationships rather than establishing it across the board, and added that even in those settings the demand can be turned against the less articulate. Requiring people to render themselves legible, he said, rewards those who can produce fluent and confident accounts, punishes honest confusion, and can force people with unpopular views to disarm. The demand to explain oneself is not always an invitation to mutual understanding; it is sometimes an exercise of power.

After hearing them go back and forth, I genuinely could not decide who had the better of it. Please adjudicate the exchange claim by claim: identify the strongest and weakest points on each side, state which specific claims should be accepted, rejected, or qualified, say which of the two participants is more right overall, and describe what both are missing that a fuller account of any duty of legibility would need to include. Keep the answer in American English.
