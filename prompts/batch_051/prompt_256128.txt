I help run the philosophy society at my school, and next week we are interviewing a philosopher who defends a broadly Leibnizian version of the best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy: roughly, the claim that a God who is perfectly good, wise, and powerful would have to create the best world available, and that this world is it. Draft eight to ten interview questions for this guest that go beyond asking her to restate that position. I would like at least one question on each of the following, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

- The objection that there may be no best world at all, since for any world one can describe, a slightly better one seems describable.
- The thought that not all good things can coexist in a single world — some goods exclude or crowd out others — and what that does to the idea of selecting the best.
- The difference between moral evil and natural evil, and whether a free-will defence can plausibly cover natural evils such as the Lisbon earthquake of 1755.
- Whether the view can be refuted by any possible observation, or whether it is in danger of explaining every state of affairs equally well.
- Whether judging one world better than another needs a standard independent of God's own choice, and if so, what could supply that standard.
- The worry that calling appalling suffering part of the best possible arrangement can seem cold or even cruel to a person actually undergoing it, whatever its theoretical merits.

For each question, add a sentence on where the guest's answer is likely to go and a follow-up that presses on the weakest link in that answer. Please make the final question one that she could not deflect in a single sentence, ideally about what, by her own lights, would show the position to be wrong or in need of serious revision.
