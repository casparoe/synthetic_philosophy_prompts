I am preparing a one-off session for an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar on reasoning under uncertainty, around fourteen students who are comfortable with basic conditional probability but have never done any anthropic reasoning. I have seventy-five minutes, and I want the session to make them feel the force of the split between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption before either term is named, and in particular to leave them genuinely unsettled by the presumptuous philosopher problem: the way the self-indication assumption appears to warrant enormous confidence about the size of the cosmos on no empirical evidence beyond the fact that one exists. Please design a runnable classroom activity or game for that setting, with exact rules, concrete materials, a pay-off scheme that needs no real money, and a seventy-five-minute timeline. The strongest design would make the group produce the disagreement itself: some holding that a fair coin between a small and a vast universe must remain even odds once you know you are one of that universe's observers, others holding that simply finding yourself existing heavily favours the vast universe, with neither camp having been told what to think. Ideally participants should only notice how strange the second position is after they see how far they were willing to bet on it.

The debrief should require no lecture up front, should not introduce the names of the two assumptions until the participants have already inhabited them, and should map specific moments in the game onto the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and the presumptuous philosopher, explaining where each side can resist and what it costs to resist there. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the real debate the activity cannot reproduce; I suspect the choice of reference class is one, but say if there are others. Please write the whole plan as continuous prose, the way one would write an email to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just ordinary paragraphs.
