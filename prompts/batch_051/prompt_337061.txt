I'm in a philosophy of biology course, and after several weeks on the species problem I still cannot state what a species is without immediately running into counterexamples. Could you work out a definition of 'species' and test it for me? As a baseline, it should get the easy cases right: humans and chimpanzees count as two distinct species, a single freely interbreeding fruit-fly population counts as one species, and an individual mule counts as no species at all. Then run your definition against the following harder cases, saying for each whether it gives the right answer, the wrong answer, or no answer, and whether you would revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.

1. A ring of populations around a geographic barrier, where neighboring populations interbreed all the way around but the two terminal populations overlap without interbreeding.
2. An obligately asexual lineage that forms a diagnosable, genetically cohesive cluster, though there is no interbreeding among its members.
3. A fossil lineage that changes gradually and continuously over millions of years, which paleontologists nonetheless divide into a sequence of named species.
4. Two bacterial groups that regularly exchange genes by horizontal transfer, yet are ecologically distinct and treated in practice as different species.
5. Two plant species that hybridize readily where their ranges overlap and produce fertile hybrids, but remain morphologically and ecologically distinct.

Finally, identify the case your definition handles least well, and say whether that failure is local or points to a deeper problem in the definition.
