I keep going back and forth on something in bargining theory. I'm convinced that Kalai–Smorodinsky got something important right about why Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives is too strong. As I understand it, their monotonicity condition says: if, for every utility level that player 1 might demand, the best payoff player 2 can get while player 1 gets that much weakly increases, then player 2 shouldn't end up worse off. That feels like a basic fairness condition—if your best possible outcome improves, and every 'how well can I do given what you're getting' point improves, it would be weird for the solution to give you less. And the Sunday/Monday example always gets me: on Sunday we choose A from {A,B}. On Monday C becomes possible, C is very good for player 2, and after bargaining we end up with B. Nash's IIA says that's irrational: since C wasn't chosen, the fact that it was available shouldn't matter. But it seems completely reasonable that C matters as a threat or reference point, even though it isn't selected. The whole set of possible options, not just the chosen one, should shape what counts as a fair outcome.

That's one intuition. The other one pulls the other way. If C is not chosen, then in what sense is it not irrelevant? A procedure that outputs B on Monday but A on Sunday, differing only because an unselected option was added, cares about something that didn't happen. I get the reference-point argument, but it also sounds like letting counterfactual junk into the answer. Why should a solution be sensitive to alternatives that are, in the end, rejected? If you're rational, shouldn't your choice be the same among {A,B} and {A,B,C} when you don't pick C? I can't shake it.

Please don't just tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for each intuition to be the one that gives. In particular: is the second intuition smuggling in the idea that a bargain must behave like an individual choice, so that 'irrelevant' alternatives are irrelevant in the same way they are in decision theory? Or is that just what rational bargaining is? And is there a reading of 'irrelevant' on which both survive, maybe by distinguishing between what determines the outcome and what justifies it? Give me the strongest version of both positions and tell me which you would give up.
