I recently read a popular philosophy essay that summarized the phenomenon of imaginative resistance roughly as follows. Readers of fiction routinely grant authors enormous latitude over descriptive facts: a story can stipulate that pigs talk, that a character has six fingers, or that the year is 1846, and readers accept these things as true in the story without complaint. But when a story stipulates an evaluative fact that conflicts with the reader's actual values—for instance, that a character's gratuitous cruelty was beautiful, or that a murder was the right thing to do—readers do not simply accept the stipulation. Instead they read it as irony, infer that the narrator is unreliable, or judge the story to be defective. The essay then drew a strong conclusion: the asymmetry shows that evaluative claims contradicting our actual values literally cannot be imagined, and the only plausible explanation is that our values track necessary truths; a world in which cruelty is right is not merely unpleasant but impossible. It ended by claiming that fiction is therefore powerless to unsettle our moral convictions, since we could not even imagine a story in which those convictions are false. I found the asymmetry itself plausible, but I suspect the essay ran together an unwillingness to imagine with an inability to imagine, and I do not see why the phenomenon would establish that the wrongness of cruelty is necessary rather than merely deeply held. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this summary: which parts are fair to the philosophical discussion of imaginative resistance, which are overstatements or conflations, how a defender of a stronger version of the view would reply, and whether the final claim about fiction's power to challenge our values survives once the summary is corrected? Please write your answer in American English.
