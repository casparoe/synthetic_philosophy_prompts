I'm giving a talk next month to a political theory workshop defending a Deweyan line about democracy, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, roughly: democratic legitimacy isn't a matter of matching an ideal procedure (a fair original position, an ideal speech situation, that sort of thing); it's a matter of how well an institution organizes public inquiry into shared problems and how well it gets revised when it fails to solve them — so legitimacy is experimental and ongoing, not procedural and one-off. My compressed argument: publics form around felt problems; inquiry is experimental at every stage, from framing a problem to testing a proposed fix; so the test of a democratic arrangement is how well it structures that experimental inquiry, not whether it was set up by the right procedure. The audience is mostly deliberative democrats and liberal theorists, plus two Habermasians who suspect 'experimentalism' is just pragmatism with the normativity washed out, a Rawlsian who'll ask whether basic rights and judicial review are just more experiments, and a climate-policy person who wants to know what this says about expert disagreement under deep uncertainty. Give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, with how much damage each would do, phrased the way those people would actually put them, and for each: what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is softest — plus the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
