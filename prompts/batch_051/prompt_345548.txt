I am a journalist who covers neuroscience, animal behavior, and the philosophy of science for a general-interest outlet. I am planning a piece on where the line of consciousness falls across the animal kingdom, and after a dozen interviews I have no stable framework: each researcher draws the boundary with a flagship case and a rule of thumb, and the rules send the line to different places. A bird with no neocortex but a cognitively sophisticated pallium, an octopus with most of its neurons outside its central brain, a honeybee solving a novel problem with a brain of under a million neurons—each gets recruited to different sides of the argument. The cortex criterion collides with the bird. The complex-behavior criterion collides at both ends: flexible-looking behavior can be produced by architectures nobody wants to call conscious, and an anesthetized dog is behaviorally unresponsive without thereby being a non-conscious animal. I do not want to pick a side by picking a case; I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata for a theory of the distribution of consciousness across animals. What must such a theory get right to count as adequate for saying, or for explaining why we cannot yet say, which animals are conscious and in what way? Give me the requirements, with the case or consideration that makes each a requirement. Include the distinctions the theory cannot collapse—nociception from pain, responsiveness from experience, the standing capacity from its current exercise, which animals are conscious from what a conscious animal is conscious of—and the structural choices it cannot avoid, such as whether consciousness is binary or graded and, if graded, along which dimension. Include the findings from neuroscience and evolutionary biology that any candidate must not contradict, especially the independence of complex behavior from any single structure and the convergent evolution of sophisticated nervous systems in distant lineages. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of one research camp, and flag any that cannot be satisfied together. Close by saying which single requirement, applied honestly, does the most to eliminate the popular rules of thumb I keep hearing, and why. A table for the requirements is fine; the reasoning behind each entry is what I need.
