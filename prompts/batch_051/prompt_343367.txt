Ive been reading around the political obligation debate recently and it feels like its been running in place for decades. On one side are the voluntarists, who think you owe the law obedience only if you've done something voluntary—consented, promised, or at least tacitly accepted benefits under conditions that amount to consent. On the other side are the non-voluntarists, who think obligation comes from fair play, gratitude, natural duty, or just membership in a political community. And then the philosophical anarchists keep insisting there's no general obligation to obey the law just because it is the law, since most people never give anything like free, informed consent and receiving benefits or being a member isn't the same as undertaking an obligation. Every argument has its standard reply, every reply has its standard objection, and nobody moves. Ive been wondering whether the whole modern dispute has the shape Hume was trying to diagnose in "Of the Original Contract"—his target was divine right versus social contract, and ours is non-voluntarism versus voluntarism, but the structure might be the same.

Here's the passage:

"I shall venture to affirm, That both these systems of speculative principles are just; though not in the sense, intended by the parties: And, That both the schemes of practical consequences are prudent; though not in the extremes, to which each party, in opposition to the other, has commonly endeavoured to carry them."

My hypothesis is that this names the actual diagnosis: each side is right about something, but only in a sense its defenders wouldn't accept, and each side's practical conclusions are only reasonable once you moderate them down to something the defenders would find too weak. If that's right, the impasse isn't going to be broken by another counterexample; it's a disagreement about which sense of "consent" or "obligation" is doing the work, plus a disagreement about how far each side's practical conclusions are willing to go. But I keep wavering between that and two other readings: that it's just a verbal dispute, or that the two sides are asking different questions—one about how obligations are acquired, the other about how institutions justify themselves.

Diagnose the contemporary debate. Take a definite position on what kind of disagreement it really is, using the Hume passage as your central clue and the actual moves in the debate as evidence, then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—or argue that no progress should be expected and why. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey of possibilities: pick one, defend it, and give an honest hearing to the strongest reason to prefer a rival reading.
