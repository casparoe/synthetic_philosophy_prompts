I spent my working life on insect physiology and behaviour, and now that I'm retired I've ended up on the welfare advisory board of a company that farms black soldier fly larvae for animal feed. The board meets quarterly and has to decide weather to sign off on process changes — things like adding a chilling or stunning step before drying, changing stocking density, whether to count larvae in our welfare reporting at all — and we mostly have to decide in the meeting with whatever numbers we already have, not go away and commission a research programme. I've been reading philosophy lately, mostly decision theory and animal ethics, and the thing I keep circling back to is what I gather is called the scale argument: roughly, you can't dismiss insect suffering just by saying the probability they feel anything is low, because the number of insects affected is so vast that even a small probability still gives you a very large expected amount of suffering. I find that convincing in the abstract, but I have no idea how to make it run in a two-hour meeting with colleagues half of whom think the whole thing is a distraction. The operation processes tens of millions of larvae a day, so the "vast numbers" part isn't abstract for us.

What I'd like is a decision procedure, written for me rather than for philosophers, that turns that thought into something usable. It should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and — this matters to me — where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is a values question rather than a fact question. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. We can usually get rough numbers for throughput, how many larvae are affected, the cost per larva of a change, and how long the animals live, but we almost never have anything solid on the probability of insect sentience or on how bad the suffering would be; so tell me what the procedure should do when the key numbers can't be had, and how the procedure should treat a genuinely uncertain probability rather than pretending we know it.

Then run the procedure on two or three concrete cases. First, whether to install a blast-chilling step before the larvae are ground, which would cost a meaningful amount and slow throughput but would plausibly reduce suffering if they can suffer. Second, whether our welfare reporting should include larvae at all, given that leaving them out keeps our scores clean and including them would force uncomfortable comparisons with vertebrate numbers. Add a third case from this sort of work yourself. And I'd ask you to make at least one of the worked cases one where an honestly applied procedure gives an answer I am not going to like — I'd rather see the procedure be honest than convenient. Flag the cases the procedure can't settle, where a person has to take responsibility. I don't want a reading list or citations; just the procedure and the worked cases.
