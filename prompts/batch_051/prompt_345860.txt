A long illness in my family has recently forced me to think hard about whether the mind is anything over and above the body, and I have found I cannot even assess that question until I settle what 'physical' is supposed to mean. I therefore tried to fix the term's meaning by recording my own spontaneous reactions to particular cases. They follow a rough pattern, but not a clean one:

- A granite pebble is physical.
- An electrostatic field around a charged wire is physical.
- The particular sequence of nucleotides in one of my chromosomes is physical.
- The information encoded by that nucleotide sequence is physical, but only as a pattern in the molecule, not as a thing in addition to the molecule.
- A shadow cast on a wall is not physical; it is just a local absence of light.
- A hole in a wall is not physical, for the same reason.
- The centre of mass of a thrown hammer is not physical; it belongs to my description of the hammer rather than to the hammer itself.
- A debt of £50 between two friends is not physical, though it is real enough to damage the friendship.
- The rule of castling in chess is not physical.
- The feeling of nausea I had this morning is physical: it is a state of my body, even though its felt quality does not much resemble a field or a molecule.
- The number 17 is not physical.

Two criteria seem to be pulling against each other. On the first, 'physical' means something like 'part of the concrete, spatiotemporal causal order'; this counts the pebble, the field, the gene, and the nausea, but it also seems to count the shadow and the hole, which I rejected, and it may even count the debt and the castling rule, since they are realised in spatiotemporal goings-on. On the second, 'physical' means something like 'posited by the physical sciences'; but if I read 'the physical sciences' as current physics, the criterion is hostage to revision, and if I read it as some idealised completed physics, I can no longer say in advance what it includes. Even setting that aside, this criterion seems to leave the felt quality of the nausea out of account, and it makes me unsure what to say about the information in the gene.

Is there a single principle — or at most a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts? Please state each principle precisely, show how it classifies my cases, say which of my verdicts it must overturn, and give its verdicts on a few cases I have not listed (for example, a black hole's event horizon, a speed limit, a dream, and the fact that salt dissolves in water). If you think no non-ad-hoc principle captures every verdict, I would rather hear which verdicts to abandon.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, with no preamble and no restating of my question, and then give the reasoning. Please write in British English.
