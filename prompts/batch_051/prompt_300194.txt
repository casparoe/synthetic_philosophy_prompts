Write a focused dialogue of roughly 1,200 words between two equally capable philosophers, Nadia and Owen, about the following case.

The case. For eleven years, a shared online world has hosted a region called Port Vitale. A resident named Eli has spent thousands of hours building and maintaining a civic archive there: maps of the region across its changing history, transcribed oral histories supplied by other residents, records of the world's internal governance, and detailed models of buildings that no longer exist. The company that runs the world announces that it will delete Port Vitale permanently, and the deletion is carried out. Eli insists that something real and irreplaceable has been destroyed, and that the loss is not exhausted by the loss of his own time, effort, or attachments.

Nadia defends virtual realism: she holds that some virtual objects are real, public, durable artefacts rather than private fantasies, and that destroying them can be a genuine harm that is not reducible to harming the interests of the people involved. She is sophisticated, not naive. She accepts that virtual objects are implemented in servers and code, but she argues that contracts, money, and musical works are likewise realised in physical or institutional vehicles, and she treats that analogy as central.

Owen defends virtual anti-realism: he holds that so-called virtual objects are ultimately states of databases combined with shared make-believe, and that the only genuine harms are harms to persons and their interests. He is also sophisticated. He concedes that Eli has suffered a real loss and that the social practices around Port Vitale matter greatly, but he denies that accounting for this requires positing real virtual objects, or that the sentence "the archive was destroyed" is literally true.

The dialogue should be genuine back-and-forth: each must respond to the other's strongest point rather than talking past it, and neither may be a strawman. Have them examine what "real" could mean here; the analogy with social artefacts such as contracts and musical works; whether Eli's loss is better described as losing something or as losing access to something; and whether a world can have a history whose records are themselves among the things wiped out. Do not include a narrator or stage directions beyond the speakers' names. End the dialogue with the disagreement narrowed and its remaining core clearly stated.

Please write your answer in British English.
