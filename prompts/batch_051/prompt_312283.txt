I'm putting together a handout for a small seminar on probability and inductive inference, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree that sorts people by what they actually conclude from Bertrand's paradox, not by whichever label they'd apply to themselves. Quick context so we're on the same page: the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle lands at 1/3 if you pick two endpoints independently on the circumference, 1/2 if you pick a radius and then a point uniformly along it, and 1/4 if you pick a midpoint uniformly inside the disk. My students can all follow the calculation, and they all sense that the principle of indifference is doing something strange, but they draw diffferent lessons from it.

So I want the tree to start from "I accept that the three methods give three different numbers" and end in a named position about what this shows about the principle of indifference. Branch nodes should be answerable without already knowing the names of the positions: for instance whether "random chord" means anything before a generating procedure is specified, whether requiring the answer to be invariant under rotations and translations is enough to single it out, whether each parameterization is really asking a different question rather than answering one question differently, and whether a physical procedure (tossing long straws at the circle, repeated many times) settles the matter or still leaves a judgment call. The leaves I want at minimum are: the underdetermination/frequentist view (the abstract problem has no answer until a setup is fixed), the subjective Bayesian view (the three answers are three different prior choices and logic forces none of them), the invariance-based objective Bayesian view (the right symmetries pick out a uniquely correct use of indifference), the "indifference works fine relative to a chosen parameter" view, and the stronger skeptical view that the paradox simply refutes the principle of indifference for continuous spaces. Add another leaf or two if the branching naturally generates them, but don't drop any of those five. Also tell me which pairs of leaves the tree cannot actually separate and why, and note any combinations of branch answers that are empty, with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody lives there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just not much defended. The students have had one course in probability but no measure theory, so keep the nodes concrete; I'd rather the tree sort them by what they say about the circle than by how they define themselves.
