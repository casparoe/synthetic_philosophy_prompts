I am a high school junior and the newly elected Speaker of my school's Student Assembly. My main job is to chair the general meetings: I maintain the agenda, state each motion before debate begins, and decide whether questions from the floor are in order. I have begun to notice that the exact wording with which a motion or a question is placed before the Assembly does a great deal of silent work. A motion may begin, "Given the administration's failure to consult students," before proposing some action; a question from the floor may ask, "How should the Assembly respond to the recent rise in arbitrary detentions?" In both cases, claims that have not actually been established in the room—that the administration failed to consult students, that detentions are arbitrary and increasing—are simply taken for granted. When I restate a motion in my own words, I seem either to endorse those background claims or to get accused of editorializing when I strip them out. I recently read a little about presupposition, accommodation, and David Lewis's picture of conversation as a kind of scorekeeping in a language game, in which speakers do not only add assertions to a shared conversational scoreboard but also quietly place certain propositions into the common ground unless someone objects. I find the picture persuasive, but I am not certain how it should change what I actually do as a chair.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of language who works on pragmatics and the dynamics of conversation. Please describe the most important implications of the scorekeeping and accommodation picture for someone in my position. I would like the implications organized around the practical decisions I face: how to phrase agenda items; how to state a motion neutrally without laundering the mover's presuppositions; how to handle loaded questions from the floor; when it is worth challenging a presupposition rather than letting it be accommodated; and how to explain to the Assembly, without jargon, why a chairperson's restatement of a motion is never a wholly neutral summary. Please think carefully, and if it helps, begin by brainstorming and briefly working through a small concrete example of a presupposition being accommodated in a meeting before drawing out the general implications.
