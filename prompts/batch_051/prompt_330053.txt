I work for a company that builds scientific computing libraries. Our users are mostly engineers doing simulation and control, and they assume that if our API returns a complex number or an ordered list of roots, the result is canonical: the same input produces the same output everywhere, across runs, platforms, and versions. In practice we keep hitting objects that are only defined up to a symmetry. A user computes the square root of a negative real number, or asks for the roots of a polynomial, and the answer depends on choices we made somewhere in the internals. Support tickets arrive when a patch or a different BLAS backend flips the sign or reorders the results, even though the underlying mathematics has not changed.

In *Science and Hypothesis*, Poincaré makes an observation about exactly this situation in the context of complex numbers: once you have called one of the two imaginary square roots *i*, every theorem remains true if you later systematically replace *i* by its counterpart. The two roots are interchangeable; what matters is the structure of relations between them, not which one carries the name *i*. I want that argument turned into an engineering procedure, not just a philosophical observation.

Please write me a decision procedure my team can run before we ship any function or data type whose output is mathematically non-unique up to symmetry. The procedure should be a checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says no, and where it stops being algorithmic and hands the decision to a human because the issue is a convention rather than a bug. It should also tell us how to document the residual symmetry when we do ship. Assume my colleagues know linear algebra and complex arithmetic but no Galois theory or philosophy.

Then run the procedure on three cases: returning the positive square root of a negative real number, enumerating the roots of a polynomial in a deterministic order, and returning a matrix whose eigenvectors are only determined up to scaling. Flag which case the procedure would reject even though our current code ships it, and say what we should do instead.
