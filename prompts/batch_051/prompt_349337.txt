Here's a proposal someone floated in a group I'm in, and I want to know how the mechanism would actually behave, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. There's a platform where freelancers get paired on one-off jobs where each person can either cooperate (share the useful info, split the tedious half) or defect (hide the info, dump the tedious half on the other guy). It's genuinely one-shot: you never get paired with the same person twice, and the jobs are anonymized enough that you can't retaliate later. Someone suggests giving everyone a public "cooperation score." You start at 100, cooperating adds 5, defecting subtracts 15, the score is capped at 150 and floored at 0, and it's calculated only over your last 20 interactions. Also, if you leave the platform for 30 days, your score resets to 100. Before each job you can see the other person's score and they can see yours, but you can't refuse the pairing. Jobs vary hugely in what's at stake — some are an afternoon's pocket money, some are a month's income — and the score treats them all the same. Treat this as a mechanism to analyze, not a moral proposal. What does it actually incentivize? How would people game the last-20 window, the 30-day reset, and the fact that stakes aren't weighted? What happens to newcomers, to someone who makes one big early mistake, to the market for the high-stakes jobs? And which of the failure modes could actually be patched without just opening a new hole somewhere else?
