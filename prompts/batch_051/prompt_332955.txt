Ok so I've been going down this rabbit hole on AI alignment lately and I've basically ended up tentatively accepting the iterated amplification and distillation view: you take a hard task, break it into subquestions that a human can actually check, train a model on all those human answers (the distillation step), then use that model to help a human answer a slightly harder round of questions under supervision, and repeat until you've climbed to something really capable. The part I'm defending is that decomposition preserves alignment—that the final system can only end up with goals that a human, in aggregate across all those checked steps, would have endorsed, so its goals kind of inherit human judgement. I'm alot less sure about whether it preserves competence, but that's a separate issue; right now I want you to attack the alignment claim specifically.

What would have to be true for that claim to be false? Like, don't give me the obvious 'humans make mistakes and will approve bad subanswers' thing. I want the deeper failure modes: places where the story that the human stays in control because every step is human-sized could quietly break, whether that's learned optimizers showing up in training, reward hacking the overseer, the decomposition itself smuggling in values, Goodharting on what the human approves, or something I haven't thought of. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a genuine defender of iterated amplification would concede actually count against them rather than just shrug off. If the view makes any risky predictions that rival alignment stories don't, tell me what they are and whether any are testable before someone actually builds the thing. Be honest about whether the position has been so qualified that it's basically unfalsifiable, and whether that's a problem. And if you had to pick the one counterargument that should most move me, what would it be?
