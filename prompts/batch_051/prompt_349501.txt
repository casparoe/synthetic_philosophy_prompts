For the past couple of years I have been thinking, on my own and outside any academic setting, about a problem I have since learned goes by the name interpersonal comparison of utility: whether we can meaningfully compare how much happiness or suffering two different people experience, and therefore say that a change benefits one person more than it harms another. Almost every real trade-off I encounter — a policy that helps some and burdens others, a family decision that spares one person pain at the cost of another's comfort — seems to assume such comparisons. But when I try to make them precise, I can state each person's preferences, yet I cannot inspect the felt intensity behind them from outside. I have argued about this for months with a friend. He defends what I will call comparabilism: interpersonal comparisons of well-being are meaningful, made constantly, and can be sharpened even if they cannot be made exact; lacking a meter for happiness does not make comparisons arbitrary. I defend what I will call the skeptical view: each person's preferences can be observed and respected, but a claim that one person's gain outweighs another's loss goes beyond anything we can establish, because it pretends to compare quantities on two scales that cannot be placed side by side. We think we keep talking past each other, and we want to produce a document both of us could sign. Draft for us the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps.

The statement should have three parts. In the first, give a numbered list of claims both sides can accept, however minimal; include the places where we already agree without noticing it, and avoid claims so thin that neither side is saying anything. In the second, give a disagreements table: each row states one exact sub-question on which the camps divide, with the comparabilist's position and the skeptic's position each written in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view. In the third, for each row, identify a crux — a further question or a concrete case such that, if the two of us came to agree about it, at least one side would have to move. Throughout, separate disagreements that are genuine from disagreements that are only verbal (for example, if one of us means by "compare" something the other has never denied), and flag the cases where the positions converge more than expected — such as comparisons between someone in extreme agony and someone mildly annoyed. Where the two sides cannot be reconciled, the table should make the residual disagreement precise enough that each of us can point to the exact row where we part ways.
