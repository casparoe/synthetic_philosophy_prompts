I have been trying to work out what the bent-stick illusion shows about perception, and I keep arriving at the following argument, which unsettles me. (1) When I look at a straight stick half-submerged in water, I have an experience as of a bent object. (2) Whenever I have an experience as of an F, I am directly aware of something that is F. (3) Therefore, in the illusion, I am directly aware of something bent. (4) The stick before me is straight, not bent. (5) Therefore, in the illusion, what I am directly aware of is not the stick; it must be something else, an appearance or sense-datum. (6) An accurate, veridical perception of a genuinely bent stick could be subjectively indistinguishable from this illusory experience. (7) Experiences that are subjectively indistinguishable must be of the same fundamental kind. (8) Therefore, in veridical perception too, I am directly aware of an appearance or sense-datum rather than the stick itself. (9) Since the same reasoning applies to any ordinary case of seeing, I am never directly aware of the physical objects I seem to see.

I half-suspect that step (7) is doing something sneaky, but I cannot say exactly what. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and why? If step (7) is the problem, give the strongest reading of it on which it is true, and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading. I have also encountered a view I believe is called disjunctivism, according to which veridical perception and illusion are fundamentally different kinds of mental state despite being subjectively indistinguishable; if that view is correct, which step does it reject, and does rejecting that step leave the rest of the argument standing?
