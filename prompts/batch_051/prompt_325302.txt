I'm a philosophy PhD student working on epistemic injustice, and my advisor keeps pushing me to get clearer on the difference between credibility deficits and credibility excesses. Here's a case from my own department that I've been turning over.

Our department's seminar code has this clause, which I'm copying word for word from the handbook:

'Participants shall evaluate every presented claim solely on its content. In particular, no participant may discount, or grant in excess, credibility to a speaker on the basis of the speaker's gender, race, accent, disability, age, or institutional rank.'

Now the case. A grad student in my department has a high-pitched voice and a strong non-native accent, and she has good reason to think she gets talked over and receives less engagement in Q&A than male native speakers giving equally good talks. For a recent colloquium she presented remotely and ran her mic through a real-time pitch filter that made her voice sound like a middle-aged man's, with the camera off and a title slide up. The talk was her own work. In Q&A she gave sharp answers, and the audience treated her as a serious authority — including the two people who usually interrupt her. Nobody knew it was her.

So: did anyone violate the clause? On one hand, the audience didn't know her gender, race, or accent; they were responding to a manufactured voice. On the other, she did receive more credibility than she would have under her real voice, and the clause explicitly says not to grant credibility "in excess." Also, if credibility is supposed to track content alone, and content alone wouldn't have earned that deference, is she gaming her colleagues in a way that is itself an epistemic injustice? Or is she just forcing the department to live up to its own rule, since the whole point was to stop voice/accent/gender from driving credibility?

I keep going back and fourth on this because the rule was plainly written to stop people from discounting speakers, and I doubt the drafters ever imagined a speaker filtering her own voice. That's the tangle I want pulled apart.

Act as an expert on social epistemology and on interpreting rules. Think it through, brainstorm a bit before settling, and then commit to a definite reading — no hedging at the end. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Ideally the five cover: plain-wording violation; what the clause's purpose says; what the drafters would say if asked; whether the speaker did anything wrong even if the letter of the rule wasn't broken; and which interpretation is honest rather than special pleading.
