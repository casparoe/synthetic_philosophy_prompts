I'm teaching a philosophy of mind/ethics course this term, and I keep coming back to Nozick's experience machine but feel like the standard discussion gets stuck at the same place (people just say they wouldn't plug in because of status quo bias or whatever, and then we move on). I want to assign something that pushes past that, so I've been thinking about the following case, which I may or may not have read somewhere and half-remember, so treat it as a constructed example.

Suppose we could build an experience machine for a whole life, not just a temporary stay, but with a difference: the machine doesn't just give you pleasant experiences. It gives you the exact experiential texture of a genuinely meaningful, challenging, sometimes sad life -- one with real grief in it. From the inside, you grieve a dead friend; the grief feels exactly like grief, with all its phenomenology, its weight, its tendency to color everything for months. But the friend is simulated, the loss is simulated, and (this is the key) you never know that while inside. The machine's designers tell you the truth only before you enter. Memorably, one of them says: "The sadness in there is real sadness; the fact it is about something real is the only thing we cannot give you." Is that actually a difference that matters, or does the phrase 'real sadness about a real thing' beg the question?

I'd like your position on this, but specifically: I want you to argue for the view you find least comfortable here -- that a life in such a machine could be just as good as, or better than, a corresponding non-machine life, or the opposite. Then, after giving the argument, step back and tell me how confident you are in the premises you had to lean on hardest, and single out the one point you're least sure of. Keep the focus on well-being (what makes a life go well) rather than on identity, autonomy, or whether you could be deceived indefinitely -- those always seem to me like separate issues.
