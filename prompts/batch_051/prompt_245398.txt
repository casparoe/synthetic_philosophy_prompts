I am a PhD student in criminal law theory, and for the past year I have been auditing my supervisor's advanced seminar on the moral structure of criminal liability. The part of the course that has most unsettled me concerns the justificatory limits of strict liability: when, if ever, a criminal conviction may be imposed on someone whose conduct was voluntary, who satisfied every standard of reasonable care, and who bears no first-personal fault for the harm. She works entirely through hypothetical prosecutions, and I have kept a meticulous log of her verdicts. The entries that seem to matter are these. (a) A pedestrian is struck from behind by a cyclist, staggers, and knocks a bystander into traffic, killing her; no conviction, since the fatal movement was not a voluntary act. (b) A food importer employs every reasonable inspection measure, but a pathogen eludes the checks and several customers are made ill; a food-safety regulation authorizes a modest fine and no imprisonment; convict and fine, because the importer voluntarily entered a tightly regulated activity and must internalize its residual risk even when careful. (c) A motorist drives at twice the speed limit through a residential street and kills a child; convict of a serious offence, with full desert-based punishment, because he knowingly imposed a grave and unjustifiable risk. (d) A homeowner has a large tree inspected by a certified arborist and follows the advice, but an unprecedented windstorm topples it and kills a passerby; no conviction, since the realized risk was not of a kind whose foreseeability could plausibly be assigned to the homeowner's choice to keep the tree.

Next week she is to give her verdict on a case I have been drafting in my head. A mountain guide leads a paying expedition along a corridor known for unpredictable rockfall. He has taken every professional precaution: he reviewed geological reports, consulted the local weather service, chose the season of lowest incidence, and warned the clients explicitly of the residual danger. A boulder falls and kills one of the clients. He is prosecuted for a serious offence carrying substantial imprisonment, not a modest regulatory fine. The prosecution's theory is simply that he voluntarily chose to lead the expedition through that corridor, that rockfall was a foreseeable type of risk attaching to that choice even under best practice, and that the risk materialized; no one disputes that he took every reasonable precaution, and the clients' advance consent to the risk is stipulated not to be a defence to the charge.

Assuming I want to predict her verdict while remaining faithful to the record she has built, what should I predict, and which earlier case is the closest analog? I keep returning to two readings of her record, both compatible with everything she has said. On the first, she holds a fault principle with a narrow carve-out for public-welfare regulatory offences carrying modest, non-stigmatizing penalties; on the second, she holds a broader risk-imposition principle on which voluntarily choosing an activity that foreseeably raises the relevant type of risk suffices for conviction, with precautions bearing only on sentence. Does the new case force a choice between these readings? What would each require her to say about the guide, which reading of her record is the better interpretation and why, and what single further hypothetical would most cleanly confirm which of the two readings she holds?
