My housemate has been chewing on this argument about nuclear deterrance and I can't shake the feeling it's too clean. He thinks it shows rational states can't really deter anyone; I half-suspect there's a slip somewhere but I'm not sure where.

(1) Suppose country A announces that if B launches a nuclear first strike, A will retaliate with its whole remaining arsenal.

(2) Once the attack is incoming or has landed, that retaliation can no longer defend A's cities or people; it can only add another wave of destruction and maybe worse (fallout, nuclear winter, dragging in allies).

(3) So at that point, retaliating is against A's interests, and a rational A would not do it.

(4) B's leaders can see all of this, so they won't take A's threat seriously.

(5) A threat that isn't taken seriously can't deter.

(6) Therefore A can't credibly threaten retaliation, and rational states are left without a nuclear deterrent—deterrence only works because someone is irrational or bluffing.

I suspect the move from (3) to (4) is doing something with the word rational, or that (3) ignores the difference between a state's interests before the threat and at the moment it would have to carry it out. I also once read that states try to get around this by leaving the response partly to chance or making launch automatic, so that at the last moment there is no fresh decision for a leader to make. Not sure if that saves the argument or just changes the subject.
