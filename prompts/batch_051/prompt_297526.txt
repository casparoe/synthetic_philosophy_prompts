I sit on an independent appointments panel for senior roles in a public integrity body — ombudsmen, auditors, police complaints commissioners — and we assess candidates several times a year, with our recommendation going to the relevant minister for final sign-off. We keep judging incorruptibility as 'no scandals and no conflicts of interest', which we know is thin, and we also have no way of distinguishing genuine incorruptibility from simple rigidity: a candidate who cannot be moved by evidence, by mercy, or by a legitimate change of circumstance is not what we want, but our current questions cannot tell the two apart. Please write us a reusable assessment procedure, grounded in a worked-out conception of incorruptibility as a disposition to resist the wrong sorts of pressure while remaining appropriately answerable to the right ones. The procedure should set out the questions to ask in order, what evidence would count as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the judgement to a person because the matter is one of values rather than facts. Include worked applications: at least one case where the procedure rejects a candidate we would ordinarily have cleared, and one where it clears a candidate we would ordinarily have flagged. Say plainly what the procedure cannot detect by paper and interview. The panel members are lawyers and former civil servants, not philosophers, and we have about an hour per candidate on top of the written file. Please write the answer in British English.
