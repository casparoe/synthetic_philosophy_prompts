I've been using a definition of akrasia that goes like this: someone acts akratically when (i) at the moment of acting she judges that a different action is better all things considered, (ii) she is able to do that action, (iii) she voluntarily does the worse one anyway, and (iv) afterward she still stands by the judgment she didn't act on. But now I'm not sure it handles this case. A guy decides at 10pm, with no particular pressure, that he should get up at 6 and run; he thinks morning exercise is better than sleeping in, for his health and his mood. At 6am the alarm goes off and he hits snooze, and while he does it he genuinely thinks that right now, more sleep is better than the run — the run is far away and the bed is right here. This happens twice more. At 9am he's annoyed and says the 10pm and 9am versions of himself were right, and the 6am version just caved. Is the 6am version akratic on those four criteria? Or does the case show that criterion (i) — the judgment that the other action is better has to be present at the moment of acting — does most of the work, while the later endorsement in (iv) pulls the other way? And if this is exactly the pattern hyperbolic discounting predicts, is it still fair to say he acted against his better judgment, or do the criteria themselves need rewriting?
