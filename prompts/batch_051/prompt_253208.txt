I have been trying to settle a long-running argument about what should and should not count as an organism, and I have arrived at a criterion I would like checked before I commit to it. I call it the principle of non-rival futures: an entity is an organism just in case none of its parts has a realistic future in which that part flourishes while the entity, or the lineage it belongs to, fails (dies or stops reproducing as that lineage). By a part I mean a component that is developmentally or metabolically integrated with the rest of the entity, and by a realistic future I mean one reachable under ecologically ordinary conditions rather than one requiring laboratory rescue. The criterion appeals to me because it sorts the cases I care about in an intuitive way. A worker termite has no realistic future in which it thrives while its colony collapses, so the colony passes the test; the zooids of a Portuguese man o' war are in the same position, so a siphonophore passes; a coral branch can fragment and go on to do well after the parent colony dies, so a coral colony fails and looks more like a collection of parts than a single organism. It also gives me a principled line on holobionts: because a host's gut microbes can generally survive in the environment or colonize another host after this host dies, a holobiont fails the test and should be treated as an especially intimate community rather than an organism.

I know there is a substantial literature on biological individuality, and I suspect I have partly reinvented one of its ideas — perhaps something in the neighborhood of evolutionary individuals, reproducers, or bottleneck accounts of individuality — but I cannot tell whether my principle is just one of those ideas under a new name or whether it adds anything. I would like an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Please tell me (1) what the strongest, most charitable version of the principle of non-rival futures would be, (2) which existing concepts it is closest to and whether it is genuinely distinct from them, and (3) where it breaks, ideally with two or three cases in which it draws the wrong line or smuggles in a hidden assumption.
