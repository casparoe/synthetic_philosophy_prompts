In The World as Will and Representation, Schopenhauer defends the claim that all pleasure and satisfaction are in themselves negative—nothing but the release or absence of pain—while pain alone is a positive datum of experience. Ordinary moral thinking seems committed to the opposite asymmetry. We treat pain and pleasure as commensurable magnitudes of opposite sign: a headache is a negative entry, a pleasant afternoon is a positive entry, and enough of the second can cancel the first. We also usually judge that causing a pain is worse than failing to produce an equally intense pleasure, even though the two acts would yield the same net balance on the ordinary ledger. Take Schopenhauer's claim seriously. Is there a principled justification for the ordinary practice of treating pleasure as a positive benefit that can offset pain, or is that practice simply the refusal to believe his thesis? Give the strongest defense of the ordinary asymmetry you can, then say whether it survives, and whether a thinker who accepted Schopenhauer's account could coherently compare lives of different pain-to-pleasure ratios at all.
