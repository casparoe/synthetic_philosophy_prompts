Last month, at dinner with two friends from my writing group, I mentioned that I'd drafted a character who can't choose between two exactly equal good futures—equal down to the last detail of what she cares about. Dana rolled her eyes and said real people don't freeze that way, and if they do it's not depth, it's a mechanical fault. Ravi said the truest thing in the chapter was the freezing, because choice can genuinely run out. They talked past me for an hour about an old puzzle, Buridan's ass, and I wrote down their argument in my notebook as best I could. I wont tell you which of them I was privately leaning toward.

Here's the back-and-forth.

(1) Dana: "Buridan's ass is a fake problem. If the two options are exactly equal, then each is permissible, and a rational agent is allowed to just take one. Rationality rules out choosing an option worse than an available alternative; it doesnt demand a uniquely best option. The only irrational thing the donkey can do is stand there and starve, since eating either bale of hay dominates starving. The whole puzzle is an artifact of a false theory that action requires a decisive reason."

(2) Ravi: "But 'just take one' names something the donkey still has to do. It has no more reason to perform the left-bale-choosing than the right-bale-choosing, and no more reason to adopt a coin-flip policy than to do either. You haven't closed the gap; you've moved it up a level, from choosing between the bales to choosing between the acts. A coin is not a reason, it's a piece of metal. Unless you smuggle in some non-rational trigger—habit, will, a literal twitch—the donkey's reasons haven't moved it an inch toward either bale."

(3) Dana: "That's a level-confusion. The permission to take the left bale is not some further option on the same menu as the two bales; it's the standing rule that if you are indifferent among permitted options, taking one is fine. An agent can have that rule built in without having chosen it, because at some point you hit bedrock about what it is to be a chooser. Demanding a reason for the tie-break just re-raises the same mistake one floor up, and you can decline to build that extra floor."

(4) Ravi: "Then you've given me the point with nicer posture. If the tie-break is built in rather than chosen on reasons, then in a genuine tie the action is explained by the donkey's constitution, not by its practical reason. So practical reason does run out—that's exactly the fact the puzzle exposes. Real agents sometimes freeze when no reason speaks, and the ones who don't freeze aren't in possession of a hidden better reason; they simply have some non-rational mechanism doing the selecting. That's why the puzzle deserves a chapter, not why it's fake."

They made me referee, and I honestly couldnt. Please go through the argument one move at a time and say whether each move is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification for each; then say who is right about what, and who is more right overall. Also tell me how confident you are in that verdict and single out the point you're least sure of.
