So I'm in a class on language and society and my philosophy friend got me into an argument I can't get out of. We were arguing about whether "bad grammar" is a real thing or just a way of dressing up dialect prejudice, and he wrote out the following argument. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) A grammar is just a description of the system a speaker actually uses to produce and understand sentences.
(2) If a speaker actually produces a sentence, then that sentence was produced by the system they actually use.
(3) So every sentence a speaker actually produces is grammatical in their own language.
(4) To call something "bad grammar" can only mean that it deviates from the grammar of the language being spoken.
(5) By (3), a speaker's own utterances never deviate from the grammar of their own language, and there's no non-arbitrary external grammar to hold them to.
(6) Therefore nobody ever speaks ungrammatically in their own language; "bad grammar" is just a label for low-prestige varieties, plus maybe slips and typos, which aren't really grammar at all.
(7) So the whole idea of a grammatical mistake is confused, kind of like a planet failing to follow its own orbit.

I can't except (7). I want to keep the idea that someone can make a genuine grammatical error by their own lights — like when I mean one thing and can tell I garbled it, or when a non-native speaker asks me to correct them. But my friend says self-correction and typos are performance problems, not grammar, and non-native speakers are just learning a different system, so the argument still stands.
