i'm in a class on reputation and platform design right now, and i keep chewing on an idea i came up with that i can't decide is actually insightful or just goodhart's law with extra steps. i've been calling it the cheap karma principle, or maybe gresham's law for reputation. the claim is: in any karma or reputation system, when there are multiple routes to a score, the cheapest route (least effort, least connected to the actual trait the score is supposed to track) will get used the most, and over time the score comes to track whatever the cheapest acceptable route still requires. so a platform can only ever build a reputation metric that measures the easiest way to move the metric, not whatever the designers hoped it would measure.

some cases that make me think it's right: reddit karma mostly tracks a willingness to post a lot and be agreeable, not insight, because volume and timing are the cheapest levers. ebay feedback has inflated to the point that anything below about 98% basically just means "this seller is catastrophic," because leaving positive feedback is nearly free and most transactions are fine, so the score can't separate decent from excellent. uber ratings seem like the same story: five stars is the path of least effort for riders, so the distribution is squashed near the top and small differences like 4.7 vs 4.9 don't mean what they look like. stack overflow is the exception i keep failing to explain away: rep still seems to track technical skill reasonably well, and my only reply is that its cheap routes (asking, editing, review queues) haven't fully poisoned it yet, or maybe the cost floor for a good answer is naturally just high enough.

i know this is in the neighborhood of goodhart's law and campbell's law, but i think mine is more specific because it's about the historical drift of a score rather than just people gaming a target, and it makes a prediction: after a platform introduces a new low-cost way to earn reputation, the score's correlation with quality will decline and never recover; the score distribution will compress toward the ceiling or the floor; and users will gradually stop trusting the number even while they keep using it.

give me your honest assessment please. is the principle true, trivially false, or mostly right but with fixable holes? is it actually just one of those existing laws restated? what's the strongest counterexample, and does it kill the whole thing? also say what would change your mind about it: what kind of evidence or argument would make you reject the principle, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
