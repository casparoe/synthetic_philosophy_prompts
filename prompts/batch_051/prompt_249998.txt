While developing a feature on the appeal and the unease of radical self-modification, I came across an argument that seems to prove far more than anyone advancing it intends. I have not been able to find the step that fails, and I would like a precise diagnosis for the piece.

Consider a person who freely and with full information chooses a transformation of the kind some transhumanists defend: not a mild improvement in memory or attention, but a deliberate replacement of the values, projects, and motives from which the choice itself was made.

(1) A choice is justified by appeal to a person's autonomy only if the person who later lives with its consequences is the same person who made it.
(2) Personal identity over time is carried by psychological continuity: the overlapping chains of memory, projects, values, and concerns through which an earlier person persists into a later one.
(3) The transformation in question does not extend those chains but breaks them. It sets the later self's values and projects against the old ones, and the old self's reasons do not survive the change except as objects of the later self's recollection.
(4) When the chains are broken, the later self is not identical with the earlier self; it is a different person, produced by the earlier person's choice.
(5) Therefore the earlier person has no more authority to bind the later person than one person has to bind a stranger.
(6) The later person's retrospective assent cannot supply what is missing, since the values with which she would assent are themselves effects of the choice she would be ratifying; her endorsement would be circular.
(7) So a radical transformation is a choice that nobody is in a position to make on behalf of the person who must live with it, and autonomy cannot authorize it.
(8) The same reasoning, however, applies to ordinary identity-forming choices: religious conversion, marriage, emigration, psychotherapy, and changes of career also replace the values and projects from which they were made, and should therefore be equally unauthorized.
(9) Since autonomy plainly does authorize at least some of those ordinary choices, and since the difference between them and the stipulated transformation looks like one of degree rather than kind, some earlier step must be false.

What I need for the feature is this: identify the first false step, explain why it seemed acceptable as the argument moved through it, and say what a transhumanist and a critic of radical enhancement would each have to pay to give it up. I would rather preserve the idea that a competent adult can voluntarily authorize at least some deep changes to herself than accept a principle that makes every transformative choice an act of unauthorized self-creation, but I want to know what that preservation costs.
