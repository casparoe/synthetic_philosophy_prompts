Here's an asymmetry that's been bugging me. Suppose a woman on unemployment benefits quietly does a few hours of cash-in-hand cleaning each week to keep the lights on. If the welfare office finds out, she can be sanctioned, made to repay the money, and treated in the local press as a benefit cheat. Now suppose someone who owns a second home rents it out on Airbnb most of the year and never declares the income. When the tax authority catches them, the usual outcome is that they pay the back tax, maybe a small penalty, and everyone moves on. Both concealed income from the state; both kept money the state was entitled to under the rules; if anything the landlord kept more. But the first gets moralized and the second gets treated as a paperwork problem. Is there a principle that justifies this difference, or is it just that we've decided one group is a problem and the other isn't? I want the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can build — maybe it turns on the direction of the transfer, on being a claimant rather than a payer, on the amount, on enforceability, on blameworthiness, on the thought that one is taking from a pot while the other is failing to contribute to it, on something else. Then test that principle against nearby cases: a pensioner who fails to declare a small private pension, a contractor who does cash jobs on top of a full-time salary, a company that misclassifies workers to dodge payroll taxes, a landlord who claims a home-office deduction for a room they actually rent out. Which of those should be treated like the first case and which like the second, on your principle? At the end tell me whether you'd keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or accept that it isn't defensible.
