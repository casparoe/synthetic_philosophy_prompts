Retired condensed matter physicist here, a few years into reading philosophy of science, and Ive ended up with a cluster of views about reduction and emergence that I can't quite get to cohere. I can't tell whether they do cohere and I'm just not seeing the distinctions, or whether one of them has to go. I'd like an audit, not a lecture. Here's the set.

(1) I'm a physicalist in the sense that I think fundamental physics is causally closed: every physical effect has a complete physical cause at the micro level. So there's no downward causation — the whole can't push the parts around in a way that outruns the microphysics. If the microphysical story already fully accounts for the effect, then a higher-level cause on top of it feels like double-counting.

(2) But I'm not an eliminativist. I think thermodynamic, chemical, and biological descriptions latch onto real patterns and kinds. I'm happy to say the pressure in the tank caused it to rupture, the ligand binding to the receptor caused the cascade downstream, the structure of the population explains how fast selection acts. Those don't feel like loose talk; they feel like real causes and real explanations.

(3) I think strong emergence is almost certainly false. The idea that genuinely new fundamental forces or laws switch on at a certain level of organization seems to me like magic, and every proposed example I've seen looks in principle derivable from lower-level dynamics. The in-principle part matters: the fact that we can't do the derivation in practice is about us, not about the world.

(4) But in-principle derivability isn't the whole story either. The renormalization group and universality classes seem to show something more: huge families of microscopically different systems (a spin lattice, a fluid near its critical point, a binary alloy) fall into the same universality class, with the same critical exponents, and the details drop out for a principled reason. The order parameter and correlation length end up looking more robust than any particular microphysics. I want to say RG is tracking something real without letting in new laws, but I can't articulate what real adds over being in-principle derivable.

(5) I find multiple realizability arguments against reduction mostly overrated. The usual move — that the same higher-level kind is realized by many different lower-level structures, so it can't be reduced to any one of them — strikes me as contradicted by the physics I know best. The same macrostate or critical behavior showing up across different micro details is standardly explained by symmetries, dimensional analysis, conservation laws, and the RG flow; it doesn't point to any irreducible higher-level glue. So I dont buy multiple realizability as a reason to think higher-level kinds are nonphysical.

(6) And yet I can't make biological function and selection history fit this picture. The claim that the heart evolved to pump blood is about the history of a population, not about the current molecules in any one organism. If (1) and (3) are right, I'm not sure what makes a historical-functional explanation anything more than a convenient redescription of the current microstate. But I also refuse to accept that evolutionary explanations are just useful fictions for creatures with finite brains.

So the package is bottom-up causal closure, no strong emergence, realism about higher-level patterns and causes, skepticism about multiple realizability, and a stubborn commitment to functional explanation. Does that hang together? Treat it as an audit: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and what would have to give to fix each problem, ordered by how severe the problem is. I honestly don't know which commitment is the weak one, so don't read the order of the list as my priorities.

One more thing: as you make claims about any of this, tell me how confident you are in them and single out the point you're least sure of.
