Recently a friend of mine who runs an oral storytelling group floated a proposal that's been rattling around my head since. The rule: start from an agreed written text of a short creation myth, read to the first teller once. From then on there's no writing and no recording. Each teller must change exactly one element—a name, a motive, a plot beat, a detail of setting—and must otherwise retell, from memory, the version they actually heared. No rehearsing from notes, no checking with an earlier teller. After every fifty retellings the group writes the current version down, and then the next cycle restarts from whatever got written. We're trying to think through what this does to the myth over, say, five hundred retellings, before we waste a year actually running it.

The starting myth: a trickster steals fire from the corpse of the sun, is caught, and is punished by being hung in the sky as the moon, but by then the stolen fire has already scattered into the stars. I care less about the symbolic reading than about how the mechanism behaves. Which parts would resist change longest, and which would flip earliest? Would the one-change-per-telling rule keep the myth coherent—each teller inherits most of the previous version—or would drift accelerate because each teller changes a different element and the choices compound? Do you think a de facto canonical version would reemerge anyway, with people treating the version they first heard as 'the real one', and does the every-fifty-retellings checkpoint anchor things so hard that the whole exercise becomes slow scribal copying? And where does the rule get gamed or quietly broken first, and can you patch that without gutting the experiment?
