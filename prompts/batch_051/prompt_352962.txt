I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about risk and consent, because I keep finding myself defending different positions depending on which example somone brings up. I finally wrote my commitments down and now I want to know whether they hang together. Here's the list:

(1) You can wrong a person by imposing a risk on them without consent, even if the harm never comes about. If I point at a sleeping stranger a gun that has a one-in-six chance of being loaded, and I pull the trigger and it clicks empty and they never wake, I've still done something seriously wrong.

(2) The natural explanation of (1) is that the wrong is located entirely at the moment of acting, in the ex ante chance I impose. Nothing that happens later adds to or subtracts from the wrongmaking; the fact that the chamber turned out empty doesn't make it less wrong.

(3) For standing background risks of living around other people—driving, industrial emissions, air travel—I'm inclined to say permissibility should be settled by an ex ante test with a consent gloss: the practice is okay if the people exposed would reasonably accept the risk given the benefits it gives them back. I don't mean anyone literally signs anything; the acceptance is hypothetical.

(4) But when a risk is spread very thin—each person faces a tiny chance and no one can be picked out in advance—it feels wrong to talk about particular individuals being wronged ex ante. A one-in-a-million death risk seems small enough that the ex ante change to any one person's situation is negligible, so the real wrongs, if any, happen only later, when the harm actually lands.

(5) So I also want to say the ex post facts can't be irrelevant. If a city knows its roads will kill a predictable number of people next year, it owes something actual—serious safety measures, compensation to the families, a real public process—not just a per-person ex ante calculation showing each resident was made better off on balance.

(6) And, all things considered, I think driving in the city is permissible, and I don't want that to be special pleading just because I happen to drive.

I know a lot of people would say (1) and (6) straightforwardly contradict each other, and I suspect that's the easiest objection and maybe not the interesting one. What I can't tell is whether (2) through (5) are actually coherent, which pairs are only superficially in tension, and which single commitment I'd need to revise to make the whole package hang together. Could you audit the set: go through the tensions in order of how severe they really are, say which ones dissolve on a better reading, and for each real problem tell me the cheapest fix and what it costs?
