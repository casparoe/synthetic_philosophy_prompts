I have been trying to locate the flaw in an argument that seems to show that, under Bayesian updating, the choice of a prior is practically irrelevant. I want a diagnosis, not a general primer on convergence results.

Here is the argument:

1. Rational agents update their degrees of belief by conditionalization.
2. Consider two agents who share the same likelihoods but begin with different priors over a finite set of hypotheses H. Both priors assign positive probability to every hypothesis in H, and H contains the true hypothesis.
3. Under the standard Bayesian convergence results, with independent and identically distributed observations, each agent's posterior probability for the true hypothesis converges to 1 with probability 1 under the true hypothesis.
4. If two sequences each converge to 1, then their difference converges to 0. So for any tolerance you name, there is some finite time after which the two agents' posterior probabilities for the true hypothesis differ by less than that tolerance.
5. Any real decision is made at some finite time. But in principle one can simply wait until the relevant time mentioned in (4) has passed, so the difference in priors can be made as small as one likes before acting.
6. Therefore, for any practical decision, the prior matters only for how long one must wait before choosing; two rational agents with different positive priors cannot rationally sustain their disagreement once enough shared evidence is in.
7. But that conclusion is false. A person with a wild prior—say, one in a billion on the truth and the rest on a single false competitor—is not in as good a position as someone with a reasonable prior, and scientists with different priors can share a career's worth of data and still reasonably disagree.

I would prefer to hold onto (1) and (3). Please say which step or steps fail, explain why each one looked safe, and say what it would cost to reject it.
