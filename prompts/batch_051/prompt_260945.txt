I've been teaching a seminar on the epistemology of ethics for a few years, and I keep noticing a pattern among students that I don't have a name for. The same students who take other people's word without hesitation — that a policy is legal, that a clinical trial was badly designed, that a community is likely to revolt — become much more guarded the moment the conclusion is moral. They will accept a colleague's claim that a practice is exploitative, understood as a way of drawing attention to features of the practice, but they will not accept the colleague's further claim that the practice is wrong, in the sense of letting it settle what they ought to do. One student writes essays in which a bioethicist's verdict about a case is treated as the considered position to engage with, yet says that if she were the doctor she could not adopt that verdict as her reason for acting until she had done the reasoning herself, in her own voice. Another student grants that his aunt is morally wiser than he is and accepts her factual account of a family dispute, but he refuses to accept her statement that forgiving is the right thing to do; he says he needs to see for himself why forgiveness should win out over resentment or distance.

This does not look like a general distrust of testimony, since the same students defer happily on factual, legal, and predictive matters; it also does not look like subjectivism, since they continue to treat some moral judgments as better supported than others. My question is whether what I am describing is a recognized phenomenon in the literature on how we form moral beliefs from what others tell us. I have a rough sense that there is discussion of whether there is something wrong with forming moral views at second hand, and of whether our moral judgments have to be our own in some special sense, but I cannot tell whether the pattern above is that idea, a special case of it, or something distinct. If it is distinct, what would you call it? More important than the label: how exactly should its boundaries be drawn, which established concepts is it adjacent to and how does it differ from each, and under what conditions could this stance be a rational response to the nature of moral judgment rather than a bias? In answering, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
