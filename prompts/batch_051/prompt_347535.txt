I'm auditing a course on historical methodology, and a passage from the ancient Greek medical treatise On Airs, Waters, and Places has been nagging at me. The work is traditionally attributed to Hippocrates. In the section comparing Asia and Europe, the author argues that Asia's mild, uniform climate makes its inhabitants intelligent but lacking in spirit, so they tend to submit to kings, whereas Europe's harsher and more variable climate produces hardier people who will fight to remain free. He seems to be offering climate not merely as one influence among many but as the underlying cause of political regimes. When I tried to bring this up in discussion, the instructor labeled it environmental determinism and then moved on to other questions.

I have since read modern historians who say that climate and disease should be brought back into history as historical actors, yet they also insist they are not determinists. I can't tell whether the Hippocratic argument is rejected because it is false, because it is politically unacceptable, or because it contains a specific intellectual mistake that later scholars have learned to avoid. My suspicion is that it is mostly political discomfort: the text says something unflattering about Asians, so people dismiss the whole approach. Am I missing a conceptual error? Please point out the exact confusion in my suspicion, and then explain how a modern historian can say that climate and disease are historical actors without buying the Hippocratic conclusion. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
