I'm a software engineer and I've never taken a philosophy class, so I'm going to mess up the vocabulary. My team works on a small product where any one of us can merge to main and push straight to prod with no second reviewer. It mostly works, but a while back someone shipped a config change that took the site down for most of an afternoon. Afterwards he said he genuinely thought it was the right call, and I believe him. Then I read about something called the unilateralist's curse, and now I have an argument stuck in my head that seems to prove way too much, and I cant find the step where it goes wrong.

The argument goes:

(1) In any group where each member can act on their own, a risky action happens if and only if at least one member judges it to have positive expected value.

(2) For a bad action, each member has some independent chance of wrongly judging it to be good. Say there are five of us and each has a 10% false-positive rate.

(3) So the chance that at least one member wrongly ships the bad thing is 1 - (0.9)^5, roughly 41%. The group is therefore much more reckless than any individual member.

(4) But any member who is about to act knows they are being selected from the optimistic tail: the very fact that they, and not the others, crossed the threshold is evidence that their judgment is the overconfident outlier.

(5) So in any unilateral-decision setting, each member should discount their own positive judgment and defer to the group's pooled judgment, acting only when basically everyone agrees.

(6) Therefore no one should ever take a risky, irreversible action unilaterally: no solo deploys, no single researcher publishing a half-baked result, no whistleblower going outside the chain of command without sign-off.

I want to keep (1) and (2), and I definitely don't want to live with (6), because (6) says the one person who spots a real incident at 3am is supposed to sit on their hands while the team sleeps. But I can't tell which step I actually have to give up to stop the argument from reaching (6). Which step is the load-bearing one, and what do I lose by rejecting it? If rejecting that step doesnt fully break the chain, tell me what weaker version of the argument survives. And separately, under what conditions is unilateral deploy authority actually fine, since switching to unanimous votes would kill our velocity?
