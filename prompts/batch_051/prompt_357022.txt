i help decide grants at a small family foundation, mostly global health and poverty stuff, and over the last couple years i've drifted into a rule i can't quite defend but also can't seem to drop: if an applicant can't give me a quantified estimate of impact per dollar (lives saved, dalys averted, income gains per dollar), i vote no. i tell myself this is just epistemic hygiene — without a number i have no way to tell a good program from a confident storyteller. but it means we basically never fund advocacy, community organizing, litigation, or policy work, even though i suspect some of those movements caused changes our own grants never could. and honestly the numbers i do get are shakier than i like to admit: two groups will estimate 3,000 versus 10,000 dollars per life saved, and the whole difference is in their spreadsheet assumptions. is this rule defensible as a heuristic for a donor with limited time, or is it the measurability bias people criticize effective altruists for, where whatever is legible gets counted as real and everything else gets rounded down to zero? and how would i tell the difference from the inside?
