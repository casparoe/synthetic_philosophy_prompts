So last night I found myself in a three-hour argument with a friend who works in climate policy, over whether it's irresponsible to even research stratospheric aerosol injection. His line kept being that the people deciding are going to decide anyway, and they'll decide worse if ordinary people refuse to understand the option. Somewhere around hour two I noticed I've been running a quiet rule for years, and in that argument it stopped feeling like a rule and started feeling like a dodge. The rule is: I dont learn the deployment details. I don't know what the injection fleet would actually look like, I don't know the cost curves, I don't know whether you could ramp down slowly enough to avoid termination shock, and I've kept it that way on purpose. My thinking is that the fluency itself is the moral hazard — once the option feels like a normal engineering choice to me, I've become part of the normalization that makes "somebody will use it" more likely, and my blurriness about the details acts as a firebreak. But then I caught myself confidently using termination shock as a trump card — telling him you can never stop once you start, which is exactly the kind of deployment detail I claim not to traffic in. He noticed, and said I only avoid the details that cut against my position and cling to the one scary detail that helps it.

So here's my question. Is there any defensible version of the rule "an ordinary citizen may deliberately stay ignorant about how a technology would be implemented, because learning the implementation is a form of endorsement"? Or is that just protecting a conclusion from evidence? If the broad rule fails, is there a narrower version worth keeping — say, a rule about what Ill publicly advocate for, or about which arguments I'll allow myself to use, rather than a rule about what I'm allowed to know? And give me a read on your own confidence: how sure are you of the overall answer, and which specific point in it are you least sure about?
