I spent my working life as an experimental physical chemist, and I have recently been reading about self-amending rules—Peter Suber's Nomic, the puzzle of Article V of the United States Constitution, and the general problem of whether a rule about rule-change can be applied to itself. I run a monthly philosophy discussion group for about a dozen adults, most of whom have no formal philosophy training. Next month I would like to spend 75 minutes not lecturing on this, but having the group run into the problem themselves.

Please design a runnable activity for 12 adults, no background assumed, 75 minutes total. It should be a structured game or simulation in which participants are members of a small constitutional convention that must change its own rules. The activity should be arranged so that they encounter the self-reference problem—the moment where a rule about how to change rules must be applied to itself—before anyone names it. I want the rules and materials to be as simple as possible, the payoffs or points to be symbolic rather than monetary, and the debrief to map specific moments of play onto the underlying logical structure. The activity should not depend on participants having heard of Nomic or Article V.

Please include: the initial rules and materials; the step-by-step flow with timing; a scoring or success condition that creates real pressure without money; and a debrief that names the key moves. Also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of self-reference the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator afterwards.

Begin your response with the activity itself, with no preface or commentary.
