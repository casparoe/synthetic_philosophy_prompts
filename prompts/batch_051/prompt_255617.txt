I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether ordinary citizens may ever kill or depose a tyrant. My friend kept insisting that Thomas Aquinas settles the matter in the *Summa Theologiae*, II-II, q. 42, a. 2, reply to objection 3, where he says:

> A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10).

My friend took this to mean that because the tyrant's regime is unjust, any individual subject may resist it. But I wonder whether Aquinas's position can be cut off from the political assumptions he inherited. In his setting, public authority over a tyrant typically came from above or from the community as a whole: an emperor might depose a lesser prince, the pope might act through the Church, or the people who originally instituted a ruler might withdraw their consent. In a modern constitutional democracy, however, citizens generally have no superior ruler above them to whom they can appeal, and "the people" are simultaneously the source of public authority and the people subject to the tyrant. Does Aquinas's argument against private tyrannicide survive this change, or does it now imply that any competent citizen, as a member of the sovereign people, may act with public authority? Identify which premise in his reasoning is actually put under pressure by this development, and say whether there is a repaired version of the argument that remains defensible without licensing private violence too easily.
