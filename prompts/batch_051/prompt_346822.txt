I'm taking a seminar on Cold War strategy and one of my classmates keeps reducing nuclear brinkmanship to a game of chicken: two cars drive at each other, the first to swerve loses, so the only way to win is to make the other driver believe you absolutely won't swerve. From this he concludes that in a real crisis the winning side is the one that seems more willing to let the collision happen, which means the rational policy is to deliberately give up a bit of control: tie your hands, delegate launch authority, cultivate a reputation for not backing down, maybe act a little crazy. I see that chicken captures something real about credibility and resolve. But I also suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear: chicken is symmetric and one-shot, its purely zero-sum over who backs down, and the cost of "losing" is just embarrassment, whereas a nuclear crisis has two sides that both badly want to avoid actually crashing, plus allies, domestic audiences, conventional options, escalation ladders, and years of repeated interaction. I'd like an audit of this analogy rather than a yes/no: what maps onto what in the chicken picture, where the mapping breaks, which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just surface differences, and whether the "a little irrationality is rational" conclusion survives once they're on the table. If there's a better analogy for brinkmanship, tell me what it is and what it handles better. And at the end say how confident you are in the overall verdict, and single out the point you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the argument is softest than have it sound uniformly certain.
