I teach formal epistemology, and I have been collecting my own verdicts about a family of puzzles concerning self-locating belief. The verdicts feel stable individually, but I suspect they do not all cohere. I want to know what principle—or small set of candidate principles—best captures them, and where the pressure points are.

Here are the cases.

1. Standard Sleeping Beauty. On Sunday evening a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, I will be woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, I will be woken on Monday and again on Tuesday; between the two awakenings I am given a drug that erases my memory of the first, and my waking state is the same on both days. Each time I wake, I am asked for my credence that the coin landed heads. My verdict: 1/3.

2. Many awakenings. Same as case 1, except that if the coin lands tails I will be woken, and my memory erased, on each of one thousand consecutive days. Each time I wake, I am asked for my credence in heads. My verdict: 1/1001, and I would allow that credence to approach 0 as the number of tails-awakenings grows without bound.

3. The day question. In the standard protocol, each time I wake I am asked instead for my credence that today is Monday. My verdict: 2/3.

4. Told the day. In the standard protocol, when I wake I am truthfully told that today is Monday before being asked for my credence in heads. My verdict: 1/2.

5. The cosmic census. I am considering two hypotheses. Hypothesis One: the universe contains exactly one conscious being. Hypothesis Two: the universe contains one hundred billion conscious beings, all of whose epistemic situations are qualitatively just like mine. I have no evidence that discriminates between the two hypotheses except the bare fact that I exist. I am asked for my credence in Hypothesis Two. My verdict: 1/2; I do not think the sheer number of observers is, by itself, a reason to believe Hypothesis Two.

A simple principle keeps tempting me. For each underlying hypothesis h about the world, with prior probability P(h), let N_h be the number of waking episodes—or, in the cosmic case, conscious lives—compatible with my current evidence, and let M_h be the number of those episodes in which the proposition at issue is true. My credence in the proposition would then be:

(Σ_h M_h P(h)) / (Σ_h N_h P(h)).

This handles the first four cases straightforwardly. In case 1, heads contributes one compatible episode and tails contributes two, so heads receives (1/2 · 1) / (1/2 · 1 + 1/2 · 2) = 1/3. In case 2, heads receives 1/1001. In case 3, Monday is true in one of the two tails episodes and in the single heads episode, so the numerator is 1/2 · 1 + 1/2 · 1 and the denominator is 1/2 · 1 + 1/2 · 2, giving 2/3. In case 4, once I know it is Monday, exactly one compatible episode remains under each hypothesis, giving heads 1/2. But in case 5 the principle multiplies Hypothesis Two by one hundred billion and returns something indistinguishable from certainty in Hypothesis Two, which is exactly the verdict I reject.

So the principle is too crude as it stands. What I would like from you is this. State precisely the best version of the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that fits all five verdicts. Show in detail how each candidate handles each case, and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate. If saving all five verdicts requires restricting the principle, say exactly what the restriction is, what motivates it independently of the wish to avoid case 5, and whether that motivation is strong enough to justify the resulting complexity. If no defensible restriction is available, say which verdict should be revised and what the simpler principle would put in its place.

Finally, tell me what your preferred principle predicts about a further case I have not settled: the standard protocol, but with the coin biased 2-to-1 in favor of heads, and with one awakening on heads and two on tails. I deliberately withhold my own verdict on this case.
