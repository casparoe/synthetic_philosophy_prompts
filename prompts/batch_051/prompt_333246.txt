I keep noticing a failure mode in my own consequentialist reasoning that I don't have a word for. When I compare two options by tracing their long-run effects, the verdict depends almost entirely on where I stop tracing. If I count only the direct effects, option A wins; if I add the second-order effects on the people immediately touched, option B wins; if I add the third-order effects — on those people's future choices, careers, and descendants, on markets, ecosystems, and institutions — A wins again, then B, and it never settles down. Worse, adding more realistic detail doesn't shrink the swings; it makes the estimate lurch harder, because each new pathway is individually plausible, non-negligible, and points in a different direction. I saw the same pattern when a grantmaking committee I sit on compared two philanthropic projects: the ranking flipped depending on whether we included effects on recipients' grandchildren, effects on the wider sector, effects on the volunteers' careers, and so on, and no stopping point seemed less arbitrary than any other. I know this is adjacent to the butterfly effect, to Knightian uncertainty (risk without usable probabilities), and to model uncertainty, but each of those feels different: the butterfly effect is about small causes producing large downstream divergence in fact, Knightian uncertainty is about missing probabilities rather than about non-convergence, and model uncertainty is about not knowing which structure is right. What I'm describing is narrower: the expected-value estimate itself fails to converge as the causal web is extended, so the bottom line is an artifact of an arbitrary truncation.
