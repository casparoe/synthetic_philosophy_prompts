Here are the criteria I use to classify a sentence as self-referential. I have three, and I want to test them against a case that seems deliberately built to evade them.

(1) Direct self-reference: the sentence contains an expression that refers to the sentence itself, as in "This sentence is false."

(2) Finite circular reference: the sentence belongs to a finite chain of sentences in which each sentence refers to the next one, and the chain eventually loops back to the first, so that the truth conditions of the first sentence eventually refer back to the first sentence.

(3) Quantificational self-reference: the sentence quantifies over a totality that includes the sentence itself, or over a totality whose truth conditions involve the sentence's own truth value.

Now consider Yablo's sequence. For each natural number n, let S_n be the sentence: "For every m greater than n, S_m is false." So S_1 says that all sentences after it are false, S_2 says that all sentences after it are false, and so on forever. No sentence mentions itself, and each sentence refers only to sentences further along in the sequence, so there is no finite chain that loops back. Yet the sequence appears to leave no sentence with a consistent classical truth value: if S_n is true, then all later sentences are false, but S_{n+1}'s being false requires that some sentence after S_{n+1} be true; and if S_n is false, then some later sentence must be true, which leads into the same contradiction.

Is Yablo's sequence self-referential? Work through the three criteria one at a time and say what each gives. Then address the harder question: if the criteria do not classify the sequence as self-referential even though it is paradoxical, does that show self-reference was not doing the essential work in the liar paradoxes, or does it show that my three criteria are too narrow and need a fourth?
