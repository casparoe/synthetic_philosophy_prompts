I keep coming back to Kripkenstein's rule-following paradox (the Kripke reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following remarks), and I want to test whether a new development actually breaks it or just gets absorbed by it. My rough understanding: take addition. You've only ever applied '+' to numbers below some cutoff (57 in the stock example). The sceptic defines 'quaddition' as addition whenever both inputs are below that cutoff, and 5 otherwise. Everything you've ever said or thought with '+' is compatible with either rule, and no fact about your memory, your inner state, or your behavioural dispositions seems to single out plus over quus—being disposed to say 68 is just a disposition, not a truth about what you meant. So the sceptic says there is no fact of the matter about which rule you were following, and meaning becomes something like licensed use inside a community, not a set of truth conditions.

Now the development: autoregressive language models trained on a giant chunk of human text generalise from exactly the kind of finite, gappy examples the sceptic says can't settle the question, and they then continue unseen cases in the 'plus' way rather than the 'quus' way—without anyone ever stating the rule outright. Does this hand the meaning-realist the fact she was missing, or does it just relocate the problem? The trained network is only one of indefinitely many functions that fit the training data, and the tie-breaker is an inductive bias nobody explicitly chose; so why should that bias be authoritative? And can the sceptic still reply that the model only has a disposition to continue one way, and that dispositions were never enough because following a rule has a normative 'should' that a language model doesn't have—no matter how good it's continuations are? Alternatively, should the paradox be re-diagnosed once we have a working machine that learns a community's continuation practices from text?
