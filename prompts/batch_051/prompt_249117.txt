I'm a retired geologist — I spent forty years assuming the world runs on regularities you can count on, which is probably why I've been a quiet cessationist most of my adult life. Lately I've been reading the New Testament more carefully and talking to some charismatic friends, and I've come around to a moderate continuationist position: I don't think the so-called miraculous gifts (prophecy, tongues, healing) were meant to shut off when the last apostle died or when the canon closed, but I also dont think they continue with apostolic authority — I take them to be fallible, testable, and subject to the church's judgment, which is a messier claim than either side usually wants.

I'm presenting this at our church's adult education hour in a few weeks. The crowd is theologically literate but not academic: maybe half are soft cessationists who would never say God can't heal today but get twitchy around anyone who claims he just did, one elder is a hard cessationist who knows the confessions cold and can do the exegesis from memory, and there's a handful of younger folks who have charismatic leanings and want this to be true. My argument in a nutshell is that the New Testament gives no clear cessation text, that the "perfect" in the passage where Paul says prophecy and tongues will pass away reads more naturally as Christ's return than as the completed Bible, and that Paul's instructions to test prophecies and weigh them suggest he expected ordinary, imperfect prophecy to keep happening — the messiness is the design, not a bug.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this specific audience: the objections I'll actually get, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, where my replies are softest, and the one objection I should be praying nobody thinks to ask.
