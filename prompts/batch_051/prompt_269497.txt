Consider how museums, auction houses, and collectors treat paintings. A canvas known to be a complete modern forgery is treated as a devalued object even when no expert can distinguish it from the original by eye: it is withdrawn from sale or displayed only as a study piece, and it commands a small fraction of the price of the original, while institutions spend large sums to conserve the original even when a modern replica would look better. Musical and literary works follow a different pattern: a new performance of a sonata or a fresh printing of a novel counts as a genuine instance of the work, even though the composer's autograph manuscript or a particular first edition carries historical value. What conception of where the value of an artwork resides does this uneven pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the pattern strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with (scarcity, investment value, historical evidence, institutional habit), and say whether the asymmetry between the visual and the musical/literary cases is principled. Finally, explain what an art world run on a purely sensory conception of aesthetic value would have to change, and what would be lost.
