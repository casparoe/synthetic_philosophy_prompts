I keep coming back to an argument a friend made in a philosophy of law discussion, and I can't find the part that's bad, but I refuse to accept where it lands. It's about "naked statistical evidence"—the blue bus case, the gatecrasher, the prison-yard case. Here it is, numbered.

(1) A legal verdict should track the probability that this defendant did the act, conditional on the evidence the court actually has.

(2) Naked statistical evidence can push that probability as high as you like. Blue bus: a bus hits your car and the only admissible evidence is that 80% of the buses on that route belong to the Blue Bus Company, so the probability the defendant is liable is 0.8. Gatecrasher: 1,000 people are in the stands at a rodeo and only 499 paid; nobody can produce a ticket, so each person is 0.501 likely to have gatecrashed, which clears the civil "more likely than not" bar. Prison yard: 999 of 1,000 prisoners attacked the guard and the camera can't distinguish faces, so any given prisoner is 0.999 likely to be guilty.

(3) So if (1) and (2) are right, a rational legal system should sometimes rule against a defendant on naked statistics alone: the blue bus plaintiff wins in civil court, the gatecrasher has to pay, and the prisoner is convicted in criminal court.

(4) But courts refuse to do this, and the refusal feels right to me. There's something deeply wrong with locking someone up when the case against him is just that he's a member of a class 99.9% of whom are guilty, with no witness, motive, trace, or confession linking him to the act.

(5) You can't explain the refusal by holding out for more certainty than the probabilities provide. The prisoner case at 0.999 leaves less room for doubt than a lot of ordinary individualized evidence (a single shaky eyewitness), and courts convict on that all the time. So the thing doing the work can't be the level of probability, only its source.

(6) But source shouldn't matter once the probability is fixed; evidence is evidence, and probabilities don't care whether their inputs are naked. If the only thing I know places the defendant in a class 999 out of 1,000 of whom are guilty, then 0.999 is what my evidence says about him. Saying "that probability is about the class, not this defendant" just renames the source distinction; it doesn't show why the distinction is rationally significant.

(7) So the refusal is either irrational, or it's a brute preference that can't be stated in probabilistic terms without giving up on treating belief as probability at all.

I'd most like to keep (2) and (4): the probabilities are real and high, and we still shouldn't convict. But I can't see how they both fit. Take a definite stand: tell me which step fails, or if the argument is valid, which premise I should bite the bullet and reject—and what that choice costs. Don't give me a menu of possible moves; tell me the one you'd make and why.
