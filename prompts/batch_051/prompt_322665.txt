I cover science and ideas, and I’m working on a piece about how much we should defer to our own future beliefs. A source of mine defended van Fraassen’s Reflection principle — roughly, that a rational agent’s present credence in H, conditional on the proposition that her future self will assign H a credence of x, should be exactly x — with the following argument.

(1) Suppose a rational agent is certain that tomorrow she will assign H a credence of exactly x, while her present credence in H is y, with y > x. (2) Since her present credence in H is y, she will today regard as fair paying y for a ticket that returns $1 if H. (3) Since she is certain that tomorrow she will assign H a credence of x, and since that future credence will make her regard as fair receiving x in exchange for paying $1 if H, she is now committed to making that second trade when tomorrow arrives. (4) The two trades together lose her y − x no matter whether H is true: if H, she receives $1 on the first ticket and pays $1 on the second, netting x − y; if not H, she pays y and receives x, again netting x − y. (5) A rational agent cannot hold credences that commit her to a sure loss in this way. (6) Reversing the two trades rules out the case y < x as well. So if she is certain that her future credence in H is x, her present credence in H must already be x. (7) Therefore, for any rational agent, the present credence in H conditional on the proposition that her future credence in H is x must be x — exactly Reflection.

Is the argument sound? If not, identify the first step that fails; in particular, does step (7) follow from steps (1)–(6), and are there rational agents whose future credences would not be covered by the Dutch-book reasoning in steps (2) through (6)?
