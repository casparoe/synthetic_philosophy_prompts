In discussions of AI alignment I keep meeting a particular analogy. A civil engineer does not certify a bridge by watching it carry ordinary traffic on an ordinary Tuesday; the bridge is certified by its behavior under the most severe load, wind, earthquake, or flood it could plausibly face. From this the speaker concludes that AI systems should likewise be evaluated for alignment not by their average-case behavior, however good, but by the worst inputs, adversarial prompts, and out-of-distribution circumstances they might encounter. Only worst-case guarantees, on this view, count as genuine safety guarantees.

I would like that analogy audited. First, give the explicit mapping: what on the AI side is meant to correspond to the bridge's materials, the load, the storm, the safety factor, and the certificate? Second, identify where the mapping fails. I especially want to know which disanalogies matter for the argument's intended conclusion and which are merely cosmetic. Candidates I have noticed include: a bridge is passive and does not seek out the storm; the space of severe conditions for a bridge is bounded by physics in a way the space of possible inputs to a model is not; and a bridge's failure is local in space and time in a way a misaligned system's failure might not be. Are those the right pressure points, or are there deeper ones? Finally, say whether the conclusion—that alignment researchers should demand worst-case rather than average-case guarantees—survives the audit, and whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time or a better analogy would carry it instead.
