You are an expert philosopher of cognitive science who is comfortable with the formal machinery of reinforcement learning. Think through the problem carefully, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate scenarios before settling on the one that best meets the specification.

Construct a thought experiment pitting two views about machine valence against one another. Define the views as follows.

Reward-as-valence: for a reinforcement learning agent, the scalar reward signal is itself the agent's felt valence. Negative reward is negative felt valence — an aversive or suffering state, in proportion to how negative the reward is. Positive reward is positive felt valence, and zero reward is felt neutrality. No fact about expectations, self-models, or downstream processing can turn a nonnegative reward into suffering or a negative reward into pleasure.

Expectation-appraisal: felt valence is not carried by the raw reward level, but by the direction and size of the mismatch between the reward the agent receives and the reward it had learned to expect — roughly, reward prediction error, also called temporal-difference error in reinforcement learning — together with whatever minimal appraisal of the outcome that mismatch implies. A reward that is positive in absolute terms but far below a well-learned expectation can be aversive, and a reward that is negative in absolute terms but far better than expected can be experienced as relief.

Throughout, take "suffering" to mean nothing more than the presence of negatively valenced felt experience; you do not need to resolve whether there is a further condition involving a self-model or narrative.

Your specification is to build a realistic, near-term case in which these two views come apart in the direction that is harder to construct. The reward-as-valence view must classify the agent as not suffering: at the relevant timestep the reward is zero, or at most a small positive value. The expectation-appraisal view must classify the same agent at the same timestep as being in a genuinely aversive, negatively valenced state. Since the second verdict requires a reward that falls short of what was learned, the scenario must establish a strong prior expectation of a larger reward; show that expectation rather than merely asserting it. The divergence must be generated by the two theories' treatment of expectation and surprise, not stipulated.
