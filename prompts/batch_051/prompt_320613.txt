I am a novelist, and the central character of my new book is a vision scientist, Elena, who was born with complete achromatopsia: without functioning cones, she has always seen the world in black, white, and shades of gray. She has made color vision her life's work and can give an exhaustive third-person account of it—the optics of wavelength, the three cone types and their spectral sensitivities, the opponent channels, the pathway from retina through the lateral geniculate nucleus to visual cortex. She can state, better than almost anyone, every physical and functional fact about what happens when light of 650 nanometers reaches a normal human eye and the person sees red. But she has never once seen red.

I want a poem that is presented as the notebook entry she writes on the night before an experimental gene therapy that may give her trichromatic vision for the first time. Please make it an Italian sonnet. The octave should compress Elena's complete, third-person description of seeing red into exact, almost textbook language—wavelength, photopigments, opponent processes, signal routing—so that it reads as knowledge closing smoothly over its object. Let the octave's enclosed rhymes feel like a sealed system, and let the sestet break or loosen that pattern. The volta, entering at line nine, should arrive just as she stops describing and tries to anticipate the first-person experience itself; the language should strain toward the experience, then refuse to hand her, or the reader, what the description promised. The formal turn should do the philosophical work: someone who knows Mary's room and the knowledge argument should recognize the thought experiment at once, while a reader who has never encountered it should still feel the difference between having all the facts and having the experience.
