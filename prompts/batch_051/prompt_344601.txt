I am a novelist currently drafting a book set in a small town whose single industry is collapsing, and I keep returning to a question that is literary and philosophical at once: how much of a life's texture — its loyalties, its pieties, its sense of what is possible — should be explained by reference to the material conditions under which it is lived? I have been using John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath as a test case. In places the novel seems to be doing something close to historical materialism: the banks, the tractors, and the collapse of tenant farming appear to drag everything else along behind them, from family roles and religious feeling to the law and the way the migrants describe themselves. Yet the book also keeps insisting on something more — Ma Joad's refusal to be broken, the gradual growth of solidarity among the migrants, and Rose of Sharon's final act of feeding a starving man — moments Steinbeck seems to present as not simply derived from economic necessity.

I would like a careful reading of The Grapes of Wrath through the lens of the historical-materialist question of whether the economic base explains everything else or only constrains it. Does the novel actually commit itself to a strong base-determines-superstructure view, or does it waver, and where? Does the novel suggest that law, morality, family structure, and inner life are ultimately explained by material conditions, or that economic forces set the terms within which something independent can emerge? I am less interested in a summary of the plot than in what the book demonstrates, scene by scene, about how material conditions do and do not determine the rest of life, and in what it might teach a working novelist about how far an economic reading of a character can go before it becomes reductive. Please write your answer in American English.
