People in rationalist spaces will tell you till they're blue in the face that sunk costs don't count: the year you prepaid for the gym shouldn't matter, the hour you already spent on a book you hate shouldn't matter. But a lot of actual commercial practice seems built on the opposite bet. Gyms sell annual memberships precisely because people will feel they have to go; coffee-shop loyalty cards hand you the first two punches free so you feel you've already started and shouldn't waste the card; battle passes and similar reward tracks are tuned so that once you've put in a week of dailies, quitting feels like throwing away the progress and the money. So what theory of rational agency, preference, or self-control do those practices presuppose? Are they just harvesting a known bias, or do they implicitly adopt some view where honoring sunk costs is a real strategy—a commitment device, a self-signal, or a way of defending a long-run project from a lazy future self? Reconstruct the implicit view (or say where the practice is underdetermined between several views) that would make the business models coherent, say what it rules out like strict forward-looking expected value reasoning, and tell me what those products would have to look like if all their customers reasoned like ideal expected-value maximizers. Please separate what's empirically settled from what's genuinely contested among people who study this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
