You're an expert on Habermas and on institutional design — think carefully, and start by brainstorming failure modes before settling into your analysis.

I'm writing a paper for a class on deliberative democracy and have been reading Habermas's account of the ideal speech situation: roughly, when we're really engaging in communicative action, the only thing that's supposed to move anyone is the pressure of the better argument, not money, status, threats, bargains, etc., and everyone affected has an equal chance to raise and challenge validity claims. A friend and I started wondering how much of that could be approximated in an actual institution, so we sketched a proceudre for our 15-person housing coop's monthly budget meetings and we're trying to figure out how it would actually go before we propose it.

The rules: (1) discussion happens on a pseudonymous forum, and usernames are rerandomized each meeting so nobody can carry status across sessions; (2) a budget proposal is posted, then there is a seven-day discourse phase before any decision; (3) everyone gets 12 speech credits, each post costs one credit, and every post must be tagged as descriptive claim, normative claim, challenge, clarification, concession, or question; (4) if a claim is challenged, its author has 48 hours to defend it with reasons or retract it — defenses are themselves claims and can be challenged recursively up to three levels deep, a claim that survives to depth three counts as standing, and one that is ignored or retracted counts as defeated, with its challenger earning two extra credits; (5) retractions and concessions earn one reputation point, while any claim that survives to the end earns two, on the theory that this rewards honest updating and defensible claims; (6) a rotating moderator can delete posts that are ad hominem, threats, bargaining, status-pulling, or otherwise strategic, but must justify each deletion under the same rules, and the deletion itself can be challenged like any other claim; (7) at the end, the proposal passes only if its standing supporting claims outnumber its surviving standing objections, otherwise it is revised or dropped — there is no separate preference vote.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract. Treat it as a mechanism: work through what it actually incentivizes, who ends up with power, where it gets gamed first, what kind of speech the tagging and credit system selects for, and whether the final output would track the better argument or just track something with better endurance. Then say whether the failure modes are patchable without smuggling back in the very background conditions the ideal speech situation was meant to bracket.
