I am a PhD student in intellectual history, and I keep circling back to the standard anti-Whig argument: that writing the past as a story of progress toward the present is anachronistic, flatters the winners, and systematically distorts what past agents could actually do, because the historian supplies the endpoint in advance and then selects and arranges evidence to reach it. That argument was aimed at narrative historians. Now suppose the relevant progress is no longer supplied by a narrator but emerges from long-run quantitative series—life expectancy, violence, literacy, political freedom—drawn from large historical datasets, and suppose a loose movement of economists and technologists now treats these series as real improvements that should be deliberately accelerated. Does the anti-Whig argument survive this development? Say which premise, exactly, is under pressure, and whether a repaired version can preserve what was right about the critique while allowing the measured improvements to count as genuine. I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in the politics of any particular movement.
