I work as a distributed systems engineer, and I have been reading Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop with a philosophy discussion group. One of his claims will not leave me alone. He denies that a self is the sort of thing that could be stored in one place; it is, he argues, an abstract pattern, a self-referential loop of symbols implemented in a particular brain, which can also be implemented more coarsely in other brains. After someone dies, he suggests, the pattern does not simply stop: lower-fidelity versions of it continue to run in the minds of people who knew the person, and in that sense the "I" persists in a distributed and degraded form.

This struck me as almost a restatement of how I already think about the systems I build for a living. In a replicated service, no single node is the service; the service is a pattern of state and behavior spread across nodes, with no canonical copy. It tolerates the loss of any one node as long as enough replicas remain and can coordinate. The genuinely difficult problems in my field concern exactly the things Hofstadter's picture seems to leave open: divergence between replicas, partial failure, consistency protocols, and the question of when two copies count as the same enough to be one system. So I want to know whether this parallel is deep or whether I am doing what every era does, projecting our current technology onto the mind.

Please be precise. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what is a node, what is a replica, what is the distributed state, what counts as a write, and what would correspond to conflict resolution or anti-entropy. Then say what would have to be true of selves, brains, and social cognition for results from distributed systems theory, such as quorum requirements or eventual consistency, to transfer. Finally, name at least one thing the analogy seems to predict about persons that you think is false, and explain whether the parallel survives that failure.
