Over the past year I have been in therapy, and my therapist has repeatedly used the word "rumination" to mark a habit of mine that she wants me to curb. I had always assumed rumination was just self-examination that had gone too far — a difference of degree, not of kind. But she treats it as importantly different from the self-examination she also encourages, and I have found I cannot state the border between them, even though I feel it clearly in particular cases. I would like a working definition of rumination: one sharp enough to separate harmful repetitive self-focused thought from productive self-examination, and sharp enough to handle all of the following cases: (1) lying awake replaying a tense conversation and enumerating what one could have said; (2) doing that same replay every night for a month, arriving nowhere; (3) journaling about a day's anxiety and ending the entry with a plan; (4) discussing an old relational pattern in therapy; (5) noticing thoughts arise and pass during meditation without following them; (6) looping through "why am I like this?" without staying for an answer; (7) a work postmortem that turns a failed project into a list of lessons; (8) monitoring one's own thinking for signs that it has become rumination.

My intuitions are firm on some of these — (2), (6), and (8) feel like rumination, while (3), (4), and (7) feel like something else — but I cannot articulate the criterion, and (1) and (5) leave me genuinely unsure. Case (8) especially bothers me: if examining one's own thinking can itself slide into rumination, then the usual advice to "notice when you ruminate" begins to look self-defeating. Propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, identify which verdicts surprise you or seem least secure, and say whether the definition implies that trying to detect one's own rumination is itself a risk.
