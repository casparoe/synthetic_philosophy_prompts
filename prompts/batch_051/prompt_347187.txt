I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people talk about persuasive technology, and I want to know whether it is principled or just a way of condemning the companies we already dislike. Consider two things that are, on their face, doing the same kind of thing. A social media platform uses infinite scroll, autoplay, and unpredictable social feedback — likes and comments arriving on a variable schedule, the same basic mechanism that keeps people at slot machines — and it is routinely described, in popular commentary and in tech-ethics writing, as manipulative: a dark pattern that hijacks attention and leaves users feeling they have spent an hour they never chose to spend. Yet a meditation app that uses streaks, badges, and prompts about a feed of friends' progress to pull me back each morning is described, often by the very same people, as a helpful nudge that builds a worthwhile habit, and a language-learning app using almost identical streak and point mechanics is praised for making practice automatic. In both cases, the designers have studied reinforcement schedules; in both cases they intend to change behavior and make it more automatic; in both cases the outcome is arguably something the user can endorse, and in both cases the company is also serving its own subscription or advertising revenue.

Acting as a professional philosopher who works on manipulation, autonomy, and the ethics of technology, help me see whether this asymmetry can be justified. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate distinctions a defender of the asymmetry might reach for: the content of the habit being formed, the user's prior endorsement of the outcome, the transparency of the mechanics, who ultimately profits, how much of the user's time the product can absorb, whether the device is content-neutral or commands attention. Then test each candidate against nearby cases: a gamified habit-tracker that a company sells to employers to keep workers productive, a social app deliberately capped at ten minutes a day, an educational app for children that uses the same mechanics and whose goals are set by the parents. Which distinction best explains and justifies the ordinary judgments? Does the asymmetry survive scrutiny, or does consistency force us either to condemn the meditation app too or to credit the social platform with more than we do? I want a verdict about whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or given up, and at what cost.
