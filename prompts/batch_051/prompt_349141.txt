So I just got diagnosed with end-stage kidney disease — I'm fine, but the waitlist where I live is around five to seven years and my sister is being tested as a live donor right now. Somewhere in week two of dialysis I fell down a rabbit hole about whether we should just legalize paying kidney donors, and I've realized almost everyone argues from one pet case (price gouging, plasma, votes) and then extends the vibe to everything else. Before I read one more proposal I want the full spec: what does a theory of which markets are off-limits actually have to get right?

Give me the requirements list. Don't give me a bibliography and don't tell me to read the famous people — I've seen them. I want the criteria, with the case or consideration behind each, and it has to be good enough to actually govern a real policy question: should we allow a regulated market for kidneys from living donors, and on what grounds? Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or just a particular camp's preference.

The theory has to handle at least the following without hand-waving or saying they're just different: kidney sales (living, with and without a price cap); plasma, where we already pay donors in the US and people can do it twice a week; price gouging on water and generators after a hurricane; selling your place in an organ queue; buying votes; surrogacy; paying people with addictions cash to get sterilized. It has to say which feature puts each inside or outside the line.

It also has to respect some distinctions rather than collapse them. The difference between a market being gross or off-putting, and being wrong — I do not care if someone is squeamish about paid plasma. The difference between a market that exploits a seller and one that corrupts the good being sold; those feel like different objections and the theory should say whether they're really one. The difference between selling your own organ and a third party brokering someone else's. And the difference between an objection about unfair allocation (only rich people get kidneys) and one about commodification itself (selling kidneys would be wrong even in a world with equal purchasing power). The theory has to say which of those distinctions are load-bearing and which are vibes dressed up as principle.

It can't contradict a few facts im stuck with now: people die on the waitlist; banning sales doesnt make black markets disappear, it makes them unregulated; and paid plasma and unpaid blood donation already coexist fine in the US, which has run the experiment. A theory that has to pretend otherwise to save itself is a non-starter.

Then the two things I most want: which single requirement is the most discriminating — the one that, on its own, rules out most of the standard positions people actually hold — and whether any two requirements can't be jointly satisfied. If there's a conflict inside the list itself I want it flagged plainly, not buried.

Format: use exactly these sections in this order. "Requirements and the case for each" (about 45%, with each marked non-negotiable or camp-specific), "Distinctions a theory can't collapse" (20%), "The most discriminating requirement" (10%), "Where the list overconstrains" (15%), "Quick verdict on the main camps" (10% — the consent/welfare camp, the corruption camp, and the repugnance-as-a-real-cost camp, scored against the list; no named authors, I just want the positions tested).
