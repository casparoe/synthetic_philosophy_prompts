I work on the canonical-form engine of a computer algebra system that also supplies normal forms to a formal-mathematics library and to automated theorem provers, so the system must produce literally the same canonical expression for mathematically equivalent inputs and must never produce a normal form that is unique only up to an unspoken conjugation. Several times a month, in a design review, I have to decide which of several symmetric objects gets to be canonical: which root of x² + 1 is stored internally as i and which as −i, which conjugate of an algebraic number is primary, which primitive element of a finite field is used to build a representation, what sign a square-root simplification like sqrt(x²) returns. The team is mostly engineers without training in philosophy, the meeting runs an hour, and the decision has to be defensible to the release engineer who signs off on soundness-critical changes. We currently make these choices ad hoc, and every so often something subtle breaks: two derivations that ought to agree produce expressions that differ by a conjugation, or a normal form turns out not to be unique because a symmetric choice was hidden inside it.

I understand, at least in outline, the philosophical thought that mathematical objects are defined only up to isomorphism or automorphism and that there is no fact of the matter which root of x² + 1 is i; the labels are arbitrary conventions. But I still have to ship a canonical form, so I need a way to tell when that arbitrariness is genuinely harmless and when the choice of label does real work — for instance, when later operations are not equivariant under the symmetry, or when the object must later be embedded in a larger structure that itself breaks the symmetry.

Please write a reusable decision procedure we can run each time we choose a canonical representation: a checklist in words with the questions in order, the conditions under which each question counts as answered, where we may simply pick a convention and move on, where the symmetry is only apparent and the choice is forced, and where we must stop and either hand the decision to a human or explicitly encode the symmetry rather than choosing a representative. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) choosing an internal representative for the two roots of x² + 1 over the reals; (2) choosing a primitive element for a finite-field representation; and (3) choosing the sign in a simplification rule such as sqrt(x²). Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer my team will not like — for example, by refusing to let us treat a choice as a harmless convention when it is not. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do, for instance whether it can ever tell us which branch is correct or only keep us from confusing convention with fact.
