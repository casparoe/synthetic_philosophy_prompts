I am a student in an upper-level course on the philosophy of computation, and I have arrived at a view of the Church-Turing thesis that I want to stress-test. My position is that the thesis, in its original epistemic role, is neither a mathematical theorem nor a stipulative definition of the word computable, but a corrigible hypothesis about the extension of our pre-theoretic notion of effective computability: the claim that the functions computable by an idealized step-by-step procedure, one that could in principle be carried out by a human clerk following explicit rules without insight, coincide exactly with the class captured by Turing machines and the equivalent formalisms. The convergence of independent explications, together with the persistent failure of anyone to exhibit an intuitively effective procedure outside that class, is what supports the thesis; but because one side of the proposed identity is informal, the thesis could in principle have been false, and its epistemic standing is therefore closer to that of a well-confirmed empirical generalization than to that of a proved theorem.

I want an account of what would count against this specific position, rather than an account of what would only count against the bare first-order thesis. Please: (1) inventory the possible defeaters, including at least the following candidates, and for each say what the world would have to be like for it to obtain: a widely accepted example of an effectively computable function that is not Turing-computable; a convincing argument that the informal notion of effective computability is too vague or context-sensitive to support a stable identity claim; a demonstration that the convergence of the various formalisms is less independent than it appears, so that their agreement carries less evidential weight; and a physical result showing that some real process can compute a function beyond the Turing limit, thereby unsettling the distinct physical Church-Turing thesis that every physical process is simulable by a Turing machine. (2) For each candidate, say whether it undermines my meta-level reading, whether it merely pressures the first-order thesis, and whether a defender of my reading should concede it as counting against them or deflect it as a change of subject. (3) Assess which of these are live possibilities and which are merely logical. (4) Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude with an honest assessment of whether my position is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a virtue of a stable conceptual analysis or a sign that the position has insulated itself from evidence.
