I'm rebuilding a high-school civics curriculum, and I've hit a wall on the one term the rest of the course leans on. Every textbook I open says that democracy is rule by 'the people,' yet none of them says who the people are, and the obvious answer — 'the citizens' — only pushes the problem back one step: someone had to decide who counts as a citizen, and that decision bound people who had no say in it. I've come to think this is not an oversight in the textbooks but a structural puzzle in the very idea of democratic self-rule, and I want to teach the puzzle honestly rather than paper over it.

I want a working definition of 'the people' in the political sense: the body whose members are entitled to participate in a community's binding collective decisions — not a summary of what any particular country's law currently says, but a normative standard against which the law's inclusions and exclusions could be judged. I have nine cases I want the definition to classify correctly. (1) An adult citizen, born and continually resident in the town, who votes in every local election. (2) A tourist three weeks into a month-long stay. (3) A citizen who emigrated thirty years ago and returns only for short visits. (4) A foreign national who has lived and worked in the town lawfully for fifteen years, pays local taxes, and sends her children to its public schools. (5) A sixteen-year-old who will miss the voting-age cutoff by four months for a referendum that will bind the town for decades. (6) A commuter who works in the town five days a week but lives in the neighboring municipality. (7) A resident of twelve years who lacks legal immigration status and runs a small business in town. (8) A person serving a prison sentence for a serious crime committed in the town. (9) The future residents who will inherit the town's debts and infrastructure but cannot yet speak for themselves.

The first strikes me as clearly in and the second as clearly out; the other seven I keep changing my mind about. The principles I keep trying are that membership should track who is subject to the community's rules, or whose interests are materially affected by its decisions, or who must live with the long-term consequences of those decisions — and each handles a few of the cases and then breaks. Propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, say which verdicts are least secure, and defend or revise whichever surprising verdicts it delivers.
