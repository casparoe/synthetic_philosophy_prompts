I help run a small reading group on the history of economic thought, and last week we got stuck on a passage in Aristotle’s *Politics*, Book I, Chapter 10 (1258b), where he appears to condemn interest as the most unnatural way of getting wealth:

"As this is so, usury is most reasonably hated, because its gain comes from money itself and not from that for the sake of which money was invented. For money was brought into existence for the purpose of exchange, but interest increases the amount of the money itself (and this is the actual origin of the Greek word: offspring resembles parent, and interest is money born of money); consequently this form of the business of getting wealth is of all forms the most contrary to nature."

The argument began because one member of the group works on a decentralized lending protocol. The protocol lets users deposit a dollar-pegged stablecoin; it automatically lends those deposits out to traders who want leverage, holds their collateral, and credits depositors with interest paid in more of the same stablecoin. The developers describe this as "putting idle money to work." One reader insists this is exactly what Aristotle was condemning: a depositor hands over money and receives more money back, without making or transporting anything, so the gain comes from money itself. Another reader counters that Aristotle was not imagining credit markets in which the borrowed money is actually used to buy assets and take on risk; on that view the interest is a return on real economic activity, not offspring of the coin. A third reader says this misses the point of the passage, which is about what money is *for*, not about how productive the borrower is.

What I would like is a short analytical note that untangles how Aristotle’s passage should apply to this case. First, say what the passage, read literally, does and does not reach. Second, reconstruct the purpose of the passage within Aristotle’s broader contrast between natural wealth-getting and wealth-getting from exchange, and say whether that purpose reaches the protocol. Third, give your best assessment of what Aristotle would most plausibly have concluded if he had been confronted with this protocol in a thought experiment. Finally, tell me which of these three ways of applying the text—letter, purpose, or predicted author judgment—should control here, and why. Please keep the note to no more than 750 words, not counting any quotation of the Aristotle passage.
