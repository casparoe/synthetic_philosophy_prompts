In a debate about whether psychiatric classifications such as ADHD, schizophrenia, and major depression divide the world at its genuine joints or along partly conventional lines, a colleague of mine pressed a strongly realist case through an analogy. It went roughly as follows. A surveyor's map of a coastline describes the coast. Maps get revised as instruments and observations improve; an inaccurate map can mislead the people who navigate by it, and their subsequent activities may even reshape the shoreline here and there. But remapping does not alter the geology, and no one thinks that changing the map is changing the coast. Psychiatric classification, he argued, is the same in all essentials: the diagnostic manual is a map of conditions that exist independently of our mapping of them. Receiving a label may change a patient's self-understanding, behavior, and treatment, and those changes can be entirely real; the underlying condition, however, is untouched. Revisions of diagnostic categories are therefore best read as progress in describing a stable territory, not as changes in the things described — in principle, later manuals are more accurate maps of one and the same shoreline.

I found myself thinking about the phenomenon Ian Hacking called looping effects — the way classifying people can change the people classified in ways that feed back into the classification itself — but I could not articulate exactly where the map analogy gives out.
