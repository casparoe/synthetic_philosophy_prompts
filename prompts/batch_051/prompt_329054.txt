I am a software engineer working on model training infrastructure, and I keep coming across a slogan from colleagues who work on evolutionary methods: natural selection is a "stupid optimizer" — slow, blind, and local compared to gradient descent — so gradient-based learning has little to borrow from it. I want to turn a half-formed suspicion about this into a proposal for a three-month, one-person internal research fellowship (about 3000 words, no large compute budget). Please write the proposal.

My suspicion is that the usual diagnosis conflates two different properties of natural selection. The first is its lack of gradient information: it cannot compute derivatives through the genotype-phenotype map, so it hill-climbs in a high-dimensional space. The second is the non-stationarity of its objective: the fitness landscape changes as the population itself evolves — because competitors, predators, and prey are evolving too, and because organisms alter the environments they later face. My hunch is that the second property does most of the work in making natural selection look stupid, and that if this is right the familiar conclusion points the wrong way: what gradient-based learning should borrow from natural selection is not gradient-free search but better ways of assigning credit and stabilizing learning when the objective moves under the learner's own outputs.

My working formulation of the question is this: is the "stupid optimizer" verdict best explained by natural selection's lack of gradient information, or by the non-stationarity of the objective that its own products partly construct — and what does the answer imply about which properties of natural selection, if any, gradient-based learning should import? Treat this formulation as a starting point and refine it if the proposal requires.

Write the proposal with: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; an explanation of why existing ways of drawing the evolution-versus-gradient-descent comparison leave the question open; a concrete approach for making progress, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives such as a literature synthesis or a large-scale evolutionary simulation; the possible outcomes, including what would count as a negative or failed result, and which considerations would decide between them; and an honest assessment of what could make the project too big for three months and how you would narrow it. Do not include a bibliography; refer to positions and arguments by name, and I will supply any citations later.
