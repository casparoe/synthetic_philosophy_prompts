I've got an essay in the works on the old sphex wasp case—the wasp that drags its paralyzed cricket to the burrow, pops inside to inspect, comes back out, and then, if some meddling experimenter has nudged the cricket a couple inches, re-drags it to the threshold and re-inspects, forever, never just pulling the thing inside. The standard moral is that the wasp's apparent intelligence is a put-on: looks like careful provisioning, turns out to be a rigid little loop, and the uncomfortable part is that humans might be sphexish too, just with longer scripts. I think that moral confuses two seperate things, and I want to build an essay around the confusion rather than around the wasp itself.

The move I want to make is this. The wasps failure isn't being mechanical. Any skill a human has is a stack of subroutines too—typing, driving, tucking in a shirt. The wasp's failure is that its completion check is cue-bound rather than goal-bound: "cricket now two inches from where I left it" resets the whole task, even though the burrow hasn't changed and the cricket was never actually lost. A slightly different wasp-or-robot, just as mechanical, could have the rule "if the cricket is still within reach, put it in the burrow," and it wouldn't loop at all. So the looping doesn't show the wasp is an automaton in a way that the non-looping robot isn't; it shows the wasp has a badly wired exception checker.

That means sphexishness, if it's going to earn its keep, should be defined as something like "re-running completed subgoals because the success condition is keyed to a local cue rather than to the goal," not "acting from routine." Rigidity itself isn't the vice. A good agent is rigid about the goal and flexible about the means; the wasp is just rigid about the wrong layer.

That also reframes the free will worry. The scary thing about the wasp isn't that it follows a script; it's that it has a script with no "does this still matter?" layer. Humans do have something like that layer—fallible, causally embedded, not magic—and free-ish agency might just be a pile of rigid subroutines plus a supervisor that can catch an exception and re-route. So freedom is second-order, and sphexishness is the absence of the supervisor, not the presence of routine. That lets us use the word for very un-waspy cases: the perfectionist who redoes the whole assignment because one paragraph reads wrong, or the bureaucrat who re-runs the full form over a cosmetic mistake. Those people aren't automatic in the simple sense; their supervisor is misfiring. That's a sadder and more interesting moral than "don't be a creature of habit."
