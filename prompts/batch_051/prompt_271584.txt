I'm a third-year undergrad in a philosophy of science course right now and I've developed a habit I can't tell is actually good epistemology or just an aesthetic prejudice that's learned to talk like epistemology. My rule is: when I'm choosing between two explanations — in physics, econ, or even mundane stuff like why a friendship fell apart — I treat elegance as evidence. If one story has fewer moving parts, no tacked-on exceptions, and the conclusion falls out of a clean underlying principle while the other story just accumulates clauses and patches, I don't just prefer the first for convenience; I think it's more likely to be true, and I'll often keep believing it for a while even when the data momentarily looks worse for it. I know that last part sounds bad, but I'd always assumed elegance was a sign that you'd hit the real structure underneath things.

The thing that got me worried was a lecture on the Copernican case (or maybe I'm garbling it). My prof made the point that for a while the neater sun-centered system wasn't actually better at the numbers than the old epicycle apparatus, and that a lot of smart people went with the uglier one for good, empirically modest reasons. Then later I caught myself doing it in low stakes: a friend proposed one clean explanation of my recent burnout — basically "you took on too much and it finally caught up" — while the actual situation was a messier mix of sleep, a bad living situation, and something chemical maybe. I definately gave the clean version more weight than it deserved just because it was the intellectually prettier narrative. So now I don't know whether my rule is defensible in general and I misapplied it, or whether the rule itself is the problem.

The course has also touched on the idea that simplicity can be justified as a guard against overfitting, since a model with fewer free parameters has fewer ways to fit noise. But elegance feels like more than that — it includes symmetry, unity, surprise, a kind of of-course-it-had-to-be-that-way feel — and I'm not sure those extra parts inherit the same justification. Also, elegance looks like a property of how the theory is written down rather than a property of the world, and I don't see why my preference for a certain style of description should track what's true. So what I'd love to know is whether there's a defensible version of my rule, and under what conditions (if any) elegance actually counts as evidence rather than decoration. And since I'm pretty sure the people who actually work on this disagree about parts of it, could you make an effort to tell me which parts of your answer are settled and which are genuinely contested, instead of presenting a contested view as the consensus? I'd rather have the messy map than the pretty one.
