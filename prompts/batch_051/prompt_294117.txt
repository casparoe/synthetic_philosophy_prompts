I got into sports betting about a year ago, and now every disagreement in my group chat ends the same way: someone says "just maximize EV" like that settles it. I finally looked up what expected value can do when a bet has infinitely many possible outcomes, and I read about a coin-flip game (I think its called the Pasadena game) that has been rattling around my head ever since.

The game is this: flip a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads is on flip n, you get paid (-1)^(n-1) × 2^n / n dollars. So flip 1 pays +$2, flip 2 pays -$2, flip 3 pays +$8/3, flip 4 pays -$4, and so on, with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16... When you multiply each payoff by its probability and sum them up, you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges only conditionally. That means the positive terms and the negative terms each blow up to infinity on their own, and by reordering the terms you can make the total come out to any number you like, or diverge. So depending on who you ask, this game has no expected value, or a whole range of possible expected values, or the one number you get by adding the terms in their natural order (about 0.693, ln 2).

Now I'm suspicious that "just maximize EV" is covering up a bunch of different claims, and some of them might not even be defined in a case like this. I want you to take the slogan apart. What are the precise things someone could mean by "maximize expected value" when they say it to end a argument about whether to take a bet? Separate the versions that only make sense for finite gambles from the ones that try to say something about infinite ones. For each version, tell me whether its trivially true, a substantive but defensible principle, actually false, or simply undefined in messy cases like the one above. And I need you to commit: pick the version you think is the right principle, and tell me whether someone can still defensibly say "just maximize EV" after seeing that coin game. Don't lay out the options and leave me to decide.
