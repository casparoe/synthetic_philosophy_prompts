I chair a small law-reform commission advising the justice ministry of a common-law jurisdiction. We have been asked to settle the commission's position on whether the jurisdiction should create a limited legal status for highly autonomous systems, such as driverless freight vehicles or automated care dispensers that act in the world without a human deciding each step. The commission is divided. One faction, which I will call the extensionists, argues from the corporate analogy: legal personality is not a certificate of humanity but a practical device, and we already extend it to companies, which cannot think or feel, simply because it is useful to have a single entity that can contract, be sued, hold assets and owe duties. A registered artificial agent, they say, would do for autonomous systems what incorporation did for the firm. The other faction, the restrictionists, replies that the analogy fails precisely where it matters. Corporate personality works, they argue, because it aggregates and simplifies a dense web of human relationships; there are always identifiable shareholders, directors, employees and creditors behind the fiction, and the law can look through the veil when the fiction is abused. A stand-alone artificial agent would float free of any such web. On their view, the remedy for the much-discussed responsibility gap is to tighten product liability and operator duties, not to conjure a new person into existence.

My own worry, which I would like the memo to address rather than elide, is that both factions are treating the corporate analogy as a static argument from classification, when the real question is dynamic. Granting or withholding a new legal status would change the incentives of manufacturers, insurers and users: it would alter what they build, how much they supervise, and how they structure their affairs to keep liability away from themselves. One commissioner has added a twist the group has not yet digested: even where corporate personality works, it is routinely criticised for enabling exactly the kind of responsibility-shielding the restrictionists fear, so the analogy may be a warning rather than a precedent.

I need you to draft the commission's decision memo to the ministry. It must recommend one of three positions: (i) recommend a registered artificial-agent status with limited liability and a compensation fund; (ii) recommend against any such status and instead expand product-liability and operator-liability rules; or (iii) recommend a minimal liability-conduit status, under which the system is recognised only as a point through which liability is channelled, with no independent rights, assets or personality beyond that. The memo must give an actionable recommendation and the reasoning for it, engage seriously with the strongest arguments on each side, and treat honestly the strongest consideration that cuts against your recommendation rather than burying it. Add a short confidential annexe for my eyes only, written in your own voice, stating how confident you are in the recommendation overall and singling out the single point you are least sure of. Write the memo in British English.
