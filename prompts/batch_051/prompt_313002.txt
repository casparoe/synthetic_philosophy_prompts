Two friends in my reading group got into a long argument about a specific passage in the 1922 abridged edition of Frazer's The Golden Bough. We were reading the part where he describes scapegoat rituals: he says, roughly, that in various cultures the guilt, disease, or misfortune of a community is treated as if it were a substance that can be transferred to a person or animal, and then expelled or destroyed along with the carrier. He emphasizes the mechanism of transfer over questions of blame or justice.

Maya thought this was the most important passage in the chapter. She said the value of Frazer's idea is that it shows how a group can convert diffused anxiety into a concrete target and get relief from simply removing the target, even though the underlying problem hasn't been addressed. She called it "ritual as sanitation."

Jordan pushed back. He said Frazer's "transfer" language is doing a lot of work and actually hides what's happening: the scapegoat is nearly always someone already marginal or despised, and the ritual isn't really moving guilt anywhere; it's an organized act of violence against a person the community has already decided is expendable. So Frazer makes it sound like a neutral mechanism when it's really a moral decision about who can be hurt.

Maya replied that Jordan's point is compatible with Frazer's: the reason the victim is marginal is precisely because you need someone who can be expelled without breaking the community apart. The transfer mechanism explains the selection criteria. She said Jordan was criticizing the moral content, but Frazer wasn't trying to justify the practice; he was trying to identify the conceptual logic that makes it feel effective.

Jordan said that's too clean. He said if the mechanism were just transfer, any vessel would do, but in the cases they'd read about the vessel is often a slave, a prisoner, an animal, or a person kept in degraded conditions at public expense. That means the ritual doesn't start with guilt and then find a vessel; it starts with a class of people already marked as worthless and uses the guilt language to legitimize attacking them. So Frazer's account isn't morally neutral—it's a description that makes scapegoating seem more rational than it is.

Maya came back and said that's exactly the point: the passage is valuable because it exposes the cognitive moves that allow groups to treat people as instruments of purification. She thinks Jordan is reading Frazer as if Frazer endorsed the rituals, when in fact the whole force of the passage is deflationary—it shows us that our own practices might work the same way.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through this exchange move by move, say who is right about what, and give an overall verdict? I'm especially interested in whether Frazer's "transfer" language is neutral as Maya says or already morally loaded as Jordan says.
