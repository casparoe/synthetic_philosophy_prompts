An article in a general-interest philosophy magazine summarises the problem of petitionary prayer roughly as follows. If God is omniscient, he already knows what you need before you ask; if he is perfectly good, he has already resolved to do whatever it would be best to do; and if he is immutable, nothing you say can move him to do something he was not already going to do. Petitionary prayer therefore looks idle: at most it changes the person praying, not what God does. The article concludes that the only serious defence is therapeutic — prayer is justified in much the way meditation is, because it calms and refocuses us — and that anyone who expects prayer to make a difference to God is committed to a naïve, bargaining picture of the divine.

I suspect this is too quick, but I am not sure exactly where. How fair is this as a summary of the problem and of the responses actually on offer? Please go through it claim by claim: say what a careful defender of a classical theist position would accept, where the article conflates distinct claims or offers a false dichotomy, and give a committed verdict on whether the concluding claim survives these corrections.
