I am the deputy prosecutor of a special tribunal set up after a civil war to try the deposed regime and its allied militias. Over the next two years my office will screen several thousand potential charges — mass expulsions, killings of detained civilians, sexual slavery, cross-border raids into a neighbouring state — and in a large subset the conduct was not clearly criminalized under the domestic code in force at the time, or was arguably authorized by the regime's own decrees. I want a reusable decision procedure, in the form of an ordered checklist or set of tests, for deciding when my office may responsibly charge conduct that was not plainly a crime under the applicable law at the time of the acts. The procedure must be short enough to fit in a field manual, usable by junior lawyers with only basic training in public international law, and reviewable by our pre-trial judges. It should draw on the legal and moral debates surrounding the post-Second World War tribunals at Nuremberg and Tokyo: the principle of legality, _nullum crimen sine lege_, set against the conviction that some acts are so wrongful that declining to prosecute them is the greater injustice, and the persistent objection, pressed in the dissents at Tokyo, that such tribunals amount to victors' justice — punishing the defeated under standards the victors would not apply to themselves. I want the procedure to force us to mark explicitly every point at which a legal test bottoms out in a moral or political judgment, so that no such judgment can be passed off as a neutral step in the checklist.

The procedure must keep distinct at least the following: (1) applying an already-existing international criminal norm to new facts, which is compatible with legality, versus creating a prohibition after the fact, which is not; (2) retroactive substantive criminal law versus the retroactive establishment of a court to apply pre-existing law; (3) fair notice to the accused versus the gravity, scale, or systematic character of the harm; and (4) the question whether we would bring the same charge against our own side's forces for the same conduct. It should also tell us what to do when the pre-existing norm was still crystallizing at the time of the acts, when conduct was lawful under the regime's own decrees but contravened a broadly recognized customary prohibition, and when the legally sound result is an acquittal that the new government and the public will find intolerable.

Now apply the procedure to three cases. Case A: a campaign of mass expulsion of an ethnic minority organized by the defeated regime's interior ministry, not clearly criminal under the old domestic penal code, but argued to fall within the customary prohibition on crimes against humanity as it stood at the time. Case B: the deposed leadership's cross-border attacks on a neighbouring state, which the new government wants charged as crimes against peace on the Nuremberg model, even though the individual criminality of aggressive war was contested in the relevant period. Case C: a commander on the victorious side is accused of the same class of expulsions as in Case A. For each case, say what the procedure counsels and identify the step at which the conclusion will be hardest for my office to accept. End by stating what the procedure cannot settle: which residual decisions remain irreducibly moral or political, and where the checklist is most likely to be gamed into a rubber stamp.
