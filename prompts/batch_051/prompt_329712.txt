(1) The Grice-and-Strawson-ish reply: we can all sort the clear cases in practice — 'bachelors are unmarried' on one side, 'bachelors are bad at dancing' on the other — even if nobody can define 'analytic' in more basic terms, so demanding a non-circular definition before you're allowed to use the distinction is holding it to an absurd standard.

(2) The self-undermining reply: Quine's own paper has to help itself to notions like synonymy and sameness of meaning in order to even state which statements are supposed to be analytic, so if those are all suspect he can't get the attack off the ground without borrowing the thing he's trying to dissolve.

(3) The behaviorist-assumption reply: Quine seems to take for granted that meaning-talk is only as good as the behavioral and dispositional evidence behind it, but he never really argues for that in the paper; if you allow irreducibly semantic notions, then failing to define analyticity in his terms is less a refutation than a change of subject.

(4) The meaning-change reply: Quine's line that you can hold any sentence true if you're willing to revise enough elsewhere works only if you quietly treat changing what your words mean as changing your mind; but that distinction between meaning-change and belief-change is basically the analytic-synthetic distinction, so the argument ends up using it instead of eliminating it.
