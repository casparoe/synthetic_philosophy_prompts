The competitiveness constraint, as I have seen it argued on the forums I usually read, runs roughly as follows. In a field of many comparable labs selling substitutable models, alignment work that does not improve capabilities is a pure cost: the safety-conscious lab becomes less competitive, its rivals eventually outcompete it, and the residual "alignment tax" is pushed towards zero. Safety spending therefore has to be made capability-relevant, or at least kept cheap. The argument rests on particular assumptions about the market: many price-taking competitors, capabilities that are easy to compare, and customers who reward capability and little else. The frontier no longer looks like that. A handful of labs hold large compute advantages; their models are not close substitutes; and the work that gets filed under "alignment" — red-teaming, refusal tuning, scalable oversight, evaluation-driven post-training — has become entangled with what makes a model usable, saleable, and acceptable to enterprise customers, insurers, and regulators. I want to know whether the argument survives this changed environment, not what policy should follow. Which premise is actually carrying the weight? Does the change undercut it, or can the argument be repaired into a narrower claim that still does real labour? If the alignment tax has not vanished but has turned into something else — a compliance cost, a moat, a reputational asset — say so, and say what that does to the original conclusion. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write in British English.
