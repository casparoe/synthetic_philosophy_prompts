I'm a software engineer and ended up as the de facto tech lead for a team that keeps getting itself into multi-quarter death marches. We have a go/no-go review every two weeks on a couple of struggling projects, and I keep noticing the same pattern: someone says "we've already spent six engineer-months on this, we can't just throw that away," and then we greenlight another two weeks. Awhile ago I read about the dollar auction - the game where an auctioneer sells a dollar bill and the twist is that the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid without getting anything, so once two people start bidding they each keep bidding past a dollar because quitting means taking a certain loss while one more increment looks like the only way to avoid losing. Our planning meetings feel exactly like that, except the currency is engineer-weeks and credibility in front of the VP. I also vaguely remember that "sunk cost fallacy" may be too simple a label for what actually goes wrong - something about how quitting means accepting a definite, publicly visible loss while doubling down preserves the chance of not losing face, and maybe sometimes doubling down is even rational because of reputation or genuinely useful information from the next milestone.

I want something I can actually use, not a philosophy essay. Could you turn whatever the dollar auction and the escalation-of-commitment stuff actually show into a procedure I can run in the 45 minutes before one of these reviews? It should be a checklist or decision flow in words, grounded in the structure of the game rather than the usual "just ignore sunk costs" platitude. My teammates have no background in any of this and will roll their eyes at jargon, so the steps need to be plain-spoken, and the VP wants to know why we killed or kept something, so each step needs a one-sentence rationale. Please build in the hard cases: when continuing actually is the right call because the next increment buys real information, or because killing the project would have its own costs (reputation with a customer, losing an engineer who cares about it, the team reading it as "we punish people who take risks"). Also be honest about where the procedure stops being a procedure and a human has to make a judgement call. Then walk it through two or three worked examples, including at least one where the procedure tells me to kill a project I'm personally attached to, and one where it says to keep funding a project I'd otherwise have cut just because it looks ugly. If there are places where you're not sure the research actually supports a step, just say so rather than making the checklist overcertain.
