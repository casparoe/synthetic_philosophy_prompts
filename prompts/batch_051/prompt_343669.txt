ok so i fell down a wikipedia hole about aumann's agreement theorem and now i can't decide what to do with it, because it seems to prove something my brain refuses to accept.

the theorem as i understand it: suppose two people start with the exact same prior over all the possibilities, and both are strict bayesians who update only by conditioning on whatever private info they get. then if it becomes common knowledge between them (they both know the numbers, know the other knows, and so on) that one person is 70% confident in some claim and the other is 40%, that situation is actually impossible — the two numbers have to be equal. so two fully rational people with a shared prior can't have a known disagreement.

i find that setup really attractive. it gives a clean precise story for where disagreements could come from, and whenever real people don't converge, you can always say some condition was violated. but i also have this firm intuition that two reasonable smart people can look at a ton of the same public evidence and still end up at different levels of confidence about, like, whether there's a god or whether a recession is coming, and they can know the other person disagrees, respect them, and still not change their mind. that feels like real life, and it also feels too convinient to always tell those people they must have started from different priors or must be updating badly.

so i'm stuck. which one should give way? should i treat the intuition as a fact about sloppy humans that doesn't touch the theorem about ideal agents, should i reject the theorem or say it secretly needs an extra condition, or are the two actually compatible and i'm just running together different senses of disagreement or knowledge? i specifically want to know what would justify each of those moves, not just which side you'd pick.

also, please separate what's actually settled about this from what's genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't present one contested reading of the theorem as the consensus.
