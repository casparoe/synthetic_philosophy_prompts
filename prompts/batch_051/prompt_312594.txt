What is it for a physical object to be complex? I want a working definition that counts a functioning bacterial cell and a mechanical wristwatch as complex, counts a single helium atom and a flawless block of ice as not complex, and gives a definite answer, one way or the other, for each of the following: a snowflake; a long polymer whose monomer sequence is random; a large flawless quartz crystal; a shuffled deck of cards; a virus particle; the same wristwatch after it has been completely disassembled into a heap of parts, where the heap itself is what gets judged; and a termite mound.

State the definition first, then run it through every case in the list. Where the definition delivers a verdict you are unwilling to accept, say so explicitly and revise the definition rather than adjusting it silently to fit your intuitions.

One issue I want kept in view. A purely informational approach would measure complexity by incompressibility, which seems to make a random polymer sequence about as complex as anything could be. A construction-based approach, of the kind associated with assembly theory, would instead measure complexity by something like the minimal number of recursive assembly steps needed to build the object from elementary parts, where joining already constructed parts counts as one step; on this sort of view, a high step-count combined with many independent copies of the object is the signature of a selective, non-chance construction history. I am not asking you to settle what assembly theory itself says. I am asking your definition to make sense of the apparent tension between being hard to describe and being hard to build, and to say whether those are two windows onto one property or two different properties sharing a word. A bonus would be an explanation of why the ordinary term 'complex' so easily runs together order, difficulty of construction, and function, and of where those features come apart among the cases above.
