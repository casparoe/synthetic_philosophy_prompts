I'm in an upper-level aesthetics seminar this semester called something like Fakes, Forgeries, and Why Originals Matter, and I'm writing my final paper for it. The longer we go, the less I trust my own use of the word "forgery." I can point to clear examples, but I can't state what makes them forgeries in a way that doesn't fall apart on the weird cases, and it's been loosing me sleep because my whole paper depends on it. So I'd like a working definition of a forged artwork: not a lit review, just the definition itself, applied to the cases below. If your definition needs to distinguish between an object being a forgery and an act of forgery, that's fine — I mainly want to see how that distinction does work in the hard cases.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

1. A newly painted canvas in a convincing 17th-century Dutch style, artificially aged, signed with a made-up "Vermeer" signature and date, and sold with a fake provenance as a rediscovered Vermeer. I take this to be a paradigmatic forgery.

2. A canvas print of The Starry Night from the museum gift shop, clearly labeled "reproduction," sold to tourists. I take this to clearly not be a forgery.

3. A student's extremely careful copy of a de Kooning, made years ago as a technical exercise with "after de Kooning" written on the back. A dealer later removes that inscription, invents a story about it being an unrecorded work, and sells it as a lost de Kooning. Is the painting itself a forgery, or is the forgery something the dealer does to it?

4. A contemporary artist paints an original composition in another living artist's very recognizable style, signs that other artist's name, and presents it as a one-off conceptual piece about "passing" in the art world. The gallery label and press release explicitly say it is not by the named artist. Forgery or not?

5. A restorer repaints around 80 percent of the surface of a badly damaged Rembrandt self-portrait, and the museum hangs it as "Rembrandt, restored." Most of what you now see was painted in the last few decades. Is the thing on the wall a forgery, an original, or does it need a third term?

6. An ancient Roman marble copy of a lost Greek bronze, displayed honestly as "Roman copy after Polykleitos." I don't think it's a forgery, but I want the definition to say why that judgment isn't just me deferring to the museum label.

After you run the definition through all six, tell me which verdict you'd be least comfortable defending, since that's probably where the definition is weakest and where I should spend my revision time.
