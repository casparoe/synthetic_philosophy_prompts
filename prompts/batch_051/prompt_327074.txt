For the past two years I have helped run a small open-source research account that documents state-linked propaganda by collecting and dissecting the media those networks circulate. The platform we post on has had the following clause in its manipulated-media policy since 2017:

"Users may not upload, share, or post media that has been edited, synthesized, or otherwise manipulated in a way that misrepresents the words, actions, or events it depicts. This prohibition applies whether or not the user adds commentary, context, or a disclaimer, and whether or not the user created the manipulated media."

Recently I posted a thread in which the first item was a low-tech manipulated clip from a coordinated propaganda network — a candidate made to appear to say something they never said, by splicing and mistranslating a caption — reposted without alteration, followed by annotated stills identifying the splice, the audio edit, and the caption error. A banner across the first frame read "manipulated; shown for analysis." The post was removed and my account warned; the moderator's message quoted only the first sentence back to me.

I am less interested in the practical appeal than in what the rule, properly interpreted, actually requires. Walk through what the textual meaning, the evident purpose of the clause, what its drafters would have said if asked in 2017 about a frame-by-frame debunk, and what a reasonable reader of the policy would each conclude about my thread. Are these readings in conflict here, and which should control, and why? I would also like you to address a complication: a much larger account later quoted my thread in a screenshot that cropped out the annotation banner and all the subsequent stills, and the screenshot circulated as if the original clip were real. At what point in a chain of resharing, if any, does a reproduction made for critique become the very reproduction the clause was written to stop, and should that downstream risk alter how the clause applies to my original post?
