I've got a policy when I read philosophy: a thought experiment can only ever illustrate a view, never argue for it. When one is meant to change my mind, I only take the intuition it pumps seriously if either (a) I already had that reaction before I could see which conclusion I was being steered toward, or (b) I can cash it out into a non-story argument from principles I already accept. And the more vivid the scenario—the more I can picture faces, lighting, the whole scene—the less I trust it, because it feels like the scene is doing the work while the argument coasts. Last week a friend argued with a case about two nurses who both gave a fatal dose of the same drug; one had skimmed a warning label, the other had been handed a misprinted chart. I had a strong feeling the first nurse was more blameworthy. But I noticed I could picture it too well—the crinkled label, the exhausted morning shift—so by my own rule I threw the intuition out entirely, even though I couldn't yet say whether it was wrong. My friend says my rule is a machine for ignoring any counterexample with good production values, and that an intuition's being vivid tells you nothing about whether it's tracking something real. Is my rule defensible? If not, is there a version that keeps what's good in it—some protection against being pushed around by a good story—without turning into a way of never updating on cases I don't like?
