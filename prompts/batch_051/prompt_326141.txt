I'm drafting a paper for an epistemology workshop on the foundations of accuracy-first epistemology, and I'd welcome comments on the argument as I've worked it out so far. My aim is to challenge the assumption that the central epistemic norm is to have a credence function that maximizes expected accuracy as judged by the agent's own current credence function, where expected accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule. I suspect this approach smuggles a kind of doxastic self-trust into the very structure of rational requirements: the agent's current perspective is treated as the authoritative base for evaluating how well her possible future states accord with the world, even in cases where the agent has higher-order evidence that her current perspective is unreliable. I want to argue that the rational requirement should instead be stated at the level of an evidence-relation model the agent accepts, not at the level of the agent's current probabilities, and that these can come apart in systematic ways.

Here are my rough notes. Strictly proper scoring rules give an agent reason to report her true credences, but only if she treats those credences as unbiased estimators of the truth. If evidence about her own unreliability should lower her confidence, then a rule that says to minimize expected inaccuracy relative to her current probabilities may forbid exactly the kind of self-correction the evidence calls for. I suspect the accuracy-first program has been conflating two distinct demands: a norm of alignment with evidence, and a norm of global accuracy over a partition. My hunch is that the second norm is parasitic on the first, and that once we separate them, the real epistemic work is being done by a prior commitment to evidence-alignment rather than by accuracy maximization alone.
