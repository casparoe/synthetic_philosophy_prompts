I began sitting with a small Zen group this year after a long stretch of burnout, and the teacher keeps returning to Dōgen's notion of being-time, uji. The claim—that time is not a container in which beings happen, that each being is time and the now is not a point passing through an empty medium—has stopped sounding like a metaphysical koan and started sounding like a description of how I mislive most of my days.

I also help run a philosophy-and-film group of about ten people, and next month we are watching Groundhog Day, a film everyone in the group knows well enough to recall scene by scene. I would like a serious reading of the film through uji, not a vague lesson about being present. Track Phil's early treatment of the repeated February 2 as a neutral, repeatable container he can exploit for later outcomes, and where the film turns toward his acting within the repeated day as if each iteration carries the whole of time. But stay honest in both directions: where does the film genuinely dramatize something close to Dōgen, and where does it instead depend on a picture of moral progress and accumulating character change that uji would resist? Anchor the reading in specific scenes and structural turns rather than in paraphrase of the philosophy, and please don't rely on quotations or outside sources, since I want the interpretation to stand on our shared recollection of the film itself. End by saying what the film, at its best, makes a viewer feel that the philosophical view alone cannot.
