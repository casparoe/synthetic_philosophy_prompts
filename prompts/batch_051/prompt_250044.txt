A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether a plan to clear a few acres of scrubland for a housing development is a tragedy because of the animals it would displace, and we both treated the harm to those individual animals as among the strongest reasons against it. On the way home I noticed an asymmetry in how we, and most conservation-minded people, speak. When a habitat is destroyed, we count the individual animals harmed; when a habitat is left intact, we almost never count the individual animals who starve, sicken, or are eaten alive within it, nor the many further generations an intact habitat will produce only for more of the same. The same people who call habitat destruction cruel because of what it does to wild animals call habitat preservation compassionate, without asking whether a wild animal's life is, on balance, good for that animal. I want the strongest principle you can find that justifies counting the suffering in the first case while permitting us to ignore it in the second; test it against nearby cases, and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, including whether the failure of that principle would leave us indifferent to, or even in favor of, habitat destruction. Write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of an email or a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
