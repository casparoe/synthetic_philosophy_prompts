My friend is an engineer working on an agent that proves things about its own decision procedure, and a late-night reading binge on Löb's theorem has him convinced the whole project is dead. The agent is supposed to trust its future selves: if the current version can prove that a later version will prove some claim, the current version is allowed to adopt that claim and stop rechecking. He wrote me the chain below and says he can't find the bad step:

(1) A consistent system rich enough to talk about its own proofs cannot prove its own soundness — it can't prove, in general, that whatever it proves is true (the Löbian obstacle).
(2) An agent that cannot prove its own soundness has no right to trust its future reasoning.
(3) Any agent worth deploying has to trust its future reasoning at least locally; otherwise it would second-guess every inference forever and never act.
(4) So no self-checking formal agent of this kind can be a rational agent.
(5) Humans trust their own future reasoning all the time — we finish a proof and move on — and we don't think that makes us irrational.
(6) So either the theorem is being misapplied, or human self-trust is secretly irrational, or one of (1)-(4) has to go.

He says the only step he's unwilling to lose is (5); but when he tries to drop any of (1)-(4), he finds he can't say what the resulting system is actually entitled to trust. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compulsory, and what giving it up costs, both for his formal agent and for the human case.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
