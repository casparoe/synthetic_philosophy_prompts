I have been working with a principle in the ethics of panpsychism that I call compositional non-duplication, and I would like an honest assessment of it. As I understand constitutive panpsychism, it holds that the microphysical constituents of a normally functioning human brain—on some versions, the fundamental physical particles themselves—are subjects of experience, and that those micro-subjects constitute the larger, unified subject that I am. My principle is addressed to anyone who accepts that picture. It says: when an experience is genuinely integrated into a higher-level subject, the well-being it constitutes should be attributed to that higher-level subject for purposes of moral aggregation; one may not add both the higher-level subject's pain and the corresponding pains of its constituent micro-subjects to the same ledger, because that would count the same experiential facts twice. Lower-level states may be counted only when they are not integrated into any higher-level subject.

So much for the definition; now for the assessment I am seeking. First, does the principle do any real work, or does it simply assert that the combination problem has been solved? To know which lower-level states are already included in a higher-level subject, it seems I need an account of how micro-experiences compose a unified macro-experience rather than merely co-existing with it; without that account, integration is a placeholder. Second, how should the principle handle partial or fluctuating integration: a split-brain subject whose hemispheres are sometimes unified and sometimes not, a person in a dissociative state, or micro-structures that seem to produce suffering the larger subject cannot access? Third, is the pressure I am responding to genuine? I want to avoid two results: a moral explosion, in which trillions of micro-subjects swell the total harm of a single ordinary pain, and a moral deflation, in which a person's suffering is treated as nothing over and above the summed pains of their parts. Is there a way to avoid both without a principle of roughly this shape, or have I misdescribed the alternatives? Finally, I would be glad to know whether I have reinvented a position that already exists in recognizable form; but my primary interest is in whether the principle is defensible on the merits.
