I'm trying to nail down what I actually think about confirmation, specifically the raven paradox, by going with my gut on a bunch of cases. I want to know what principle, if any, captures all of these, and which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.

Here are the verdicts:

1. A black raven lands outside my window. I think this confirms the generalization that all ravens are black, but only a little.

2. I notice my sneakers are white. I don't think this confirms the generalization that all ravens are black at all. But here's the rub: I'm happy to say that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to all non-black things are non-ravens, and my sneakers are a non-black non-raven. So I seem to be treating two equivelant statements differently. That bugs me, but I still want to say the sneaker is just irrelevant.

3. Over a year, a research team catches and photographs 400 ravens across six continents, and every one is black. I take this as strong confirmation — way more than 400 times case 1.

4. The same team carefully catalogs 400 non-black objects around their lab (mugs, a green towel, shoes, a yellow bike helmet), and none is a raven. I don't think that confirms all ravens are black at all; if it does, it's so little I cant tell it apart from zero, and I certainly don't feel like case 4 is the same kind of boost as case 3.

5. Now the case that really gets me. I'm told a covered cage holds one bird, and it's either a raven or a white swan. Before I look, the possibility that it's a non-black raven is live, so looking seems like a genuine test of all ravens are black. I lift the cloth and see a white swan sitting there — a non-black non-raven. And I do feel like this confirms all ravens are black a bit, because a spot where a counterexample could have shown up turned out not to have one. But it's the same kind of object as the sneaker in case 2 (non-black, non-raven), and now I'm giving it a completely different status.

6. Last one. I read that 10,000 ravens have been logged over the centuries and all are black, but the records come from observers who would only have logged an animal as a raven if it looked black and would have classed a somehow non-black raven as a different bird. I want to say this is almost worthless as confirmation, even though the sample is huge, because the data couldn't have shown a counterexample even if one existed.

My tentative rule is something like: an observation confirms a generalization only when the observation was part of a procedure where a counterexample could have shown up — a live test. That rule seems to make case 2 and case 5 come apart, which I like, and it makes case 6 worthless, which I also like. What I can't figure out is how that rule handles the logical equivalence point from case 2, and whether it's even coherent to say that confirmation attaches to the way the observation came about rather than to the observation itself.

So: is there one principle of confirmation that gets all six verdicts, or close? If not, which verdicts have to go, and why? State the resulting principle as precisely as you can, and tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed: someone sets out to find a non-black raven, searches for years, finds none, and reports only that they found none — does the absence of a sighting confirm all ravens are black, and how much does it matter whether they'd have been believed if they had found one?
