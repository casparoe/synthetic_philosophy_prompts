Here's an argument I've been chewing on about whether "is 2 an element of 4?" even has an answer. I keep going back and forth on whether the problem is step 2 or step 4—or whether the whole thing is basically right and I should just live with the conclusion.

Background I'm assuming: on the von Neumann way of coding the naturals, 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4 comes out true. On the Zermelo way, 4 = {{{{∅}}}} (a one-membered set whose only member is 3), so 2 ∈ 4 comes out false. Both codings satisfy all the arithmetic; they only disagree about set-membership facts. The structuralist moral people draw is that "is 2 an element of 4?" isn't a real question about the numbers.

Here's the argument spelled out:

(1) According to structuralism, a number like 2 or 4 is just a position in the natural-number structure, individuated entirely by its structural relations to the other positions (successor, addition, multiplication, and the like).

(2) If a question about a number can't be settled by those structural relations, then structuralism says there's no fact of the matter: it isn't a geniune question about the number.

(3) Whether 2 is an element of 4 can't be settled by the structural relations, because set-membership isn't one of them. (The two codings above agree on all of arithmetic and still disagree about this.)

(4) So structuralism implies that "2 is an element of 4" is neither true nor false.

(5) But "2 is an element of 4" is a perfectly well-formed sentence about two objects that exist, and for any such sentence bivalence holds: it is either true or false.

(6) So structuralism is false.

I suspect (5) is where a classicist will dig in, but I also worry that (2) is doing something sneaky and that (4) overstates what structuralists actually say. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails first, and under exactly what reading? If it is valid, is rejecting bivalence for sentences like this really such a bad bullet to bite? End your answer with \answer{valid} or \answer{step N} for the first step that fails.
