Two people in my probability study group got into an argument I couldn't settle. Nina said if an event has probability zero, then it can't happen — that's the whole point of assigning probabilities in the first place. Marcus said that can't be right: throw a dart at a dartboard and it lands at some exact point, but every individual point has probability zero, so a probability-zero event not only can happen, it happens every time you throw. Nina replied that the dartboard is an idealization — you never actually measure an exact point, so all you know is that it landed in some tiny region with positive probability, and the zero-probability point is an artifact of the model. Marcus said that's dodging the real question: in the standard model each exact point is an outcome, so if she refuses to count any point as possible, the model treats every actual throw as impossible, which is absurd. Nina then said fine, but that just shows possibility is a separate notion from probability: probability zero doesn't settle what's possible, and probability one doesn't mean "must happen," which is why probabilists say "almost surely" instead of "surely." Marcus said that proves his point — the almost surely/surely distinction only exists because zero-probability events can happen. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific claim, and are they actually disagreeing about anything by the end?
