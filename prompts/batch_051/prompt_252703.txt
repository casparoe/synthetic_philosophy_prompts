My flatmate keeps pushing an argument about subsidiarity and I can't find the hole in it. He works on EU stuff, so his examples are Brussels and the member states, but he says the same logic covers Washington and the states or Berlin and the Länder. (1) The whole point of subsidiarity is that a higher level of government should act only when the lower level can't adequately handle the problem itself. (2) Whether the lower level can handle it adequately is a judgment call, so somebody has to decide it. (3) If the higher level decides, it has every incentive to find the lower level inadequate whenever it wants to act, and nothing stops it, since it's judging the limits of its own power. (4) If the lower level decides, it has every incentive to find itself adequate, and nothing stops it either. (5) If an independent body decides, then the real power to allocate competences has moved to that body, which is just a new higher level, so we're back at (3). (6) So subsidiarity can't actually allocate anything: it either lets the center do what it wants or hands the subunits a permanent veto. He says the same structure applies to any constitutional limit on any power, which makes me suspect it proves too much. I don't think (6) can be right, but I'd like to keep (1), because otherwise the princple is just a slogan. Which step should I reject, and what do I give up if I reject it?
