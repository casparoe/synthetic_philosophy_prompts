I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people talk about knowing, and it's bugging me because the whole "eliciting latent knowledge" (ELK) research program seems to ride on it. We'll happily say a student "knew the material" when she blanks on an exam and writes nothing — the knowledge was in there, it just didn't come out. But when a language model gives a wrong answer and someone then trains a linear probe (a little classifier that reads off the model's internal activations) that decodes the correct answer, most ML people I know refuse to say the model "knew" it and just failed to say it. They'll say the model contained the information, or that the probe is doing the knowing, or that the activations merely correlate with the right answer. So the same sort of unreportable knowledge counts as knowledge for a human and as mere "features" for a machine. Is there a principle that actually justifies that asymmetry, or is it just folk Cartesianism about humans colliding with folk behaviorism about machines? I want the strongest defense you can build and a honest verdict on whether it holds up. Test it especially against the case where the probe decodes the right answer perfectly across held-out contexts while the model, left to itself, never says it once. Then tell me whether ELK's core assumption — that a model can really know things it doesn't yet report — survives your verdict, and say what would change your mind or where your reasoning is most likely to break.
