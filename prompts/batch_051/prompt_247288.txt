A reading group I'm in has spent two years arguing about Malthus, and I suspect a lot of the fighting is the two sides talking past each other. The two camps are roughly these. The Structuralist thinks Malthus's core mechanism is right but early: populations press up against whatever the current floor of subsistence is, living standards get pushed down unless the floor gets lifted, and the fact that this didn't happen globally after 1800 doesn't refute the mechanism because the floor was lifted—by fossil energy, the Haber–Bosch process, the Green Revolution, trade—rather than removed. The Falsificationist thinks "right but early" is exactly the kind of auxiliary-hypothesis rescue that should make us distrust the whole apparatus; a view that keeps getting saved whenever its predictions fail, by relocating the coming constraint from land to soil to phosphorus to the atmosphere, is not making predictions at all.

They've finally agreed to co-author an adversarial collaboration statement, and I want you to draft it. Don't write it as an essay about them or a debate; write the actual document they would sign.

The document should have three parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. Make these genuinely non-vacuous—not just "population and resources interact." Include what each side now concedes about post-1800 history, about what Malthus himself got right and wrong in the original Essay, and about finitude. If either side is making a concession it cost them something to give, the wording should show that.

2. A disagreements table. Rows should be the real remaining disagreements, with columns for: the issue, the Structuralist's position stated in wording the Structuralist would accept, the Falsificationist's position stated in wording the Falsificationist would accept, and a note on whether the row is substantive or at least partly verbal. Flag any rows where you think the two sides have been using the same word, probably "scarcity," in different senses.

3. A cruxes section. Each crux should be a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them. For each crux, include a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance.

I suspect two of the cruxes are (a) whether the nineteenth-century European fertility decline was itself a Malthusian preventive check—a negative feedback where rising incomes lowered fertility—or an independent institutional and cultural shift that broke the mechanism from outside; and (b) whether the Simon–Ehrlich bet on whether a basket of five metals would rise in inflation-adjusted price over the 1980s is a fair test of the Structuralist's view or a straw man, given that the prices fell. Include both of those, and add one or two more of your own—soil loss, peak phosphorus, or the limits-to-growth simulations are the obvious places to look.

Keep the tone like something two stubborn people would actually co-sign after a long and slightly grudging negotiation, not a victory statement. Don't cite literature or ask the reader to go look things up; the whole point is to write a document both of them can sign on the spot. And be careful with the disagreement wording: each side should read there own formulation and think yes, that's what I actually believe.
