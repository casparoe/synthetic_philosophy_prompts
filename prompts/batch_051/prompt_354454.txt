I am in a modern world history course this year, and our teacher keeps returning to the claim that, over the long run, history tends toward justice. What bothers me is that my classmates seem to apply two different standards to the evidence. When a society abolishes slavery, extends the vote, or writes human rights into law, everyone treats the event as a window into history's real direction — as if it reveals the underlying trend. But when a society falls into civil war, genocide, or a generation of tyranny, the same people describe it as a detour, an exception, or noise that does not really count against the trend. In effect, improvements are treated as data and horrors are treated as something to be explained away.

Why should the optimist be allowed to count the hits and ignore the misses? I can imagine a defense: perhaps moral gains tend to stick because they create institutions, expectations, and beneficiaries who will defend them, while atrocities are unstable and eventually corrected, so the asymmetry tracks a real difference in how durable the two kinds of events are. But I can also imagine that this is just a bias in how we narrate the past — we choose a story of progress and then file events accordingly, and people living through a catastrophe often felt that it was permanent. Please construct the strongest case that the asymmetry is justified, test it honestly against cases where a regression looked permanent to the people inside it, and tell me whether you would keep the belief that history is, on the whole, moving toward justice, revise it, or give it up.
