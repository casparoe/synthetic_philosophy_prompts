I've been keeping a record of a colleague's rulings about conversational moves. She thinks of a conversation as a scorekeeping game: at each stage there is a scoreboard, and one component of the scoreboard is the set of propositions the participants are treating as common ground. An utterance typically presupposes certain propositions, and she treats a move as acceptable only if its presuppositions are already on the scoreboard or can be quietly added to the scoreboard as the move is made — she calls the latter accommodation. I'm trying to reverse-engineer the rule behind her rulings.

Her rulings on six cases:

1. The scoreboard contains the proposition that France is a republic. A speaker says, 'The king of France is bald.' Ruling: unacceptable.

2. The scoreboard contains nothing about John's family. A speaker says, 'John's sister is a doctor.' Ruling: acceptable; the proposition that John has a sister is accommodated.

3. The scoreboard contains nothing about Sam's habits. A speaker says, 'Sam stopped smoking.' Ruling: acceptable; the proposition that Sam used to smoke is accommodated.

4. The scoreboard contains nothing about how the speaker voted, though the conversation has been about the recent election. The speaker says, 'I regret voting for her.' Ruling: acceptable; the proposition that the speaker voted for her is accommodated.

5. The scoreboard contains the proposition that B has never had so much as a parking ticket. A, addressing B, says, 'When did you stop speeding?' Ruling: unacceptable, even though the scoreboard does not strictly contradict the presupposition that B used to speed.

6. The scoreboard contains nothing about B's family. A, addressing B, says, 'I was sorry to hear your son didn't get a scholarship.' Ruling: unacceptable.

Now consider a seventh case. The scoreboard contains nothing about B's family. B does have a daughter and would be glad to have that fact acknowledged; he has simply not mentioned her in this conversation. A, addressing B, says, 'Did your daughter get a scholarship?'

What should I expect my colleague to say about the seventh case? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the analysis: the rule that best explains her first six rulings, the other rules that fit those six rulings equally well, what each candidate rule predicts about the seventh case, which rule you would bet on and why, and a further case that would cleanly separate any candidates that remain tied after the seventh case.
