I am a PhD student in legal philosophy. In a reading group I attend, two students got stuck on the absurdity doctrine. The text at issue was Blackstone's discussion of statutory interpretation in the Commentaries: he says, in substance, that when a statute's literal text would yield a result that no reasonable lawmaker could have intended — because it is internally inconsistent or at odds with the statute's evident purpose — a judge should depart from the ordinary meaning of the words rather than enforce the absurd result.

One participant, Hale, argued that this passage is the legitimate ancestor of the modern doctrine: a court may set aside the ordinary meaning of a provision when that meaning yields absurdity, because law is a rational order rather than a brittle verbal command. The other, Rania, replied that this makes the exception swallow the rule — once absurdity is a live category, judges will invoke it to displace any result they dislike, and the discipline of a written text is precisely the refusal of that licence. Hale came back with a distinction: Blackstone's absurdity is not mere bad policy; it is an internal incoherence, an incompatibility with the statute's own structure, or a result that cannot plausibly be imputed to a rational lawmaker. Rania answered that the distinction collapses under pressure, because what counts as incoherent depends on contested background assumptions, and courts now apply the absurdity canon in cases where the result is harsh but perfectly coherent.

Then give the best version of each side. Finally, say what you think a court should actually require before departing from the plain text, and whether Blackstone's passage, fairly read, supports either of them or something more modest.
