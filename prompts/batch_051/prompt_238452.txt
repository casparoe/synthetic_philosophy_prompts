I sit on my state bar's character and fitness committee, and we're reviewing an applicant who had a single serious blemish: as a college junior he paid someone to write a term paper for him, got caught, and was suspended for a semester. That was nine years ago, and since then there's been nothing—steady jobs, some volunteer work, a glowing reccomendation from his current employer. Our rule lets us deny admission on moral character grounds, and the live question is whether that one old incident tells us enough about his honesty to justify keeping him out of the profession. After yesterday's meeting I got into a long late-night argument with a friend who's a social psychologist, and her whole line was that I'm committing the fundamental attribution error: the situationist evidence says behavior is driven by circumstances and small features of the environment way more than by stable traits, so trying to read his character off one undergraduate act is exactly the kind of inference the data should make us suspicious of. She even said that if character and fitness requires that kind of inference, then maybe character and fitness is the thing we should be questioning, not the applicant. I didn't have a good comeback then and I've been turning it over since.

I need to circulate a decision memo before Thursday. Please write it. It should recommend a concrete outcome—admit him, deny him, or admit with conditions like probationary admission or mentoring under a supervising attorney—and it should genuinely contend with the strongest version of my friend's argument instead of just reasserting that character matters. The committee needs reasoning we can defend if he appeals, or if the bar comes asking questions. One formatting request: write it as continuous prose like an email or letter, so no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
