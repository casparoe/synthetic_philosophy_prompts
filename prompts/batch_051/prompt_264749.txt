I am a journalist writing a feature on the expansion of psychiatric diagnosis and the worry that psychiatry increasingly classifies, and thereby reshapes, ordinary distress. One clinician I interviewed dismissed this worry with an analogy: a map does not create the mountains it charts, and the diagnostic manual is only a map of psychological terrain that was already there. A philosopher I interviewed later objected that human classifications are importantly different, because the people classified know about the classification and adjust themselves in response to it — what Ian Hacking called looping effects — so the map may alter, and not merely record, the terrain.

Act as an expert philosopher of psychiatry and audit that map–territory analogy rigorously. Work through the question step by step, and begin with a correspondence table: what in the psychiatric case is supposed to correspond to the map, the cartographer, the mountains, the surveying, and the terrain's independence from the map. Then identify where the analogy breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the clinician's conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, give a verdict on whether the clinician's conclusion survives — namely, that expanding the diagnostic manual just records a pre-existing landscape and cannot itself influence how common a condition is or how those classified experience themselves — and, if the analogy fails, state what better analogy you would substitute and why.
