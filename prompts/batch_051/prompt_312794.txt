I keep running into accounts of reflective equilibrium that defend the method by pointing at one feature—revisability, or considered judgments, or wide coherence—and I'd like the full spec before I read any more. Write the desiderata that an account of reflective equilibrium as a method must meet in order to count as a method of moral/political justification, rather than a redescription of how people rationalize whatever they already think. For each requirement, give the case or failure it's guarding against: the person whose considered judgments are just inherited prejudice, the situation where principles and judgments both shift so conveniently that nothing was really at stake, the problem of knowing when you're done, and the need to seperate the entry conditions for a considered judgment (what gets a judgment into the pool at all) from the revision conditions (what it takes to throw one out). Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of particular camps.

Then flag any pairs of requirements that look jointly unsatisfiable, and say which single desideratum does the most to rule out the usual readings—the coherence-as-mere-consistency one, the wide-equilibrium one that pulls in everything from sociology to metaphysics, and the "it just describes what we already do" one. Don't bother with a full scorecard of theories; I care more about the requirements themselves and where the tension is.
