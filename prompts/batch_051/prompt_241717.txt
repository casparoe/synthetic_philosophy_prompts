Fire investigators and insurance-fraud units routinely treat the timing of a claim as evidence of fraud. A house fire that breaks out days or weeks after a new policy is purchased, or shortly after coverage is sharply increased, is singled out as a red flag for arson-for-profit, and at trial the temporal coincidence may be presented to the jury as though the sheer unlikelihood of the event is itself suspicious. Yet a familiar statistical consideration seems to pull in the opposite direction. The birthday paradox shows that in a large set of opportunities, events that are extremely improbable for any particular individual are almost certain to happen to someone by pure chance. Across an insurer's portfolio of hundreds of thousands of policies, innocent policyholders will almost certainly have fires soon after purchase even in a world with no fraud. A related multiple-comparisons point compounds the problem: if one is free to notice many candidate patterns after the fact (the fire came after a coverage increase, after a divorce, after a business slump, after a threatening message), some pattern will look remarkable by selection alone.

What theory of coincidence, and of the evidential value of improbability, does this red-flag practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose the naive view that an event's improbability for a given policyholder is, by itself, strong evidence against chance, or is it compatible with the more careful view that the relevant comparison is between the rate of recent-policy timing among fraudulent fires and the rate among accidental fires, given that a fire has already occurred? Reconstruct the practice's implicit theory, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, say what would change if investigators and courts took the birthday-paradox and multiple-comparisons objections seriously: would the coincidence need to be treated as a reason to investigate rather than as a reason to infer guilt, would practitioners have to weigh the base rate of fraud among fires and the number of opportunities for coincidence, and under what conditions could the timing evidence remain genuinely probative?
