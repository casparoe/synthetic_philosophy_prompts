I made the mistake of telling my friend, who works in product design at a travel app, that I would rather have a pleasant, same-y life than a stressful, adventure-filled one, and she told me I had just described a nicely decorated waiting room. Her actual claim, once we got past the insults, is that novelty is not just an adaptation-reset button for pleasure but an ingredient in well-being on its own. Specifically, she thinks this is true for the remembering self rather than the experiencing self: sample people's moods across a week, and someone doing the same pleasant things each day might look exactly as happy moment-to-moment as someone doing new things, but at the end of the week the second person will judge the week as better, tell a better story about it, and be more willing to relive it. So novelty, she says, is a component of retrospective, evaluative well-being even when it adds nothing to felt affect.

I'm not sold. It seems at least as plausable that novelty only earns its keep by slowing hedonic adaptation, which would make it a maintenance cost for pleasure rather than a nutrient. Or that "new" is just a sloppy proxy for autonomy, challenge, or meaning, and once those are controlled for, novelty does no independent work. She also insists on a distinction between objective novelty (the activity is new in the world) and felt novelty (the person reports that the experience felt different from their usual), and claims only felt novelty matters; I don't know whether we should grant her that before the study even starts.

So how would you actually test her claim? Design a concrete study—conditions, sample, measures, timing, controls, and the exact pre-registered contrast.
