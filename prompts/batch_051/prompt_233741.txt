I've been going back and forth on the what-is-life question—not the popular stuff, the actual back-and-forth in astrobiology and philosophy of biology—and the thing that's stuck with me isn't any particular definition, it's the debate itself. As far as I can tell, everyone agrees on the facts about the usual edge cases: viruses, endospores, mules, worker bees, prions, synthetic cells with stripped-down genomes. And yet the dispute just doesn't move. The metabolic people and the genetic people talk past each other; someone points out that seeds and spores sit dormant for years without doing anything metabolic, so metabolism can't be necessary; someone else points out that crystals grow and replicate in a loose sense, so replication can't be sufficient; then everyone nods and the next paper proposes the same list with one more phrase bolted on. Meanwhile a third camp says life is a matter of degree and the whole crisp-boundary project is misguided, but then nobody can say degree of what. My working theory, for what its worth, is that the impasse isn't empirical. It's that the different camps want the word 'life' to do different jobs: one wants a real essence, one wants a detection criterion for looking for life elsewhere, one wants a line in the sand for practical or even policy purposes, and each camp grades every proposal by its own rubric without saying so. But I've also heard there's a genuine buried question underneath—something about the difference between a system that just repeats a pattern (fire, crystals) and one that can produce new kinds of patterns, and we don't currently have a crisp formal account of that. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not another definition or a survey of definitions. Is this a verbal dispute, people answering different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical or conceptual question, or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—or should we expect none?
