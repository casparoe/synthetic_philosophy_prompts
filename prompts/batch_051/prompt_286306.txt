You're a philosopher who works on the concept of rights — the old debate about whether rights are trumps, side-constraints, or just instruments for tracking interests — and you've also served on a licensing board, so you know what a mixed committee can actually follow when it's tired.

I chair the discipline committee of a state board of nursing. Every month we review complaints against nurses and decide whether to dismiss, require monitoring or remedial education, suspend, or revoke a license. The board's legal mandate is to protect the public. Over and over, a nurse or their counsel will say the nurse has a right to X — a right to refuse to participate in certain kinds of care on conscience grounds, a right to free expression in what they post online, a right to privacy in the impairment monitoring program, a due-process right not to be treated as dangerous on the basis of past conduct alone. The committee tends to either cave the moment it hears the word "right" or wave it off as a fancy way of saying "I want to."

I want a reusable procedure for the committee: something on two pages max that tells a non-philosopher how to decide wether a claimed right should constrain or block our public-protection reasoning in a given case, and when saying "right" is doing real work rather than just re-describing interests we could weigh anyway. In particular, should the right operate as a trump (you just can't do the thing, full stop), a side-constraint (you can do the thing only in some exceptional circumstance, and the burdon is on us to show it), or an instrument (it's a strong interest, weigh it)? And when is the concept of a right actually pulling weight — that is, when would the decision differ from a plain weighing of harms and benefits?

Think carefully before you write. Start by brainstorming the main ways philosophers have distinguished these roles for rights, then translate that into a checklist a committee member can apply without the theory. The committee includes non-lawyers and non-philosophers, so define any technical term in a clause. We decide cases at monthly meetings, often with fifty pages of record and maybe twenty minutes of discussion, and I sign every decision letter myself, so the result has to be defensible on appeal. The procedure should flag hard cases and mark each step that's a judgment call rather than a mechanical application of the checklist.

Apply the finished procedure to three cases we actually see: (1) a nurse refuses to participate in a reproductive-health procedure her hospital offers, citing conscience; (2) a nurse with a large social media following posts false and misleading claims about vaccines and becomes the subject of a complaint; (3) a nurse in our monitoring program for past opioid misuse asks us not to disclose that history to a new employer who has asked the board directly whether the nurse had ever been disciplined. Include at least one worked case where the procedure gives the committee an answer we won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
