I have spent enough time in rationalist forums to know that a claim which survives every possible observation is not much of a claim, but I also suspect that rule gets applied too crudely to long-run predictions. The case I keep returning to is Malthusian scarcity. The prediction that population would outrun the food supply did not fail outright on its original timetable; it was postponed, qualified, and reissued after each missed deadline, and its defenders now say it was right but early, not false. That move worries me because a prediction that is genuinely early and a prediction that has been immunized against refutation seem to make the same observable response to a failed deadline, namely not yet. I am not asking for a verdict on Malthus in particular, and I do not want a taxonomy of philosophy of science labels; I want the test. Is there any observation, measurement, or decision procedure that could distinguish a long-run prediction that is genuinely early from one that has been progressively rescued with ad hoc qualifications? Design the strongest test you can, say what result would count for and against treating a postponed prediction as early rather than unfalsifiable, and be explicit about what no test could settle. Write your answer in continuous prose, as in a letter or email; do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
