ok so i'm in high school and last night at dinner my cousin Lena (college junior, pre-law) and my dad got into it about a school board decision to keep unvaccinated kids out of school during a measles outbreak, and my gov teacher wants me to write a short reflection on the argument but i honestly can't tell who's right about what. Can you go through their actual back-and-forth move by move and adjudicate it?

Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Lena said the decision is a violation of parental rights. Parents have the right to decide what goes into their kid's body, and a right is exactly the kind of thing that can't be overriden just because the board ran the numbers and decided exclusion would produce better outcomes. If rights bend to a cost-benefit score, she said, they aren't rights.

(2) My dad said no right is absolute: if a parent refused a life-saving blood transfusion for their kid, the state would step in. And in this case two rights collide, the parent's right to make medical decisions and the other kids' right not to catch a preventable disease. When rights collide, you have no choice but to weigh them against each other.

(3) Lena said "weighing" rights is already giving up on the concept. If a right only wins when the overall balance favors it, then a right is just a strong interest with a fancier name. The whole point of calling something a right, she said, is that it wins even when the outcome math says violating it would produce better results. Otherwise we should be honest and just say "interest."

(4) My dad said that's too fast. A right doesn't have to be infinite to be real. Free speech has exceptions (incitement, for example) and nobody concludes free speech is meaningless. A right can function as a very high bar: the state needs a really serious reason to override it, like an actual outbreak, not just any marginal improvement.

(5) Lena came back that a high bar is still a sliding scale. You've just given rights a huge weight inside the same utility calculation, so utility is doing the real work and rights are decoration. She said if you actually want rights to be more than weights, you have to treat them as side constraints, things you can't do to a person even when doing it would make the outcome go better. Weighing assumes both options are in the same currency; a side constraint says the option on the table is the kind of thing you don't get to trade away.

(6) My dad said he doesn't buy "spooky trumps." The reason we act like some violations are off the table is that the people who would get to violate rights would abuse the power, and the cases where "it would work" usually only look that way because we're ignoring long-run risks and unintended effects. So rights are still instruments, just long-run and systemic rather than case-by-case.

Then give an overall verdict: who is more right, and what are both of them missing? And specifically: on my dad's view, does the word "rights" still do any real work, or is Lena right that we should just talk about strong interests and drop the concept?
