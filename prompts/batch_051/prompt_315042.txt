I've been noticing an asymmetry in how I treat the closing of the places where people actually gather, and I'm not sure it can be defended. The Catholic parish a few blocks from me closed last year and the diocese merged it with a bigger one. People in the neighborhood talked about it exactly the way you'd expect: the loss of a community, a place where you'd see the same faces every week, something a new parish could never replace. I found myself agreeing, even though I never went there. Then a few months later the chain coffee shop on the corner closed. The same five or six older men had sat there every morning for years; they talked more than anyone at my parish ever did. Nobody called it the loss of a community. The men themselves just shrugged and said they'd find another place. I also remember my old gym closing, and people who saw friends there daily posted about it once and moved on. So I'm left with this: I'm ready to treat the parish closing as a genuine loss of community, but I'd be embarrassed to say the same about the coffee shop or the gym, even though the day-to-day good looks identical—routine, recognition, faces you know, somewhere to be. Is there a principle that justifies this? What account of community as a good would make the parish count and the coffee shop not count, without just being snobbery about which institutions "really" count? And if no principle survives, which of my reactions should I give up: should I be mourning the coffee shop, or should I stop mourning the parish?
