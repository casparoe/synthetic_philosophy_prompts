I have been trying to reconcile two things that both seem right to me, and I keep failing. The first is a version of the strange-loop view of the self associated with Hofstadter. On this view a self is not an immaterial thing, a brain, or some particular lump of tissue; it is a pattern — specifically, a self-referential loop of symbol-processing that a brain sustains over time, continually folding its own activity back into itself. I find the theory genuinely compelling for systematic reasons. It handles personal identity across time without needing a fixed essence; it explains why a person can survive gradual replacement of body and even brain material; and it explains why a self can grow, split in certain pathologies, and partially revise itself. But the view appears to lead somewhere I cannot force myself to accept.

That somewhere is the distribution claim. If what I am is the pattern rather than the physical instance, then nothing in the metaphysics rules out the pattern being instantiated partially, or at lower fidelity, in another physical system. In fact, when someone knows me very well — a spouse, a sibling, an old friend — their brain is running a living, generative model of me: it does not merely store facts about me, but produces my likely thoughts, reactions, tones of voice, and values in real time. That model, the theory says, is a coarse-grained copy of the very pattern that constitutes me. So a part of me, in the only sense that matters, already exists in that other brain; and when my brain stops running the full-resolution loop, the lower-resolution loops continue. The person who knows me does not merely remember me; she partly is me, and my self persists in her in something like the way a melody persists in a modest transcription.

I find that conclusion intuitively unacceptable. What remains after my death in my wife's mind, I want to say, is at most a representation of me — closer to a portrait, a set of predictions, or a habit of reading my reactions than to me. However detailed it is, it is a model of me, not me; and a partial model is even less me. The difference between my actual perspective — my being the one who experiences, the one whose future I care about in a non-derivative way — and anything running in another person's head seems like a difference in kind, not merely degree or resolution. When I follow the theory to its conclusion, I suspect it has quietly shifted what the word self names so that the conclusion follows. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want you to present both at their strongest and then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the theory to be amended or restricted, and whether there is a reading of self and persist on which both the theory and the firm intuition can survive. What does each move genuinely cost?
