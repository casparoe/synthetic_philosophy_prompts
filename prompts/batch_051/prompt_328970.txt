In a research-ethics seminar we have been working through the standard moral justification for random assignment: Freedman's clinical-equipoise criterion. On that account, a randomised trial is permissible when the community of expert clinicians is genuinely uncertain about which arm is preferable, so that no participant is knowingly given a treatment the field regards as inferior, and the enrolling physician's obligation to act in the individual participant's interests is preserved by that communal uncertainty rather than by the physician's private hunch. The relevant uncertainty is located in the external expert community at the moment the trial opens, and the trial is an ethically constrained interruption of that community's disagreement.

I am trying to work out whether this argument survives the shift toward adaptive platform trials, the sort that have become prominent in oncology and in the response to the pandemic. These trials persist indefinitely within a disease area, governed by a master protocol under a standing committee; arms are added and dropped as data accrue; allocation ratios shift toward arms that are performing better; and there is no single opening moment at which the community's uncertainty can be assessed. In such trials, equipoise appears to be engineered and maintained by a statistical rule rather than discovered in the community: an arm is kept open while its posterior probability remains within preset bounds, and closed when a threshold is crossed, even if many external clinicians have held strong views about it throughout. The relevant 'community' becomes the governance group and its formal rules, not the broader expert field.

My question is whether Freedman's clinical-equipoise argument survives this development or quietly turns into a different justification — something like expected information gain or expected benefit to future patients — that no longer requires a premise of communal uncertainty at all. Which exact premise is under the most pressure: that uncertainty must be located in the external expert community, that it must hold at a single point in time, or that uncertainty is what vindicates randomisation rather than the value of the knowledge produced? And can the principle be repaired into a weaker form that still does real ethical work for perpetual adaptive trials, or should we simply drop the language of equipoise and say plainly that the trial is justified by its expected contribution to the care of future patients? I want to know what happens to the argument itself, not a verdict on any particular platform trial.
