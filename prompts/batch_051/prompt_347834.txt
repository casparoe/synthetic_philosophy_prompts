Our congregation is considering adopting a strict regulative principle of worship as a working rule of church government, and I need an analysis of how the rule would actually behave once it is embedded in a congregation, rather than a judgement on whether the principle is right. The proposed rule is that, in corporate worship, an element of the service is permitted only if there is a positive scriptural command for it or a clear scripturally approved example of it; the mere absence of a prohibition is not sufficient. To stop this from remaining vague, it would be paired with a mechanism: any member may, once per quarter, file a 'warrant request' against a single element of the service. The elders then have six weeks to produce a positive warrant and defend it to the church meeting; if the meeting is not satisfied, that element is suspended from the following Sunday until a warrant is produced. By 'element' we mean the constituent parts of worship, such as music, set prayers, creeds, visual symbols, the place of the sermon, and the seasons of the church year; 'circumstances', such as the time of meeting, length, seating, and lighting, are explicitly allowed to be settled by ordinary prudence.

Walk through how this institution would in fact operate. I particularly want to know: which elements will attract the first warrant requests and why; how the element/circumstance distinction will come under pressure and where it will begin to leak; where the rule quietly relocates power in the congregation; what second-order effects it will have on the content of services, on the sorts of people who join or leave, and on the pastoral role of the elders; and how the mechanism will be gamed, both by sincere advocates of the principle and by people pursuing unrelated grievances. I also want to know which failure modes could be fixed by adjusting the mechanism and which are intrinsic to the principle itself.
