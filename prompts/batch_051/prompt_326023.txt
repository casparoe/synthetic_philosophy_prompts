I have a rule about existence proofs that I can't tell is a good epistemic filter or just a constructivist hangup. When someone proves an x exists by the roundabout route — assume there isn't one, derive a contradiction, stop — I barely update; all I take away is 'ok, the claim isn't impossible.' I know intuitionistic logic builds in exactly this scruple: from 'it's absurd that no x exists' you can't infer 'there is an x.' And I've noticed I carry the same rule outside math: if a political argument shows only that some acceptable compromise must exist without actually specifying it, I treat that as no progress at all. But I suspect this rule is too strong. Ruling out impossibility is information — it can license a search I'd otherwise give up on, and it can overturn something I believed. How do I tell when a nonconstructive existence proof should actually move me or license action, and when my default discounting is the right amount of constructivism?
