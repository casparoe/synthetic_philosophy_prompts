I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so I will put this in my own terms. I have been reading about the idea that what a person believes or expects can change what they literally see; I believe the term is cognitive penetration of perception. As I understand the claim, it is not merely that your beliefs change how you interpret or report what you saw, but that they change the perceptual experience itself: the scene looks different, not merely judged different afterward.

The criteria I have picked up for deciding whether something counts as cognitive penetration are roughly these:

1. A cognitive state (a belief, expectation, desire, or something similar) has to be what does the causing.
2. What gets changed has to be the perceptual experience itself, not the later judgment, memory, or report of the experience.
3. The influence has to be direct: if the belief only works by making you look somewhere else, squint, move closer, or selectively attend to part of the scene, that is supposed to be a different and uncontroversial mechanism, and it does not count.
4. The resulting experience has to be of the same basic kind as ordinary perception — for example, actually seeing something as red, rather than merely feeling sure that it is red.

Now the case I want to test against these criteria. During code review, a colleague leaves a comment on a pull request: "there is a bug in the parse loop." I open the file, and before I have worked out what the bug is, the flagged region of code looks wrong to me. It is hard to describe: the line seems visually heavier or somehow malformed, and the tokens read as broken, the way a misspelled word looks wrong before you can say which letter is off. The effect is stronger when I trust the reviewer and disappears when I think the reviewer is unreliable. If I scroll past the line without knowing where the comment points, nothing looks off. Once I find the bug and understand it, the line stops looking wrong and just looks like code again.

Is this cognitive penetration of perception, or does it only look like it? In particular, I cannot tell whether the comment is changing my visual experience of the code, or merely directing my attention to a region (which criterion 3 would rule out), or making me judge the line as buggy after the fact (which criterion 2 would rule out). Does the case force me to revise the criteria themselves — for instance, is the line between attention and perception, or between seeing a word and reading it, less clean than my criteria assume? Please work through it criterion by criterion and say where the criteria underdetermine the answer, rather than just giving a verdict. And please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on perception, rather than presenting one side of a live dispute as the consensus.
