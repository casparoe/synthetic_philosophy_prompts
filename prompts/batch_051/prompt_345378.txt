Here is an argument about iterative factored cognition that I need evaluated precisely. Let me fix terminology first. By HCH I mean the idealization of deliberation in which a question is answered by a human who may decompose it into finitely many subquestions, attach a written brief to each, and delegate each subquestion to a fresh human who sees only that brief and may decompose further; answers are then passed back up and combined by the delegators. By factored cognition I mean the claim that competent deliberation can in principle be reconstructed as a tree of small steps, each of which can be assigned and checked locally.

The argument below is meant to show that HCH cannot reliably reproduce the judgment of an expert E on a hard unstructured question Q, such as whether an analogy is apt, whether a proof sketch has a hidden gap, or whether a proposed trade-off is acceptable.

(1) HCH reproduces E's judgment on Q only if E's reasoning can be decomposed into finitely many subtasks such that, for every subtask, some written brief plus general human competence is sufficient for a competent human who does not share E's particular history to produce or check the result that E's reasoning requires.

(2) Consider any candidate such decomposition.

(3) Each brief is a finite text, so it can explicitly mention only finitely many considerations. For the subtasks that matter to Q, how E weighs the named considerations, which buried comparisons E draws, and when E would override a listed consideration all depend on further features of E's total background, which in turn depend on still further features, without any finite stopping point that a brief could state.

(4) Hence each brief underdetermines the required resolution: a worker can follow the brief competently, drawing only on general human competence, and still, under some possible background, resolve the subtask differently from E.

(5) But if a brief underdetermines the required resolution in this way, then any agreement between the worker and E is lucky relative to the brief, and a procedure whose success would be lucky relative to its decompositional specification is not a reliable reproduction of E's judgment.

(6) Therefore no finite decomposition yields a reliable HCH reproduction of E's judgment.

My suspicion is that something goes wrong between (4) and (5), but I cannot articulate it, and the person who gave me this argument insists that underdetermination by the brief is all that is needed. Please give a precise verdict. If the argument is invalid, identify the exact step that fails, say under which reading of its crucial expression it fails, and provide the strongest corrected version of that step. Then indicate whether the corrected argument still supports (6), and whether, even if (6) were granted, it would show that HCH cannot serve as an idealization of real expert deliberation rather than merely that HCH cannot be guaranteed to capture an expert's idiosyncratic background.
