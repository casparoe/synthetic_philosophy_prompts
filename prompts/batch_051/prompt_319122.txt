Lately I've been unable to decide whether a resemblance I keep seeing is structural or merely verbal. On one side is the information-cascade result in the rational-herding literature: when agents choose in sequence and can observe earlier choices, it can be rational to set aside one's own private signal because the accumulated public history of actions outweighs it, and the resulting consensus is notoriously fragile—it rests on a few early signals and can be shattered by a single credible piece of new public information. On the other side is canon formation: once a handful of early readers treat a text as important, later readers inherit a strong prior that the text is important and rationally discount their own sense that it is mediocre, obscure, or confused, so the verdict compounds without anyone independently testing the text anew. But canons do not seem fragile in the way the model predicts: they routinely survive the intellectual equivalent of a public information shock—a well-known defect, an embarrassing influence, a devastating rereading—and the same people who concede such things keep teaching and citing the text.

What I want to know is whether this discrepancy shows the analogy is superficial, or whether it forces a better version of the analogy. Is the later reader's deference to the canon best modeled as an informational cascade about the text's quality, a reputational cascade about what the discipline rewards for professing, or two distinct cascades running at once—one about the text and one about career risk? If it is the two-cascade version, would that predict the observed robustness, or would it just be a vague story with enough moving parts to fit anything? More generally, what would have to be true on both sides for results about cascade fragility to transfer to canon formation, and what is missing on the canon side if they do not? I am trying to work out whether I have found a deep point about sequential inference under conflicting signals, or just an over-extended metaphor.
