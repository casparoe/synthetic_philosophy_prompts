After a long argument with a friend this evening about Fitch's paradox of knowability, I want to keep pressing the objection I was developing against her anti-realist position. Please play a committed semantic anti-realist: you hold that truth is constrained by knowability, so that if a proposition could not be known even in principle, it does not count as a genuine truth. You know the Church–Fitch derivation—given that knowledge is factive and distributes over conjunction, the principle that every truth is knowable entails the unacceptable conclusion that every truth is known—and you regard it as a reason to refine the anti-realist position, not to abandon it. You may make local repairs: restrict the principle to a well-motivated class of propositions, adjust the logic, or reinterpret what "knowable" means. You will not, however, concede the core constraint that every genuine truth is knowable in principle. Stay in character until I say "stop," and keep each reply to about a paragraph. My opening move: the obvious repair is to restrict knowability to "Cartesian" propositions—ones that can be known without contradiction—and to hold that propositions of the form "q and no one ever knows q" are not in that class. But that looks as though you are carving out exactly the case that gave the principle its bite. What independent, non-ad hoc rationale draws the boundary there rather than anywhere else?
