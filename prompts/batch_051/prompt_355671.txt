You are an epistemologist who works on evidence, knowledge, and the norms of belief revision. I want you to think carefully and at length before committing, and to begin by brainstorming several candidate distinctions rather than settling on the first one that sounds plausible.

A friend and I recently argued for an evening about a mutual acquaintance who refuses to look at evidence against one of her political convictions. My friend thought she was behaving no differently from the rest of us when we decline to re-examine things we already know; I thought there was a real difference, but I could not state it cleanly. The argument exposed the following asymmetry in our ordinary epistemic judgments.

Case 1. Priya is the chief of staff to a senator. She receives a dossier of apparently authentic financial documents, assembled by a careful journalist, that strongly suggests the senator she works for has been taking bribes. She refuses to look at it. Her stated reason is: I already know he is an honest man, so this material must be misleading in some way, and I won't waste time on it. Most people, including her friends, regard this as dogmatism and a failure of intellectual responsibility.

Case 2. Now take David. He has lived with his wife for twenty years and is looking at her across the kitchen table. He receives an elaborate, professionally produced dossier that appears to establish that his wife died two years ago and that the woman in front of him is an impostor placed in his life. He refuses to examine it, giving the same form of reason: I know who my wife is, so these documents must be misleading, and I won't waste time on them. We do not usually regard David as dogmatic, irresponsible, or closed-minded; we think it is fine, even sensible, to ignore the dossier.

The structure of the two refusals is identical: in both cases, a person refuses to examine apparently relevant counterevidence on the ground that it must be misleading because it contradicts what the person already knows. Yet we judge one refusal harshly and the other leniently.

Is that asymmetry justified, or is it an inconsistency we simply live with? Work through the strongest candidate justifications seriously. Among the differences worth testing are: whether the background belief would be contested by informed people; how much and what kind of evidence supports it; whether the responding person has motives to keep it; what the counterevidence would have to be like if false, such as a hoax, a conspiracy, or an unnoticed error; and whether the person can say anything about the likely source of the error without reading the material. Test your best candidate justifications against nearby cases, such as a jealous husband who refuses to examine evidence of his wife's fidelity, a mathematician who tosses an unread proof purporting to refute a theorem she believes, a cult member who ignores evidence of a leader's crimes, and a victim of gaslighting who protects a true memory against engineered counterevidence. Then state your conclusion: which differences, if any, justify the asymmetry, how far they extend, and whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, reversed, or given up.
