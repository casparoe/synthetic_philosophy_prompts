A friend of mine works at a historical institute that is preparing a critical edition of a twelfth-century legal document from a small town in central Italy. The editorial committee is split over how to describe the language of the text. Some members call it late Latin, pointing out that the document mostly follows Latin spelling and grammatical conventions and that a medieval writer of the period would probably have said he was writing Latin. Others call it an early form of Italian, pointing out that the sentence structure and many individual forms are already clearly vernacular, and that the text contains regional features that later became part of standard Italian. The choice has practical consequences: it decides whether the edition is catalogued and funded under Latin studies or Italian studies, and which national cultural-heritage body gets to claim the text. My friend has to sign off on the committee's label, and she has asked me whether there is a fact of the matter about when Latin stopped being Latin and became Italian, or whether the label is ultimately an institutional and political choice dressed up as linguistic description. What should she think through before making the decision? Please give exactly five numbered considerations, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
