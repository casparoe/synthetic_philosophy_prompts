I work on the theory of normatively binding speech acts, and I have come to the view that an oath's binding force is a function of the sanctioning power it summons: an oath binds the swearer because, and only because, it invokes some recognized authority or institution that can call the swearer to account, whether through divine punishment, social exposure, legal liability, or the loss of one's standing before some relevant community. On this view, the words of an oath do not create an obligation by themselves; they work by placing the swearer under a threat that alters their incentives and gives the person to whom the oath is sworn a credible basis for reliance. The oath's distinctive wrongness on this account is not a sui generis moral wrong but rather a matter of incurring a debt one has pledged to pay, where the pledge's force is backed by the enforcement conditions.

I find the Greek practice of swearing by the Styx an especially revealing limiting case. The gods themselves swear by Styx, and the Hesiodic account gives the sanction in unusually concrete form: a god who swears falsely on the Styx lies breathless for a year and is excluded from the company of the gods for a further period, with the Styx water functioning as the instrument by which the oath takes hold. This looks like a powerful illustration of the view I am drawn to: even among the gods, an oath binds through a kind of cosmic enforcement mechanism, not through the mere act of swearing.

What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are, from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed sanction-theorist should themselves concede as counting against the view, rather than dismiss as question-begging or irrelevant. Among the things I want considered are: cases where the relevant sanction is absent, impossible, or unknown to the swearer and yet the oath seems still to bind; the difference between breaking an oath and merely underestimating an enforcement risk; the role of the witness, including the Styx itself, if it is not acting as an enforcer but as something that gives the oath its content or direction; and whether the view collapses into a general sanction theory of obligation in a way that loses what is distinctive about oaths. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability once it allows that the relevant sanction can be obscure, internalized, or purely normative, and whether such insulation would be a problem for a view of this kind.
