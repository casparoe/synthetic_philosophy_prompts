Since retiring from a career in geology I have been reading philosophy in earnest, and the debate over whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading keeps bringing me back to one question: whether the distinction itself does the moral work people want it to. Augustine's On Lying (De Mendacio) states the view I want to test with unusual precision: "…he may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he thinks it to be so as he says although it be not so; and, that he may say a true thing, and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, although in reality it be so as he utters it. For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie." I would like a reading of Shakespeare's Othello through this lens. Iago's characteristic work is done through true statements, omissions, and insinuation rather than bald falsehood—though at least one crucial claim, Cassio's supposed dream, is a flat fabrication. Does the play treat Iago's truth-telling deception as any less blameworthy than his outright lie, and does it vindicate or complicate Augustine's claim that whether one lies is settled by the speaker's mind rather than by the truth or falsity of what is said? Please attend to what Iago actually says and does, including the places where he lies outright, and say what drama adds to the philosophical argument that the papers do not.
