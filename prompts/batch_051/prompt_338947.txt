Here's a passage from William James's 1884 paper "What is an Emotion?" that I keep coming back to — he's just been arguing that the bodily changes come first, and that the felt emotion is our perception of them:

"Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

I've built a decision rule out of this. As I read it, James is saying that once you subtract the bodily changes you're left with something cognitive — a judgment about what to do — and the feeling itself is just the perception of your body's disturbance. So when I have a strong reaction to something (a nasty email, a scary headline, a person I instantly dislike), I now treat the feeling as information about my body, not about the situation. I ask what the bare judgment would be if I was physically calm, and I go with that. In practice I discount fear and anger almost to zero as reasons.

But I've started to suspect I'm over-reading the passage, or that I've dressed up a bias as a rule. The leftover judgment isn't necessarily the same judgment minus noise: the bodily response might encode something the calm cognition doesn't have, and the "exciting fact" is still something I perceived. So my question is whether the habit is defensible, either as a reading of James or just as a policy, and where the passage stops licensing it and my own inference takes over.
