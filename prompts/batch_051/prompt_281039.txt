In current refugee practice, two people can flee conditions that threaten their lives and yet receive radically different treatment. A dissident fleeing imprisonment and torture by state agents is recognized as a refugee with a strong claim to asylum; a farmer fleeing a drought that has destroyed a harvest and left a child facing starvation is normally classed as an economic or environmental migrant and denied the legal protection of asylum, with no comparable right to enter or remain. The apparent rationale is that the dissident has been targeted by human agents, whereas the farmer has been harmed by nature and economic forces. I want to know whether this asymmetry can be justified by a defensible principle rather than merely explained by history and state interests. Give the strongest principle you can find that would generate these different outcomes, test it against hard cases such as state-engineered famine, climate change driven by the policies of wealthy states, and criminal gang violence that the persecution standard struggles to encompass, and offer an honest verdict on whether the distinction should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned — and what that verdict implies for the obligations of receiving states.
