I hold a broadly Augustinian view of time, drawn from Confessions XI: past and future have no mind-independent being; the past exists only as present memory, the future as present expectation, and the passing present as present attention; time as experienced is a stretching-out of the mind. The passage I have in mind is the one in which Augustine turns the question of measuring time toward the conclusion that time is measured in the mind, since the past is no longer and the future is not yet. I want to know what would count against this position — not just what its critics have said, but what a careful defender of it would acknowledge as real evidence against it. Please give me a ranked map of its exposure: what observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or developments in, for example, philosophy of physics, empirical time perception, or the metaphysics of tense would be decisive, which would be merely awkward, and which would force a qualification rather than abandonment. In particular, I want to know whether the view is committed to an unacceptable consequence (say, that no temporal relation holds between two events before any mind exists, or that time would be different for differently structured minds), whether it collapses into a B-theory of time or into a form of phenomenalism, and what predictions it makes that rival views do not. I would personally find most persuasive: (a) evidence that temporal experience is not prior to but constructed from atemporal mechanisms; (b) a clear account of how to reconcile mind-dependence with the apparent mind-independent success of physics; (c) a case where the view cannot say something true about past and future that ordinary thought must say.
