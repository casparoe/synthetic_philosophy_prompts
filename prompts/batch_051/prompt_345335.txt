I’ve been trying to get clear on whether the wavefunction in quantum mechanics is something real in the world or just a way of encoding what we know. In a discussion group, someone defended the second view with an analogy that seems to be in common use. He said the wavefunction is like a weather forecast. A forecast is not itself a storm; it is a summary of what might happen at some place and time. Likewise, he said, the wavefunction is not a physical object like an electric field; it is just a summary of possible measurement outcomes, and asking what it is made of is like asking what the 60 percent chance of rain is made of. His conclusion was that the only real question is whether the probabilities are correct, not whether the wavefunction is real.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. In a weather forecast, there is an actual atmosphere with definite temperature, pressure, and humidity, and the forecast is uncertain only because our information is limited. In the quantum case, the analogous underlying weather would have to be some definite state of the electron that the wavefunction summarizes. But I have read enough to know that quantum mechanics does not seem to let us assume that definite state without adding something extra—and that some results rule out at least the simple versions of that idea. So it looks like the analogy might be quietly importing hidden variables into the picture.

Can you audit the forecast analogy as an argument? Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the atmosphere, the actual weather, the forecast, the probability, and the observation. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for the conclusion and which are incidental. Finally, say whether the conclusion that the wavefunction is only a bookkeeping device and not something real survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time.
