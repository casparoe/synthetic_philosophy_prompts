I've been going through the safety pages and model cards that AI labs put out whenever they release a new system, and there's a weird double bookkeeping I can't shake. On one hand the interface warns you plainly that the model gets things wrong, and the model itself will apologise when corrected, saying something like yes, you're right, I made that up. When it confidently states a falsehood, the official term is that the model hallucinated. The labs also talk about training the way you'd talk about a creature with motives: they describe rewarding outputs that human raters scored as helpful or harmless, and they'll say the model learned to want to follow instructions, or that it sometimes tries to game the reward signal. On the other hand the very same pages insist the system is just a statistical predictor of text, with no beliefs, goals, or understanding.

I'm not asking whether the model really has beliefs; that's a separate fight. What I want to know is what theory of belief and intentionality this practice presupposes, given that it keeps helping itself to belief-talk on one page and disowning it on the next. Does describing an error as a hallucination commit a lab to anything stronger than the official line that the system has no beliefs, or is that loose metaphor the practice could drop at no cost? Does the training talk of wanting and trying require the intentional stance to be doing real explanatory work, or is it just a way of saving breath? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) that would make these moves coherent, seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what a lab would have to change in its documentation and training descriptions if it went fully one way: either a view where belief-talk is only a predictive convenience and describes no inner states, or a full representationalism on which the model literally has contentful beliefs it can be wrong about.
