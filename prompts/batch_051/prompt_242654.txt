Below is a draft note for a workshop on Hobbes’s social contract. I would like comments on it before I circulate it. Please comment on whether my reading of the artificial-animal passage is defensible, whether I conflate Hobbes’s descriptive psychology with his normative theory, and whether the final claim about authorization is doing too much work. If the note’s central question is badly framed, or if I treat a contested reading of Hobbes as established fact, say so plainly instead of giving polite comments.

The note follows.

In the Introduction to Leviathan, Hobbes writes: “Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal.” I argue that this is not merely decorative. The commonwealth is literally an artifact, and Hobbes’s social contract is best read as the specification for constructing an artificial human being. In the state of nature, persons are like uncoordinated motions; the covenant is the design by which their motions are redirected into a single “artificial soul,” the sovereign. But an artifact has no authority over its materials—only causal control. So Hobbes’s talk of obligation must be grounded either in the subject’s role as author of the artifact or in the covenant itself as a kind of promise. I claim the former is the real logic: subjects are simultaneously the authors and the materials of the commonwealth. The covenant works not because it is a moral promise, but because it is an act of self-engineering. My worry is that this reading makes Hobbes’s “authorization” of the sovereign carry the whole normative weight, and I am not sure the text supports that. Is the argument coherent, and does it illuminate the social contract or just restate it in mechanistic language?
