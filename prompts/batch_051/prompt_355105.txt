Me and a mate spent most of Sunday night going round in circles about many-worlds, and by the end I realised we were basically re-enacting a standoff that has been stuck in the literature for decades without anyone visibly moving. I'm less interested in who's right than in what kind of disagreement it actually is, so I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

Here's the standoff as I can reconstruct it. Both sides accept the same physics: the wavefunction never collapses, decoherence produces effectively independent branches, and all the branches are equally real. They also agree that an observer who keeps measuring will, in the overwhelming majority of branches by amplitude, see frequencies matching the Born rule. The fight is only about what an agent should expect or bet before a branching measurement. One side says there is genuine self-locating uncertainty: before a spin measurement with amplitudes √0.9 and √0.1, I should be uncertain about which of my future continuers I'll be, and decision-theoretic consistency forces me to weight them 0.9 and 0.1 — anything else makes my preferences incoherent or exploitable. The other side says that uncertainty is fake: both continuers are me, there's no single thing I will turn out to be, so credence talk is a category mistake. On that view the only honest options are agnosticism about probability or admitting the Born rule is an extra postulate tacked on by hand; the decision-theoretic move is circular because it quietly builds amplitude-weighting into its axioms about rational preference.

My working theory is that the two sides are answering different questions: one is trying to define a rule for rational betting under branching, the other is trying to find a probability that a single world will be actual — and the second thing just doesn't exist in many-worlds, so that question is being stretched past the context where the concept fitted. That would make it a conceptual or verbal disagreement rather than an empirical one. But I cant tell whether that's right, becuase I might be missing a hidden formal fact — for instance, whether branch number is even well-defined once decoherence only gives approximate branches — that would break the tie.

So diagnose it, properly. What kind of disagreement is this, and what would count as progress given the diagnosis? And be honest: is it the sort of thing a theorem, an experiment, or a distinction could settle, or was it never a single question to begin with?
