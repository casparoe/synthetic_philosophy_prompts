Here is an asymmetry in ordinary judgment that I want to know whether anything really justifies. Most people have no trouble making sense of a person who says of an intense but ordinary sensation — a dazzling light, a loud noise — that they can describe its intensity and quality and yet it does not bother them in the least. The sensation and the disliking are treated as two things that happen to have come apart, and the report is not refused as contradictory. But change the case to pain. A person says that a cut or a burn is intensely painful, that they can locate it and tell you it is sharp or burning, and yet that it is not unpleasant or distressing to them. Here most people will say that the person is at least a little conceptually confused, or that there is something wrong with them that makes the report not quite a report about pain. Yet this second description matches pain asymbolia: in these patients the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain seems to survive while the affective-motivational dimension is absent. No comparable syndrome is needed for us to accept the brightness case; we accept it before any neuropsychology.

The asymmetry is this: we treat the unpleasantness of pain as partly constitutive of what it is to feel pain, while we treat unpleasantness as a detachable accompaniment of other intense sensations. My question is whether anything justifies that asymmetry, rather than merely explaining it psychologically. The best candidates I can see are: (i) the meaning of the word ‘pain’ simply includes badness, so someone who denies the badness is not feeling pain but something else; (ii) pain’s biological role is to protect the body, so a pain that fails to motivate withdrawal is defective in a way a bright light that fails to bother is not; and (iii) in normal life the two dimensions are so tightly fused that the distinction is a clinical curiosity with no authority over our concepts. I would like you to construct the strongest version of each candidate, test it against the asymbolic case and against nearby cases — an itch that is intense but not aversive, a feeling of suffocation that terrifies one person and not another, a craving that the person fully repudiates — and then give a verdict. Should we say that the asymbolic person really does feel pain, and therefore that pain’s badness is always a separable component that normal pain merely screens from view? Or is there a defensible sense in which the badness is part of the pain after all, and the asymmetry should be kept?
