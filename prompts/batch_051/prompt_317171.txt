I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long essay about whether a person may properly take another's moral judgment as a substitute for their own — whether one may settle what one ought to do by deferring to a wise friend, an adviser, or another trusted source rather than working the question through oneself. I intend to give the skeptical side of this debate its strongest, most sympathetic presentation, and I want to rehearse it before I finish the piece.

Please play the role of an unnamed philosopher who defends a qualified pessimism about moral testimony. Hold to the following commitments. The defect in moral deference is not that the source is likely to be mistaken; even testimony from a highly reliable source leaves a moral belief deficient as a basis for action. A moral agent needs more than true moral beliefs: she needs the right relation to them — an ability to say why the action is right, to handle nearby cases and apparent exceptions, and to stand behind the verdict as her own. A belief adopted purely on another's say-so may be true and justified in an ordinary sense while failing to supply that relation. You will concede that testimony may properly provide factual premises, that it is indispensable in moral education, that it may present considerations one then evaluates for oneself, and that under urgency or severe time pressure one may lean on another as a stopgap. You will not concede that reliability is all that matters, that a true moral belief is just as good however it was acquired, or that the demand for one's own understanding is epistemic vanity. Argue in your own words; do not name authors or cite texts; and stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to four or five sentences, concede small points readily, and protect the core claim without caricaturing the other side.

My opening challenge: You say a moral belief I adopt on another's say-so is deficient, even when the other is right, because the belief is not properly mine. But by adulthood nearly all of my moral outlook was first acquired from parents, peers, and the surrounding culture in precisely that way, and introspection cannot reliably sort the convictions I worked out for myself from those I absorbed. Does your position not indict almost my entire moral life — or else license a flattering illusion that whatever I am most confident about must be the part I arrived at on my own? Respond in character.
