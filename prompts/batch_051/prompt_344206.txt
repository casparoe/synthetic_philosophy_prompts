I want a thought experiment built to a spec, and I want it anchored in a passage from Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (Part I, Section I, Chapter I). Here's the passage: "By the imagination we place ourselves in his situation, we conceive ourselves enduring all the same torments, we enter as it were into his body, and become in some measure the same person with him, and thence form some idea of his sensations, and even feel something which, though weaker in degree, is not altogether unlike them."

Construct a case where a spectator, following exactly that Smithian mechanism, reaches an impartial-spectator-style verdict of full propriety about another person's emotional response—but the person herself insists that the response was not at all the one the spectator entered into. Constraints: one paragraph, ordinary workplace, no deception, no missing factual information on the spectator's part. The gap has to come from the difference between what the spectator would feel in the person's external situation and what this particular person actually feels given her interior constitution, not from the spectator not knowing the facts. After the scenario, walk through what Smith's account says about the case and why the person dissents from it. Then say which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work and what happens if you delete it, and how a defender of Smith would most plausibly respond. Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario, (2) the spectator's Smithian judgment, (3) the person's inner report and why she rejects the spectator's read, (4) the working feature and what breaks if removed, (5) the best Smith-defender reply.
