We've been doing Grice in my semantics/pragmatics class, and the professor has a one-liner he reaches for whenever anyone asks whether a sentence "really" means what the speaker seems to be doing with it. Last week someone asked whether "Some of my friends are coming to the party" actually means not all of them are coming, or just leaves it open. The answer was: "You can mean more than you say." Then the same line came back when we discussed "Can you pass the salt?" — is it literally a question about ability, or a request? — and again when someone asked why saying "It's cold in here" can get a person to shut a window. Each time the slogan is used to close the discussion, as if it already decides which facts belong to semantics and which to pragmatics.

But I've started to think the phrase is doing more work than its earning. It could mean something completely uncontroversial, like "a speaker can communicate more than the literal content of her words," which basically everyone would grant. Or it could mean something much bigger: that sentence meaning and speaker meaning are two wholly different kinds of thing, or that literal meaning is just a fallback we lean on when we can't infer, or that anything the speaker conveys beyond a thin literal core is never part of the sentence's semantic content — which would rule out "some" literally meaning "some but not all" by definition rather than by argument. Those are not the same claim, and some of them look false to me. I also can't tell whether the slogan, on any interesting reading, actually settles the "some" case: couldn't "not all" be part of the standing meaning of "some" in some dialects or uses, and still be something a speaker can cancel or exploit sarcastically? I want to be able to answer back.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "you can mean more than you say" might be standing for, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false. Then tell me which reading the professor is implicitly relying on in the "some" case, and whether that reading actually gives him his conclusion. And one more thing: is there a coherent reading of the slogan that is compatible with "some" literally encoding "not all," or would that be a different slogan entirely? Hard cap: 900 words. A bulleted menu is fine, but don't let the bullets eat the analysis.
