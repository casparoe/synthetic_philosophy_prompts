I'm taking a graduate seminar this term on the norms of assertion, and I've been trying to extract from my own reactions to cases what the correct rule for flat-out assertion actually is. My tentative principle is the knowledge rule: assert flat-out only if you know. But my verdicts keep refusing to line up neatly behind it, and I'd like to know which candidate norm best captures my pattern of reactions—or, failing that, which of my verdicts I should give up. The candidate norms I'm weighing are: assert only what you know; assert only what is true; assert only what you reasonably believe; and assert only what you yourself believe, a sincerity requirement.

Here are the cases I've been using, with my fairly settled reactions. (1) Before a lottery drawing, I tell a colleague, "I'm not winning tonight." The odds are one in several million, so I'm almost certainly right, but I still feel the statement was too strong—slightly arrogant—and I should have hedged. (2) I check a reliable rail app, tell a stranded traveler, "The last train leaves at 11:40," and it turns out the schedule changed an hour earlier and she misses the train. I have a hard time calling my assertion improper; it was unlucky rather than a violation. (3) A friend tells me my street will be repaved on Monday because the city put a work sign at the corner. The sign had actually fallen from the next street over and landed facing mine, but the city really is repaving my street on Monday, unbeknownst to him. I'm inclined to say his assertion was fine, since it was true and he had every reason to believe it. (4) With no evidence and no inside knowledge, someone announces, "The merger will be finalized next Tuesday," just to sound decisive, and it happens to be true. That strikes me as improper even though it was true; he had no business saying it. (5) A salesperson tells me, "The engine was replaced under warranty," believing it was never replaced, in order to close the sale; the engine was in fact replaced under warranty. That also strikes me as improper, even though the sentence was true—he didn't believe it. (6) If someone says, "The restaurant is closed, but I don't know that it's closed," or, "It's raining, but I don't believe that it's raining," the utterance feels broken on its own terms, whatever the facts are. (7) If instead I say, "The restaurant might be closed" or, "I think it's closed," I'm comfortable doing so on much weaker grounds; so the rule I'm after applies only to flat-out assertion, not to hedging. (8) An emergency-room doctor tells an anxious parent, "Your child has no neck fracture," on the basis of a normal exam and a high-quality x-ray but before the specialist radiology read; there remains a small chance she's wrong. I judge that proper—indeed, just what she should say. This last case feels uncomfortably close to the lottery case, but I can't say why I treat them differently.

Can you help me find a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely, run it through all eight cases, identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and tell me whether I should revise those verdicts or accept a more complex rule instead. Then apply your preferred principle to two further cases I'm still unsure about: a political pundit who asserts something true on the basis of weak evidence that he has worked hard to fit to his desired conclusion; and a teacher who asserts a falsehood that is well supported by the assigned textbook but has in fact been superseded by recent scholarship.
