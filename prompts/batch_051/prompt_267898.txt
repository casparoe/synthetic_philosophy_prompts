For a handout in an undergraduate seminar that crosses philosophy of mind and philosophy of religion, construct a decision tree for the question: can what cannot be said still be known? Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of labels, and phrase each branch node as a question a student could answer without already knowing the positions. Use at least these branch distinctions: whether all genuine knowledge is propositional; whether negative claims (such as denying that the ineffable is finite or temporal) can themselves constitute knowledge of it; whether the ineffable state has a felt, phenomenal character; whether acquiring a skill or a reordered way of acting can count as knowledge; and whether the ineffable can be shown or enacted even where it cannot be stated. Place a leaf at every defensible combination of answers and name each leaf where a named position exists — the propositional-knowledge-only view, apophaticism or negative theology, the acquaintance or knowledge-by-presence view, the know-how view, the expressivist or fictionalist view, the silence or antimetaphysical view, and any others you take to be in the space. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Aim for between eight and twelve leaves. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, note it and say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Finally, flag any two positions your tree cannot separate and state the further question that would separate them.
