I've been teaching a unit on the history of logic this term, and we keep coming back to the old Lewis Carroll dialogue where Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept the conclusion of a modus ponens by writing down more and more "if...then..." sentences, and the Tortoise politely accepts every single one of them and still won't move to the conclusion. What's been bugging me is how differently people cash out the moral. Some say the dialogue shows an inference rule isn't just one more premise. But the cognitive scientists I hang out with say that's armchair stuff: in any real reasoner, following modus ponens is just a causal disposition that can be redescribed as another conditional, so the difference between using a rule and accepting a conditional premise is only a philosopher's distinction with no empirical content.

Take that last claim and design the best test of it you can. Concretely: suppose I have a reasoner — a person, or a language model doing step-by-step reasoning — who accepts P, accepts "if P then Q", and says, if asked, that modus ponens is valid. What setup, manipulation, or measurement would you use to get evidence about whether there's a real difference between (a) genuinely following the rule and (b) merely accepting a pile of conditionals, including one more conditional like "if P and if P then Q, then Q"? I'd like the test designed from scratch, not a reference to some study. Be specific about what you'd record, what outcome you'd take as evidence for a real rule/conditional distinction, what outcome you'd take as evidence it's just a verbal distinction, and weather the cleanest possible result could settle whether the Tortoise ought to accept the conclusion — I suspect the ought part stays beyond the data no matter what. Give me a committed answer: pick a single best test design, defend it, and tell me flat out what it would and wouldn't show, rather than laying out a menu. And keep it in American English, please.
