I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have agreed to give a ninety-minute guest session at a summer workshop for high-school students who are learning how to conduct interviews and write fairly about what people said. I want the session to give them a working version of Paul Grice's distinction between what a sentence means and what a speaker means by uttering it on a particular occasion, with conversational implicature as the central idea. The students are bright but have no background in philosophy of language: they can quote a source accurately, but they tend to assume either that a speaker communicates nothing beyond the literal wording or that, once you notice a suggestion, you are free to read into the words whatever the occasion makes plausible. I want them to leave with a third picture, in which an implication is genuinely communicated but is generated by recognizable norms of cooperative talk, so it must be something a listener can work out from what was said and the shared situation, and it can be withdrawn without the speaker falling into literal self-contradiction.

Please design the teaching plan for that session rather than the explanation itself. Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one; the single running example or case that will carry the idea and what it must and must not include; the misconceptions the students will likely bring or form, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; and a final check that would show me whether they have grasped that an implicature is calculated, defeasible, and distinct from both entailment and free association, rather than merely learned the word. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and why, especially whether the full apparatus of Grice's maxims or the distinction between conventional and conversational implicature should be postponed or built in implicitly rather than named.
