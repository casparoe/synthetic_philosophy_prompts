I have no academic background, but I have been thinking on my own about a question concerning authoritarian rule and would like it worked through carefully.

The argument now often called performance legitimacy, developed around cases like China and Singapore, runs roughly as follows: even where citizens have little say in choosing or removing their government, sustained economic growth and public order can make the system stable, and can even supply a kind of legitimation distinct from democratic consent. People become visibly better off and walk safe, predictable streets, and on that basis they offer conditional acceptance of one-party or dominant-party rule.

I want to know whether this argument survives two changes since the growth takeoffs it was built to explain. First, growth has shifted toward capital-intensive, increasingly automated sectors, so a country can report strong GDP growth while median incomes stagnate and secure employment for ordinary people shrinks. Second, the digital systems that deliver the regime's vaunted order—identity registries, cameras, payment and platform monitoring, official data pipelines—also give the ruling party far more control over how performance is measured, filtered, and presented to the public. Performance is thus becoming both less broadly distributed and less independently observable than the original argument assumed.

Are these changes fatal to the argument, or do they only put pressure on one of its premises? Please identify which premise each change bears on—for example, the premise that citizens can distinguish real performance from managed appearances, the premise that the benefits of growth reach ordinary people, or the premise that order is a benefit regardless of how it is produced. Then say whether the argument is dead, merely weaker, or repairable; if it can be repaired, give the strongest version that still deserves the name performance legitimacy and state what that version can and cannot justify. Keep the response to at most 700 words.
