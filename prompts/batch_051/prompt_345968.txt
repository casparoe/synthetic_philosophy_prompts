I build ranking models for a video platform, so my day job is basically inferring what people want from clicks, watch time and skips, then optimising for it. I keep circling back to a result from social choice I half-remember: Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. The way I understand it, if each person's preferences satisfy the expected utility axioms, and the group's preferences satisfy them too, and there's a Pareto condition (whenever everyone prefers A to B, the group prefers A to B), then the group's utility function has to be a weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions. Clean.

But the theorem seems to assume the individual preferences are already there, well-formed and coherent, and the social choice just adds them up. In a real recommender system you never see preferences like that. You observe behaviour, fit preference estimates from noisy data, and those estimates are often intransitive and context-dependent. Worse, the system feeds back into the very preferences it's aggregating: what people click on depends on what the recommender showed them, so the platform is partly constructing the preferences it then treats as input. The three moving parts in the theorem — individual vNM-rationality, social vNM-rationality, the Pareto condition — at least one of them looks broken, but I'm not sure which one actually fails, or wether it matters.

So here's the question: does Harsanyi's aggregation theorem survive this? If you think it still goes through, say what I've got wrong about the setup. If not, identify which premise breaks and why, then say whether there's a repaired version that keeps something like the weighted-sum conclusion under weaker assumptions we could actually satisfy. I care about the argument, not the policy. And could you answer in British English? Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
