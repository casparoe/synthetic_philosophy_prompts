I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in how we treat risk that I cannot tell is rational or just a bias. Suppose two features pose the same downside at the same stakes, and both carry a 2% point estimate of causing an outage in their first week. For one, the 2% comes from a long run of very similar releases, so the probability can be treated as known. For the other, it comes from a novel or poorly understood component, so the estimate itself is uncertain. We treat the second as far riskier: we add safety margins, route around it, or delay launch even when the delay produces no new information, and we blame an engineer who skips those precautions much more harshly than one who skips them in the known-distribution case. Yet the best point estimate is identical, and a naive expected-cost calculation comes out the same either way. Is there a defensible principle that treats these as genuinely different problems? I want the strongest decision rule you can construct that distinguishes risk (known probabilities) from ambiguity (probabilities that are themselves uncertain) and that could justify the uneven caution, plus an honest verdict on whether it survives once the costs of the extra caution are explicitly counted and no new information is being gained. If no principle works, say whether the asymmetry should be kept anyway as a useful engineering heuristic or given up. Hard limit: 500 words.
