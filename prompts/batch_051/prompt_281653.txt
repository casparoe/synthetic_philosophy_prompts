Take the preface paradox and make the higher-order evidence lopsided. The author has carefully checked every sentence of her 1,000-sentence book (10 chapters, 100 sentences each) and believes each one; she also has a well-confirmed belief that exactly one sentence in the book is false, located uniformly at random among all 1,000 sentences. Then an automated audit flags chapter 3. She knows the audit really does locate the guilty chapter, in this sense: for whichever chapter contains the false sentence, the audit names that chapter with probability 0.9, and if it makes a mistake it names one of the other nine chapters uniformly. So when the audit says chapter 3, what should her posterior probability be that a randomly chosen chapter-3 sentence is the false one, and what should it be for a random sentence elsewhere? More to the point: does the original preface-paradox tension survive in this localized form — can she still rationally believe each individual sentence in chapter 3 while being nearly certain one of them is false? Do the standard exit routes (rejecting closure of rational belief under conjunction, or giving up the Lockean move from high credence to full belief) work the same way here, or does one of them now become forced?
