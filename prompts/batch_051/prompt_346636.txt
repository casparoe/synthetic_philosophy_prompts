I keep noticing a pattern in explanations of large social outcomes and would like to know whether it has an established name. A historian accounts for the early industrialization of one part of Europe by invoking that population's religiously grounded discipline, thrift, and suspicion of luxury; an economist accounts for differing rates of entrepreneurship between two communities by invoking their respective commercial cultures. In both cases, the explaining disposition is read off the very practices it is supposed to explain rather than measured independently. When the same outlook appears without the outcome, the response is that local circumstances interfered; when the outcome appears with a different outlook, the response is that the cause there was different. The explanation is never placed at risk. Is there a name in the philosophy of the social sciences or historiography for this configuration, in which a cultural or value-based cause is inferred from its effect and then protected from counterexamples by piecemeal qualifications? I recognize that it resembles ad hocness and unfalsifiability, but I want to know whether the cultural-causal version has its own concept and boundaries. A bare label is less important than an exact account of what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie; if no established name exists, I would want a carefully defined proposal instead. Please answer in American English.
