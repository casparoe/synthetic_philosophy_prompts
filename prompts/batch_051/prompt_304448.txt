I am a novelist working on a book whose protagonist is a forger able to produce paintings that are indistinguishable, even by close expert inspection, from the works they imitate. The book keeps pulling me toward a philosophical question I have not been able to settle on my own: when a perceptually perfect copy takes the place of an original, is anything of aesthetic consequence lost? I have been collecting the standard deflationary responses — the arguments that say no, that the original's superior value is not presented in the experience of the work but is imposed on it from outside. As far as I have been able to determine, there are six distinct arguments of this family, and they seem to me to cover the familiar moves:

1. The argument from perceptual equivalence. If two works are indiscriminable to careful looking, everything available to aesthetic experience is the same; the preference for the original must be a preference for a fact known about the object, not a difference found in the object.

2. The argument from the genealogy of aura. What gets called the original's aura is, on the reading of Benjamin that has been most useful to me, a residue of cult use and of the object's unique existence at a particular time and place; reproduction dissolves that residue, which suggests the authority of the original was a historical halo rather than a visible feature.

3. The argument from exposure. Van Meegeren's forged Vermeers were admired while they were believed genuine and then depreciated after their origin came out, though the painted surfaces did not change; what rose and fell was an attribution, not a quality present in the work itself.

4. The argument from duplicate-tolerant arts. Music and literature manage without painted originals: a performance or a copy can be a fully genuine instance of the work. If the original-copy contrast does so little there, its force in painting is a local rule of a particular practice, not a standing requirement of aesthetic experience.

5. The institutional argument. Whether two physically identical canvases count as the same work, or as original and impostor, is settled by experts, catalogues, provenance, and the market; the original's extra value is the value of a certified token, conferred by the apparatus of the artworld.

6. The argument from scarcity and signaling. Originals are unique and cannot be shared the way a reproduction can; the premium they command behaves like a positional good, and devotion to unique objects is hard to tell apart from the signaling and rivalry that surround trophies and limited editions.

I want a seventh argument of the same deflationary family that is not on this list. It must not be a rewording of any of the six, not a special case or application of one of them, and not a combination of them. A genuinely new argument would locate the source of the original's inflated value in a different kind of fact — a different place to point and say that is where the distinction is doing more work than it merits. Set the argument out clearly, and then give me an honest appraisal of whether it holds up or, under pressure, collapses back into one or more of the six.
