So I spent last night arguing with my friend about whether there's really a moral difference between lying and misleading, and I came away not sure the distinction is tracking anything more than a vibe. The asymmetry we kept circling: a used-car seller who says "No, it's never been in a flood" when it has is a liar, and everyone treats that as clearly wrong. But a seller who says "I always kept it garaged and never had water issues" — technically true but carefully worded so the buyer walks away with the same false belief — gets treated as much less bad, sometimes just "cagey" or "sleazy," and lots of people don't think its wrong at all. Same with a politician who says they have "no plans" to cut pensions while holding a plan, or a teenager asked about drugs at the party who says "I was at Jake's watching a movie." In each pair the speaker intends the same false belief, expects the same result, and produces the same harm. But our judgment is sharply different.

I want to know whether that asymmetry can actually be justified. Give me the strongest principle you can find that distinguishes false assertion from merely engineering a false inference — maybe something about what a speaker is entitled to, what the hearer has a right to expect, or what the words literally commit you to — and then test it honestly against messy cases: misleading answers under oath, statements that are technically true only by luck, someone trying to trap you, or cases where the false inference is made explicit. Does the principle survive, or is the ordinary distinction just an accident of how we police sentences rather than speakers? Be honest about whether we should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
