After forty years in physical chemistry, where infinity mostly meant the limiting end of ever finer finite approximations, I have retired into philosophy and become a little obsessed with Hilbert's hotel. I've agreed to open the first meeting of a retirement reading group on Cantor, finitism, and the actual infinite with a poem, and I would like your help composing it.

Please act as a poet with a working knowledge of philosophy of mathematics. Before you draft, think carefully and, if it helps, sketch in prose how each recurrence of the two refrains should shift in tone. Then write a villanelle about Hilbert's hotel. Use the two returning lines to stage the standing dispute the paradox leaves me with. One refrain should voice the hotel manager's actual-infinite conviction that there is always room, provided one is willing to accept a completed infinite collection: it should sound cheerful and harmless when it first appears, then stranger each time it returns after another act of rearrangement. The other refrain should voice the finitist's objection that the hotel never exists as a finished totality that can be counted as one thing: it should begin as a mild reservation and, by the closing quatrain, harden into the honest alternative to the manager's arithmetic.

Let the intervening lines carry the story: the hotel is full; a single guest arrives and each occupant moves from room n to room n+1; then a coach arrives with countably many passengers and each occupant moves from room n to room 2n so that all the odd-numbered rooms stand free; and somewhere in the middle tercets the poem should make plain that the manager's hospitality and the odd fact that a whole can be matched to one of its proper parts are two faces of the same thing. In the closing quatrain, bring the two refrains together without resolving them, so the reader is left holding the real choice between completed infinite collections with their counterintuitive arithmetic and a finitism that denies the hotel exists as a completed whole at all. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mathematics would not wince, should show the idea rather than define it, and should leave someone new to the material with the paradox rather than a verse summary.
