I'm a journalist who writes about ideas at the point where a thinker's big claim meets the historical record, and I'm working on a piece about a specific test of that. The claim is Hannah Arendt's banality-of-evil thesis. Arendt reported on Adolf Eichmann's 1961 trial in Jerusalem and argued that what made Eichmann monstrous was not sadism or ideological fanaticism but something closer to the opposite: an inability to think from the standpoint of another person, a life conducted in bureaucratic clichés and careerist self-absorption. The general claim she drew from the case was that modern evil can be carried out by people who are, in a disturbing sense, thoughtless and ordinary.

The development I want to put to the thesis is this: later researchers, above all Bettina Stangneth in Eichmann Before Jerusalem, reconstructed the so-called Sassen interviews, recorded conversations Eichmann held in Argentina in the 1950s with a circle of former Nazis and sympathizers. In those conversations Eichmann presents himself not as the hollow transport administrator of the Jerusalem dock but as an ideologically committed man who took pride in his role. Stangneth's conclusion is that the unthinking-bureaucrat persona was in part a courtroom performance, and that the historical Eichmann was more deliberate, more committed, and more self-aware than Arendt took him to be.

What I'm trying to sort out is what this does to the philosophical argument, not just to the biography. Does the new evidence kill the banality-of-evil thesis, or does it only show that Arendt was wrong about how thoughtless this particular man was while leaving the deeper claim about organizational evil intact? Or is the thesis now stranger than it first looked: evil can be both ideological and banal, and the self-deception of a boastful Eichmann is itself a form of thoughtlessness? I'd like the piece to identify exactly which premise of Arendt's argument the new material puts under pressure, say whether a repaired version of the thesis remains defensible, and spell out what that weaker or revised version would claim.

Please structure the response in five sections: The argument (~250 words), The new development (~200 words), Where the evidence bites (~250 words), What survives a repair (~300 words), Bottom line for the piece (~150 words).
