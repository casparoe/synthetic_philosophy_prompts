In the transhumanist-adjacent corners of the rationalist blogs I read, I keep meeting the hedonistic imperative — the project of using biotechnology to abolish suffering itself, across sentient life, rather than merely treating its causes or blunting its worst intensities — defended through a single analogy. Suffering in sentient organisms, the argument runs, is like an inherited disease: it was kept around because pain and fear once performed signalling and avoidance functions, but it now persists as a liability, misfires at intensities and in environments evolution never anticipated, and a mature civilisation would no more leave it in place than it would leave smallpox in place merely because smallpox is part of nature. I can feel the analogy doing a lot of under-the-surface work. It makes engineering out the capacity to suffer look like eradication rather than alteration; it makes opponents look like people romanticising a pathology; it borrows the moral clarity of public health for something far less conservative than a vaccination programme. I want an audit of how far it actually holds. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the pathogen, the symptoms, the host, the natural infection, the vaccine, the eradication programme. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the hedonistic-imperative conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and say what the analogy quietly imports from disease that the target lacks — pathology, harm without offsetting function, a healthy baseline to return to, an external and eliminable cause, something else. The question I need answered is whether the conclusion survives once those imported features are set aside: does the analogy license abolishing the capacity to suffer, or only something weaker — and if the latter, say what the weaker conclusion is. If there is a better analogy available, give it and say what it licenses. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate the question. Use British English throughout.
