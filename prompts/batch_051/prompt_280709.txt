For the past several years I have been defending a position I have come to think of as neo-Lockean property theory for personal data. In the Second Treatise of Government, chapter V, Locke argues that a person has property in their own body and in the labor of their hands; when they mix that labor with something previously unowned, the thing becomes theirs. I have been extending this argument to the digital sphere. The behavioral, biometric, and relational facts that platforms collect about us—where we click, how long we hesitate, whom we contact, how our faces respond to a stimulus—are not, I argue, unowned raw materials sitting in nature. They are traces of our activity, produced through our embodied and cognitive labor, even when the production is effortless or even involuntary on our part. Therefore, I have argued, they should be treated as belonging to us in something like the way Lockeans treat the fruits of labor.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this position. I do not want only the familiar objections—that Locke’s sufficiency proviso fails for intangible goods, that data can be used by many without depletion, or that the collecting firm adds its own labor to the raw facts. Instead, I want a map of the view’s exposure. Which possible legal test cases, economic arrangements, moral intuitions, or technological developments would undermine the neo-Lockean position, and which would merely force qualification? Which of these should a committed Lockean concede as genuine costs, and which should they dismiss as category mistakes? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, I would like you to distinguish evidence that would show the view collapses into a rival—data-as-relation, say, or property as a mere bundle of use-rights—from evidence that would only show the view needs auxiliary principles. And I would like you to say honestly whether the view is well insulated, and whether that insulation is a vice or a virtue.
