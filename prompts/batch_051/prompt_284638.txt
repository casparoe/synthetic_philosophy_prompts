I direct the model-review section of a regional flood-management authority. We evaluate hydrologic and hydraulic models submitted by consultants for three purposes: regulatory floodplain maps, levee certification, and reservoir-release decisions. The review team consists of five engineers; we can spend about two working days per model submission, and we cannot call on a mathematician for most cases. We frequently see cases where a small change in a design-storm input or a roughness parameter shifts the predicted 100-year flood extent across hundreds of properties. Consultants sometimes argue that because a model is calibrated to observed events, its deterministic output should be accepted; at other times they argue that the system is chaotic, so the maps are meaningless and no one can object to their assumptions. I need a reusable procedure to decide when a model prediction is trustworthy enough for the particular regulatory decision it supports.

Use this passage as your conceptual starting point. In Science and Method, in the chapter on chance, Henri Poincaré observes that for certain systems a very small difference in initial conditions can produce a very great difference in final phenomena, and that prediction then becomes impossible. He illustrates the idea with the thought that meteorologists are uneasy because an error of a tenth of a degree somewhere might be enough to prevent them from predicting a cyclone. Do not quote the passage back at me; translate it into tests and decision rules.

Write the procedure as a checklist or flowchart in words. It should include:

- the questions to ask, in order, about model sensitivity, ensemble spread, validation history, numerical stability, and decision thresholds;
- what counts as an adequate answer to each question;
- where the procedure stops with a no, and what the reviewer should do instead;
- where the procedure must hand the decision to someone because the question concerns values, local risk tolerance, or political priorities rather than model quality;
- how to distinguish chaos-like sensitive dependence from ordinary parameter uncertainty, calibration error, and numerical instability.

Then apply the procedure to three cases from our files:

1. A consultant submits a single deterministic 100-year floodplain model calibrated to three historical flood events. The model is stable under changes in time-step size, but the flood extent shifts substantially when Manning's n is varied within published ranges.
2. During an incoming storm, an ensemble reservoir-inflow forecast tracks observations well for 36 hours and then the members diverge enormously, with peak inflows ranging from 50 to 300 cubic metres per second. Our operators must decide whether to begin pre-releasing water.
3. An estuarine marsh-loss model used to evaluate a development permit shows that as sea-level rise is increased in 1 cm increments, wetland area changes gradually until a threshold is crossed, after which the model produces a sudden collapse.

Make sure at least one of the worked cases ends with an answer my team would not like. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will leave unresolved, and which judgments it will merely make look mechanical.
