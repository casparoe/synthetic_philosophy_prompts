I'm a new TA marking my first batch of short-answer papers on Smith's Wealth of Nations and I've gone round and round on this one, so I'd like a second opinion on the mark. The question I set was:

Smith writes, in the opening of Book IV, Chapter VII, "Of Colonies": "THE interest which occasioned the first settlement of the different European colonies in America and the West Indies was not altogether so plain and distinct as that which directed the establishment of those of ancient Greece and Rome." Explain what Smith means by "not altogether so plain and distinct," and how that contrast with the ancient colonies sets up his later argument about Britain's American empire.

Here's the student's answer, exactly as submitted:

The passage turns on a contrast Smith draws between ancient and modern colonization. For the Greeks, the motive was plain: the mother city had a small territory, so when the population grew too large for it, part of the people were sent out to settle elsewhere. The colony was treated as an emancipated child, free to govern itself, make its own laws, and fight its own wars. Rome's motive was also plain and distinct, though different: Roman colonies were plantations that served as garrisons in conquered provinces and gave land to the landless poor, but they remained under the authority of the mother city. Smith's point in saying the European motive was "not altogether so plain and distinct" is that the European settlement of America arose from no such necessity or evident utility, but mainly from the search for gold and silver and later from the confused projects of statesmen and merchants. This matters for his later argument because, on Smith's account, the British Empire in America was built on the same kind of confusion: the monopoly of the colonial trade diverts capital from better employments and enriches a small class of merchants at the expense of the rest of the nation, so that Britain ends up bearing the whole cost of defending colonies from which it derives no real benefit. Smith therefore rejects empire as such, and urges Britain to give up the American colonies immediately as the only rational way to avoid further loss.
