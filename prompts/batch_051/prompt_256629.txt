I recently spent an evening arguing with a friend about the absent-minded driver problem, and we ended up talking past each other, so I am hoping for a careful unpacking of the conceptual issue rather than just the calculation. The setup we used was the standard one: a driver on a highway intends to take the second of two consecutive exits. He knows that taking the first exit leaves him at a dead end worth 0, taking the second exit gets him home worth 4, and missing both means a long detour worth 1. The catch is that he is absent-minded: whenever he reaches an intersection, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second exit, so whatever rule he follows at one intersection he will also follow at the other. If he commits to always exiting, he gets 0; if he commits to always continuing, he gets 1. But if he commits to exiting with probability 1/3 at each intersection, his expected payoff is 4/3, higher than either deterministic plan.

What my friend and I kept circling around is why this should count as rational, and what the puzzle is really showing. At whichever intersection the driver actually finds himself, one of the two available actions is conditionally dominated: at the first exit, continuing dominates exiting, and at the second exit, exiting dominates continuing. The mixed strategy therefore looks as though it deliberately sometimes takes a conditionally dominated action at a location the driver might actually occupy. My friend took this to mean that the randomized plan is only optimal in an artifact of the model, and that a rational driver should simply resolve to keep going and then exit at what is in fact the second exit. I want to defend the idea that the driver's inability to distinguish the intersections changes what it even means for an action to be dominated, but I had trouble making the point precise.

Could you explain, first, in intuitive terms why the randomized plan outperforms both deterministic plans even though it appears to throw away value at each individual intersection; second, whether and in what sense the driver at an intersection would be rational to follow the 1/3-exit plan rather than deviating from it; and third, what general lesson this problem is usually taken to illustrate about decision-making under imperfect recall, self-locating ignorance, or dynamic consistency? I am less interested in the algebra of the maximization than in the conceptual diagnosis of where my friend's reasoning and the planning-time reasoning come apart.
