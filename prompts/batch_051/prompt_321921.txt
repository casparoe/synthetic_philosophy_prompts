An insurance co-op is designing an internal capital-allocation bot with a strict anti-Buridan rule: if two project proposals come out within $x of each other in expected value, the bot is not allowed to choose between them on its own. It has to flag the tie and open a week-long auction where members bid their own dividend shares—burned on use—to push the decision one way. The board thinks this avoids the absurdity in the passage in Spinoza's Ethics, Part II, Proposition 49, Scholium, where a man equally hungry and thirsty and equally distant from food and drink would perish rather than choose. Walk through how a rule like this actually behaves in practice: what it does to which proposals get submitted, how members bid strategically, where the first sophisticated gaming shows up, and whether the failure modes can be patched without reintroducing the original problem.
