I'm in a reading group and got into it with a friend over William James's essay "The Will to Believe." The part we kept coming back to is near the start, where he grants that objective evidence and certitude are fine ideals, but says on this actual planet they're almost never there exactly when we need them; so if we tried to wait for proof before any big commitment, we'd never decide anything that matters. My friend says this leads to a ridiculous conclusion and then uses it to prove James refutes himself. I can't find the flaw.

His argument goes like this:

(1) James says our passional nature may decide whenever a genuine option can't be settled on intellectual grounds.
(2) A genuine option has to be live, forced, and momentous.
(3) Liveness is just a feeling of plausibility, and feelings are shaped by passional nature, habit, wanting, etc.
(4) So if you want a proposition badly enough, you can make it live for you; there's no external test for liveness.
(5) Forcedness and momentousness are easy to arrange too: frame it as P or not-P and imagine the stakes as huge.
(6) Evidence never definitively settles the big questions, at least not in time for decision.
(7) Therefore James licenses believing whatever you want to believe, just because you want to.
(8) But that erases the line between faith and wishfull thinking, which is absurd and also exactly what Clifford warned about.
(9) So James's principle is self-defeating.

I'd rather keep James than accept that, but I'm not sure which step I'm allowed to reject. Is the flaw in step (4), or does rejecting it force me to adopt some weird account of what makes an option live? What exactly am I committed to if I want to save the principle?
