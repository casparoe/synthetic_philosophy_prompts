I'm writing a piece for a philosophy-and-film workshop on advance directives and the authority of the earlier self, and I want a close critical reading of the 2014 film Still Alice (based on Lisa Genova's novel) through that debate. The philosophical crux I want the reading to hold onto is whether the considered wishes of a person's earlier, competent self should govern what happens to the later, impaired self, or whether the experienced interests of the person as she now is should take priority. The film gives this a hard dramatic form: Alice, a linguistics professor with early-onset Alzheimer's, records a video in which her earlier self instructs her future self to take a lethal dose of sleeping pills once she can no longer answer a set of personal questions; later, when that future self finds and watches the video, she works through its instructions with difficulty, finds the pills, and is on the verge of swallowing them when her caregiver's arrival interrupts her and she drops them, forgetting what she was doing. I want the reading to take the film's particulars and the philosophical positions equally seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other. Does the film ultimately vindicate precedent autonomy — the idea that the earlier Alice's judgment about what her whole life should mean keeps binding force — or does it quietly side with the later Alice's present interests, given that the later Alice still seems capable of reaching for something like love even as language fails her? Press the details that ought to matter: that the directive can succeed only if the later self retains enough executive control to carry it out; that the later Alice's failure is not refusal but the very loss the directive anticipated; and that the film may be prying apart dignity as the earlier self conceives it from well-being as the later self actually lives it. Where does the film complicate the tidier positions in the philosophical debate, and what does that complication add that the arguments alone do not? Keep the whole reading under 900 words.
