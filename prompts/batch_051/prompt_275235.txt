I keep running into an asymmetry in how I feel about existence and nonexistence, and I want somone to either fix it or tell me it's fine. If a couple has a child knowing it will have a short and extremely painful life, I'm confident they've done something wrong. But if a couple just doesn't have a child — one who would have had a long and happy life — I don't think they've done anything wrong, and I don't think they've failed anyone. Yet both decisions compare a possible person's existence to their nonexistence; the only difference is whether the life would have been bad or good. Is there a single principle that delivers both verdicts, or am I just refusing to count happiness that was never lost? Here's the part that matters to me: strong longtermism says our biggest moral priority should be making sure there's a flourishing future with vast numbers of lives. It seems to need the idea that good lives that don't happen are a kind of loss, or at least that creating good lives is a real gain. If the ordinary asymmetry holds, does longtermism collapse? If it doesn't hold, why are we all so comfortable with people choosing childlessness? I want the strongest principle you can find and an honest verdict on whether to keep the asymmetry, reject it, or qualify it.
