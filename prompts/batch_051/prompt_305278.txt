I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on Kant’s theory of peace and its relation to nuclear deterrence. I am drafting a paper that reads Stanley Kubrick’s Dr. Strangelove (1964) through Kant’s Toward Perpetual Peace, and I would appreciate critical comments on the reading before I build the paper around it.

The reading centers on the last of the preliminary articles, where Kant argues that even in war a state may not employ methods of hostility that would make mutual confidence impossible in a future peace — his examples are assassins, poisoners, breach of capitulation, and incitement to treason. I want to treat the film’s Doomsday Machine as the limiting case of this principle: an automated, omnicidal retaliation whose whole point is that no further intention, restraint, or relation of trust can survive once the mechanism is set in motion. But the film seems more interesting than a mere illustration, because Kant’s article is framed for conduct within an ongoing war, whereas the Doomsday Machine is a peacetime deterrent — and, absurdly, a secret one, so it cannot even do the deterring it is meant to do. I therefore want to ask whether the article can be extended to peacetime deterrence postures without distortion, and whether the film in fact shows that the publicity, republican accountability, and human control on which Kant’s larger argument depends are precisely what the machinery of modern war dissolves.

I would like your comments on three points: (i) where the reading fits the film, and where it has to strain against or ignore what is on screen; (ii) whether the film should be read as vindicating, satirizing, or forcing a revision of the Kantian principle; and (iii) whether the film contains a deeper point — about automatism, secrecy, or rational administration — that Kant’s framework in Perpetual Peace cannot register, and that an honest reader of the film should bring out even at the cost of the proposed reading.
