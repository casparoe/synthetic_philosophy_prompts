I am a bioethics PhD student who sits on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and I want to check whether our past decisions on advance directives commit us to more than we realize. Our precedents: (1) A woman with early Alzheimer's signed, while competent, a directive refusing a course of intravenous antibiotics if she later developed pneumonia in advanced dementia; when that occurred she was calm and showed no respiratory distress, but IV treatment would have required repeated restraint, and we honored the refusal. (2) A man left minimally conscious by a stroke had made only a passing remark to his wife about not wanting to live in such a state; we declined to withdraw artificial nutrition and hydration. (3) A woman with bipolar disorder wrote a directive refusing mechanical ventilation during a severe depressive episode, while judged competent, and then formally revoked it two years later when stable; we accepted the revocation. (4) A man with dementia had a written directive requesting every life-prolonging intervention, but he now cries out and struggles during treatment; we overrode the directive rather than subject him to prolonged restraint and sedation. The new patient is a woman with advanced dementia whose earlier directive refuses oral antibiotics for a urinary tract infection. She now has a UTI causing fever and intermittent agitation; she cooperates when the medication is offered and seems calmer afterward, and a short course would likely return her to her prior contented state. If we want to be consistent with our precedents, how should we decide? Which precedent is the closest analogue? Identify the several principles that fit our track record, such as precedent autonomy, current experiential welfare, or refusal/demand asymmetry; say what each implies here; offer your best guess with reasons; and specify one further case that would most cleanly distinguish the leading contenders. Keep your answer to 450 words at most.
