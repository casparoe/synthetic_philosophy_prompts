I'm on the compliance committee of an international NGO that runs maternal health clinics in several countries, and we're stuck on a case that's exposing a real split on the committee. One of our country directors paid the secondary-school fees (about $400) for the daughter of a mid-level health-ministry official while we were waiting on a permit for a new clinic. The director says the timing is being overread: the official is a longtime family friend, back-to-school gift-giving is normal and expected there, refusing would have been a serious insult, and no explicit promise was ever made. Our headquarters policy says any benefit to an official or their family is bribery, full stop, and half the committee wants to fire him. The other half says applying that rule here is exactly the kind of Western anti-corruption imperialism our organization claims to reject, and notes that the local staff association's own guidance treats occasional gifts under $500 to officials' families as legitimate relationship-maintenance.

We need to circulate a short decision memo to the board by friday: fire him, keep him with a warning and revise our guidance, or something in between. The memo should actually say what makes a transfer corrupt—is it the intent, the reciprocity, the secrecy, the fact that the recipient holds public office, the harm to third parties, something else?—and whether this kind of transaction should be judged by one universal policy or by local norms about gifts, patronage, and social obligation. It should also engage seriously with the strongest argument on the side we don't end up on, since whichever way we go, half the committee will be furious and will quote the memo back at us.
