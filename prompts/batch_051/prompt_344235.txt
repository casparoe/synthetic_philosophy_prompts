I am on my university's promotions committee and am responsible for drafting the written recommendation to be circulated before our vote in three weeks, and I would like you to draft it. The candidate is an associate professor being considered for promotion to full professor. There is no question of serious misconduct, and the candidate clearly meets the stated criteria for scholarship, teaching, and service. The committee is split over a two-year pattern of small failures. The candidate is routinely ten or fifteen minutes late to meetings and classes, misses internal deadlines by a day or two, and repeatedly makes small commitments—a letter of recommendation promised by a given date, comments on a junior colleague's draft, an agreement to sit on a thesis defense—which then go unfulfilled, usually with a brief apology and no acknowledgment that a promise was broken. Each episode, taken alone, is the sort of lapse everyone commits; cumulatively, several members argue, they amount to a settled unreliability and a quiet disregard for colleagues and students. Others respond that promotion must rest on scholarship, teaching, and service, not punctuality, and that turning minor lapses into a moral verdict is institutional pettiness. I have recently watched a long professional collaboration dissolve under just this kind of accumulated small breach, so I now suspect the issue is neither trivial nor safely ignored; I want the memo to take it seriously rather than split the difference. Please write a short recommendation with an actionable bottom line—promote, defer with conditions, or deny—and include, where relevant, a concrete time frame or conditions. The reasoning should work through the question the committee keeps circling: whether promises too small to insist on individually still bind, or whether their moral force emerges only in the aggregate; whether punctuality and promise-keeping are one kind of obligation or two; and what an institution is actually doing when it makes such a pattern material to promotion. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side its full weight before explaining why the recommendation goes the other way.
