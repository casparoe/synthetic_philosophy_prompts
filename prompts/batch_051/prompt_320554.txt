Since I retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have been attending a small philosophy discussion group. At our last meeting, two members—Daniel, a former engineer, and Marta, a retired schoolteacher—fell into a dispute over whether it is ever rational to give serious credence to a conspiracy theory. Daniel held that it clearly can be; Marta held that, as a class, such theories merit a strong default of disbelief. I found myself unable to referee them, partly because both seemed to be scoring real points while getting further apart. I reproduce their exchange as best I can.

(1) Daniel began by saying it is a historical fact that real conspiracies occur. The Tuskegee syphilis study hid what was being done to patients for decades; the tobacco industry ran a coordinated campaign to suppress evidence that smoking causes cancer; and Watergate was a criminal conspiracy inside the White House. Given that, he argued, ruling out a conspiracy hypothesis simply because it is a conspiracy hypothesis is a genetic fallacy—one would be rejecting a claim because of its source or label rather than on the evidence.

(2) Marta replied that no one denies conspiracies occur. The problem, she said, is that contemporary conspiracy theories have a characteristic structure. They disqualify official sources in advance, so no official denial or investigation can count. And they treat the absence of direct evidence as evidence of a cover-up, which makes them immune to refutation. A hypothesis that cannot be disconfirmed is a poor bet, so even if some conspiracy theories turn out true, the sensible prior against any particular one should be very low.

(3) Daniel responded that those two features—distrust of official sources and persistence in the face of apparent disconfirmation—are also present in the early stages of many theories that turned out to be right. Continental drift was rejected by the geological establishment before plate tectonics became accepted, and the germ theory of disease had to fight medical orthodoxy. Scientists routinely distrust received opinion and persist in the face of anomalies. If self-sealing were a decisive epistemic vice, he said, one would have to condemn much of the history of science.

(4) Marta answered that the difference lies in the corrective mechanism. In science, persistence happens inside a community with shared methods that force the issue: predictions, replication, and the standing possibility of failure. Conspiracy theories, by contrast, usually reject the very institutions that could correct them. Moreover, she added, the verifiable conspiracies Daniel cited were not established by refusing evidence; they were established by documents, whistleblowers, admissions, and investigations—exactly the kinds of evidence that a self-sealing theory treats as contaminated.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is right about which specific points, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
