Quinn's self-torturer has come to look to me less like a cautionary tale about weakness of will and more like a counterexample to a specific axiom of rational choice. The case, as I use it, runs like this. An agent is fitted with a device whose current can be raised step by step, each increment so small that no single change registers. The agent is paid for each increment an amount chosen so that, at every step, accepting the next increment seems worth it on its own; but after many steps the agent is in severe and lasting discomfort and would pay a great deal to return to the initial setting. The conclusion I draw is that the agent is fully rational at each step, that there is no determinate point at which the rational agent should have stopped, and that rational preferences can therefore fail transitivity in a distinctive way: not by directly cycling through a triple of options, but because pairwise preferability across adjacent settings fails to transmit to a preference ordering over distant settings that are themselves clearly ranked.

I want a map of what would count against this position, not just the familiar objections someone would give on first hearing it. What would have to be true for the transitivity condition on rational preference to survive this case unscathed, and how plausible are those things? Which formal results (about money pumps, Dutch books, the representation of preference by utility, or the stability of agency over time), which empirical regularities in actual sequences of marginal choice, and which variants of the setup (precommitment before the sequence; a pain dimension the agent can introspect with precision; non-pain dimensions such as time discounting or temperature; smaller or larger numbers of steps) would do the most damage? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish the objections a defender of the position should itself concede are genuine costs from those it should dismiss as question-begging or irrelevant. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated so thoroughly that nothing could count against it, and whether such insulation would be a defect in a claim of this kind. I would personally be most moved by a demonstration that this intransitive reading cannot support a stable, temporally extended agent, or by a version of the case in which every individual step is not merely tolerable but affirmatively preferred and the intransitive conclusion still seems to me false; I would be least moved by the bare reminder that intransitive preferences make standard decision theory more complicated.
