I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been trying to understand a line of argument that has been bothering me for months. The claim, associated especially with Daniel Dennett, is that the notion of qualia—private, intrinsic, ineffable phenomenal properties—is not merely unsupported but in some sense incoherent. One of the most vivid moves in that argument involves two coffee tasters. The first, call him Chase, discovers one day that a coffee he has enjoyed for years no longer tastes good to him, and he says his taste has changed while the coffee has not. The second, Sanborn, makes the same discovery but insists the coffee itself now tastes different to him, while his standards have stayed exactly the same. Dennett treats this as an embarrassment for qualia realists: there is supposed to be a determinate fact about whether the change is in the taster's evaluative reaction or in the qualitative character of the experience, yet the two stories seem observationally indistinguishable from the inside.

I want to know whether this is actually a deep problem for qualia, or whether the example only seems decisive because it misdescribes what a qualia realist should be committed to. It seems to me the realist can say: the two tasters are making different claims about where a stable baseline lies, and we can test those claims by asking whether Chase still recognizes the old taste when he encounters it in another context or whether Sanborn can be brought to acknowledge that his standards have shifted. If so, the fact that the two reports can coexist is not evidence that there is no fact of the matter; it is just evidence that people can be wrong about their own experience in familiar ways.

What I would like is a careful statement of exactly which premise a qualia theorist must accept for the Chase–Sanborn case to be genuinely troublesome, and which versions of the qualia view would evade the argument. I am less interested in whether Dennett's larger project succeeds than in whether this particular thought experiment carries the weight it is sometimes given. Please give me an analysis of the strongest version of the argument from the Chase–Sanborn case, and then say whether, on reflection, it shows that qualia are an incoherent posit, that they are merely less introspectively transparent than sometimes assumed, or that the case proves almost nothing at all. In answering, please avoid citations; I am asking for the conceptual structure, not a literature review.
