For the past few months I have been convening a small, argumentative reading group on explanation in history, and a month ago one of its members — a retired geophysicist who now writes counterfactual novels — sent round a numbered argument I have not been able to stop thinking about, because it seems to show that the idea of historical contingency cannot even be stated. We had been discussing the old proverb about the missing nail that loses, in turn, the shoe, the horse, the rider, the message, the battle, and the kingdom, and the usual gloss that one minuscule antecedent can be the difference-maker for an enormous historical outcome. His argument runs like this.

(1) A battle is a tightly coupled, chaotic system in the rough but useful sense that small local differences are amplified into large downstream differences, so the actual course of events is only one member of an enormous space of nearby courses. (2) In any such system the difference between the actual course and any alternative course is never a single difference; it is an entire bundle of mutually amplifying small differences, which means that singling out the nail as the cause is always a convenience that ignores indefinitely many other small factors no less necessary to the outcome. (3) To be warranted in saying that the nail was the difference-maker — that, had it held, the battle and the kingdom would have been otherwise — one would need to know what would have happened had the nail been different while everything else relevant stayed as it actually was. (4) But in a tightly coupled system that is precisely what one can never know, because the other relevant small factors would also have differed, and almost all of them went unrecorded. (5) Since those unrecorded factors are by definition unavailable, no one is ever warranted in asserting any historical counterfactual of this small-cause kind. (6) Yet the lesson normally drawn from the proverb is itself a historical counterfactual of exactly this kind: had the nail held, the course of history would have been importantly different. (7) Therefore the lesson of the proverb — and with it every claim about the fragility of a historical outcome — is unwarranted; the more seriously one takes the story as evidence that history is sensitive to tiny causes, the less entitled one is to draw any particular lesson from it. (8) But that is too strong: we clearly do discriminate between better- and worse-supported claims about what would have happened, and the proverb is supposed to be a paradigm of the better-supported kind, not an example of overconfidence.

I should say where I stand. I would most like to keep the claims in (1) and (8): that there is something genuinely important about the way small causes ramify through a chaotic historical process, and that some counterfactual claims about history are nonetheless better supported than others. I cannot see which step to reject, and I suspect that every available rejection costs more than it first appears, either for the practice of historical explanation or for our right to take the proverb seriously.

Please set out your response in four sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths. "Where the argument comes apart" (about 600 words): name the step or inference you take to fail, and say whether it is a false premise or an invalid inference. "Why the step tempts us" (about 300 words): explain the pull of the failed step and why a serious person would have written it down. "What rejecting it costs" (about 400 words): state what a defender of the argument would have to surrender, and say which of my preferred claims survive and in what qualified form. "The salvage" (about 250 words): restate a defensible claim about contingency in history that keeps the proverb's insight without the overreach.
