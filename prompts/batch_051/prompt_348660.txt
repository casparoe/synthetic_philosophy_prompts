I am a retired geologist, and in the years since I left the field I have been reading political philosophy, mostly about who may act when the rules of an institution fall silent. The question has turned out to be less abstract than I expected: I sit on the twelve-member Executive Council of a small international scientific society, and we are now in what I can only call an interregnum, though our Constitution never contemplated one. Under that Constitution, the Council elects the President from among its own members by a two-thirds majority; the President serves a two-year term; a quorum is eight; and no payment may leave the Society's account without the signatures of both the President and the Treasurer. The only provision that might conceivably reach our situation allows the Treasurer to sign alone when the President is temporarily unable to act, and it was plainly drafted for illness or travel rather than for a presidency that has simply lapsed.

That lapsed presidency is precisely the problem. The outgoing President's term expired at the end of June, and the Council is now evenly divided between two candidates, neither of whom can assemble eight votes. One faction has stopped attending meetings, declaring that in the absence of a lawful President any further meeting is a nullity; their absence leaves the remaining six without a quorum. In the meantime, salaries for our two staff members have become due, contracts for the spring field conference must be signed or cancelled within a month, and the bank will not release funds without a President's signature. Some of the attending members propose that we elect an acting President among ourselves by majority vote and pass only an emergency budget, on the ground that the Society's continued existence is prior to its written rules. Others reply that any act by an unrepresentative minority would be illegitimate and would teach every future faction that whoever shows up may govern. A third proposal would have the Treasurer treat the lapse as a case of temporary inability and sign alone, but several of us regard that as a strained reading that would permanently weaken the two-signature requirement.

I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion to all Council members before Thursday's emergency meeting, and I ask you to draft it. The memo should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame; it should engage honestly with the strongest argument for the position it rejects, rather than merely repeating that unconstitutional action is invalid; and it should distinguish between what may legitimately be done now, what must wait for a properly constituted Council, and how the Constitution might be amended afterwards so that a gap of this kind cannot arise again.
