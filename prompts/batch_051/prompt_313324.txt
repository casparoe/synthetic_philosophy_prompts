For several years I have been trying, and failing, to learn a second language. Every Sunday evening I make a plan for the week: I block out forty-five minutes each weeknight, and I imagine, with real satisfaction, how much better my French will be a year from now if I simply keep the appointment with myself. Then every weekday evening, at the scheduled hour, I pick some small immediate thing instead—answering messages, watching one more episode, running an errand I have been avoiding. Each time I feel genuinely angry at myself afterward and resolve to do better the next day. The cycle has become so predictable that I have started to wonder what is actually going on.

A friend with a background in economics says I am not failing at all. He claims my preferences are consistent, but that the weight I give to a benefit shrinks as the benefit gets closer in time: in the morning the long-term payoff of an hour of practice looks large, while in the evening the next forty-five minutes of ease look larger, and I simply act on whichever is largest at the moment. He also says that "future me" is, for practical purposes, a different person whom present me continually exploits, which is why the promises I make to him are worth so little. This struck me as a cleverly dressed-up excuse, but it has also unsettled me. If at every decision point I do what I most prefer at that moment, in what sense, if any, can my behavior be called irrational? And if I precommit—for instance, by paying in advance for a tutor who will arrive at the scheduled hour and charge me whether or not I use the session—am I restoring my rationality, or am I allowing one version of myself to coerce another version who has a legitimate present-time point of view?

I am not looking for productivity tips. I want a clear account of what is going on in this kind of situation and what I should actually do once I understand it.
