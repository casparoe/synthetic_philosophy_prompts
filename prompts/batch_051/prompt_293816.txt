Last night I had a long argument with a friend who has convinced himself that a repeated coin-flip bet — +50% on heads, −40% on tails — is a sure route to wealth, because the arithmetic expectation is +5% per flip. I tried to explain the distinction between the ensemble average and the time average: the mean wealth of a large ensemble of parallel players grows at 5% per flip because a few astronomically lucky trajectories dominate the average, but a single player who reinvests the entire bankroll each flip experiences the time-average growth rate, sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.949, a contraction of roughly 5% per flip, and is driven toward zero with probability one as the game is repeated. This is the non-ergodicity point: the ensemble average and the time average diverge, and an individual lives out one trajectory, not the mean of all of them. He replied that I was letting prudence override the mathematics.

I would like that argument turned into a piece of satire. Write a mock investment prospectus, around 600 words, for a fund selling exactly this repeated-bet strategy to retail clients, written in a deadpan, formally formatted style with an investment thesis, a fee schedule, and fine print. The humor should come from getting the distinction right rather than from gesturing at it. Have the body of the document tout the +5% arithmetic expectation and the "risk control" of diversifying across a large ensemble of client accounts, while a starred footnote or a fine-print section quietly concedes that the time-average growth rate is negative, that no individual client ever receives the ensemble mean, and that the handful of enormous winners who pull up the average should not reassure the typical client. Include at least one passage in which the fund attributes a client's ruin to "idiosyncratic path dependence" rather than to the design of the product. The piece should be funny to someone who knows the ergodicity literature and comprehensible to someone who has never encountered the distinction before, and by the end a reader should have a working sense of why expected value misleads a single compounding player in a non-ergodic setting.
