I'm preparing a panel interview for a philosophy of psychology podcast focused on moral cognition. The guest is a moral psychologist who defends a specific and fairly strong methodological thesis: the empirical literature on "moral bias" is not entitled to the word bias, because classifying a judgment pattern as biased requires a standard of correct or rational moral judgment, and in the moral domain that standard is itself contested. On this view, findings such as framing effects, order effects, and omission–commission asymmetries in moral judgment are better described as expressions of different evaluative preferences or different weighting functions, and much of what passes for "debiasing" in moral psychology is really the surreptitious substitution of the researcher's first-order moral commitments for the subject's.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate this position and instead press on its weak points. I want at least one question on each of the following fronts, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give:

- Cases in which subjects, after reflection or after the frame is made salient, disown their own frame-sensitive judgment and describe it as a mistake. If the subject herself supplies the standard, does that disarm the objection that the researcher is imposing one?
- Whether reflective equilibrium, or "wide" reflective endorsement after learning about the psychological mechanisms behind one's judgment, can serve as a non-question-begging criterion for calling some of these patterns biases rather than preferences.
- Whether her argument, if generalized, would undermine talk of cognitive biases in non-moral domains as well, and whether she accepts that consequence or draws a principled line between the moral and the merely epistemic.
- The normative asymmetry in many debiasing findings: subjects often endorse and retain a debiased response for descriptive or logical tasks but not for especially charged moral cases. What does she make of the cases where subjects do come to reject their initial moral response?
- Whether "bias" is doing more than rhetorical work in the moral domain, and whether a discipline that abandoned the term could still pursue the interventions and improvements it cares about.

Please write the questions so that each one is sharp enough to force her to commit to a position: avoid generic prompts like "how would you respond to critics?" and instead formulate the tension concretely. For each question, include the likely line of reply in the follow-up, so the interview would stay adversarial even if she gives her best answer immediately.
