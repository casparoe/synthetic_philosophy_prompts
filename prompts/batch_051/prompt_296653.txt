A friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about a single puzzle in the law, and we could not close the gap. I want your help figuring out whether the asymmetry that started the argument is actually defensible, or whether we were both just rationalizing a practice that has no principled shape.

Here is the asymmetry as I posed it. A delivery driver and the company that owns the truck have each done everything a reasonable person could do. The truck has a latent brake defect that no ordinary inspection would have caught. The driver loses control and kills a pedestrian. The thought of criminally punishing the driver strikes nearly everyone as outrageous: there was no fault. But the company can be fined, and the manufacturer held liable, and very few people regard that as outrageous, even though the same death, the same absence of fault, and the same causal chain run through all three. So fault is treated as close to an absolute condition for one kind of legal response, and as close to irrelevant for another.

The obvious attempts to justify this seem to me to fail under pressure. One says criminal punishment condemns while fines merely allocate costs; but some regulatory offenses are criminal, some strict-liability crimes carry imprisonment, and a large civil penalty or the loss of a license can be more ruinous than many criminal sentences. Another says a corporation cannot be at fault the way a person can; but the fine ultimately lands on owners, shareholders, and employees, who are persons. Another says strict liability gives regulated parties an incentive to be more careful than reasonable care already requires; but that is an argument for demanding a higher standard of precaution, not for punishing conduct that no one could have prevented.

I want the strongest principle you can find that would generate this asymmetry: fault as a necessary condition of criminal punishment, but not of civil or regulatory penalties. Then test it against the hard cases, including strict-liability crimes that carry prison time, civil penalties large enough to destroy a livelihood, and the question of who really pays when an organization is fined. Tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, reversed, or given up.
