I'm building a turn-based strategy game where NPCs have their own knowledge bases and the whole design is that they're suppose to reason with finite time and working memory. The problem: if I model 'knows' with the standard possible-worlds modal logic (S5), every NPC knows every tautology and every consequence of whatever it knows, instantly — so they play like they've already proved everything. I want knowing to be something they actually have to derive step by step. Sort these candidate fixes into tiers by how much you'd trust each for a resource-bounded game agent, with a sentence on why each lands where it does: (1) syntactic database: knowledge is just stored sentences plus inference rules you actually run; (2) Fagin–Halpern awareness: you know φ only if φ holds across your possible worlds and you're 'aware' of φ; (3) Rantala-style impossible worlds: add worlds where complex formulas take nonstandard truth values so closure fails; (4) algorithmic knowledge: you know φ iff your actual algorithm can compute or verify φ within its complexity bound; (5) justification logic: knowing φ requires carrying a proof or justification for it. And if one of these is the clear winner for implementation, just say which and what tradeoffs I'd be swallowing.
