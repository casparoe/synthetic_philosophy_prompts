I'm drafting a position statement for a workshop on the idea that argumentation could be run as a competitive sport, and before circulating it I'd like a consistency check on the package of views I've landed on. My commitments are these:

(1) Argumentation is a learnable skill, and like other skills it can legitimately be assessed in structured competitions with winners and losers.
(2) The fundamental purpose of arguing is epistemic: to move arguers and, above all, an audience closer to the most accurate position on the question under discussion.
(3) Most existing competitive formats are scored by persuasion, and persuasion is a poor proxy for accuracy; such formats systematically reward rhetorical polish, evasion, and confident error, so that winning makes participants less reliable rather than more.
(4) That defect is not inherent to competition itself. A format could in principle award points for precisely the moves that improve a competent judge's epistemic position — for example, conceding when the evidence calls for it, stating the opponent's strongest case before answering it, and making the crux explicit.
(5) I would therefore have judges apply a fixed rubric of specific argumentative moves, and the rubric's central standard should be whether a move makes a well-informed judge more likely to end up closer to the truth.

I don't yet see exactly where the fault lines are, so I'd like an audit rather than a summary of the topic. Identify the five most important points of strain in this package, ordered by severity, even if some of them dissolve on inspection. For each point, say which of (1)–(5) it involves, whether the strain is genuine or merely apparent, and what the cheapest resolution would be — which commitment would have to give, and what that concession costs. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each item one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
