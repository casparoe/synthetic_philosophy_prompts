I want a working definition of 'evidence for a theory' — the relation between a fact and a scientific hypothesis, rather than evidence in the courtroom sense. The definition has to get these cases right:

1. A theory predicts a precise, not-yet-observed result, and then the observation comes in. (Obviously evidence.)
2. An old, known anomaly that a new theory explains without having been built to fit it — the usual illustration is Mercury's perihelion and general relativity, but you don't need the physics; just take any long-standing anomaly. (I think this is evidence too, but the old-evidence problem makes the standard Bayesian story awkward here.)
3. A curve fitted to a dataset and then evaluated on that same dataset. (I say not evidence.)
4. A researcher runs 30 analyses, 3 come out significant, and only those 3 get reported as evidence for the favorite hypothesis. (I say not evidence, though I'm not confident my definition will say why.)
5. A fact everyone already knew, which a new theory organizes only slightly better than its rivals. (Maybe evidence, maybe too weak — no settled view.)
6. A fact that would have raised my confidence if I'd just learned it, but I've actually known it since before I ever saw the theory. (Is this just case 2 again? If it is, I want the definition to say why.)

I suspect 'evidence' is doing two jobs: one subjective job about what would change a person's mind, and one more objective job about what bears on a theory whether or not anyone's mind changes. If you agree, give me two definitions and run all six cases through both, saying which deserves the name 'evidence.' If you disagree, give one definition and defend it. In either case, tell me the verdict you're least sure about and what would change your mind.

One more thing: seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on confirmation theory. Please don't present the claim that old evidence is fatal to Bayesianism — or, for that matter, the claim that it's a non-problem — as the consensus.
