A lecturer I heard recently summarized Grice on meaning and communication roughly as follows. Grice's topic is speaker meaning: you mean something by uttering a sentence when you intend your audience to form some response partly on the basis of recognizing that very intention. He then distinguishes what a speaker says, fixed by the conventional meaning of the words together with context, from what the speaker goes on to implicate. An implicature is the inference a hearer draws from the assumption that the speaker is following the cooperative principle, which Grice spells out as maxims of good conversation: be truthful, be informative, be relevant, and be clear. When a speaker appears to violate a maxim, the hearer reasons back to what the speaker must have meant in order to keep treating them as a good cooperative conversation partner. Thus a reference letter that dwells on a candidate's punctuality and tidy handwriting, and says nothing about his philosophy, implicates that the candidate is not much of a philosopher. Since this extra content is worked out from the assumption of cooperation rather than read off the words, it can always be withdrawn later without contradiction, which is why Grice held that implicatures are not part of the sentence's semantics at all; they are purely contextual.
