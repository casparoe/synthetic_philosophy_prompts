I chair the university committee that reviews proposals for spending the Hartley bequest, a large charitable fund whose terms require us to approve only expenditures whose expected wellbeing effects are, all things considered, an improvement. Our staff enters expected consequences in a ledger with three columns: happiness-points (positive), mild-pain-points (negative), and severe-pain-points (negative). It has long been settled among us that mild-pain-points trade one-for-one against happiness-points; the disagreement concerns how severe-pain-points should be treated. One senior colleague—the member with the strongest views about suffering—votes without written reasons, but her record over several years is consistent.

Her votes on four proposals I can reconstruct:

(a) A proposal to convert a widely used reading room into soundproof study pods. Expected effects: destruction of 2,500 happiness-points for the room's regular users, and elimination of 200 mild-pain-points among students currently distracted by noise. She voted to reject.

(b) A student-life initiative expected to create 2,000 happiness-points while causing 100 mild-pain-points, because several of its events would begin at six in the morning during finals week. She voted to approve.

(c) A medical study in which one healthy volunteer would undergo a single brief but severe pain episode—entered in the ledger as 1 severe-pain-point—in order to test a treatment expected to produce a diffuse gain of 500 happiness-points across a large patient population. She voted to reject.

(d) A proposal to convert an unused storage room into a small pain clinic, which would eliminate 5 severe-pain-points for five patients at a cost of 300 happiness-points for the staff who would lose the storage space. She voted to approve.

Two interpretations of her record are now circulating among the rest of us. On the first, she treats a severe-pain-point as a lexical veto: no amount of happiness or mild-pain relief can justify causing one, and eliminating such points enjoys lexical priority, while outside the severe-pain column she simply weighs the ordinary one-for-one sum. On the second, she treats a severe-pain-point as carrying an enormous but finite weight of roughly 1,000 happiness-points, again combined with the ordinary one-for-one treatment of the other columns. I believe both interpretations fit the record we have; the vote before us may force the issue.

Next week we consider a revised version of proposal (c). The new protocol again requires one healthy volunteer to undergo a single brief but severe pain episode, recorded as 1 severe-pain-point, but the expected diffuse benefit is now 2,000 happiness-points spread across 100,000 people—roughly two-hundredths of a happiness-point each. All other features are unchanged from the earlier study.

Please tell me: (i) how each of the two interpretations of her severe-pain-point treatment rules on the new proposal; (ii) which prior case is the closest analog and why; (iii) which vote you would advise if we want the committee's record to remain as coherent as possible; and (iv) what further case would most decisively settle which interpretation she actually holds. If you judge the four precedents to genuinely underdetermine the vote, say what principle a "Reject" vote would commit us to and what principle an "Approve" vote would commit us to, and then give your best guess.

Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...}, where the braces contain your advised vote and a short label for the principle behind it—for example, \answer{Reject — severe pain is a lexical veto}.
