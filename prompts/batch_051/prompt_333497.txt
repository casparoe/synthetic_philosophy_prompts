A colleague in my philosophy-of-physics group has been pressing the following argument, and although the conclusion is plainly intolerable, I cannot find the step that carries the blame. Each premise taken alone looks nearly definitional, yet the argument appears to show that a repeatable experimental result is impossible. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) A gauge transformation maps a representation of an electromagnetic field configuration onto another representation of the very same physical configuration; the two differ only in how the configuration is labelled.

(2) Therefore a quantity that changes value under a gauge transformation does not correspond to a real feature of the physical configuration; only gauge-invariant quantities are physically real.

(3) The electromagnetic potentials A and φ are not gauge-invariant: the substitution A → A + ∇χ, φ → φ − ∂χ/∂t leaves the electric field E and the magnetic field B unchanged, while it changes the potentials.

(4) Hence E and B are the real, local content of the electromagnetic field, and the potentials are surplus structure. In particular, wherever E and B both vanish, all local gauge-invariant data about the field vanish.

(5) In the Aharonov–Bohm arrangement, a long solenoid confines a magnetic flux to its interior. Outside the solenoid E and B vanish, but A does not. A charged particle follows a path that stays in this E = B = 0 region while enclosing the solenoid.

(6) The detected interference pattern is measurably shifted relative to the no-flux case; the shift is proportional to the line integral of A around the particle's path, which equals the enclosed magnetic flux.

(7) The flux case and the no-flux case are indistinguishable with respect to all local gauge-invariant data along the particle's path — at every point the particle visits, E and B vanish in both cases — yet the two cases are distinguished by a measurement made in a localized region of the detection screen.

(8) Therefore a locally manifested difference corresponds to no difference in local gauge-invariant field content. By (2) and (4) the two cases should count as the same physical situation along the path, while by (6) they do not. The standard reading of gauge freedom as mere surplus structure thus proves too much: it renders a real, repeatable experimental outcome physically impossible.

I am most reluctant to give up (1) and (6), since (1) seems to be what makes gauge freedom intelligible and (6) is experimentally established; but I cannot see which of the remaining steps overreaches, and I suspect that a correct diagnosis may force a revision of what is meant by the local content of a gauge theory.

Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting the offending step commit one to? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
