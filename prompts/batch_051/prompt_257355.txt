I am a novelist, and I have agreed to give a session at a literary festival on human dignity in human rights. The audience will be mostly other writers and serious general readers, not philosophers. I want to structure the session around a short passage from Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals. In the passage, Kant distinguishes between value of an ordinary sort, where something can be made good by an equivalent or substitute, and value of another sort that cannot be made good by any substitute; only the second, he says, should be called dignity. I also want to bring in Ruth Macklin's later complaint that dignity is useless in medical ethics because it either collapses into autonomy and respect for persons or does no work at all. Please plan how I should teach this disagreement rather than explain it to me: the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why, the one example or case that should carry the session and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form — I suspect they will think dignity is just a solemn word for autonomy, or else a religious relic — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, what I should openly set aside at this level, and one question I can put to them at the end to see whether they have understood the structure of the dispute rather than simply taken sides. Please also say how confident you are in your reading of the Kantian passage and single out the point you are least sure of.
