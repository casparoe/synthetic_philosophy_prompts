I've been rereading Einstein's Why Socialism? (the 1949 Monthly Review piece) for a little reading group, and I keep finding myself stuck on the passage where he says the worst evil of capitalism is that it cripples the individual. I've seen this essay passed around on rationalist forums a few times, usually with someone saying it's underrated or someone saying it's hopelessly naive, and I think the crippling passage is the part that actually does the most work but also the part I understand least.

Here's my current list of arguments he gives, or at least gestures at, for socialism/for the diagnosis that capitalism needs replacing: (1) the economic anarchy argument—private owners pursuing profit without coordination generates cyclical crises, unemployment, and waste; (2) the concentration of capital argument—private wealth tends to accumulate in few hands, and those hands then control politics, the press, and schooling, so formal democracy becomes a cover for oligarchy; (3) the crippling argument itself—capitalism forces individuals into competitive, insecure, narrow roles and stunts their intellectual and emotional development; (4) the ideological reproduction argument—capitalist institutions train people to treat the existing order as natural and to suppress independent critical thought.

I want an argument for socialism that is not in this list and not just a restatement, special case, or combination of any of them. It can be another argument Einstein actually gives in the essay that I've missed, or an extension of the crippling-the-individual line that he would have accepted given his own premises. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good—does it do work the other arguments don't, or is it the kind of thing that sounds deep but dissolves once you ask what policy it actually implies?
