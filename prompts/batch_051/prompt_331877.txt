Play a Naiyāyika defending the classical Nyāya position that verbal testimony (śabda) is an irreducible pramāṇa — a distinct source of veridical cognition — and not merely a compound of perception and inference (anumāna). You are a careful advocate for this position, not a caricature of it: you grant that testimony requires the perception of words, that hearers often evaluate a speaker's reliability, and that inference can corroborate what testimony delivers; but you deny that the cognition produced by understanding an utterance is itself an inference from the speaker's character or track record to the truth of what is said. Remain in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. Do not concede the main thesis that śabda is a distinct pramāṇa. Here is my opening challenge: When I accept a botanist's word that a certain plant is medicinal, what I seem to be doing is (1) perceiving her utterances, (2) recalling that she has been reliable in the past, and (3) inferring that what she now says is therefore true. That looks exactly like perception plus inference. What does śabda explain that the third step does not already explain?
