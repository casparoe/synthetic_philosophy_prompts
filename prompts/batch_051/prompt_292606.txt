The exam question was:

"Define loss aversion and the endowment effect, explain the standard account of how they are connected, and assess whether the endowment effect should be classified as a bias or can properly be treated as a legitimate expression of preference. Your answer should keep the empirical question and the normative question distinct."

The student wrote:

"Loss aversion is the tendency, built into prospect theory's value function, for losses to be weighted more heavily than equivalent gains, so that the value function is steeper below the reference point than above it. The endowment effect is the difference between willingness to accept and willingness to pay: in the classic mug experiments, owners demanded roughly $7 to give up a mug while non-owners would pay only about $3. The standard explanation is that ownership shifts the reference point, so that parting with the mug is coded as a loss and acquiring it as a gain, and loss aversion makes the loss loom larger, producing the gap.

It follows that the endowment effect cannot be accounted for by any rational model. Standard theory predicts that WTP and WTA should coincide, because the mug has a fixed value to the agent, and whether one owns it cannot change what it is worth. The observed divergence is therefore a bias: people are anchored to an arbitrary reference point and over-weight their current holdings. Once we recognize that they are evaluating changes rather than final states, the anomaly dissolves; rational agents would evaluate final wealth positions.

The rival view, that the endowment effect is a legitimate preference, is hard to sustain. It requires accepting that a mere change of frame—owning rather than not owning—can alter the value of an identical final allocation. But framing dependence of this kind is precisely what a genuine preference cannot exhibit; preferences are defined over consumption bundles, not over descriptions of bundles. The burden therefore rests on those who call the endowment effect a legitimate preference to explain why the canons of rational choice should be relaxed to accommodate an experimental artifact."
