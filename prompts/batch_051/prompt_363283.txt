I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I am trying to pin down the relation between two concepts that are constantly run together: Marxian exploitation and Marxian alienation. I would like you to construct a case in which a worker is alienated in all four of the dimensions identified in Marx's 1844 Manuscripts — estrangement from the product of labor, from the activity of labor, from species-being (the reduction of labor to a mere means of subsistence rather than a free expression of human capacities), and from other human beings — yet is not exploited in the technical sense: no surplus value is extracted from the worker's labor by a distinct class that owns the means of production. The case is for testing whether these two wrongs really track one another, and in particular whether ending exploitation would by itself end alienation. Constraints: the scenario must be a realistic contemporary working arrangement, not a pre-capitalist artisan economy, not a state-socialist planned economy, and not a parable; it should fit in a single paragraph; and each verdict should be generated by the objective structure of the situation rather than stipulated — do not simply report that the worker feels estranged, and do not make the absence of exploitation true by definitional fiat. After the scenario, do the check: for each of the four dimensions of alienation, identify the concrete feature of the case that produces it; for exploitation, identify the concrete feature that prevents surplus value from being extracted. Then explain which structural feature does the real work, how the case would collapse if that feature were removed, and how a Marxist who denies that full alienation can exist without exploitation would most plausibly attack the case.
