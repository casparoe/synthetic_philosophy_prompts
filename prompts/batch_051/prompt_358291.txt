That brought back the procreative beneficence argument from my undergrad bioethics module: roughly, that if you are choosing among possible children, you should select the one expected to have the best life, not merely one without a diagnosable disorder. I had always excepted it in the abstract, but now it hits differently.

The original argument seems to assume a fairly determinate ranking of embryos by expected wellbeing, because with single-gene disorders the prognosis is relatively clean. Polygenic screening is a different epistemic situation: the scores are noisy, population-averaged, weakly predictive for any individual, and they do not aggregate into a single expected life — someone has to decide how to weight a lower schizophrenia risk against a lower predicted educational-attainment score, and the clinic's ranking quietly encodes those weightings.

So here is what I want: does the procreative beneficence argument survive this shift, or does it collapse once best expected life becomes a vector of noisy, normatively loaded scores rather than a clear verdict?
