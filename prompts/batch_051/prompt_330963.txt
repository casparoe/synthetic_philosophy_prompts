I run a math circle at a high school, about 16 kids ages 16-18, and I have 90 minutes. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of Russell's paradox and the limits of naive set theory — but they shouldn't hear either name until the debrief. No lecture up front; I want the paradox to show up in the rules.

Here's the kernel I have in mind, and I'd like you to build it into a runnable game with a timeline, roles, payoffs, and a debrief. The idea is to give them physical "collections" made of paper bags and index cards: each bag has a label on the outside (like "the collection of all things with a blue dot") and cards inside naming its members. A bag is a member of itself just in case its own label is inside it. Have them sort bags into two bins — "contains itself" and "doesn't contain itself" — and score points for getting each one right, checked by looking inside. Start with easy cases so the rule feels mechanical and boring. Then introduce an innocent-looking rule: any description of the form "the collection of all ___" entitles you to a bag, and the new bag's members are exactly the bags satisfying the description. Have them, as a group, construct the bag described as "the collection of all bags that do not contain themselves" (they should write that card themselves if possible). Then make them sort the new bag into one of the two bins and score it like all the others. It should fall apart in their hands: whichever bin they pick, the scoring rule tells them their wrong until someone says the game is broken.

Flesh out the exact materials, the rules and roles (so the quiet kids have a job too), how to keep it from dragging, and a payoff structure small enough that kids stay playful but real enough that they care about being right. Include a timeline for the 90 minutes. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea: unrestricted comprehension, self-membership, the diagonal move, and where someone might try to escape (deny the bag exists; ban self-membership; reject that every bag must be in exactly one bin). Also say how the barber version is different — there you can just say "no such barber," but here the comprehension rule already promised the bag exists — and when in the debrief to bring that up. Tell me what will predictably go wrong when real kids run it and how to recover, and be honest about which part of the actual Russell paradox the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
