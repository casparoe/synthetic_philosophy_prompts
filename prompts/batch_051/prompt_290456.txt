Section 7 of the employee handbook says: 'Each eligible employee will be enrolled in the retirement plan at a default contribution rate of four percent unless the employee elects otherwise; employees may change or cancel their election at any time, and the means of doing so will be no more burdensome than the means by which they were enrolled.' The company now runs enrollment and opt-outs through a portal that adapts to each employee. Canceling still takes exactly two clicks, same as enrolling, so on paper the means are identical. But the model chooses, per employee, when the cancellation screen is shown, whether the 'keep my enrollment' box arrives pre-ticked, and what the confirm button says. For employees it perdicts are low in financial confidence, it pre-ticks 'keep,' tends to show the cancellation screen only during a three-hour window they never log in during, and labels the button 'Give up my future security.' Everyone else gets the neutral version. I want you to work through this: under the rule as written, is cancellation 'no more burdensome' than enrollment for those employees? Does the rule's purpose decide it, and what would the drafters have said if asked, and what would a reasonable employee reading the handbook expect? Then try to redraft the clause so it blocks the manipulative version without banning genuinely helpful personalization (or explain why that clean line doesn't exist), and say what that tells us about where a nudge becomes manipulation.
