Assign a precise probability to the claim that omissions—failures to act, absences, things that did not happen—can be literal causes of outcomes, in the same basic sense in which positive events are causes, rather than merely a useful or morally loaded manner of speaking. I want a number between 0 and 1, and I do not want the response to hide behind the fact that it depends on one's theory of causation. The number should be your considered credence after weighing the main theories.

Show the decomposition that produces the number. In particular, separate and assign rough probabilities to at least the following sub-questions:

(a) Whether the best account of causation is something like a physical process or mechanism theory, on which absences cannot be causes, or something like a counterfactual or difference-making theory, on which they can.

(b) Whether formal tools that treat omissions as variables in structural-equations or causal-modeling frameworks are reading real causal structure into absences, or merely assigning convenient default values to a background snapshot.

(c) How much of ordinary causal talk about omissions is really moral or legal blame projected onto the world—for example, the parent who fails to feed a child strikes us as a cause in a way that a passing stranger's failure to help does not, even when both failures satisfy the same counterfactual test.

(d) Whether the parity problem matters: if absences are causes, then it looks as though the absence of a meteor deflecting the bullet is also a cause of the victim's death, along with indefinitely many other absences. Does this explosion of causes seriously erode the case for literal causation by omission?

(e) Whether describing something as an absence is just a coarser-grained redescription of a world that contains only positive things and events, so that causation by omission is true at the level of useful description but not at the level of fundamental ontology.

Then explain how these sub-questions combine to yield your final number, identify the single sub-question to which your final number is most sensitive, and state what one further argument or thought experiment would most likely move your number.
