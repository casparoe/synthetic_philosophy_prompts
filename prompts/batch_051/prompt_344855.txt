When the last speaker of a language dies and the language passes out of use, people widely describe this as a loss to humanity, and comparable rhetoric surrounds the disappearance of an art form or a traditional practice with its last practitioners. This grief often persists even when nobody was coerced, nobody is left bereaved, and the forms died from neglect rather than deliberate destruction. Yet no one treats the failure of a possible language, art form, or tradition ever to come into existence as a loss of the same kind; we keep no account of the cultural forms that might have been. Both situations contain an absence of a distinctive human form of life from the world, but only the first is mourned. If languages, traditions, and art forms have value beyond the people who hold them, it is not obvious why that value should attach only to forms that happen to have existed.
