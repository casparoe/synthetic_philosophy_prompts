You are grading an undergraduate philosophy of language midterm. Below is one short-answer question and a student's answer.

Question: State Russell's theory of definite descriptions and apply it to the sentence The present King of France is bald. In particular, say what Russell takes the sentence to mean, whether he takes it to be true or false, and how his theory treats the negation of that sentence.

Student's answer:

"Russell holds that a sentence of the form 'The F is G' is not a subject–predicate sentence in which a denoting phrase picks out an object and says of it that it is G. It is really a quantified conjunction: there is at least one F, there is at most one F, and whatever is F is G. Thus The present King of France is bald is analyzed as ∃x(Kx ∧ ∀y(Ky → y=x) ∧ Bx). Since there is no present King of France, the first conjunct is false, so the whole sentence is false. The sentence is nevertheless meaningful, because the description is an incomplete symbol: it has no meaning in isolation but is eliminated in the analysis. By the same reasoning, The present King of France is not bald is true, since it is simply the denial of a false sentence. Russell's larger lesson is that surface grammar misleads us into thinking a description must refer to an object in order for the sentence to be meaningful; on his view the sentence is meaningful and false even though no such object exists."
