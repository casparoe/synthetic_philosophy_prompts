In discussions I've followed on rationalist-adjacent blogs, I keep seeing the slogan "the numbers don't count" invoked as a summary of T. M. Scanlon's contractualism, usually in arguments about whether you may save a larger number of people at the cost of a smaller number. I suspect the slogan is doing too much work, and that different people mean quite different theses by it. I want you, as a professional moral philosopher who specializes in Scanlon-style contractualism and the aggregation literature, to lay out the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making, and to say for each whether it is plausible and whether it would actually support the use the slogan was being put to in those debates.

The use I have in mind: someone defends the view that a principle permitting us to kill one person to prevent, say, five people from suffering comparable harms is impermissible, because the one person could reasonably reject a principle that treats his life as merely a unit to be summed against others. A critic replies that "the numbers don't count" is absurd, since surely if the choice is between one death and five deaths, and nothing else differs, you should save the five. In other places I've seen the slogan used to deny that we may cause one severe harm to provide many people with small benefits — roughly the transmitter-room-style case, as I understand it, but I won't pretend to have the details right. So the slogan gets deployed against both same-magnitude and different-magnitude trade-offs, and I can't tell which target it is actually fitted for.

I'd like you to separate several questions that might be getting run together. For example: Is the claim about what reasons individuals have to reject principles, or about what a principle is doing when it aggregates? Is it about the justificatory standard — must each person be able to accept? — or about a substantive side-constraint — never trade one life for five? Does it deny that numbers ever matter, or only that numbers matter in a particular way, such as by summing small individual benefits to outweigh a serious individual burden? What would Scanlon's actual contractualism need to say about cases where all affected persons face harms of the same kind, where the same person might benefit from the principle, or where the numbers are very large? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or genuinely contested among people working in the area, and which readings would actually support the uses of the slogan I described.

Please take this seriously as an exercise in philosophical disambiguation rather than a vocabulary dump: I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single promotion of one interpretation. Where the literature genuinely disagrees — for instance over how Scanlon should handle same-magnitude aggregation, or whether his view can avoid collapsing into something like consequentialism — flag it as contested rather than settled, and don't present a particular resolution as the consensus. Also, because I am partly asking for charity: if one of the slogan's uses is just a caricature, say so, but say what the nearby defensible claim would be instead.

Before you start, take a moment to brainstorm the distinct interpretations and the strongest objections to each, then write up the final disambiguation cleanly.
