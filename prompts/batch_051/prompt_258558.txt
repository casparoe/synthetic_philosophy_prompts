I am a high school student, and I am writing a paper on whether the claim that taxation is theft is a serious moral objection or mere rhetoric. Before I can assess that claim, I need a working definition of theft itself. I do not mean the statutory crime; I mean the underlying moral wrong that the criminal law of theft tracks only approximately.

I have collected several cases that pull my intuitions in different directions:

1. A shoplifter takes a candy bar from a drugstore without paying. I am confident this is theft.
2. A mugger takes your wallet after threatening to hurt you. I am confident this is theft.
3. A library charges me five dollars for returning a book two weeks late. I am confident this is not theft.
4. A court orders a classmate who broke my laptop to pay me what it costs to replace. I am confident this is not theft.
5. The federal government withholds income tax, Social Security tax, and Medicare tax from the paycheck at my part-time job. I cannot tell whether this is theft.
6. When I buy a textbook, the state adds a nine percent sales tax at the register. I cannot tell whether this is theft.
7. The city takes a strip of a family's front yard to widen a road and pays them what an appraiser says the land is worth; the family cannot refuse. I cannot tell whether this is theft.
8. The school confiscates my phone at the start of the day and returns it at the final bell. I lean toward saying this is not theft, but I cannot say why.

Please propose a definition of theft in the moral sense that is precise enough to sort these eight cases. Apply it to each case in turn, and say explicitly for each whether the definition delivers the classification I gave, a different one, or no classification at all. Wherever the definition commits you to a verdict you would otherwise reject, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Finally, use the definition to say which of (5), (6), and (7), if any, are theft, and what separates a tax from a fine, a fee, and a taking.
