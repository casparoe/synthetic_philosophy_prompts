I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about satisficing, because my verdicts on cases don't line up neatly with either side of the argument, and Im starting to suspect the debate is less tidy than it looks. Go by my gut responses, not by what I take myself to believe. An economist friend keeps insisting it all washes out once you put search costs into the utility function, and I want to believe him, but I'll give you the cases that resist it.

When a flat-buyer sets explicit criteria (two bedrooms, under £300,000, within forty minutes of work) and offers on the first place that clears all three, I think she's being perfectly rational, even though a nicer flat might have come on the market the next week. When someone scrolls Netflix for three minutes and then just watches something with a 6.8 rating, I think that's fine, not a failure of rationality. When a chess engine stops searching at a fixed depth because its compute budget ran out, I think that's rational too. When an A&E doctor stops at the first serious diagnosis that fits and starts treatment without ruling out rarer alternatives, I think she's acting well, but only because the ward is busy; if she had an hour and a cheap test, stopping would be a mistake.

Here's where it gets murky. When an academic hiring committee stops interviewing once it finds one candidate above a modest threshold, even though there are two hundred shortlisted applicants and interviews are cheap, I think they've gone wrong. And when someone marries the first partner who clears a perfectly reasonable but modest bar, I think they've made a mistake, but I cant say what distinguishes them from the flat-buyer. Is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these verdicts, and which of them does it force me to drop? And what does your best principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a safety regulator who accepts a good enough risk assessment and stops looking for rare, catastrophic failure modes?
