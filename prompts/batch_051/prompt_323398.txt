I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so my habit is to turn philosophical claims into something I can specify precisely or step through like a decision procedure. Recently a colleague asked me for help deciding whether to accept a job that would have changed more than their schedule; it would very likely have changed what they cared about. I gave them an ordinary expected-value analysis: list the outcomes, estimate probabilities, and weight each outcome by how good it would be for them now. They pushed back with what I later learned is called the transformative-experience problem. As I understand it, the worry is that when an experience changes your values, the utilities you assign before the choice may not be a stable currency for evaluating what happens after it, so the expected-value calculation can be ill-posed rather than merely uncertain.

Please build me a thought experiment to specification. I want a decision problem in which an ordinary expected-value analysis returns a confident take-the-option verdict, while someone trying to honor the transformative-experience critique concludes that the expected value is not merely hard to estimate but undefined. Constraints: make it a realistic choice an actual person could face, such as a career move or a serious long-term commitment, with no science fiction and no interventions that work by stipulation. The transformation in the case must be a genuine change in what the chooser values: not just the acquisition of new information, not just a predictable change in the intensity of an existing pleasure, and the scenario should show rather than assert that the values shift. After presenting the scenario, walk through the reasoning each framework gives and why; identify the single structural feature that creates the disagreement; say what would happen to that disagreement if the feature were removed; and finish by explaining how a defender of ordinary expected-value reasoning would argue that the case is rigged or repairable. I am asking because I want to see whether the transformative-experience critique should actually change the advice I give people, or whether it only matters in edge cases.
