Okay here's one I keep going back and forth on with a friend. The Rietdijk–Putnam argument, the way we learned it, goes roughly like this. In special relativity, every inertial observer gets their own global now-slice through spacetime, and observers moving relative to each other have tilted slices. So two people who pass each other on the street can disagree about whether a distant event is already past, happening right now, or still to come. Since no inertial frame is privileged, none of these verdicts is the uniquely true one; but the event either is real or it isn't, so it must be real regardless of what we call it. Run that for enough events and the future stops being merely future: everything is just as real as the present, and you get a block universe. Fine. The thing that bugs me is that this whole setup lives in flat Minkowski spacetime with global inertial frames. The actual universe is curved and expanding, and in general relativity there are no global inertial frames covering everything; now is at best a local notion rather than a slice you can extend across all of space. So I keep wondering whether the argument survives that change. Is the flat-spacetime global-slice stuff just scaffolding, and the real premise is something like the idea that no frame is privileged, in which case the argument should survive or even get stronger? Or does the argument secretly lean on being able to tilt a global now-slice, in which case general relativity kills the proof but maybe leaves the block view standing for other reasons? I'm interested in the mechanics of the argument, not the physics textbook details: which premise is doing the work, and what would a repaired version, if any, actually claim?
