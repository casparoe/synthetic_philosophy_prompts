I've spent most of my life doing field ecology, and I've only recently started reading the philosophy of games, so if my terminology is off, that's my excuse and I'm sticking to it. Lately I keep returning to a clause in the rulebook of an old cooperative campaign board game my daughter left at the house when she moved out. The game runs through a book of scenarios, each with a map, enemy patrol routes, and a story blurb. The rulebook has this sentence, which I'll copy out exactly:

'A player may not use an action, or a series of actions, for any purpose other than the purpose stated for that action in the scenario.'

The case I can't shake is this. In one scenario you have to carry a package out of a locked warehouse before the guards find you. The 'rest' action says you spend one turn in place and recover one wound, and the scenario description says its purpose is to let the team patch up after a fight. A player found that if the team just rests in the entryway over and over, the patrol schedule, which runs off the turn counter, eventually cycles all the guards through a chokepoint and leaves the exit unguarded for three turns. Then you walk out. Every individual rest is legal, and nobody at the table thinks it violates the letter of the rest action itself. But the scenario was plainly written as a tense sneaking puzzle, and the designers clearly never imagined it being played as a waiting game. So my question is whether that clause catches what the player did, on the three readings I keep seeing in legal interpretation — the words, the purpose, and what the designers would have said if someone had asked them. Wich reading should win here, and why?

And here's the part that actually keeps me up. The 'games as art' people say a game's medium is the player's agency — the set of choices and obstacles the designer builds. If every rest was legal, then the waiting solution was already inside the designed possibility space, even if no one noticed. So is the player's solution part of the work, or a hole in the work? And does a clause like that protect the work itself, or only one intended performance of it — and can it even carry that weight in practice?
