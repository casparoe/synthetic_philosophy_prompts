I was appointed this year to my city's memorials and public-art commission, and the first item I chaired — a request to rename a square commemorating a local eugenicist — ended in a split vote and a note from the city attorney that we could not state the grounds for our own decision. The commission handles several such matters each term: requests to remove or reinterpret monuments, to rename streets and buildings, to discontinue old commemorative ceremonies, and to authorize new memorials. The members are not historians or philosophers. We meet monthly, each case gets at most an hour, and our recommendations go to the city council, so they must survive public scrutiny and possible legal challenge. I want a single reusable procedure we can run on any request.

I have been reading two opposed lines of thought about memory, and I want the procedure to keep whatever is true in both rather than resolving them in advance. As I understand it, Avishai Margalit argues that memory is not merely a psychological mechanism: a community can be under an ethical obligation to remember certain people and events, above all the victims of radical evil, and that some forgetting is a betrayal of the dead. David Rieff argues, against the automatic moral prestige of memory, that collective remembrance can be cultivated to keep grievances alive and make reconciliation impossible, and that forgetting is sometimes the more decent choice. I do not need a summary of these positions; I need a checklist that embodies the pressure between them.

One further complication, from our actual docket: forgetting is not a single action. A request may ask us to demolish a statue, move it to storage, rename a street, refuse a proposed memorial, let a plaque and its annual ceremony lapse, or alter a site's interpretation. I suspect a good procedure will force us to separate these before letting us compare the goods they each claim to serve.

Please write the procedure. It should be a sequence of questions in order, with enough guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a refusal, and where it stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values. It must be usable by lay members in under an hour, and it should produce stated reasons rather than a bare verdict.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing the reasoning in compressed form: (1) a request to remove a much-photographed statue of a colonial administrator from the 1890s; (2) a proposal to let a small 1920s memorial to war dead decay without repair and to discontinue its annual ceremony; and (3) a proposal to erect a new memorial to the victims of a mass shooting that occurred in the city five years ago. At least one outcome should be one the commission will not want to deliver, and the procedure should make clear why it must nonetheless consider it.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot settle: which cases are better decided by elected officials, which by the communities most directly affected, and which questions no procedure can take from us. Throughout, flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and identify the argument or consideration that would most change how you classify a case.

Keep the whole answer to no more than 1,200 words.
