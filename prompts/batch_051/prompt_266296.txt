Construct a thought experiment, built to order, on the extended mind thesis. The framework’s central tool is the parity principle, which I will formulate without defending it: if an external resource does something that we would unhesitatingly classify as a cognitive process, or as the vehicle of a mental state, if it were happening inside the head, then we should give it the same standing when it happens outside the head. Alongside this, much of the extended-mind literature adds a further condition for the special case in which the external resource is supposed to store a person’s beliefs or memories: the stored content must have been consciously endorsed by the subject at some earlier time, and it must be present in the resource because of that endorsement. Call this the historical-endorsement condition.

Your task is to build an ordinary-life case in which these two commitments come apart: the parity principle says that an external device is carrying one of the subject’s beliefs, while the historical-endorsement condition says it is not. Constraints: no science fiction, no neural implants, no brain lesions, and do not reuse the familiar amnesia template of a person with degraded biological memory keeping a notebook. Use a healthy adult and a widely available piece of consumer technology, and let the reliance be unforced — the subject should fall into it as part of a normal routine rather than being described into it. The device also should not be a self-authored notebook in which every entry has trivially passed through the subject’s own endorsement; use the sort of resource that can acquire contents by defaults, syncing, or someone else’s curation — the precise mechanism is yours to design. The case is for testing how much independent work the historical-endorsement condition is really doing once the present-time functional profile is right, so the divergence must be generated by the two principles rather than stipulated.

After you have the case, run these checks. State exactly what the subject believes, what the external vehicle is, and which unreflective behaviors show that the person treats the device’s contents as their own. Then run the parity reasoning step by step: identify the internal cognitive structure or state the device is functionally equivalent to, and explain why parity yields its verdict. Then run the historical-endorsement reasoning step by step: explain why some of the stored contents were never consciously endorsed at the time they entered the device and why the condition therefore withholds the verdict. Then isolate the single feature of the case that produces the divergence, and say what happens to the two verdicts when that feature is removed or a normal moment of endorsement is added back — do they collapse into agreement, and in which direction? Finally, give the best reply available to a defender of the extended mind framework: should they amend the historical-endorsement condition, demote it to a heuristic or symptom rather than a criterion, or argue that the case has been misdescribed? Identify which reply is cheapest and what it concedes.
