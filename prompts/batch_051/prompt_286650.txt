I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I write a recurring column on the ethics of food and consumption. I keep encountering a family of arguments that all run roughly like this: it is permissible to use an animal-derived product when the animal was not bred, raised, or killed to produce it and the product would otherwise go to waste—for example, eggs from rescued hens or shearings from rescued sheep. Freegans, committed vegans, and people simply trying to reduce their consumption draw sharply different lines on these cases, and I want a consistent way to handle them rather than reinventing the argument every deadline. Please turn the most defensible principle underlying this waste/byproduct view into a decision procedure I can run: an ordered set of questions that decides whether someone who otherwise rejects animal products may use a particular animal-derived item. Make it usable in under fifteen minutes with no specialist ethical vocabulary, and clearly mark the steps where the procedure bottoms out in a factual question—say, whether using the item would increase demand, or how the item came into one’s hands—or in a value judgment, such as whether waste is itself a moral loss, where I would have to supply the answer. Then apply the procedure to three cases: eggs laid by rescued hens in a backyard sanctuary that would otherwise be discarded; wool from rescued sheep that must be shorn for their health, where the sanctuary sells the wool to fund its rescue work; and meat from a deer killed by a car, retrieved by someone who otherwise eats no farmed animal products. Make sure at least one application yields a verdict a zero-waste reader will resist, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.
