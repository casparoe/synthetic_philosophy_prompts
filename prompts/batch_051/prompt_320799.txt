Here's where I've landed: (1) discounting your own future well-being just because it is later is fine, as long as it is exponential; hyperbolic discounting is the mistake, not because of the caring-less-about-your-future-self part but because it makes your preferences reverse as time passes. (2) Money-pump arguments basically work: if there's a sequence of trades you'd accept one by one and it gurantees you end up with less of what you wanted, that's a defect in you, not just an artifact of a shady bookie. (3) Ulysses contracts are rational, sometimes admirable, and a sophisticated hyperbolic discounter who knows they'll cave should bind themselves. (4) But I also think a person is always free to reoptimize from their current values — I don't see why my past self gets to boss around my present self, so "resolute choice" has always sounded like rule-worship to me. (5) And then, in a way I can't quite defend, I think the naive hyperbolic discounter (the one who doesn't know they'll reverse) is in better shape than the sophisticated one, because at least the naive isn't knowingly making a plan they'll abandon.

I keep going in circles about whether this is a consistent package.
