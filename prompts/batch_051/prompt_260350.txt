Since retiring from a career in evolutionary biology, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I am preparing a paper for a mixed ethics workshop at the university where I now attend seminars. My thesis is that the relation between consequentialist requirements on the one hand and the virtues and certain deontological constraints on the other is commonly drawn in the wrong direction. I want to argue that traits such as intellectual honesty, epistemic humility, and justice as a settled disposition, and constraints such as fidelity, are not merely instrumental to or derivative from the promotion of good states of affairs. They are better understood as partly independent preconditions: the virtues underwrite an agent's capacity to recognize and respond to value reliably, and the constraints protect the background conditions of trust that make shared deliberation about the good possible. Consequently they can sometimes constrain the pursuit of the best consequences rather than serve it. The audience will be roughly half consequentialists who think of the virtues as useful decision procedures, one Kantian who suspects that any independent constraint must be arbitrary without a formal principle, and two particularists who are skeptical that stable character traits carry much normative weight. Prepare me for the question period. Please order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase them as the people in that room would phrase them, and give me for each a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I should be hoping nobody raises.
