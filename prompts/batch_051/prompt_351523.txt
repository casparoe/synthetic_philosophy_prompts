I've been preoccupied lately by how much my own values have changed: the things I cared about intensely at twenty now feel almost foreign, and I can't point to any single moment when I changed my mind. I kept most of the same words—'kindness,' 'justice,' 'beauty'—but the felt weight behind them shifted. That has made me suspicious of the comfortable idea that a person, let alone a civilization, can decide once what matters and then hold it fixed. I joined a small reading group on longtermist ethics recently, and while I find the abstract arguments about utility functions and about a future civilization's values getting locked in interesting, I want something that makes the problem feel concrete rather than argued.

Could you write a short story that dramatizes one specific mechanism by which values might drift across generations even when everyone sincerely intends to preserve them? I'm especially interested in the thought that each generation makes only tiny, reasonable, even admirable adjustments to the values it inherits—adjustments no one could object to in isolation—and the cumulative result is something the founders would find horrifying, or perhaps simply unintelligible. Set it around a small institution explicitly founded to preserve a founding charter of values across centuries, a lay order or learned society that has kept the charter's readings and examinations unchanged, and tell it through the eyes of someone who begins to notice the discrepancies between what the words once meant and what they now license. Make the drift feel earned rather than a failure of vigilance: the people involved should be thoughtful, self-critical, and neither villains nor fools. End on the question of whether the founders' values were actually betrayed or simply translated into a new idiom the founders could not recognize, and resist a moral lesson in favor of leaving the problem vivid and unsettled.
