I write about science and ideas, and for the past several months I have been following a long-running exchange between two thinkers who hold sharply different views about what makes someone morally serious. One defends what I'll call the rule-fidelity position: the serious moral agent is, first and foremost, someone who can be relied on to follow the same public, impersonal rules as everyone else, because only such rules protect us against special pleading, against favors quietly done for ourselves, and against the slow unraveling of the coordinated life we share. The other defends what I'll call the conscience position: rules are necessarily generalities, and generalities sometimes misfire, so the serious moral agent must remain capable of recognizing when a rule would have her do something wrong in this case and of breaking it on her own considered judgment, accepting whatever penalty follows rather than transferring responsibility to the rule itself. Each describes the view as an ideal-type rather than a claim about any actual population, though one traces the emphasis on order to a distinctively German moral idiom and the other traces the emphasis on conscience to a distinctively American one; each also concedes that the rival view is a genuine virtue taken one step too far.

Write the document this adversarial collaboration should have produced. I want a numbered list of claims both can sign without reservation; then a table setting out where they still part company, with each position worded so that its own defender would accept the wording; then a list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of the parties. For each crux, provide a concrete case or test both sides would accept as genuinely bearing on it, and record in advance what each side would predict about that case. End by saying whether any part of the residual disagreement is merely verbal, and if so which part and why.

I have my own guesses about what the cruxes are, but I would rather you reconstruct them from the positions than accept my list. My guesses are these: whether order has value in itself over and above the goods it enables; whether a person can ever have private warrant, inaccessible to others, for treating herself as an exception, or whether the risk of self-deception means she should always defer to the public rule; and whether the right unit of moral assessment is the individual act or the pattern of action over time. If the real cruxes turn out to be different, substitute them. The concrete cases I have seen them circle around include a pedestrian facing a red light at an empty intersection at 3 a.m., and an employee who breaks a signed confidentiality agreement to expose genuine institutional wrongdoing; use those or better ones. The finished document should be something both of them — and I — can read and say, yes, that is our disagreement, made exact.
