Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over. In ordinary life we treat updating on new information as close to the definition of rationality, sometimes even a moral virtue: if someone learns they're in the bad branch of a plan and refuses to revise, we call them stubborn or irrational. But there's a line of decision theory—updateless decision theory—that says that in a certain class of cases this is exactly backwards. When your decision and the evidence your about to see are correlated in the right way, the policy that does best is the one you'd have locked in before you saw the evidence, and the version of you who updates once the bad news arrives guarantees a worse result. (Imagine a plan that only pays off if both the lucky and unlucky versions of you stick to it; the unlucky one, updating on the bad news, defects.) Is there a principle that tells us when updating is a virtue and when it's a trap? If there is, does it still underwrite the ordinary admiration we have for people who revise their plans when new evidence arrives, or is that admiration just a heuristic that happens to break down in precisely the cases that matter?
