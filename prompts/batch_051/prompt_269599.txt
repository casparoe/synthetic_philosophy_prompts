I'm a retired chemical oceanographer who's gotten into philosophy of technology late in life. I've been reading the autonomous weapons debate, and the term meaningful human control keeps showing up as a slogan, but I want to know what it would amount to as an actual operating procedure. I spent my career watching field instruments get gamed by ships' crews, so I care about how a rule behaves once it meets incentives.

Heres a mechanism I'd like you to stress-test. Suppose a military adopts this rule for a loitering munition cleared to engage vehicles autonomously inside a kill box. Before the system fires, the operator must actively approve within 20 seconds; if the operator doesn't respond, the shot doesn't happen. Meanwhile, a cold reviewer in another room may veto within the same window; if the cold reviewer doesn't respond, the shot goes ahead. The operator sees the full sensor feed, but the cold reviewer sees a deliberately stripped display: only the system's classification (e.g., tank, technical, civilian vehicle), its stated confidence, the munition's position, and the time — no live video, no place names, no operator identity. The cold reviewer may veto but can't pick targets. Whenever the cold reviewer actively approves or vetoes, they must type a one-line reason; non-responses get logged as no-response approvals. The complete log of votes, reasons, and names is published after 30 days.

What does this incentivize for the operator, the cold reviewer, the system's designers, and the commanders who set the kill box? How would each of them game it? What are the likely second-order effects once everyone adapts, and do the stripped display and the 30-day publication make the failure modes better or worse? I want concrete drift dynamics, not a restatement of why meaningful human control matters.

  - "actor": which human, system, or rule element this row is about.
