I'm a novelist, not a political scientist, so forgive the phrasing. I'm writing a book set in a fictional city-state that's basically Singapore with the serial numbers filed off, and the whole plot turns on what I've seen called performance legitimacy: whether the place's prosperity and order actually justify the way it's run. My problem is I've ended up believing a bunch of things at once and I can't tell whether they hang together, and I'd rather find out now than have a reviewer do it for me.

Here's where I've landed:

1. Legitimacy is about process. A government that isn't meaningfully chosen by the governed isn't legitimate, full stop, no matter how well it governs.
2. But I also think its absurd to call a regime that has kept a country peaceful and broadly prosperous for three generations illegitimate in the same way as a thieving junta. Performance has to count for something normative, not just as decoration.
3. I don't actually care much whether people would tick "yes" in a hypothetical poll. What matters is whether the regime is genuinely good for them, whether or not they'd say so.
4. Singapore and China shouldn't be lumped together. Singapore built predictable rules, institutions, and some real accountability; China leans more on fear, censorship, and a party line. They're not equally illegitimate even though neither is a democracy.
5. Legitimacy is partly a story. A regime is legitimate in practice when it can give people an account of itself they can live with, even if they never chose it. If that's too novelist-y, tell me to drop it.

Do these hang together? Which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it? Rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than treating them all as equal, say what the cheapest fix is for each, and tell me if there's one revision that cleans up the most in a single move.
