I have been trying to make sense of the way people—including many people who work on animal cognition—distribute confidence in animal consciousness, and I keep encountering an asymmetry I cannot derive from any principle I can state. When a dog flinches, whimpers, licks a wound, or avoids the place where it was hurt, almost everyone treats the behavior as a window onto felt pain. Yet when structurally similar behavior appears in an animal a little farther from us, the same inference is suddenly downgraded to reflex or mere computation. A trout given a noxious injection rubs its snout against the gravel and reduces normal activity, and the rubbing is suppressed by an analgesic. Honeybees trained with one odor that predicts sugar and another that predicts a bitter punishment will, after the hive is shaken as if by a predator, begin withholding responses even to ambiguous odors between the two—a pessimistic pattern of responding that in mammals would be taken as evidence of a negative emotion-like state. In all three cases the behavioral logic looks similar: the animal behaves as if a state is aversive, adjusts its trade-offs accordingly, and the adjustment is undone by something that should blunt the hurt. But the dog case is treated as nearly conclusive, the fish case as perpetually controversial, and the bee case by many as not even worth taking seriously.

I want to know whether this gradient of credence can be justified without smuggling in the conclusion. Is there a principle—about neural architecture, evolutionary continuity, behavioral flexibility, the costs of false positives, or anything else—that licenses counting a dog's whimper as strong evidence of felt pain while refusing to count the fish's analgesic-sensitive guarding or the bee's pessimistic choices as evidence to some nonzero degree? Give me the strongest version of such a principle you can construct, test it against the cases above and against nearby cases such as an octopus solving a novel problem or a mammal with severe cortical damage that still exhibits pain-related behavior, and then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
