A friend and I spent a long evening arguing over Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert, specifically his claim that theater cannot improve an audience's character because even a play that seems to condemn vice only flatters the spectators' sense of their own decency; we kept returning to his test case, Molière's The Misanthrope. Rousseau's charge is that by making Alceste's uncompromising honesty ridiculous and allowing the accommodating Philinte to come off as reasonable, Molière teaches the audience to prefer polished insincerity to difficult virtue, so the play disarms rather than corrects us. I pushed back: Alceste is not honesty in the pure state Rousseau implies—he is also self-righteous, jealous, and socially incompetent—and the play seems to me more uneasy about its own laughter than Rousseau's reading allows. We never resolved it. I want a careful critical reading of The Misanthrope through Rousseau's critique of theater. Where does his diagnosis of the play capture something real, where does it flatten the play, and could a well-judged production genuinely produce the kind of self-examination Rousseau says theater structurally cannot produce?
