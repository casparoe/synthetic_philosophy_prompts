After thirty years in atmospheric physics I retired and started sitting in on the philosophy department's reading group, and somehow I got roped into running the Q&A for a guest lecture. The speaker defends a broadly Aristotelian line on money and exchange — roughly, that money is a conventional measure that makes goods commensurable for the sake of need, and that there is a real difference between using money as a tool for a household's needs and the activity of accumulating money for its own sake, which he treats as a distortion. He has heard every obvious objection about gold being sterile and about fiat money, so I don't want questions that just let him restate the position.

Could you write me maybe nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, that push on the actual joints of the view? In particular, I want questions that force him to say where the line between natural and unnatural acquisition does actual normative work, to deal with the fact that money has to make unlike things commensurable without claiming they really are, to explain what exactly is wrong with interest when the borrower uses the loan to buy something productive, and to say whether the view survives once money is fiat and basically informational. Vary the follow-ups: sometimes the likely answer is a distinction, sometimes its a concession, sometimes it's a redescription. I care less about gotchas than about forcing him to draw lines he can't later blur. Just give me the list of questions with their follow-ups, no host banter.
