I am giving a talk at a workshop on symmetry, identity, and possibility. The audience is dominated by philosophers of physics and structuralist metaphysicians; a majority are already inclined to think that Black's two-sphere universe — two exactly similar iron spheres two miles apart in an otherwise empty space — does not force us to abandon the Identity of Indiscernibles. Among them, one faction thinks the two-sphere description is a misleading redescription of a single sphere; another thinks the spheres are weakly discerned by the symmetric relation of being two miles apart, and that this is enough for distinctness; a smaller group of neo-Aristotelians is suspicious of the whole method of possible-worlds counterexamples, because they think worlds must be built up from particulars we already take for granted.

My thesis is that Black's universe genuinely contains two numerically distinct spheres and that neither rescue works: the one-sphere redescription cannot say what the two-mile interval is an interval between, and the weak-discernibility reply mistakes an irreflexive relation for the sort of intrinsic difference the principle was meant to require. I conclude that the Identity of Indiscernibles is false as a necessary truth, and that the true principle in the vicinity is weaker: within any asymmetric configuration, each object is pinned down by its qualitative profile — a claim I plan to call Indiscernibility-Only-in-Asymmetry.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased the way they would phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is; for each, note which part of the room is likely to press it, give a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest; and include the objection I should be most afraid of even if no one asks it.
