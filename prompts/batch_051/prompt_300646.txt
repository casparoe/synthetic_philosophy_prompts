I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the bench I have been reading philosophy rather seriously, though I am not trained in bioethics. I now sit on the Medical Necessity Review Committee of a regional health system. Our charge is to decide whether a requested intervention should be approved as medically necessary therapy or declined as elective enhancement, and we try to decide in a way that is consistent with our own earlier rulings. I want to understand whether some principled rule actually explains what we have done, and what that rule commits us to in a hard case now before us.

Our prior decisions fall into two groups.

We approved the following as therapy: recombinant growth hormone for a child with confirmed growth-hormone deficiency; a cochlear implant for a profoundly deaf child; erythropoietin for anemia secondary to chronic kidney disease; an antidepressant together with psychotherapy for moderate major depressive disorder; donepezil for mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease; laser refractive surgery for moderate myopia; and breast reconstruction after mastectomy for breast cancer.

We declined the following as elective enhancement: growth hormone for an otherwise healthy child at the fifth height percentile whose parents wished her to be taller; erythropoietin for a competitive cyclist with a normal hematocrit; methylphenidate for a university student without an attention-deficit diagnosis who wanted help studying; cosmetic breast augmentation for a woman with clinically normal breasts; propranolol for a violinist with ordinary performance nerves and no anxiety diagnosis; and modafinil for an otherwise healthy resident with no sleep disorder who wished to work longer shifts.

The case now before us is this. A 71-year-old retired engineer has no psychiatric or neurological diagnosis. Over several years he has noticed a decline in word-finding and working memory. Formal neuropsychological testing places him in the bottom decile for his age but still within the broad normal range; he remains independent in everyday activities. He describes the decline as deeply distressing and has given up public lecturing because of it. He is requesting a low-dose stimulant together with a structured memory-training program, expressly to restore what he takes to be his earlier level of function.

I would like you to address four questions. First, identify at least two distinct principles that would make our earlier decisions jointly consistent. Second, say what each of those principles implies about the present case. Third, give a recommendation: if we wish to be consistent with our own precedents, should we approve or decline, which of our earlier decisions is the closest analog, and what principle would we be adopting with each possible choice? Fourth, describe one further case that would cleanly separate the leading candidate principles, and say what our decision in that case would reveal.
