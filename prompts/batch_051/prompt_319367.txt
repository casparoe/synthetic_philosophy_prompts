Last night I had a long, rather exasperating argument with a friend about whether it is wrong to stay home from voting in an election whose outcome is already settled. His central move was some version of the question, "What if everyone did that?" At first he seemed to mean something about consequences: if everyone stayed home on the same reasoning, turnout would hollow out and democratic life would erode. When I answered that my staying home does not cause everyone else to stay home, he shifted to a different complaint — that I would be making myself an exception to a rule I need other people to follow. When I then asked why I must want others to follow that rule, he answered by describing, once again, the bad outcomes if they did not. The same four words seemed to be doing two different jobs, and he moved from one to the other whenever either came under pressure.

I have since noticed the same double life in the phrase elsewhere. A neighbor justifies watering his lawn during a water restriction by saying one person's sprinklers will not make a measurable difference; another neighbor replies that if everyone judged their own contribution negligible, the reservoir would run dry. There the appeal is plainly to cumulative harm, and the act — watering one's lawn — would still make sense even if everyone performed it. But in another exchange, a colleague says it is wrong to pad the experience on a résumé. When I object that a handful of padded résumés will not collapse the job market, she shifts to a different point: I would be relying on employers' willingness to trust résumés while quietly making mine untrustworthy, and if everyone in my position acted the same way, the practice of evaluating résumés would lose its point and my own strategy would no longer accomplish anything. That sounds less like cumulative harm and more like the act quietly depending on a background practice that it would destroy if everyone did it.

The phrase thus seems to run together at least two distinct tests: one asking what would happen if the act were performed across a population, another asking whether I can accept my own conduct as a rule for anyone else in my position while counting on the very practice my conduct undermines. I am trying to find established vocabulary for the distinction and for the move of sliding from one test to the other mid-argument. Does each test have a recognized name? Where exactly is the boundary — are there acts that would remain perfectly intelligible if everyone did them and yet are still ruled out, alongside acts whose intelligibility collapses when everyone does them? And is there a standard term for quietly substituting one test for the other? If no standard term exists for the substitution, I would appreciate a proposed term, defined sharply enough to separate it from ordinary equivocation, from moving the goalposts, and from simply reasoning about rules rather than about individual acts.
