I work as a software engineer, and through the startup scene I have gotten interested in the idea of optional governance: private cities, charter cities, and seasteading, which all treat jurisdictions as something a person should be able to enter and leave the way users switch between platforms. The philosopher I keep coming back to is Robert Nozick, because he seems to have worked out the underlying logic decades before these projects existed. My understanding of the final part of Anarchy, State, and Utopia is that Nozick argues the best feasible society is not one particular utopian blueprint but a framework for utopia: a minimal state that enforces basic rights and otherwise lets individuals form, join, and leave communities built around different visions of the good life, so that many utopias coexist and compete for members rather than one vision being imposed on everyone at once.

Nozick died before seasteading became a visible movement and before the current wave of charter-city and private-governance projects got under way. I have been arguing with a friend about whether he would have seen a seastead or a charter city as the natural extension of the framework for utopia, since it multiplies exit options and lets communities form and compete, or as a distortion of the idea, since it tries to escape or bypass the protective state rather than presuppose one. I would like a reasoned prediction rather than a yes-or-no. Given Nozick's actual commitments, reconstruct how he would most likely have evaluated the core features of private cities, charter cities, and seasteading: where he would have been enthusiastic, where he would have hesitated, and what principle or distinction he would have used to decide whether a given project counts as a genuine extension of the framework rather than an abandonment of it.
