An epistemology working group has spent a term on the dogmatism paradox, and two of its members—one who defends a dogmatist view of the rational force of knowledge, and one who defends a thoroughgoing evidentialism—have agreed to replace another round of replies with an adversarial collaboration. Their shared starting point is this. If a subject knows p, then she can apparently know in advance that any seeming counterevidence to p is misleading, and that conclusion seems to license her in dismissing such evidence without examining it; yet this conflicts with the ordinary fallibilist conviction that newly encountered evidence should always be able to move us. The dogmatist holds that, at least in some cases, the subject's knowledge gives her a standing reason to treat apparent counterevidence as misleading and to decline to reopen the question. The evidentialist holds that encountering apparent counterevidence always exerts rational pressure, so a knower who refuses to engage with it is to that extent irrational, even if her original belief amounted to knowledge.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. It should open with a numbered list of claims both accept about the setup—including what 'misleading' and 'dismissing' can and cannot mean here, what closure under known entailment would commit them to if accepted, and what both grant about a subject who knows p before any counterevidence appears. Then provide a disagreements table that states the residual dispute exactly, with each side's position worded as its own defender would accept it: the dogmatist's thesis should not come out as mere stubbornness, and the evidentialist's thesis should not come out as a demand to give up knowledge. After that, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side—and, for each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, recording each side's prediction about it in advance. The aim is a constructive text they could both put their names to, not a verdict on who is right.

The group suspects that at least two apparent disagreements may be partly verbal: one about whether 'dismissing' means refusing to look at the evidence at all versus considering it and then discounting it because it is known to be misleading, and one about whether the subject is being evaluated before or after she learns that the evidence exists. Address these suspicions, state which reformulations (if any) would let both sides sign the same sentence, and say plainly where no reformulation works rather than forcing agreement.
