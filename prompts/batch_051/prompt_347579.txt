I hold a version of the Weberian view that culture can be a genuine cause in large-scale historical explanation. Specifically, I take the spread of certain Protestant attitudes—worldly asceticism, the treatment of ordinary work as a calling, and the reading of commercial success as a sign of one’s standing before God—to have been one significant, independent, difference-making cause of the rise of modern rational capitalism in parts of early modern Europe. The claim is probabilistic and conjunctural rather than a claim about sufficiency or laws. I want you to play it straight: what would have to be true, or what evidence and arguments should move me, for this view to be wrong or materially limited? The difficulty is that values are themselves shaped by economic and institutional conditions, so the assessment must engage the question whether the cultural strand can be shown to do independent causal work rather than merely accompany or rationalize other changes. Distinguish considerations that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward; say which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible; and give an honest assessment of whether the view, once all its qualifications are in place, still risks anything at all—and if so, whether its insulation is a problem for a causal-explanatory claim of this kind. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars of the topic, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
