Alright, here's one for this week. Write a short dialogue between two characters, Dana and Marcus, about the "main character" intuition — the nagging sense that other people might be NPCs, i.e., bodies going through the motions with no inner life, or at least no consciousness you can ever get a grip on from the outside.

Dana's line: the evidence badly underdetermines other minds. Behavior, brain activity, testimony, the feeling that your friends are "there" — all of it would look exactly the same in a world of very good zombies. So she takes the first-personal asymmetry seriously and doesn't think it's obviously question-begging to privilege her own case.

Marcus's line: that asymmetry is symmetrical — from where anyone stands, the others look like the possibly mindless ones — so it gives no non-arbitrary reason to demote everyone else. And he thinks the NPC hypothesis, taken as a real epistemic option rather than a movie conceit, is self-undermining: it has to be motivated by other people's words and behavior, but then it explains away the adversarial structure of the very conversation the characters are having.

The thing they should actually fight about is whether the intuition can be quarantined. Dana should argue she can assign real credence to the NPC hypothesis while continuing to treat other people exactly as before — morally, conversationally, epistemically. Marcus should argue the quarantine fails: once you genuinely take others to be possible NPCs, even at low probability, that has to show up somewhere, in how much you trust testimony or what you think you owe people. Make both characters sharp, and don't let either just restate their opening position. They should respond to each other's actual moves, and the dialogue should eventually locate where the disagreement really lies — whether it's about evidence, warrant, or the relation between belief and action. Don't force a tidy resolution; it's fine if they just manage to pinpoint the exact point where they seperate.
