I recently began teaching a course on political propaganda and had to decide whether to show my students an actual recruitment video from a proscribed organization, presented as an object of analysis. While preparing the course I noticed that the major platforms' moderation rules, and several institutional policies governing classroom and journalistic use, draw the line not by the material itself but by how the person reproducing it presents it. The same video that would be removed as glorification or recruitment is permitted when a journalist embeds it in a report, a researcher archives it, or a teacher screens it with a preface saying it is being shown to be analyzed and condemned. The content is identical; what changes is the framing, the stated intent, and sometimes the institutional identity of the poster. Some policies even treat a short excerpt accompanied by critical commentary as acceptable while treating a full mirror of the same footage with a one-line disclaimer as removal-worthy. This strikes me as philosophically loaded. What theory of how propaganda does harm does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that the harm is carried primarily by the poster's endorsement or the audience's uptake, rather than by exposure to the material itself? Reconstruct the implicit theory of harmful speech that would make this distinction principled, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where the practice is underdetermined because several rival theories would license it. Then tell me what a platform or institution would have to change in practice if it instead adopted an exposure-based or contagion-based view, on which reproduction itself spreads the propaganda regardless of framing. End with a final line beginning 'Summary:' that states in one sentence the theory you ultimately attribute to the practice.
