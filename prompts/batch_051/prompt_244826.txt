Write an ironic public notice, around 500 words, in the voice of a fictional Ministry of Legal Knowledge, written as if for publication in an official gazette. It should announce that henceforth every citizen will be conclusively presumed to have read, understood, and retained every statute, regulation, bylaw, and piece of administrative guidance currently in force, including provisions not yet published and provisions drafted in languages the citizen cannot read. The piece should work as a sustained reductio: take the familiar legal fiction that ignorance of the law is no excuse and extend it with bureaucratic exactness to its logical conclusions — for example, a compliance hotline that cannot answer questions because answering would constitute legal advice, rules amended while a prosecution is already pending, and a paid subscription offering early notice of future offenses. Keep the voice deadpan, official, and entirely free of any admission that the presumption is unfair or impossible; the ministry must never wink or slip into self-awareness, and the satire should come from exaggerating real features of how legal systems treat ignorance of the law rather than from arbitrary absurdity. By the end, a reader should be left with the genuine question of whether it is fair to bind people to rules they cannot possibly know, and what the presumption of knowledge is actually doing in a legal system.
