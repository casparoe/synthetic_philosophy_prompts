I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep asking modelers—economists, climate people, epidemiologists, sometimes a fisheries scientist—why their comfortable running a model whose assumptions they'll openly say are false. I keep getting the same five justifications. There's the predictionist one, Friedman's move: only the model's output has to be right, and the assumptions are just scaffolding. The negligibility one: the false assumption drops a factor small enough that it wouldn't move the result. The tractability one: idealize enough to make the mathematics solvable, then add realism back later if you can. The every-model-is-false shrug: since no model is true, it seems arbitrary to single out one assumption as the problem. And the isolation one: the model is a deliberately stripped-down mechanism meant to capture a single causal force without interference from the rest of the world. I want one more justification that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it—ideally one a thoughtful modeler would actually own up to. Then tell me honestly wether it's any good, meaning it could genuinely carry weight in a scientific argument and not just sound clever.

Lead with the proposed new justification in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my list.
