Take the Danish cartoon affair of 2005–06 — Jyllands-Posten commissions and publishes a dozen cartoons of Muhammad, there are protests and boycotts and attacks on embassies, and in the end the Danish state (which, remember, still had a blasphemy law on the books at the time) decides not to prosecute — and analize it through the distinction between blasphemy, sacrilege, and offence against the sacred.

I'm not asking for a moral verdict on the paper or the protesters. What I want is a carefull mapping: which parts of the episode are actually blasphemy in a strict sense (an attack on the deity or on doctrine), which are sacrilege (violating or misusing a sacred object or space), and which are only offence against the sacred (a community's feeling that something it holds sacred was disrespected). Say where each category gets purchase, and where it starts to slide.

Then turn it around: what does the episode do to the three categories? In a secular state, does offence against the sacred remain a distinct, usable middle category, or does it collapse into ordinary offense, group defamation, or hate speech once 'the sacred' is just whatever a community claims for itself? And does the fact that the cartoons are representations — images published in a paper — rather than acts like burning a Qur'an or vandalizing a shrine put them outside sacrilege by definition, or is that too tidy? Be explicit about where the distinction does real work and where it's doing none.
