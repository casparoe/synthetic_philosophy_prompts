One of my students is writing a paper on strict liability in the criminal law, and I've been trying to put together a clean list of the main arguments for punishing someone who had no mens rea at all — not negligence, not recklessness, nothing. So far I've got four: (1) the public-welfare argument, that for regulatory offenses like selling adulterated food the law isn't really condemning anyone, it's just enforcing a standard of care, so culpability is beside the point; (2) the deterrence argument, that if you know you'll be held liable regardless of what you knew or intended, you'll take every precaution humanly possible; (3) the evidentiary argument, that mens rea is miserable to prove, especially against corporate actors, and without strict liability a lot of genuinely dangerous conduct would escape; and (4) the voluntariness argument, that the defendant freely chose to enter a risky domain — selling liquor, running a factory — and so takes the burden of finding out the facts. Is there a fifth argument I'm missing? It has to be genuinely different from these four, not a restatement, a special case, or some combination of them. And I'd like you to say whether you think it's actually any good, or whether it falls apart once you poke at it.
