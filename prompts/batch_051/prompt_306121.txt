I cover psychology and moral philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I am writing a piece on why compassion—so often treated as the moral emotion par excellence—can go sour in ways that neither side of the contemporary empathy debate quite captures. You are the expert I am consulting: someone who works on the structure of empathic concern and on the historical debate between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche.

Set aside the secondary literature for now. I have been reading the primary texts. In On the Basis of Morality, Schopenhauer maintains that an action has moral worth only if it is motivated directly by another person's weal or woe, with no further self-regarding consideration; he makes compassion the sole genuine moral incentive and describes it, more than once, as an immediate participation in another's suffering in which the felt boundary between self and other becomes attenuated. Nietzsche, especially in Daybreak and The Gay Science, reverses the polarity: he treats pity as a dangerous affect that can demean its object, inflate the pitier's sense of power, preserve weakness, and multiply suffering in the world, while also allowing for spontaneous help and shared joy that do not have this structure. My piece is not primarily historical, so I do not need a scorecard of who got what right. What I need is the prior thing: the requirements that an adequate psychological-cum-ethical theory of compassion and its pathologies would have to meet.

I want you to write the desiderata for such a theory. For each requirement, give the concrete case or distinction from ordinary experience, moral psychology, or clinical observation that makes it a requirement, not a mere preference. In particular, the theory should have to:

- separate genuine other-directed concern from concern that is ultimately self-interested or self-soothing;
- say which forms of identification with another's suffering are morally relevant and which collapse into fusion, misattribution, or boundary-loss;
- distinguish empathic distress that produces avoidance from the kind of concern that produces approach and aid;
- explain why pity can feel like compassion while functioning as a status move, a demand for gratitude, or an unconscious protection of the pitier's self-image;
- respect the difference between feeling with someone and feeling for someone, and explain why that difference matters;
- account for the asymmetry between sharing pain and sharing joy—why one is more likely to become pathological, and what that implies for the scope of compassion.

That list is not meant to be complete; revise or expand it if the phenomena demand.

Then do two further things. First, identify which single desideratum does the most work in separating a Schopenhauerian account—on which compassion is the immediate participation in another's suffering and the sole ground of moral worth—from a Nietzschean account—on which pity is often a disguised form of self-valuation or life-denial. Second, state whether any pair of desiderata is in tension, such as the demand that genuine compassion involve some identification with the other and the demand that it respect the separateness of persons, and say how deep that tension is.

Please begin by brainstorming the central cases and distinctions informally, then present the desiderata as a numbered list with a short argument for each, and mark each as non-negotiable or merely a camp-specific preference. I am less interested in famous labels than in the carving: which features of the phenomenon a theory must get right, and which demands are actually parochial.
