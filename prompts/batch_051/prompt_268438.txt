Teaching the St. Petersburg gamble again has me poking at a variant, and I'd like you to check my head. Ordinary game: flip a fair coin until it lands tails, and you receive 2^n dollars if the first tails comes on the nth flip. The expected value is infinite, and the puzzle is usually put as why won't anyone pay a fortune for it. Here's the variant. The coin is biased so it lands tails with probability .51 and heads with .49 on each toss; everything else is unchanged. I make the expected value about fifty-one dollars — finite, not infinite. Fine. But my intution is that I still wouldn't pay forty dollars to play exactly once, and I'd still reach for the usual remedies: diminishing marginal utility of money, log utility, the fact that no actual banker could honor the tail payouts. But those remedies were designed to fix an infinite-expectation problem, and there is no infinite expectation here. So: have I turned the St. Petersburg game into a different puzzle, or is it the same puzzle with the infinity peeled off? Work through what the log-utility move actually implies for the biased game — set current wealth at, say, ten thousand dollars — and tell me whether my reluctance to pay forty is sensible or whether the 51/49 change should silence it. If the reluctance survives, does that show the St. Petersburg paradox was never really about the infinite expectation so much as the variance, the shape of the tail, or something else?
