I am a novelist at work on a book in which one of the central characters, an evolutionary biologist who studies animal communication, defends a distinctive position on how honest signalling works. A long stretch of the novel turns on a dispute between her and a philosopher of biology, and the integrity of that stretch depends on her position being coherent enough that her opponent can attack its real fault lines rather than my own confusions. I have drafted seven commitments for her, each of which seemed individually defensible:

(1) The handicap principle holds in its strong, general form: a signal can be stably honest only if it is differentially costly, in the sense that the marginal cost of producing a stronger signal is lower for a signaller in better condition or of higher quality. (2) Index signals, such as antler size that is tightly coupled to body size, are honest signals of a fundamentally different kind: their reliability comes from a physical constraint that makes them unbluffable, not from strategic cost. (3) Nestling begging is honest because begging costs energy and attracts predators, and the net benefit of begging more intensely is greater for genuinely hungry chicks, so well-fed chicks invest less in it. (4) Gazelle stotting in front of an approaching predator is best read as an honest signal of condition: the display is too costly to sustain for a gazelle that could not maintain a hard chase. (5) Where the interests of signaller and receiver overlap a great deal, cheap signals can be honest without any differential cost; she takes the coordination signals of mutualists to be a serious independent route to reliability, not a footnote to the handicap principle. (6) She holds that the whole theory should be framed in terms of evolutionary stability rather than common knowledge among rational agents, since signallers do not deliberate. (7) She also holds that most honest human testimony is stabilized by the threat of being found out and punished rather than by any cost of speaking, and that this shows cheap talk can be stable even outside cooperative contexts.

I do not know where the real tensions are in this list, and I do not want to decide which commitment to sacrifice before I know what is broken. Please audit the position rather than lecture on it: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity, and say what would have to be given up for each and what the cheapest overall revision would be. Just as important, flag any pair that appears to conflict but dissolves on a careful reading, so that I do not write the character's opponent as attacking a non-problem. I would rather be told that one commitment is doing more work than the others, or that two of them rest on motivations that cannot both be kept, than be given a general survey of the field.
