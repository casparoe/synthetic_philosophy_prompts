Here's something I've been going back and forth on while teaching my decision theory seminar. The revealed-preference view says utility isn't a psychological quantity at all; it's just a numerical summary of choices, so "she chose x because x maximizes her utility" is no more explanatory than "she chose x because she chose x." But I can also tell a story where a person has a stable internal scale of value that actually causes their choices. I want to know whether that's a merely verbal disagreement or something I could in principle test. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a person has a stable, causally efficacious utility function, not just an after-the-fact redescription of choice. Be concrete: what data, what controls, what counts as a favorable result for each side. Then be honest about what a postive result would and wouldn't settle — does it rescue the explanatory reading, or does it just show behavior is regular enough for a bookkeeping model to absorb too? You're a philosopher of economics. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few candidate tests before you pick one.
