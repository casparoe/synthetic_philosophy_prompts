I'm writing up a short philosophical self-description for a discussion group on parenting and honesty, and I want a consistency audit before I have to defend it in front of people. Could you act as a professional philosopher who works on trust and the ethics of deception? Think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming which pairs of commitments could possibly clash before you settle on your answer. Here's where I've landed:

1. Deliberately lying to your children is presumptively wrong, largely because it erodes trust, and parent-child trust is what the whole relationship runs on.
2. Doing Santa, the tooth fairy, and the Easter bunny in the ordinary way is fine, maybe even good.
3. What separates these from other fictions isn't that the parents do a good job signaling pretense. Its the opposite: Santa works because the pretense is not flagged, and that's what makes it magical rather than just a story.
4. Once a child is old enough to ask flat-out whether Santa is real, you should tell them the truth rather than doubling down.
5. I think the trust damage from these rituals is usually minor in practice, because kids don't seem to experience the reveal as a personal betrayal—they kind of graduate into the adult side of a shared cultural script.
6. In fact, part of the value is that the child gets a low-stakes, controlled lesson in not taking adult pronouncements on authority.
7. A world with no Santa at all strikes me as a little poorer for children, but a world where parents routinely lied about everyday things would be much worse.

I honestly can't locate the worst joints. Go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give and what it would cost. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and how you'd word the revised commitment.
