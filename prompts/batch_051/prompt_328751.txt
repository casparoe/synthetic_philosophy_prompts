I keep chewing on a design choice that runs through basically the whole social internet, and I want someone to nail down what it quietly assumes. Take a site that gives each account one public reputation number — Reddit karma, Stack Overflow points, whatever. Every upvote is identical to every other upvote, they just pile into a single total, and then that one total is made to do a bunch of different jobs at once: it sorts and filters whole comment sections, it controls who gets to post or downvote or edit, and it sits next to someone's username as a signal to total strangers that this person is credible. People also farm the number like its a currency.

Here's what I'm after. What does that practice presuppose about what reputation, or trust, actually is? In particular, does it have to suppose that there's some single underlying thing — call it general trustworthiness, or contribution quality — and that each little vote is a rough measurement of it? Or is that a story the sites don't need to commit to, so the number is just a filter that works okay without any deep theory under it? And here's the inconsistency that nags at me: the system treats reputation as global and portable, one number for every context, but the way I actually trust people is wildly context-sensitive. I'll trust someone to explain calculus who I wouldn't trust on health policy, and a person can be a stellar answerer in one domain and a crank in another. Is the practice genuinely inconsistent there, or is it just philosophically underdetermined — compatible with several different theories of trust at once? I'd like you to pull apart what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me: if you rebuilt the thing on the context-sensitive view instead, what concretely would have to change in how the number gets collected and used?
