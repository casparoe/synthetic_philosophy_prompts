For the past year I have co-run a discussion group whose membership splits, almost evenly, into two camps. The first camp holds that the deep determinants of why some nations are rich and others poor are institutions: the rules, property rights, constraints on elites, and enforcement mechanisms that shape incentives, with the differences between inclusive and extractive institutions doing most of the explanatory work. The second camp holds that institutions are largely an intermediate variable: they operate within, and are themselves shaped by, deeper endowments such as soil, climate, disease burden, terrain, access to navigable water and trade routes, and the cultural repertoires that those endowments selected for, so that treating institutions as the deep cause mistakes the channel for the cause. After a year of arguing past one another, we have decided to force the two sides into a proper adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the document such a collaboration would produce. I am not asking you to summarise the literature, referee the debate, or propose readings; I am asking for the joint statement itself.

Please organise the document as a numbered list of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be non-vacuous and should include both substantive matters both camps can sign (for example, concerning the scale and persistence of cross-country income gaps, the correlation between institutional quality and prosperity, and the limits of what cross-country regressions, single case studies, and historical natural experiments can each establish about causation) and procedural matters about the standards of evidence each side expects the other to meet. In the disagreements table, give each residual disagreement in neutral wording, then give each camp's own preferred formulation of its position, phrased so that the camp itself would accept the wording. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. We have two standing candidates for cruxes — the divergence between North and South Korea, and the post-1500 reversal among former colonies — but you should add or replace them as you see fit. For each crux, spell out the piece of evidence, case, or natural experiment that both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp expects it to show.

Be explicit about which disagreements are substantive and which are partly verbal (for instance, disagreements about whether informal norms count as institutions, or about whether the question is one of channels versus ultimate causes). Throughout the document, separate what is settled among people who know this material from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Use British English.
