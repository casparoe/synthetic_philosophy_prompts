I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been reading Cesare Beccaria's Of Crimes and Punishments. The passage that has lodged in my head is his account of the proper end of punishment. As I understand it, Beccaria argues that punishment cannot be justified as revenge for a harm already committed, because the past cannot be undone. Its only legitimate end is preventive: to keep the offender from causing further harm and to deter others from committing similar crimes. He pushes this to the point of saying that if a punishment is not needed to prevent future harm, inflicting it is not justice but mere violence.

I find the forward-looking theory built on that passage compelling. It gives punishment a clean, almost computational justification: every penalty is evaluated by its expected future reduction in harm, not by any backward-looking fact about what someone deserves. It also gives a decision procedure: if a proposed punishment does not materially prevent future crime, it should not be imposed. This fits the way I naturally think about system design, where a feature is justified by its expected effects rather than by what happened earlier in the codebase. It also gives a natural rationale for rehabilitation-focused prisons: education, job training, and humane conditions are justified exactly insofar as they make repeat offending less likely, not as kindness.

But I have an intuition that the theory contradicts, and I cannot shake it. Suppose someone commits an especially cruel crime and then, immediately afterward, suffers a medical event that leaves him permanently unable to understand, remember, plan, or act. The crime was never discovered, and it never will be, so incapacitating him prevents nothing and punishing him deters no one. The forward-looking view says the state may not punish him at all. My intuition says the state should punish him anyway, because of what he did, even though the punishment changes nothing about the future.

I would like the strongest version of both the consequentialist theory of punishment and the retributivist intuition, followed by a negotiated verdict. In particular, should the intuition be debunked as an atavistic vengeance response that the theory correctly filters out? Should the theory be amended to give backward-looking desert a limited role? Or is there an interpretation on which both survive, perhaps by treating the intuition as a side constraint on the design of preventive institutions rather than as a rival justification for individual sentences? I want to know what it would cost to abandon each side and how one would tell which cost is higher.
