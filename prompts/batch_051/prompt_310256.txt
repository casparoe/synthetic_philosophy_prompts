Hey — I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of social science, and I'm writing the take-home final for the week we spent on whether culture can be a cause. I want one essay question built around Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, but the whole point is that it can't be answered by summarizing the thesis. The question should force candidates to actually say what it would mean for a value, a religious ethic, or a broader cultural outlook to be a cause of something like the rise of capitalism, rather than just a story we tell after the fact. It should also make them take seriously the reverse-causation worry (maybe early capitalist practice selected for and rewarded the relevant attitudes) and the worry that culture-style explanations collapse into either mushy appeals to mentality or disguised materialist explanations where culture is epiphenomenal. I'd also like the question to push them past name-dropping toward actually using the idea of an elective affinity (a religious ethic and an economic ethos fitting each other without either strictly determining the other) and toward Weber's distinction between an explanation being adequate at the level of meaning and adequate at the level of causes. Please write: (1) the question itself, aimed at someone with 24 hours and about 1,000 words; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme of 20 total marks. For the marking scheme, use a three-band rubric (A / B / C) that spells out the specific distinction or move each band has to make, not a vague descriptor like shows understanding, and then list three or four common failure modes with the marks each should lose. And please don't ask for citations or a bibliography — the point is to test whether they can reason with the ideas, not who they've read. One more thing: make sure the question doesn't just let them define elective affinity and move on; it should make them use it to adress the causation problem, and the rubric should seperate students who can faithfully reconstruct Weber from students who can actually run the causal-explanatory argument themselves.
