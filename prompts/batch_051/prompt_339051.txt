I am a novelist working on a book whose protagonist is a professional forecaster and decision analyst; she treats it as close to axiomatic that an ideally rational agent can never be made worse off by receiving costless information. In a key chapter, a rival character tries to dislodge this conviction, and I want the scene to run on the real fault lines of the position rather than on a caricature of it.

The position in question is the result usually called Good's theorem. As I understand it, the theorem says: for an ideal Bayesian expected-utility maximizer facing a well-defined decision problem, acquiring free information cannot lower the expected utility of the decision, because the agent may always ignore the information, and the expected value of choosing the act that is optimal conditional on the information is at least the value of the act the agent would have chosen without it.

I want an honest map of what would count against this principle as a guide to rationality, not a bibliography. In particular:

1. Identify the load-bearing assumptions — that the information is genuinely costless and ignorable; that the agent is an expected-utility maximizer with a single stable utility function over outcomes rather than over beliefs or states of mind; that no other agents' knowledge and expectations enter the payoff; that the agent faces no dynamic-consistency or precommitment problem; and any others you think carry weight.

2. For each assumption, give a concrete case in which a person resembling my protagonist would be rationally better off not acquiring the information. State whether the case is a genuine counterexample to the principle or only a case outside its explicit scope.

3. Sort the defeaters from decisive to merely boundary-drawing. A defender of the theorem can plausibly concede that it says nothing about strategic settings, about agents who cannot ignore what they learn, or about belief-dependent utility; say which concessions are harmless qualifications and which quietly hollow the principle out.

4. Assess whether the principle, once the standard qualifications are added, has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that is a defect in a claim that originates as a theorem rather than as an empirical generalization.

5. Say which of these failure modes are in live dispute rather than mere logical possibilities, and what evidence or argument — formal, conceptual, or experimental — would most move the debate.

Write the analysis in American English.
