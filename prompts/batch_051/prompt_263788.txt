I've been trying to pin down what I actually think about decisions where the probabilities aren't nailed down, because my gut keeps going different ways depending on how the case is set up. A friend and I have been kicking around urn examples over lunch. Here's where I've landed.

Start with two urns. Urn A has 50 red balls and 50 black balls. Urn B has 100 balls, but I'm not told the red/black mix. A bet pays $10 if the color you name comes out. I'd rather bet red on A than red on B, and I'd also rather bet black on A than black on B. But if I'm forced to bet on B and only get to pick the color, I'm dead indifferent between red and black. Now make A lopsided: 1 red and 99 black, and keep B mysterious. I'd take the red bet on B over the red bet on A, at ten dollars anyway, though for the black bet I'd still take A. So I'm not simply known-better-than-unknown; when the known option is bad enough, I run toward the mystery. Even wierder to me: I'd rather bet on an urn whose red count was fixed by a table of random digits than on an otherwise identical urn a roommate carried in from a storage unit, even though for both urns the honest description of my information is just that the count could be anywhere from zero to a hundred. And if you tell me Urn B's red count is between 40 and 60, I mostly relax and treat it as nearly fifty-fifty, even though that's still a big spread. With one-dollar bets, none of this seems to matter to me; with a thousand dollars on the line, it matters a lot.

Is there one principle behind these verdicts, or close? I'd especially like to know whether the first two urn preferences can both be saved without giving up something else, which of my verdicts have to go on the cleanest account, and what the resulting principle would say about a case I keep avoiding: whether it makes sense to pay money, over and above any improvement it makes to my expected dollar winnings, just for someone to tell me the actual mix before I commit.
