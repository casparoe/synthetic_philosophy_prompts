For several years I have kept a file of letters from readers about the ending of one of my novels. The ending is not ambiguous: the book's worst character prospers, quietly and thoroughly, and the reader is left with him there. The letters that interest me are not those that call the ending contrived; they are those that call it unfair. The writers know that it is true to life, and that is exactly what disturbs them. They write as though the world had failed an obligation by not settling the account. Yet many of these same readers, in the same letters and in conversation, also dismiss as wishful thinking the very idea of a settling mechanism. They do not believe in anything like karma, in a judgment after death, or in a cosmic principle by which vice is repaid with suffering and virtue with happiness; to adopt any such doctrine, they say, would be superstition. So I am left with an asymmetry in ordinary judgment that I cannot resolve. The same people judge the world unjust when the wicked flourish and the good are broken, and they judge it naïve to suppose that the world could be otherwise. They seem to hold, at once, that outcomes ought to track moral desert and that no tracking may rationally be expected.

I want to know whether this pair of judgments can both be kept, or whether one of them is a mistake and should be given up. The obvious reconciliation is that the first judgment merely expresses grief rather than a real claim about how the world should be; but the language of injustice survives long after the grief, and it is applied to strangers and to long-finished wrongs. The opposite reconciliation is that the second judgment reflects only a lack of evidence and is compatible with hoping for a moral universe; but the people I am describing do not merely lack belief, they treat that hope as an intellectual and moral failing. Give me the strongest principle you can find that generates both judgments, then test it against at least one nearby case, such as the bystander who refuses to correct a local injustice because he says the cosmos is indifferent, or the claim that a child's suffering is deserved because of wrongdoing in a forgotten prior life. If the asymmetry survives, defend it against the harder of these cases; if it does not, say whether we should stop calling the world unjust or whether we owe cosmic moral order more credence than these readers allow.
