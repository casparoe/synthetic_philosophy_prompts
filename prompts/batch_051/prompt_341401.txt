I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about whether it's good to be excellent at something when nobody else ever benefits from it or even notices. Going case by case with my gut:

A pianist spends two decades learning a horrendously difficult piece, then plays it exactly once, alone, in a soundproof room, with no recording. I think that's genuinely worth doing; the music matters even if no one hears it.

A mathematician proves a cute little theorem about some sequence nobody has ever cared about, writes the proof in a notebook, and the notebook is destroyed before anyone reads it. I still feel the discovery was good, though I also feel a real loss.

A mountaineer climbs an unclimbed face on a remote ridge, nobody sees it, no account is published. I admire that, maybe more than if they'd done it for an audience.

A runner trains for eight years to break three hours in the marathon, then does it on a treadmill in a basement at 2am, with the display taped over, and never checks the time or tells anyone. I want to say sure, that's a real achievement, but honestly it feels like less of one than the mountaineer's. Like something's missing.

A chess player reaches grandmaster strength by playing an engine alone, but never plays a single rated game. I think he genuinely has the skill, but calling him excellent at chess feels off somehow.

An engineer designs a cheap water-purification system for a village that will never build it. The design works and is elegant, it just sits in a drawer. I think the design can be excellent, but the situation honestly bothers me more then the pianist case.

A calligrapher practices until each piece is flawless and then burns every piece. I find that fine, almost pure.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or nearly all of them? In particular the runner and the mountaineer feel structurally similar but different in verdict to me, and I can't tell whether that difference is defensible or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Also, what does the principle say about a scientist who makes a genuine discovery, writes it up, and then locks it away with the intention that no one ever reads it, and about someone who gets extremely good at something while slowly destroying their health to do it, alone, with no one else ever benefiting? And the one I keep going back and forth on: a kid practices a difficult violin piece until she plays it beautifully, but only ever in an empty room -- is the beauty itself enough?

Please give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with each clause motivated by the case that forces it. Then apply it to cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and whether I should overturn those.
