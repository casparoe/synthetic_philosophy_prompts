I'm a PhD student working on imprecise credences. Last week in our decision-theory reading group, my officemate — a precise Bayesian, naturally — laid out an argument that seems to show imprecise probabilties are just precise credences wearing a disguise. I think the conclusion has to be wrong, but when I try to poke at it, each step feels load-bearing. Here's the argument as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Suppose you're rationally imprecise about P: your credence is the closed interval [0.3, 0.7].
(2) That state has to be earned by your evidence: each p in the interval passes your epistemic standard, and every point outside it fails.
(3) But a set doesn't make choices. When a decision forces you to act as though you had a single number — say you have to submit one point-valued forecast, or quote a single fair price for a bet on P — you have to pick some p from the interval.
(4) If your evidence gave you no more reason to pick one p over another, then whichever p you pick is arbitrary; and a theory of rational choice that lets arbitrary picks count as optimally rational is too weak, since it can't distinguish between picking 0.32 and picking 0.68.
(5) So the selection must be guided by something beyond bare membership in the interval — by how strongly each candidate credence is supported relative to the others.
(6) But a weighting of candidate credences by relative support is exactly a probability distribution over the interval. And that distribution has an expectation: a single number q.
(7) Since q is what actually guides your choices, and since your credence in P is whatever single number does the belief-side work in choice and inference, q is your real credence in P. The interval was just higher-order uncertainty about which precise credence was the right one all along.
(8) So the imprecise state is either rationally inert — a set that recommends nothing — or else it's a redundant wrapper around a precise credence. Either way, imprecise probabilities collapse into precise ones.

I want to keep the idea that a rational agent can have a genuine interval-valued credence without a hidden sharp number behind it, and I'd rather give up (4)–(7) than (1)–(2). But I don't know how to reject them without conceding that choice can be arbitrary, and that feels like it hands the argument its conclusion. Where exactly does this go wrong, and what do I have to price in to resist it?
