By an antimeme I mean an idea that reliably resists its own retention and transmission: someone can follow it while attending to it, yet afterwards it fails to lodge in memory or to be passed on accurately. Suppose that, from roughly 2030 onward, this stops being a curiosity and becomes a technique. Call it antimemetic wrapping: given any proposition, a skilled practitioner can produce a short written or spoken presentation such that a typical reader or listener understands the proposition at the moment of exposure, but is substantially less likely to remember it accurately, to act on it later, or to repeat it to others. The effect works through ordinary cognitive channels — attention management, narrative framing, interference with retrieval — not through threats, drugs, or subliminal stimuli. It works about as well on true propositions as on false ones, is not reliably detectable by the average person, and quickly becomes cheap, legal, and commercially available. Within a few years, political campaigns, advertisers, employers, litigants, and ordinary citizens all make routine use of it; most people know the technique exists, but cannot confidently tell whether any particular message they encounter has been wrapped.

Predict, with explicit reasoning, how this capability reshapes politics, science, journalism, the law, and everyday interpersonal trust over the following twenty years. I want a chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints rather than a narrative. For each major change, say what drives it — the incentive to attack or defend memory, the way institutions collect and authenticate testimony, or the altered equilibrium once people know their own retention may have been sabotaged. Throughout, distinguish effects that flow simply from information being made hard to retain from effects that flow specifically from people's awareness that this is being done to them. Mark which predictions you take to be robust across plausible variation in how detectable the wrapping is, how expensive it remains, and how quickly institutions adapt, and which predictions would reverse under different assumptions. Finally, identify two or three empirical assumptions about memory, media, or institutions on which your overall picture most depends, and state what you would expect to happen if those assumptions turned out to be false.
