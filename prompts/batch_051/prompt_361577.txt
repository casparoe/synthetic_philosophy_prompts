I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science seminar, and in a discussion of scientific priority and credit I gave my students the following passage from Stephen Stigler’s 1980 paper “Stigler’s Law of Eponymy”:

“I have chosen as a title for this paper, and for the thesis I wish to present and discuss, "Stigler's law of eponymy". At first glance this may appear to be a flagrant violation of the "Institutional Norm of Humility", and since statisticians are even more aware of the importance of norms than are members of other disciplines, I hasten to add a humble disclaimer. If there is an idea in this paper that is not at least implicit in Merton's The Sociology of Science, it is either a happy accident or a likely error. Rather I have, in the Mertonian tradition of the self-confirming hypothesis, attempted to frame the self-proving theorem. For "Stigler's Law of Eponymy" in its simplest form is this: "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer."”

Almost immediately, my students began using the final sentence—“No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer”—as a debate-stopper. They deployed it to dismiss the way standard textbooks credit the Pythagorean theorem to Pythagoras, calculus to Newton and Leibniz, natural selection to Darwin, and so on, with the implication that these names are basically arbitrary or actively misleading.

But I am quite sure that sentence is not one claim. It seems to admit multiple precisifications depending on the quantifier, scope, what counts as a “discovery,” and what “named after” is doing. Some of those readings are trivial, some substantive, and some almost certainly false. I want to be precise in my next session.
