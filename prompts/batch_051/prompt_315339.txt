In my work as a journalist who covers the overlap of science, statistics, and law, I have been assembling a long piece on the uneasy role of probability in legal proof, and to sort out my own thinking I have been collecting immediate verdicts on a series of illustrative cases. The verdicts now appear to pull in different directions. I am less interested in what the formal rules of evidence say than in whether there is a defensible principle underneath my reactions, or whether some of those reactions have to go.

The cases are these. A pedestrian struck at night cannot identify the bus that hit her; the only buses on that road belong to the Blue Company, which operates 80 percent of the runs, and the Red Company, which operates the rest; there is no other evidence. I would not find Blue liable under the civil standard of more likely than not, although the arithmetic points to Blue. A rodeo sells 499 tickets but draws 1,000 attendees; the promoter sues one attendee picked at random, armed only with records showing he bought no ticket. I would not let the promoter collect, even though the odds that this attendee crashed are just over half. In a prison yard, video establishes that 99 of the 100 prisoners attacked a guard while one stood apart, but the recording cannot identify the abstainer. I would acquit any prisoner identified only by that video, even though it is 99 percent likely he participated; I am not sure I would even hold him civilly liable. By contrast, a store's self-checkout logs show that a particular customer failed to scan an item on 8 of his last 10 visits, and on the occasion at issue there is no event-specific evidence at all — no camera footage, no sensor anomaly, no witness — only his own 80 percent personal rate. I find myself far more willing to let the store ban him, and even to hold him liable for the missing inventory, than I was in the bus case. In a further variant, a delivery driver is sued for sideswiping a parked car, and the sole evidence is a proprietary machine-vision system reporting 95 percent confidence that the truck in the footage was his, with no disclosure of training data or error rates; I would not find him liable, and I trust this evidence less than I trust the bus company's bare 80 percent market share. Finally, in a revised bus case, Blue operates 60 percent of the runs and a single witness who is 70 percent accurate at identifying bus color says the bus was blue; here I would find Blue liable, even though neither the 60 percent figure nor the witness would have moved me alone.

My working rule so far is that a bare frequency is inert unless it is tied to something about this particular event or this particular person's conduct, but I can already see that the shoplifter case flatters that rule while the witness-plus-statistic case strains it. I would like the principle, or a small set of competing candidate principles, stated precisely enough that each clause is doing identifiable work and paired with the case that motivates it. It should also give verdicts on cases I have not yet settled: an actuarial risk score offered at sentencing that classifies a defendant as high risk on the basis of people who resemble him; a DNA cold hit in which a one-in-a-million match is found only after trawling a database of millions, with no other link between suspect and crime; and a product-injury suit resting entirely on a model's aggregate defect rate, with no proof about the particular unit that injured the plaintiff. Finally, I want an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate — above all, whether my treating the shoplifter's personal 80 percent as proof while rejecting the bus company's 80 percent is defensible, or an inconsistency I should surrender.
