I have been reading around John Taurek's argument about rescue, and I would like a distinction I have been developing to be assessed honestly. The case, as I understand it, is this: I can rescue either one stranger or, at no greater cost to myself and no extra cost to anyone else, five different strangers. Taurek's claim is that the five do not, together, have a stronger claim on my help than the one, because each of the five has only the same individual claim as the one, and claims of this sort cannot simply be added together. I find the argument more persuasive than I expected to, but I also cannot bring myself to believe that the right response is simply to toss a coin.

In trying to answer him, I have come to a distinction I call 'act-level aggregation'. The thought is that Taurek may be right that we must not aggregate at the level of the victims—that is, we must not treat five separate complaints as if they pooled into a single complaint five times as weighty. But he seems to assume that the only alternative is this sort of victim-level, or patient-level, aggregation. There may be another possibility: the numbers matter because they change the character of the action open to me. Saving one person is one action, and saving five people is not merely that same action with more of the same kind of reason attached; it is a different sort of act, a fivefold rescue, and that act has properties—such as being a certain scale of response to a certain kind of situation—that may count in its favour without presupposing that any individual's claim outweighed any other's. On this picture, 'the numbers count' would be true not because claims add up, but because the alternatives themselves differ in a morally relevant way.

I want to know whether this is a real distinction or whether I have simply re-described aggregation in act-flavoured language. Has the move already been made under a standard name that I have not encountered? And where does it break? For instance, what if the five are not reachable in a single rescue effort but only by several separate acts, or what if the choice is between five and four rather than five and one—does the view then force me to say that the numbers may no longer count? I am not attached to the label or even to the idea itself; I want to know whether it survives contact with hard cases.
