Right, here's one that's been bugging me since I used the Ellsberg urn in a lecture last term, and I'd like you to work through it properly rather than rehearse the textbook version. Take the standard two-urn setup: Urn A has exactly 50 red and 50 black balls, Urn B has some unknown proportion. Now vary Urn B like this. Its composition is fixed by a fair coin toss: heads, and it contains 60 red and 40 black; tails, and it contains 40 red and 60 black; and you never get to see the toss. Urn A stays exactly 50/50. Payoffs are the usual ones -- £100 if you draw the colour you bet on, nothing otherwise.

In the classic case, a person prefers red-from-A to red-from-B and black-from-A to black-from-B, and we usually say no subjective expected utility maximiser can do that, since no single probability assignment to B could make both preferences come out. But in the fair-coin version an SEU maximiser who reduces compound lotteries will assign red-from-B a probability of a half, same as red-from-A, and so should be indifferent on every matched single-colour bet. So my question is: in this altered case does the Ellsberg paradox still give us an argument against SEU, or has it shrunk to a quirk about compound risk? If someone still strictly prefers the known urn for both colours, what principle is she now violating, and is it the same sort of irrationality as in the orginal?

And then push it a step further. Replace the fair coin with a coin of completely unknown bias (still one toss, still 60/40 versus 40/60 depending on the side). Follow the same comparisons. Where, between a known fair coin and an unknown-bias coin, does the original paradox re-emerge, and what does that suggest about how much second-order "knownness" is needed for ambiguity aversion to be more than a dislike of risk? Quick extra wrinkle, if you think it matters: does it make a difference whether the coin is tossed before you choose or only after you've already placed your bet?
