For a grad seminar on interpretations of probability I've had to write out my own position so the students can attack it, and I honestly don't know where its cracks. I'd like a consistency audit rather than a survey: which of these actually pull against each other, which only appear to, and what's the cheapest fix? Here's where I've landed.

1. The only rational norms governing degrees of belief are coherence and conditionalization; no principle picks out one set of priors as more rational than another.
2. There are objective single-case chances: the chance that this particular radium atom decays in the next minute is a real feature of the setup right now, not merely a summary of actual frequencies.
3. The Principal Principle holds: on learning that the chance of A is x, and absent 'inadmissible' information, my credence in A should be x.
4. Actual-frequency frequentism is dead: the same event falls under infinitely many actual-frequency classes, and no non-arbitrary rule says which one gives the probability (the reference class problem).
5. But no event has a probability tout court; probabilities attach only relative to a chance setup or model. A bare particular event, apart from a setup, has neither a frequency nor a propensity.

Some of these may only be in tension on a sloppy reading. I'd like the genuine tensions ranked by severity, with what I'd have to give up and what the least costly revision costs. Don't lay out a menu of interpretations and leave me to pick; commit to which of these can't all be true together and which revision you'd make.
