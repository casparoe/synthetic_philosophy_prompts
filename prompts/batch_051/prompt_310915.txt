I teach a 12th-grade philosophy elective with 18 students and a single 55-minute period. We are beginning a unit on free will, determinism, and indeterminism. I want an in-class activity built around this passage from William James’s essay “The Dilemma of Determinism” (1897):

“I thus disclaim openly on the threshold all pretension to prove to you that the freedom of the will is true. The most I hope is to induce some of you to follow my own example in assuming it true, and acting as if it were true. If it be true, it seems to me that this is involved in the strict logic of the case. Its truth ought not to be forced willy-nilly down our indifferent throats. It ought to be freely espoused by men who can equally well turn their backs upon it. In other words, our first act of freedom, if we are free, ought in all inward propriety to be to affirm that we are free.”

I want an activity that makes students feel, before the idea is named or explained, why James thinks affirming freedom is itself an act of freedom, and why determinism makes attitudes like regret, praise, and blame awkward. Please design a runnable classroom activity with rules, materials, and a small-stakes payoff scheme that does not require real money. The students should run into the problem themselves: when an outcome is determined by a chance device, by their own earlier commitments, or by a hidden rule, what could it mean to regret it, and what would it mean to affirm their own freedom in response? Then give me a debrief that maps the moments in the activity back to James’s argument, explains what the activity cannot capture, and flags what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—especially the student who says the game only shows that people talk sloppily about what “could have happened.” No lecture up front, and do not reveal James’s name or the essay’s title until the debrief.

You are a professional philosopher with expertise in action theory and moral psychology, and an experienced designer of classroom simulations. Think carefully about the mechanics before proposing the activity.
