Analyze the ongoing replication crisis in psychology and the biomedical sciences as a case study in Cox's theorem and the claim that probability theory is the logic of plausible reasoning. Cox's theorem says that any system of plausible reasoning meeting a few consistency conditions must be isomorphic to probability; the Jaynesian reading concludes that null-hypothesis significance testing is therefore not a logic of evidence but a set of formal procedures that systematically misstate what the data support. Use the crisis to test that diagnosis in both directions. Which features of the crisis does it explain or predict — p-hacking, hypothesizing after results are known, publication bias, the habit of treating a non-significant result as showing no effect, the difficulty of publishing exact replications — and which features does it leave unexplained? And what does the episode show about the theory: does it confirm that a scientific community reasoning outside the probability calculus will drift into systematic error, or does it show only that the tools were badly used while a careful frequentist practice could have stayed honest? Keep the mapping disciplined: identify the agents, the decision rules they were following, the evidence they had, and what Cox's desiderata would have required of them instead, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
