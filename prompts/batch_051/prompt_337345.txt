I have recently been turning over a question that began, I admit, as a personal grievance. After years in a humanities discipline where a problem could be argued over for a decade without anything being settled, I moved into a technical role where you know within minutes whether what you built works. The contrast made me wonder whether clear, fast feedback is the single factor that explains why some fields progress and others seem to spin their wheels. I tried to state this intuition as an argument, but I cannot tell whether the argument is any good. Here it is.

(1) A field progresses only by discarding failed solutions and retaining better ones. (2) A solution cannot be recognized as failed unless the field receives feedback — some observable or inferable sign that the solution does not work. (3) Fields with clear, rapid feedback recognize failed solutions more quickly than fields whose feedback is delayed, noisy, or ambiguous. (4) The quicker a field recognizes failed solutions, the quicker it accumulates solutions that have survived the test, and therefore the faster it progresses. (5) Therefore, the clarity and speed of feedback fully explain the observed differences in progress across fields; a field with poor feedback should progress slowly, if at all.

I half-suspect the trouble is in step (4), because noticing that something has failed does not by itself tell you what to replace it with. But I also worry that the problem may begin earlier, perhaps in what "feedback" or "progress" is being made to mean. You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in social epistemology. Please think through the argument carefully: first brainstorm the distinct readings of the key terms, then go step by step and say whether the argument is valid as stated, which step (if any) fails, and what the strongest corrected version of the argument would actually establish.
