I have been thinking on my own about what to make of unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, and I would like to know whether the position I have settled into hangs together. My commitments are these. (1) Models in science routinely assume things known to be false—frictionless planes, transaction-free markets, perfectly rational consumers, populations without mutation—and this is usually fine rather than a defect. (2) For predictive models, what matters is a model's predictive track record, not the realism of its assumptions; I have seen this called the F-twist, and it seems broadly right to me. (3) More realism is not automatically better, and adding realistic detail often makes a model harder to use and understand without much improving its predictions. (4) But a model can mislead when its predictions succeed because false assumptions are doing essential causal work, or because two errors cancel out; in those cases the model may break down sharply outside the conditions in which it has been tested. (5) I also want to hold that explanatory models—models that aim to show why something happens—should not be judged by the same predictive standard as purely predictive models. Please audit these commitments: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which tensions are only apparent, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be.
