I'm a software engineer doing eval work for a model-training team—mostly writing the benchmarks and metrics we use to decide whether a new checkpoint is actually smarter than the last one—and I keep going in circles on what we're even measuring. I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I'm probably butchering a debate that has a name somewhere.

The view I keep getting pulled toward, from ML blog posts and papers and late-night arguments, is that intelligence just is prediction: a system is intelligent to the extent it can compress it's experience and anticipate what comes next, across as many situations as possible. I get the appeal. It's the only account I know that gives a definite, measurable, non-arbitrary answer; it unifies language, planning, and perception under one objective; it explains why scaling up next-token predictors keeps unlocking capabilities nobody explicitly wrote down; and there's no homunculus and no vague understanding module doing all the work. It also matches how we actually run things, because predictive loss is the one signal that moves smoothly during training.

But I cant get past a thought experiment. Say two systems tie on the eval. One is basically a huge memory: it stored most of the training data and interpolates, so it aces anything statistically close to what it has seen but collapses on any real distribution shift. The other is small, learned a few compact rules, does slightly worse on the familiar stuff, but keeps working when the world moves. On the pure prediction view they're tied, or the memory wins. Yet my gut says one got smarter and the other is a very expensive hash table, and I'd bet my job on the small one in production. Same feeling with the classic spurious-cue medical classifier that predicts great by reading the scanner type instead of the disease: perfect loss on the benchmark, zero grasp of the thing we actually care about. So I don't know which should give.

Should I debunk the intuition—say under-the-hood stories about understanding do no work, and generalization is already inside the prediction definition, so the lookup table only ties on a narrow eval and not on the real objective? Should I amend the theory—say intelligence is prediction under constraints like sample efficiency, or compressing into the shortest program that fits the data rather than raw benchmark loss, so the memory doesn't count? Or is there a clean reading where both survive because the eval tie was the misleading part? I want the strongest case for each side, what each move costs, and which one I should actually make.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no intro and no wrap-up—something like the theory's best case, the intuition's best case, the cost of dropping the intuition, the cost of amending the theory, and your actual verdict on which should yield or how they're compatible.
