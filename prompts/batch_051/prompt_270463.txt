I have been trying to make my working definition of methodological individualism precise enough to settle some disagreements with colleagues, and I have arrived at three conditions I want to test. I will count an explanation of a social phenomenon as individualistic just in case: (1) the only causal agents it invokes are individual people acting on beliefs, desires, and intentions; (2) any social or structural vocabulary it uses — "norm," "institution," "custom," "network," "the group" — can be paraphrased away, without loss of explanatory content, into claims about individuals and their interactions; and (3) no irreducible causal powers are assigned to social wholes, so no "downward" influence from the group to the individual is required.

The case I have in mind is a fishing town I will call Paxton. In Paxton, boats leave only after a pennant is lowered at first light. No ordinance requires this; the pennant once signaled a good tide but lost that function long ago. Privately, every fisherman now says the custom serves no purpose and that he stays in the harbor only because he expects the others to stay, and expects that leaving early would bring a few days of cold treatment. The cold treatment is not coordinated: each fisherman independently judges early departures as impatience and avoids trading with the early boat. None of them can say how the practice arose, and it has drifted over the decades — once the pennant was lowered at sunrise, now at first light — without anyone having intended the changes. The natural explanation is a self-enforcing equilibrium: each man's expectation of what others will do is sustained by yesterday's behavior, and rare deviations are punished, so the pattern reproduces itself. But two further facts complicate this. First, the custom has persisted through a complete turnover of the fishing population, so the pattern's carrier seems to be the town rather than any fisherman. Second, the fishermen's preferences do not by themselves select first light as the departure time: if the pattern had settled at midday, the same preferences and the same sanctioning dispositions would presumably have reproduced midday. What pins down first light is the accumulated history of the practice, which no current fisherman knows.

Does the explanation of the Paxton harbor custom qualify as methodologically individualistic on my three conditions? Please go through the conditions one by one, flag where the case pushes against them, and say explicitly whether the borderline is due to the case or to a defect in the criteria. I especially want to know whether condition (2) can absorb the persistence-through-replacement and the role of the unremembered history, and whether condition (3) settles anything or merely restates the point at issue.
