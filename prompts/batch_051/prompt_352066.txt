I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist, late in life, must choose between two procedures. In the first, his brain is gradually replaced, neuron by neuron, with functionally equivalent synthetic units while he remains awake and conversant; by the end no original biological neurons remain, though his memories, temperament, and self-narration continue without apparent break. In the second, a destructive whole-brain emulation, the fine-grained structure of his brain is scanned, the original dies, and a digital system wakes claiming his memories and projects. I have hired two consultants who have been arguing past each other for a year about these cases. The first, a defender of what he calls the continuation view, holds that personal survival consists in the right kind of psychological and causal continuation, and that both procedures preserve the person because they preserve that continuation, with the gradual case being the easier of the two. The second, a defender of what she calls the process view, holds that a digital emulation is a replica rather than the man himself, and that the gradual case is troubling because it leaves no determinate moment at which the original is lost, which she doubts any adequate account of a person can accept.

Please draft the document such an adversarial collaboration should have produced, written as a joint statement both parties could put their names to. It should open with a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: about what each procedure does, about which features of a person are preserved and which are not, and about what any adequate account of survival in these cases must explain. Then present a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in wording each side would accept for its own view, with no caricature. After that, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions whose answers would move at least one side—and for each crux give one concrete case or piece of evidence both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Cases you may use, provided both would accept their relevance, include: a nondestructive emulation made while the original lives on; a gradual replacement halted at forty, seventy, or ninety-five percent; and the ordinary biological changes a person undergoes across decades. Finally, specify any portion of the dispute that you judge to be merely verbal, and separate it from the genuinely substantive disagreement.
