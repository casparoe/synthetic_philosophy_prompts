People say that a large language model can make an "intuitive leap" — that it can produce something genuinely novel, an answer or strategy not already present in its training distribution, rather than merely recombining or interpolating what it has seen. I want to know whether that sentence is a testable claim or a metaphor. I am a machine learning engineer; please work in that vocabulary (training and test distributions, pre-registration, holdout construction, baselines, ablations, evaluation harnesses) and be concrete enough that I could implement what you describe.

Give me a test or evaluation protocol that could distinguish a genuine leap from skilled interpolation in a large language model. Specifically: (1) How would you construct the training data and the test items so that the relevant move cannot plausibly have been observed in training — including what you would do about the fact that for real models the "training distribution" is only partially known? (2) What result would count as evidence for a leap, and what result would count against it? (3) What baseline or control makes the comparison meaningful? (4) What is the residue: what would a positive result still not show, and which parts of the claim — "intuitive," "genuinely novel," "beyond the training distribution" — is no test of this kind able to reach?

As you go, mark each assumption your design rests on — about what counts as the training distribution, what counts as a shortcut, and where the line between recombination and novelty falls — and say how much the verdict depends on that assumption. Please separate what is well established from what is genuinely contested among researchers in this area, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
