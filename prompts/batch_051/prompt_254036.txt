So here's a thing I've been turning over. The algorithmic fairness impossibility results — most famously, the one showing that a calibrated classifier can't also satisfy equalized odds (roughly, equalized false positive and false negative rates across groups) when base rates differ, unless it's basically a perfect classifier — were all worked out for a static, one-shot setting: a fixed population, fixed labels, one decision rule. Now suppose the classifier is deployed in a feedback loop. Its decisions change who stays in the population, who gets a chance to produce the label (say, who gets policed and therefore observed to reoffend), and what the retraining data looks like downstream. Does the impossibility result survive this, or was it an artifact of the static one-shot picture? If the conflict still arises, which assumption is load-bearing; if it doesn't, is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful? I'm asking about the fate of the argument, not about which policy we should adopt.
