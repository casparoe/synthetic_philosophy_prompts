I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep seeing Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism invoked to settle arguments about whether a claim is "about the world" or "just a definition." A recent commenter summarized the paper as follows:

"Quine showed that the analytic/synthetic distinction is ungrounded. For most interesting cases, 'analytic' has to be explained through synonymy, and every familiar attempt to explain synonymy non-circularly fails, so the idea of a sentence's being true purely in virtue of meaning never gets off the ground. Quine took this to show that the distinction should be given up altogether. He replaced it with holism: our beliefs form one interdependent web rather than an observational core with definitions and logic layered on top. Within the web, no sentence enjoys in-principle protection from revision, and any sentence can be retained in the face of observations if we are willing to revise enough elsewhere. Since logic and mathematics sit in the web too, they are empirical claims, just ones far from the edge where the web meets experience. The practical upshot is that there is no principled line between questions of word-meaning and questions of fact."

I'm inclined to accept this because it matches how the forum usually invokes the paper, but someone with an academic philosophy background once told me that the rationalist version of Quine extends his conclusions past what the essay actually supports. Is the commenter's summary fair? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Quine's text supports only in a qualified way, and say whether the final upshot survives after the corrections. I want to know which pieces I can keep repeating and which I should stop treating as settled.
