Ordinary critical practice treats two failures very differently. When someone asserts a proposition that happens to be true but that they hold only as a lucky guess or a hunch, we criticize the assertion: they should not have said it, even though they believed it and it was true. Yet when someone knows a proposition, has conclusive evidence for it, and stays silent while others go wrong for lack of it, we do not usually criticize the silence in the same way. We may think them unhelpful or timid, but we do not say they violated the standard governing assertion. So we treat asserting without knowledge as a violation of that standard, while failing to assert with knowledge is not. Is this asymmetry defensible? One defense is that the norm of assertion is only a necessary condition — assert only what you know — and imposes no duty to assert what one knows. But that makes the norm a pure prohibition, and a pure prohibition seems too weak to be the fundamental standard for a practice that is, at least in large part, for transmitting knowledge: it would license permanent silence, no matter how badly the information is needed. Notice that the same problem arises for the usual rivals to the knowledge norm, since a truth norm and a belief norm, understood as necessary conditions on assertion, are equally silent about when one must speak. Defend the asymmetry against this worry, or argue that the standard governing assertion must be supplemented or replaced by one that also explains when one ought to speak. State which position requires less special pleading. Lead with your overall verdict — keep the asymmetry, revise it, or give it up — and then defend it. Answer in at most 500 words.
