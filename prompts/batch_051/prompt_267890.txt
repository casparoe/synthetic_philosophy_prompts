Recently I was asked to help adjudicate an internal dispute about whether a senior engineer's conscious decision to revert a database migration, or only the underlying neural events, caused a costly outage. Reading around the issue brought me to the causal exclusion argument, and I have settled on a provisional test for when an event or property counts as a non-redundant cause. The test has three criteria. First, the candidate must be a difference-maker: the effect must depend on it, at least probabilistically. Second, the candidate must not be rendered causally idle by some distinct, already-sufficient physical cause of the same effect. Third, the candidate must earn its place in a stable generalization; it must not be an ad hoc redescription of the physical facts.

Now consider the following borderline case. A programmer deliberates for an hour about whether to roll back a migration that is corrupting a client's data, decides to do it, and executes the rollback. The deliberation and decision are realized entirely in a specific pattern of neural activity, and a complete physical account can in principle trace the rollback all the way from those neural events through muscle movements to keystrokes. The decision is not a further event over and above the neural realization; it is what that realized process amounts to at the level of reasons and choices. Does the decision, so described, qualify as a cause of the rollback under my three criteria? Work through the criteria one at a time. Say plainly where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and whether this case gives me reason to revise the test itself.
