I keep staring at a parallel between dirty hands in politics and exception handling in software, and I can't decide if it's a real structural identity or just a cute metaphor that falls apart under pressure.

On the politics side: there's the standard dirty-hands picture (the one associated with Walzer, but I don't need chapter and verse). A leader sometimes has to do something that stays wrong — order a strike that kills civilians, lie to the public, break a promise — because every alternative is much worse. The act is justified, but the wrongness isn't cancelled out by the good outcome; it leaves a residue. Guilt, an obligation to make amends, whatever you call it: the leader should feel it even while standing behind the decision, and it shouldn't just evaporate because the numbers came out right.

On the software side: prod is down at 2 a.m. and the only way to stop the bleeding is a catch block that does something genuinely bad practice — swallow the exception, hardcode a magic value, push a fix straight to prod. It's the right call in the circumstances, but the badness doesn't vanish when the incident closes. It turns into a debt with interest: the longer the hack survives, the more it quietly costs, and somebody is supposed to go back and clean it up eventually.

The mapping I see: moral rules ↔ best practices; the emergency ↔ the exception being thrown; the justified-but-wrong act ↔ the hack in the catch block; moral residue ↔ technical debt.

What I actually want to know: is the residue in dirty hands really structured like a debt — something repayable and bounded, with interest accruing — or is it a different kind of remainder, something closer to unredeemable guilt, so the analogy breaks exactly where its doing the real work? And what would have to be true on both sides for the results to transfer, so that the software lesson about temporary fixes becoming permanent norms actually says something true about the statesman who starts treating the exception as the rule? Can you name something the parallel predicts that comes out false on one of the sides? I can already see one candidate: a bad practice is only a debt relative to goals I happen to have (maintainability, not getting paged at 3 a.m.), whereas dirty-hands wrongness doesn't seem optional in that way — but I can't make that precise, and I'm not sure it kills the analogy or just delimits it.
