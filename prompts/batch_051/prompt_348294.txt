I find myself attracted to a view on which weakness of will, as traditionally conceived, is largely a modeling artifact exposed by hyperbolic discounting. The argument is compact and powerful. Exponential discounting—discounting future value at a constant rate per unit of delay—is the only discount function that preserves time consistency: a plan I make now will still be endorsed by my later self. Hyperbolic discounting, by contrast, mechanically generates preference reversals: I judge the larger-later reward better while both are distant, then switch to the smaller-sooner reward as it becomes immediate. That, the view says, is the true signature of what gets called akrasia. In the strong form I am drawn to, there is no moment at which a single agent acts against her own best judgment; there are earlier and later selves with different discount rates, and the later one wins at the moment of choice. "Akrasia" is simply the word an observer reaches for after illegitimately assuming a single time-consistent evaluator where none exists. The theory earns its keep: it is formally unified, it reduces an unruly class of phenomena to a single mechanism, and it offers a clean decision procedure that dissolves morally loaded terminology.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake. Some of my worst episodes are not reversals across time. At the very moment I reach for the distraction, I judge that the work is better all things considered. This is not earlier-me against later-me; it is me-now simultaneously judging one action better and performing the other. So far as I can tell, the theory cannot represent that state except by assigning me a current preference that conflicts with the one I sincerely avow—which is not an explanation but a refusal to take the phenomenon at face value.

I want a negotiated verdict, not a rout, and I want the conditions under which each move would be warranted rather than merely a choice among them. Which should yield: the intuition, the theory, or the assumption that they are about the same thing? If the intuition should be debunked and dropped, give me the strongest debunking—is the contemporaneous "better judgment" a confabulation, a performance, or a stale preference dressed as a current one? If the theory should be amended or abandoned, say exactly what it must add to model a synchronic failure of agency without importing an unconstrained mystery. And if there is a reading on which both survive, state it precisely and say what would break the compatibility. End by saying what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
