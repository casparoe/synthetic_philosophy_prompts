My aunt goes to a church that says scripture is inerrant and that they just follow what it says. They dont let women be elders or teach adult men, and they cite 1 Timothy 2. But that same passage says women shouldn't braid their hair or wear gold and pearls, and half the women there show up with braids and gold earrings and nobody blinks. In 1 Corinthians 11 Paul tells women to cover their heads when they pray or prophesy, and the pastor says that rule was just for first-century Corinth, but then he'll turn around and say the Ephesians 5 commands about wives submitting are for everyone forever. They eat shrimp and wear wool-linen blends, and those old purity rules get filed under 'ceremonial' or 'civil' law, but they still quote Leviticus as binding when the topic is same-sex acts, which sits right in the middle of a bunch of purity rules. And when the New Testament tells slaves to obey their masters, they say Paul was just accommodating an ancient institution—but they don't use that accommodation argument on the gender passages in the same letters.

Here's what I want: treat this actual pattern—not the church's stated doctrine but the pattern of which commands get enforced, explained away, or ignored—as a text with its own implicit theory. What view of scripture, and especially of how cultural commands relate to transcultural commands, does a practice like this presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit hermeneutic as precisely as you can from the pattern. Is there a stable criterion distinguishing cultural from transcultural commands, or is the criterion applied ad hoc? Does the practice strictly presuppose a principled criterion, or is it compatible with several different theories? And what would the practice have to look like if you replaced the implicit view with, on the one hand, the view that every command is culturally encased and has to be translated, and on the other, the view that the plain commands bind unless the text itself repeals them?
