I am recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have only lately begun reading moral philosophy systematically. I keep a short, numbered statement of my present views on forgiveness so that I can track how they change under criticism. A few friends with more philosophical training have offered to comment on it, and before I send it to them I would like you to audit the statement.

Here are my present claims:

1. Forgiveness is the victim's decision to forswear, or at least deliberately to set aside, resentment toward the wrongdoer. It is not excusing, condoning, or forgetting, and it does not by itself restore trust or require reconciliation.

2. Resentment can be the fitting response to being wronged: it registers the wrong and asserts the victim's standing. It is not merely an unhealthy emotion that the victim should let go for the sake of their own peace.

3. Forgiveness is always elective. No victim is ever morally obligated to forgive a particular wrongdoer for a particular wrong.

4. Nevertheless, the disposition to forgive is a virtue. A person who, even after full repair and sincere amends, never forgives anyone has a defect of character, not merely an unusual preference.

5. Only the victim may forgive the wrong. A third party who was not wronged cannot properly extend forgiveness, unless the victim, or a wronged community through its authorized representatives, has delegated that standing to them.

6. Genuine forgiveness requires the wrongdoer's acknowledgment: the wrongdoer must recognize the act as wrong and take responsibility for it. Forgiving someone who denies the wrong or refuses repair is not forgiveness but servility.

7. Unconditional forgiveness, forgiving while waiving the requirement of acknowledgment, repentance, and amends, can be morally admirable, even noble, in extreme cases.

8. Forgiving too early, before the wrongdoer has acknowledged anything, can be a moral failing: a kind of cheap forgiveness that shows deficient self-respect.

9. Self-forgiveness is possible and sometimes necessary, but it is subject to different conditions: the wrongdoer must repair the harm as far as they can and recommit to their own standards. Absent that, self-forgiveness is self-excuse.

Please go through these claims and identify which of them are in tension with which. Distinguish the tensions that are genuine from those that are merely apparent and dissolve when the claims are read carefully. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be. For each apparent tension, say briefly why it dissolves. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set hang together, and what it would cost. I would rather have an audit than a lecture, so please work by the numbers.
