Here is an argument I cannot shake, though I am convinced it must go wrong somewhere. Consider two people who perform the same helpful act. Sasha helps a distressed colleague and is moved by the colleague's evident distress. Kiril also helps, but Kiril is moved only by the judgment that helping here is the right thing to do; the distress itself leaves Kiril cold. I am inclined to think that Sasha's action and Kiril's action differ in moral worth. But the following argument says otherwise.

(1) An action has moral worth if and only if the agent acts for the right reasons.
(2) The right reasons for an action are the features in virtue of which the action is right.
(3) Kiril acts because Kiril judges the action to be right; Kiril's motivating consideration is the action's rightness.
(4) An action's rightness is not something over and above the features in virtue of which it is right; it is constituted by them. So to be moved by the action's rightness is to be moved by the features that make the action right.
(5) Therefore Kiril is moved by the features in virtue of which helping is right.
(6) Therefore Kiril acts for the right reasons.
(7) Therefore, by (1), Kiril's action has moral worth on a par with Sasha's.

I suspect the argument trades on two readings of being moved by a consideration, but I cannot locate the exact step. Is the argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and what would the corrected version of each failing step be?
