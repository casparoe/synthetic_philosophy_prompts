I'm taking an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of mind this term, and we recently spent a week on a puzzle about whether the space of possible experiences open to a finite mind could, in principle, be exhausted. A classmate and I fell into defending opposite positions, and I'd like a written version of the debate to clarify it. On one side is someone who holds that the relevant catalog is finite: however large it is, the set of elementary experiential ingredients is bounded, and so the number of ways they can be combined is bounded too; after enough time, subjective novelty would have to run out, and every later experience would be another instance of an experience-type already lived. On the other side is someone who holds that this pictures experiences as objects whose identity is fixed independently of the one who has them; on that view, what it is like to have an experience depends constitutively on the history one brings to it, so completing any catalog would itself alter the background against which the next experience is felt, and novelty could never finally be used up. The sticking point between us was whether this is a substantive metaphysical disagreement or merely a difference in how to use the word "novelty"—and, connectedly, whether the identity of an experience includes the subject's relation to it or only its intrinsic features.

Please write a dialogue between these two positions. Both characters should be equally competent and charitable; neither side should be a strawman. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. Have them try to determine whether their disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and at some point have them press the distinction between novelty of type and novelty of significance. The dialogue need not end with a winner, but it should make the crux of the disagreement as precise as possible.
