I have been trying to settle a recurring argument in my working group on physics education about whether student misconceptions are best described as "theories" or as something looser, and before I take a side I want to test my own working definition against a concrete case. I use five conditions for counting a belief system as a theory in the cognitive sense: (1) generativity—it is brought to bear on a range of cases, including ones the person has not been taught; (2) internal coherence—its commitments do not obviously contradict one another; (3) explanatory import—it answers why, not merely what; (4) predictive commitment—its holder will risk expectations about unseen cases; and (5) patterned failure—when it leads away from accepted physics, it fails the same way again and again.

The case is an eighth-grader. Asked about a ball in flight just after it is thrown upward, he says there is still an upward force "in" the ball, left by the throw, that gradually runs out; when it runs out, gravity takes over and the ball falls back. He gives the same sort of account of a hockey puck gliding after the stick no longer touches it: the hit deposited force in the puck, and the puck keeps moving until that stored force is spent. Pressed on a car holding a steady sixty miles an hour on flat ground, he says the engine must keep adding force because constant motion itself uses force up. In all these exchanges he is confident and treats the idea as obviously true. But there are limits. Asked why a dropped rock falls, he does not appeal to stored force at all; he says falling is just what heavy things do. Asked whether a satellite in orbit is using up a stored force, he says he does not know and will not guess. And he uses "force," "energy," and "momentum" as names for the same stored stuff.

By my five conditions, does this student's system of beliefs qualify as a theory? Work through the conditions one at a time—met, partly met, or not met—and say which facts about the case drive each judgment and where the case underdetermines the judgment. Then say whether the case reveals a weakness in the five conditions themselves: for instance, do generativity and coherence trade off against each other here, and can the conditions distinguish one widely applied idea from a family of situation-specific ideas that merely look alike in words? End with a verdict on what this implies for the claim that misconceptions are theory-like. Also say which consideration or argument would most likely change your verdict, or where in your reasoning you are least confident, and write in American English.
