I keep going back and forth on imprecise probabilities in a way I can't settle. On one side, the precise Bayesian line feels close to undeniable: when a real bet is forced, there is a rate at which you'll take it, and that just is your credence; saying "my probability is an interval" then looks like refusing to notice what you'd actually do. It has all the systematic virtues — a unique number, a tidy decision rule, nowhere to hide. On the other side I have a firm, almost physical intuition that there is a difference in kind between my 1/2 for a fair coin and whatever number I would mumble about, say, whether a particular AI alignment approach fails before 2035. The coin number has a right answer. The AI number would be a fabrication, and the damage is real: write down 0.4 and the number starts laundering itself into later reasoning as if it had come from data instead of from a placeholder. I can see the standard move here — imprecision isn't indecision, it's a more honest representation of weak evidence — but I don't see how it survives the "you still have to act" point, since the moment I act I've de facto picked a point. I don't want a verdict on which side wins. I want to know what would have to give for the intuition to be the thing that gets debunked, what would have to give for the precision requirement to be the thing that gets amended, and whether there's a reading of "credence" on which both survive. If the reading is that imprecise probabilities aren't a psychological model but a norm about what you're entitled to report, tell me what that actually buys and wether it's more than a rename.
