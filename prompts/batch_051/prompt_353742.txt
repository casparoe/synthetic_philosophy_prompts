So I'm a distributed systems engineer, and I get suspicious whenever philosophers start talking about how parts compose into wholes. The one that keeps bugging me is the combination problem for panpsychism. As I understand it: if the fundamental stuff of the world has some tiny dab of consciousness, then when a bunch of those little subjects get arranged into a brain you need a story about how all those tiny consciousnesses add up to the one unified consciousness I've got right now. Philosophers have a name for the hard version — the subject-summing problem — and some of them try to fix it by positing a special phenomenal bonding relation that's supposed to fuse many subjects into one. In my day job I compose systems all the time: processes, threads, message passing, shared memory, consensus, partial failure, causal chains across service boundaries, all that. My hunch is that the subject-summing problem is just the ordinary problem of how a bunch of independently executing components become one system, and that phenomenal bonding is philosophers reinventing something like a consistency protocol. So here's the assignment. Restate the combination problem in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can — subjects as processes or threads or nodes, conscious unity as whatever my world would use to get a bunch of components to act as one. Then give me the report on what does not survive the translation: which pieces map cleanly onto composing distributed systems, which pieces you had to fudge or paper over to make them fit, and which pieces have no counterpart in my toolkit at all. Then level with me: are the untranslatable pieces a sign that my vocabulary is missing something real — and if so, what would I need to add to even think it — or a sign that the philosophers' talk of combining subjects is doing less work than it sounds like it's doing?
