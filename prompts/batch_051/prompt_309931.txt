Feyerabend's case against a universal scientific method is standardly summarized as the slogan that anything goes: the historical record of successful science, he argued, shows no single shared method, and any proposed method either misdescribes what scientists actually do or would rule out work we rightly regard as good science. Consider a later development: automated discovery systems, sometimes called self-driving labs, now carry out substantial parts of the research cycle — proposing hypotheses, designing and running experiments, and revising models — by executing one relatively fixed algorithmic loop, and they do so successfully across fields as different as materials discovery and parts of molecular biology. Does this development undercut Feyerabend's argument, or does it leave the argument intact? Identify the specific premise of the argument that the development bears on, say whether the argument needs repair or should be given up, and explain whether the success of an algorithmic loop in automated science is enough to show that there is a scientific method after all.
