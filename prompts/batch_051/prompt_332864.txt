Morgan's canon is usually paraphrased as a general rule of method: do not explain an animal's behavior by a higher psychological faculty when a lower one would suffice. It sounds even-handed, but its application is not. Consider how people actually describe two situations. A dog goes to the door at the time its owner usually returns, and the owner says the dog expects her; a crow, faced with food at the bottom of a tube and a length of wire, bends the wire into a hook and pulls the food up, and observers reach first for associative learning, motor routine, or trial-and-error reinforcement. Both performances could be redescribed in lower-level terms, and both show the flexibility, novelty, and goal-directedness that normally lead us to use mental vocabulary. Yet the dog gets the mental verb and the crow gets the deflationary one. The same unevenness appears in research practice: Morgan's canon is treated as a serious constraint when the animal is a bird, a fish, or an octopus, and as a formality when the animal is a dog, horse, or other familiar mammal whose inner life we describe without hesitation.

I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry. First, set out the strongest principle you can find that would license a mental description of the dog while withholding it from the crow. Candidate grounds include phylogenetic distance, neural similarity, the prior probability that a nervous system like ours supports a mind like ours, and the thought that the dog's case involves social relations we are better placed to read. Second, test that principle against a matched pair: two animals performing equally novel, flexible, goal-directed behavior, one mammalian and one only distantly related to us, where the behavioral evidence is otherwise identical. Does your principle draw a line between them, and if it does, what exactly does the line track? Third, give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up? In particular, could applying Morgan's canon evenly force us to withhold mental terms from the dog rather than extend them to the crow, and would that be a better or worse state of affairs?
