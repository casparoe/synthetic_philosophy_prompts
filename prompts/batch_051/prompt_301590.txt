Lately I have had to acknowledge that I have been treating my work as something more than one good among others. A friend put it more sharply: he said I had made an idol of it, and although the word sounded archaic to me, I have not been able to dismiss the charge. I want to set aside the question of what deserves to be valued. What I want to understand is what it is to value something in the wrong way — when the object of the devotion may be good, but the mode of valuing it is not.

I have been collecting my own verdicts on concrete cases, and I cannot yet state the principle behind them.

1. A man loves his wife, and if asked what he loves about her he names her wit, her patience, and her sense of humor. He believes that if another woman had those same qualities to an equal degree, his love would transfer to her whole. My verdict: he values his wife in the wrong way. He loves the qualities, not the person.

2. A woman loves a sonnet, but she has no attachment to the physical copy before her. A flawless copy with the same words in the same order would move her just as much. My verdict: this is a sound way to value the poem, not a misplaced one.

I recognize that these two verdicts sit uneasily together. In both cases the devotion is aimed at something repeatable rather than at a particular bearer, yet I am not willing to abandon either verdict on that ground alone.

Further cases:

3. A man keeps his late father's wristwatch. An indistinguishable replacement would not be the same thing to him, because the original was his father's. My verdict: sound. The watch is valued for its particular history, and nothing in that seems misplaced.

4. A collector loves a painting above all for its beauty. She then learns that it is a flawless forgery, visually indistinguishable from the painting it was believed to be, and she feels that her attachment has been exposed as a mistake. Here I am genuinely torn. If what she loved was the beauty, the forgery has exactly the beauty she loved. If what she loved was being in the presence of a particular painter's achievement, her attachment was not misplaced but only misdescribed. I do not know whether she has been valuing something in the wrong way or merely under a false description.

5. A father is warm and devoted to his daughter as long as she works toward the career he chose for her. When she changes course, his affection cools, though he insists he still loves her. My verdict: he has been valuing her in the wrong way all along — as a vehicle for his own ambitions and not as a separate person.

6. A man practices a religion solely for the comfort and stability its rituals give him. He does not believe that the object of its worship exists, and he privately treats the practice as a technology for calming his mind. My verdict: this is a wrong way to value the religion. But I cannot say exactly what distinguishes it from valuing a warm room or a daily run for the same comfort.

7. A person regards the national flag as if the cloth were itself the nation, so that damage to the cloth in an empty parking lot strikes him as damage to the country rather than as damage to a piece of the country's symbolism. My verdict: wrong way — a confusion of the sign with the thing signified. But I also see that flags matter because of what they stand for, and I am not sure why that fact does not partly excuse the confusion.

My tentative principle is this: a way of valuing is misplaced when it treats something properly instrumental, or local and conditional, as if it were a final end or an unconditional good; or when it falls on a substitute — a quality, a symbol, a function, a feeling — rather than on the thing itself. But this principle does not fit the first two cases cleanly. In the sonnet case, the repeatable pattern seems to be the thing itself, while in the wife case the qualities seem to be only a substitute, and I cannot say what would justify that asymmetry.

Can you extract the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts? State it precisely; say what it predicts about further cases of the same kind; and be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, it would force me to give up. I am especially interested in whether the wife and the sonnet cases can both be kept, and on what ground. Please also say what your preferred principle does with the forgery, the comfort-based religion, and the flag. Please write your answer in American English.
