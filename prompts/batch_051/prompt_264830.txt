I'm drafting a historical novel set in Paris around 1660, and there's a scene where an old Jansenist and a young magistrait argue about a verdict that just came down: a powerful noble gets a slap on the wrist because punishing him would have set off a small war. The old man keeps circling around Pascal's idea that justice with no force behind it is useless, and force with no justice in it is tyranny, and that since it's impossible to make the just strong, people have instead made the strong look just — swaddling power in custom and ceremony until it passes for rightful authority. I want to open the chapter with a short poem that performs this argument before the scene starts, so the reader feels the loop of it instead of me explaining it.

Could you write it as a pantoum? I like how the repeating lines in a pantoum trade places and change thier meaning depending on where they land — which feels exactly like what Pascal does with justice and force. Maybe six or eight stanzas, with the two returning ideas being justice-without-force and force-without-justice, and with the repetition doing the work of showing how each one keeps propping up the other. The last stanza should flip into the resolution: unable to make every just thing powerful, we make every powerful thing at least look just — and the ending should feel like a slow unmasking, not a slogan. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows Pascal recognizes the actual argument (including the role of custom in making power look legitimate), but it must be a poem first, not versified Pensées. And please no filler lines just to satisfy the form: each return should mean something slightly different.
