Last spring I experienced my first periodical cicada emergence, and what began as curiosity about the thirteen- and seventeen-year cycles has hardened into an argument I cannot quite assess. The standard natural-history story is that the broods emerge after 13 or 17 years because those numbers are prime, and a prime period minimizes synchronized emergence with the shorter population cycles of predators and parasites. A similar style of explanation appears in the honeycomb case, where the hexagonal cells are said to be explained by the mathematical result that a hexagonal tiling minimizes perimeter for a given enclosed area. I ended up defending the following argument, and I would like to know exactly where, if anywhere, it goes wrong.

(1) If our best explanation of a physical phenomenon invokes some fact as doing the explanatory work, then we are rationally entitled to believe that that fact obtains.
(2) The best explanation of the 13- and 17-year cicada life cycles invokes the arithmetic fact that 13 and 17 are prime as doing the explanatory work; the cycles avoid synchronization with predator and parasite cycles because the periods are prime.
(3) Therefore, we are rationally entitled to believe that 13 and 17 are prime.
(4) For it to be the case that 13 and 17 are prime, the numbers 13 and 17 must exist; primeness is a property of numbers rather than of physical objects themselves.
(5) If we are rationally entitled to believe a proposition, and the truth of that proposition requires the numbers 13 and 17 to exist, then we are rationally entitled to believe that the numbers 13 and 17 exist.
(6) Therefore, we are rationally entitled to believe that numbers exist.

My suspicion is that step (4) slides from a mathematical truth to the existence of objects. But I can also see attacks on step (1), since science elsewhere explains by way of idealizations, and on step (2), if primeness is merely redescribing a physical pattern rather than explaining it. Please tell me: is the argument valid? If it is not valid, name the exact inference that fails and say how the argument would have to be repaired. If it is valid, identify the premise a nominalist should reject, and give the strongest reading of that premise on which the argument either still goes through or plainly does not. Finally, does your diagnosis carry over unchanged to the honeycomb example, or does that case differ in a way that matters?
