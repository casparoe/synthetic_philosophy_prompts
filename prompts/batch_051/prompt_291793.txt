I'm a retired natural scientist — spent my career deciding when a model was good enough to trust — and I've been reading philosophy and theology for about a year now. In the church I grew up in, and in a lot of popular apologetics, the cross gets explained through an analogy: our sin is a debt we owe God, we can't pay it, and Christ pays it in our place. Some people push it further and make it a criminal penalty: we deserve the sentence, Jesus serves it, the books balance. The analogy is doing real work — its meant to license the conclusion that substitutionary atonement is coherent, that it makes sense for God to forgive because someone else made the payment.

I'd like you to audit that analogy the way I would have audited a model. Spell out the mapping first — debtor and creditor, the debt, the payment, the discharge of the debtor. Then show where the mapping breaks down or quietly imports features the theology hasn't actually defended: treating moral guilt as a transferable asset, treating punishment as a private debt owed to God rather than a response to wrongdoing, treating forgiveness as a transaction that has to balance. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic. Say where Anselm's satisfaction view differs from the full penal-substitution reading of the same analogy, and where Abelard's moral influence view just refuses to run the analogy at all. Then end by telling me how much of the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — and whether there's a better analogy that does the same work without the smuggling.

Also be explicit about your confidence: where are you sure, and what's the one point you're least sure of?
