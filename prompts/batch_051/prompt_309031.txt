I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on the foundations of welfare economics, and I keep finding myself pulled in two directions about interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing. On the one hand, the considerations that led the field away from Benthamite cardinal utility strike me as largely sound. Once one accepts that a person's utility is at most an index representing how they rank options, and that there is no independent psychological magnitude that this index measures, it becomes hard to see how the number attached to my enjoying an apple and the number attached to your enduring a migraine could be compared in any factual way. On that picture, claims that one person's gain outweighs another's loss are either nonsense or a disguised ethical judgement presented as measurement; denying primitive comparability then looks like hard-won conceptual hygiene rather than scepticism. Call this the austere view: there are no primitive interpersonal comparisons, and any comparison used in policy or moral argument must be constructed from, and justified by, something else.

On the other hand, I cannot make myself regard the judgement that a person in chronic untreated pain is worse off than a person mildly disappointed about a cancelled holiday as less factual than a judgement about inflation or temperature, nor can I see a state that treats such people as incomparable as exercising neutrality rather than moral cowardice. When I consider reforms that trade small losses to many well-off people for relief of extreme deprivation, my conviction that the deprivation counts for more is far firmer than my conviction that utilities cannot, after all, be compared. I also notice that the intuition may run ahead of the theory in one respect: I seem to have no trouble comparing levels of wellbeing across lives, but I am far less sure whether I am thereby also comparing differences in wellbeing, and I do not know whether the austere view forbids the former, the latter, or both.

Rather than a verdict, I want the strongest case for each side, followed by a negotiated answer. What exactly would it take for the intuition to be debunked as an artefact of empathy or of how we imagine other minds, and what exactly would it take for the austere view to be amended so that the comparisons become legitimate? In particular, is there a reading on which both survive—for example, one on which what is compared is not a felt utility magnitude but the verdicts of an impartial spectator who ranks the prospect of being each person under fully specified hypothetical conditions, while differences among such verdicts remain underdetermined? Tell me which of these moves is most honest here, and which would amount to special pleading.
