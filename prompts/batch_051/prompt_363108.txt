One of my colleagues has asked me to write out my working position on which animals are conscious for a small workshop on comparative cognition, and I have found that I can state the pieces clearly but cannot tell whether they actually fit together. I would like the set audited rather than polished.

I hold the following:
(i) The decisive evidence that an animal feels anything is behavioral — flexible, cost-sensitive learning and choices that weigh immediate needs against longer-term states — not the presence or absence of a neocortex or closeness to humans.
(ii) Teleost fish are sentient. Their pain-related behavior, learned avoidance, and the way they trade off threats against other needs convince me even though they lack a neocortex.
(iii) Insects are not sentient. I accept that honeybees show sophisticated learning, play-like behavior, and social transmission; I still do not think a bee has any felt experience.
(iv) Octopuses are probably sentient. I take their nervous system to be radically distributed, with most of their neurons lying outside the central brain.
(v) For any animal to feel anything, the relevant information must be made available to a unified system of evaluation and control; I am drawn to a global-workspace-style requirement, without insisting that the anatomy resemble a mammalian cortex.
(vi) I suspect that consciousness names a loose cluster of capacities — perceptual awareness, affective valence, self-related processing — rather than one natural kind, and that an animal can have some of these while lacking others.

I am not sure these cohere. Go through them and say which pairs are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is, which apparent conflicts actually dissolve and why, and then tell me, for each real tension, what would have to give and what the least costly repair would be — and which single revision would restore the most coherence while doing the least damage to the rest of the position.
