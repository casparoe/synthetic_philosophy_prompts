I have a rule I use when deciding whether to keep doing something that costs me a little and that, by itself, changes nothing. The rule tells me what I am allowed to believe about the choices of people I will never meet, and I want to know whether it is defensible or simply a machine for turning small contributions into a flattering self-image.

The rule started with a specific case. I run a small relay server for a free, community-run messaging network. My server carries a negligible amount of traffic; if I shut it down tonight, the network will work the same tomorrow. I keep it running anyway, at a small but real cost in money and attention. My reasoning is that keeping it running is information about what people most similar to me will do. I am the kind of person who keeps the relay alive, so other people like me, facing the same decision with the same values and the same distractions, are likely to keep theirs alive too. If I shut mine down, that is a sign that people like me are shutting theirs down. One machine is irrelevant, but many people like me making the same choice is not.

I have since turned this into a general policy. When my individual action cannot change the outcome, I do not automatically conclude that it is pointless. I ask what my choosing to act would indicate about what people of my sort tend to do in that kind of situation, and I act as if my choice matters through the larger pattern it reveals. I understand that my choice does not cause anyone else to choose anything; the whole move rests on evidence.

What I want checked is whether treating my own private decision as evidence about strangers, and acting on that evidence, is a sound way to handle large anonymous situations. Or am I rationalizing the pleasant thought that my tiny contribution counts? Is there a defensible version of this rule, and how would I tell from the inside whether I am following that version or just doing what I wanted to do anyway?
