Recently I had to make a consequential and irreversible decision, and I noticed that any attempt to forecast what I would do—to assign probabilities to my own options—seemed to interfere with, rather than inform, the act of deciding: the numbers I entertained read either as summaries of my current leanings or as attempts to influence myself, never as neutral predictions. I have come to hold the position that deliberation crowds out prediction: while an agent is deliberating about a voluntary choice, she cannot coherently adopt a stable, deliberation-guiding probability distribution over her own alternatives, because the first-person deliberative standpoint and the third-person predictive standpoint cannot be occupied at once. I would like an honest map of what would count against this. Which observations, cases—weakness of will, addiction, precommitment—formal results about self-knowledge or sequential decision-making, and arguments from our ability to predict other people's choices while they deliberate should most unsettle me? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, distinguish those the view's defenders would themselves concede from those only its critics would press, and assess whether the position is at risk of being qualified into unfalsifiability—and whether that would be a defect for a view of this kind.
