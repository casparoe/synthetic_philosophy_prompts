I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I am trying to turn a practice I have never had to articulate into a set of explicit commitments. I hold (a) that fictional characters are not conscious agents: nothing is it like to be one, and they have no occurrent mental states. I hold (b) that there are nonetheless right and wrong answers to questions about what a character would do, notice, or refuse to do in a given scene, and that the most reliable route to those answers is to take the intentional stance toward the character—impute beliefs, desires, and means-end rationality, then ask what anyone with those states would do. I hold (c) that a character is also a designed artifact, and that the design stance—explaining a move on the page by the author's purposes: theme, plot, reversal, echo—is often appropriate and sometimes authoritative. I hold (d) that, taking the physical stance toward the text, a character is nothing over and above a particular arrangement of marks and words, so that any true claim about a character must be made true by properties of that arrangement alone. I hold (e) that the regularities that make a character cohere are real patterns in the text, there to be discovered rather than projected, and that such patterns may be present before any reader notices them and even when the author never put them there by design.

I can defend each of these separately, but I am not sure they all fit together. When I revise, I catch myself moving from one to another to justify a single decision: one minute insisting that a character would never say such a thing, the next that the novel needs her to say it, and the next that the words on the page have already settled the matter. Before I develop this into a short essay, I would like an audit. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with which others, which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision of the set as a whole? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up.
