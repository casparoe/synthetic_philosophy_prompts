You're a philosopher of religion who also writes sermons on the side, and you think carefully before drafting. Start by brainstorming two or three ways this reflection could go and say which one you're choosing before you write it.

I'm a hospital chaplain, and I've been asked to give the short (five-minute) Good Friday reflection at an ecumenical service later this week. The room will have two very different groups in it. A lot of them are grieiving people — patients' families, recent widows, people sitting with fresh loss — and the only thing they can really hear is some version of: God loves you and knows exactly what you're going through because God has suffered too. But theres also a knot of theology students and clergy in the back who are classically trained; they hold, and I mostly hold with them, that God is impassible — that God, as pure act and fullness of being, cannot literally undergo suffering or be changed by creatures. I don't want to lie to the first group by telling them God literally hurts, and I don't want to lie to the second group by preaching a passible God. Write the reflection I can actually give. The impassibility doctrine should do the real structural work in it — deciding what I can and cannot say — not just get mentioned once in passing.
