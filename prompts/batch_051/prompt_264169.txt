I keep coming back to a passage in Adam Smith’s *The Wealth of Nations* that seems to me to be doing real work for how we talk about money, but I’m not sure it can be stretched as far as I want. The passage is:

“But this proportion must in every nation be regulated by two different circumstances: first, by the skill, dexterity, and judgment with which its labour is generally applied; and, secondly, by the proportion between the number of those who are employed in useful labour, and that of those who are not so employed.”

My friend, who is an economist, treats that passage as her starting point for thinking about what printing money does. She has given firm verdicts on several cases. She says that when a central bank creates new reserves to buy government bonds during a deep recession with high unemployment, the real wealth of the nation has not been increased, though the new money can put idle labor back to work. She says that when a government prints money to pay soldiers during a full-employment war, no wealth is created; prices just rise. She says that when a commercial bank creates a new deposit by lending to a firm that buys a new machine, the deposit itself is not wealth, but wealth may be created if the machine raises future output. She says that when a central bank swaps newly printed domestic money for foreign currency in order to hold the exchange rate down, the new domestic money adds no national wealth, even if the central bank’s foreign assets have increased. And she says that when a household hoards cash under the mattress, it is not creating wealth, only moving a claim on goods into some future period.

We are trying to predict what consistency would require her to say about a new case: a central bank during a supply-constrained period with inflation already high finances a direct cash transfer to every household. Please act as a careful philosopher-economist, start by brainstorming the candidate rules about money, liquidity, and wealth that could fit her earlier verdicts, and identify at least one such rule whose prediction for the new case diverges from the others. Then say which earlier case is the closest analog to the new one, which earlier case most threatens that analogy, and what your best prediction is for her verdict. Finally, state the implicit rule that best fits her record, explain how Smith’s passage supports or resists it, and tell me how confident you are in each part of the answer—especially the single point you are least sure of.
