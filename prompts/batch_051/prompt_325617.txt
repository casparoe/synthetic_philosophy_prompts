I'm trying to pin down what Brandom's inferentialism actually claims, and I keep returning to a one-paragraph explanation from a graduate student's philosophy blog that strikes me as plausible but possibly off. The summary runs as follows:

"Inferentialism is the thesis that the meaning of a claim is its inferential role: the set of consequences you may draw from it, together with the claims from which it may be drawn. For Brandom, this comes down to logical consequence — to grasp a claim is to know what formally follows from it and what it is inconsistent with. Since content is fixed entirely by these relations to other claims, meaning is holistic; and on this view representation and reference become secondary rather than fundamental, to be explained in terms of inferential relations among sentences. The post then concludes that, because it is each speaker's own inferential habits that establish these sentence-to-sentence links, what a speaker means is settled by that speaker's own dispositions rather than by any shared norms or worldly facts."

I have a background-level sense that two of those moves are suspicious. I thought Brandom's central notion is material inference — an inference that depends on the non-logical content of the terms, such as moving from one directional relational claim to its converse — rather than formal logical consequence. I also thought his pragmatics is social and normative, a matter of what commitments and entitlements others attribute to a speaker, rather than a story about individual dispositions. But I'm not confident, and the blog author claims to have worked through Brandom's main book. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is subtly wrong; say where a defender of Brandom would object most strongly; and then say whether the post's closing conclusion follows from anything that survives the audit, and what picture of meaning should replace it.
