I'm designing a diagnostic exercise for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar on conditionals, and I'd like you to write the instrument. It should be a sorting quiz of eight to ten items, the sort that classifies students by their verdicts on cases rather than by which label they find attractive. Each item should pose a short, concrete judgment task: compare two conditionals, judge one for truth or acceptability, assess an inference involving a conditional, or state a probability given a conditional. Offer two or three answer options capturing genuinely distinct verdicts.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these five positions: (i) the material conditional with a broadly Gricean pragmatic defense; (ii) strict implication; (iii) a Stalnaker–Lewis-style variably strict semantics; (iv) an Adams–Edgington-style probabilistic suppositional view on which conditionals lack truth conditions; and (v) a skeptical or error-theoretic view. Flag any two positions the instrument cannot cleanly separate, and say which judgment does the real separating work.

The items must be answerable by someone who has never encountered these labels. No question should ask students to choose a theory or use technical vocabulary such as possible world, truth table, assertability, or conditional probability. But let the structure teach: place an indicative/counterfactual pair of the Oswald–Kennedy sort near the top, and arrange the quiz so a student who treats indicatives and counterfactuals uniformly sorts differently from one who does not. Include at least one item each on a probability judgment about a fair die reported to have come up even, on a might-conditional, and on an inference such as antecedent strengthening or contraposition.

Close with a brief note on the empty cells of the answer space: combinations of answers no stable position can own. For each, say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored in the literature.
