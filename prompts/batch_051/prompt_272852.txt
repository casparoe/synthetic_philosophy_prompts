I run the grants program at a mid-sized family foundation — I recieve maybe five applications a month and have about 45 minutes per decision, with recommendations going to a board that doesn't want theory, just a defensible verdict. The money is real ($50k to $1.5M per grant) but we aren't solving poverty on our own, and I keep hitting the same question: should we ever fund something because it makes the world richer, more productive, more capable of doing things, even when I can't point to the specific people who will be concretely better off? My gut says no — wealth only matters for what it lets people actually do or consume. One of our trustees argues the opposite: that a society that can produce a lot, even if it doesn't consume much more, is better in itself, and that losing that capacity would be a real loss even if nobody ever exercised it.

Please turn this debate into a procedure I can actually run in my 45 minutes. I don't have economics or philosophy training, so plain language only, and don't just give me a list of questions — give me a decision procedure with tests I run in order, where each step tells me what to do with a yes, a no, and an unsure answer. The hard cases have to be handled explicitly: capacity that's likely to be used, versus capacity that's pure optionality and may stay unexercised; abundance that goes mostly to people who already have plenty; abundance of positional stuff where the value depends on having more than others; and abundance we honestly hope gets used as little as possible, like surge capacity or insurance. Wherever a test would normally want a number — a probability, a dollar figure, a headcount — tell me the fallback for when I can't estimate it, because I usually can't. For each step, mark clearly where it's really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume, and say which distinctions from the underlying debate can't be dropped without the procedure turning into a rubber stamp for whatever I already wanted to do.

Then apply the procedure to these three applications. At least one of the verdicts should go against my inclination below — if the procedure never disagrees with me, it's probably just flattering my priors, and I want you to say so.

1. A loan-loss reserve and pre-approved permits for a new industrial park in a struggling county; three manufacturers have signed letters of intent. I'd probably fund it.
2. A pilot for a cheap solar desalination method for brackish groundwater. There's no current market for it, and the pilot may produce nothing usable, but if it works it reshapes water access in dry regions. I'd probably pass on it as a moonshot.
3. An application from a business improvement district to widen roads and build a parking deck to support a new upscale retail block in an already-growing city. I'd probably lean yes, because it's exactly the make-the-city-richer kind of project our trustee likes.

After that, tell me what the procedure cannot do: what it won't catch, where it'll misfire, and which residual disagreement between me and the trustee it simply does not settle.

For the format, give me JSON with this schema:

- "procedure": an array of step objects, each with fields "step" (short name), "question" (the exact question I ask myself), "yes_do" (short instruction if yes), "no_do" (short instruction if no), "unsure_do" (short instruction if unsure), "judgment_call" (true/false), and "misfire_risk" (one sentence on how this step could mislead).
- "applications": an array with fields "case" (number), "verdict" (fund / deny / more-info), "decisive_step" (which step did the work), "confidence" (high/medium/low plus one sentence), and "against_my_inclination" (true/false).
- "cannot_do": a short paragraph on the limits of the procedure.
- "least_sure_point": the single step or judgment in the procedure you are least confident about, and why.

Finally, tell me in one or two sentences how confident you are in the whole procedure, and say where the 45-minute version quietly picks a side in the underlying debate even if I'd rather it stayed neutral.
