I have been reconstructing a generalized version of the causal exclusion argument, and it seems to prove that nothing above fundamental physics ever causes anything. I cannot accept that conclusion, but I also cannot see where the argument overgenerates. Here it is.

(1) Physical causal closure: every physical event that has a cause has a complete physical cause — a physical event whose physical properties are causally sufficient, in the circumstances, for the effect.

(2) Causal exclusion: if c is a cause of e that is causally sufficient, in the circumstances, for e, then no event c* wholly distinct from c and occurring at the same time is also a causally sufficient cause of e, except in rare and genuine cases of overdetermination (two bullets, two fires). Systematic overdetermination of ordinary effects is not an acceptable picture of the world.

(3) Mental-to-physical causation: some mental events cause physical events. For example, my decision to raise my arm causes the physical event of my arm's rising.

(4) Non-reductive physicalism about the mental: the mental event is distinct from any physical event, and mental properties are not reducible to physical properties, because mental kinds are multiply realizable. The mental is realized by the physical without being identical with it.

(5) Therefore, in the arm case, the effect has two wholly distinct candidate causes, each sufficient in the circumstances: the neural realizer, by (1), and the decision, by (3). By (2), the decision cannot be an additional, distinct sufficient cause. So the decision is excluded, and the physical cause does all the causal work. Since (4) forbids identifying the decision with the neural realizer, mental events do not cause physical events after all.

(6) But nothing in (1) and (2) is specific to the mental. Run the same reasoning on any higher-level, multiply realizable special-science kind: a gene causing a phenotype, a drought causing crop failure, a predator causing a prey animal to flee, a period of inflation causing a recession. In each case the microphysical realizer is a sufficient physical cause of the physical effect, and the special-science cause is distinct from it, so the exclusion principle excludes the special-science cause as well. The conclusion generalizes: droughts, genes, predators, and inflations cause nothing. Only fundamental physical properties are causally efficacious.

(7) That cannot be right. At minimum, it is far more revisionary than the exclusion argument is usually advertised as being.
