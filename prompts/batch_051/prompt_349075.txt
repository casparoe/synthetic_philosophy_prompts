On a rationalist forum, two commenters are arguing about Parfit’s hitchhiker. In the scenario they’re discussing, you are in the desert, a driver will rescue you only if he is convinced you will pay him $1,000 once you reach town, and you know that, as you ordinarily are, you won’t pay because by then the rescue is already sunk. Dana says the obvious rational move is to take a pill before talking to the driver that makes you the kind of person who pays; the driver can tell you’ve taken it, rescues you, and you pay. Evan says that is not a solution, because the “you” who takes the pill is choosing to be replaced by a different agent with different preferences, and the benefit goes to that agent, not to the person who made the choice. Dana replies that if the future payer is the same person in every everyday sense, refusing the pill just means dying in the desert, which cannot be what rationality recommends. Evan counters that this exposes a time-inconsistency between present and future preferences, not that rationality can be self-modifying; if you are going to permit preference-changing interventions, then the original decision problem was underspecified, because the driver should be modeled as bargaining with whichever agent exists at payment time, not with the earlier one. They go back and forth like this, and I honestly can’t tell who is right about what. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: identify which claims are correct, which are confused, and what distinction—if any—would settle the dispute.
