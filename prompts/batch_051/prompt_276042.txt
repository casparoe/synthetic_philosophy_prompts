I'm a novelist, and my current book has a plot device I can't stop thinking about. My protagonist is in a Parfit's hitchhiker type situation: he needs help from a guy who won't rescue him unless he's sure he'll be paid, and they both know that once he's home the protagonist will have no reason to pay. My fix is that he takes an honesty drug that doesn't just penalize him for breaking his word — it actually takes away some of his ability to think straight about his own future interests for a while, so he'll keep the promise because he's worse at being selfish.

A philosopher friend read the outline and said this is a live idea, that it can be instrumentially rational to make yourself genuinely less rational, because being credibly worse at choosing your own later good is what makes people trust you now. I can't tell if that's a real empirical claim or just a neat way to talk.

So here's what I want to know: if you had to test the claim that a rational agent can benefit from deliberately making themselves less rational in the future — not just binding themselves with a penalty, but degrading their later decision-making — how would you do it? Design the most concrete test you can: the setup, the manipulation, what you'd measure. Then tell me what pattern of results would convince you the effect is real, and what a positive result would still leave unsettled — in particular whether the person on the drug is really irrational later, or whether we've just redrawn what counts as their preferences.
