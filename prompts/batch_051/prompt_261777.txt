I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and my working life is spent deciding whose consciousness filters a scene: close third person, free indirect discourse, an "objective" narrator who is never actually impersonal, the implied observer whose interests leak into what is described. George Berkeley's idealism has always felt to me like a metaphysical version of this. As I understand him, Berkeley holds that a so-called physical object is a collection of ideas and that no sensible thing exists unperceived; the Master Argument adds that one cannot even conceive of an object existing unperceived, since the conceiving itself makes it an object for a mind. I want you to restate Berkeley's idealism and the Master Argument in the vocabulary I actually think with as a novelist — focalization, point of view, free indirect discourse, the implied narrator, offstage existence, narrative reliability, whatever else in that toolkit you need — and then give me a residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all?

The residue is what I care about, so press on two spots. First, Berkeley holds the world in being when no finite mind perceives it by positing God as a perpetual perceiver. Does my vocabulary have an honest equivalent, or would I have to posit something like an always-on narrator to make the doctrine come out right? Second, novels routinely imply that events happen outside any focalized or narrated scene — a marriage between chapters, a war conducted offstage — and readers do not take those events to exist only in their narration. Is that a place where my framework has resources Berkeley lacked, a place where the translation simply breaks, or a sign that Berkeley's claim is more compressed than it looks? Where your answer turns on a contested interpretation of Berkeley — for instance, about exactly what God's perceiving does — say so.

Finally, please discipline the answer epistemically: distinguish what is settled in the texts from what is debated among philosophers who work on Berkeley, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. I would rather have a translation that honestly marks where it is leaning on one reading than one that papers over the disagreement.
