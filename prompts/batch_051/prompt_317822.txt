I'm in this after-school philosphy/cogsci club at my high school, and last week we read a few pages from Ned Block's paper On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness. The part that set off the argument was near where he draws the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness: access consciousness is roughly about information being poised for use in reasoning and report, while phenomenal consciousness is about what it's like to have the experience. He also talks about super-blindsight, where someone can get information about a stimulus for use in reasoning even though there's no experience of seeing anything.

Two people in the club got stuck on whether this means a global workspace system, like the kind Baars describes, could have access consciousness without phenomenal consciousness. I wasn't sure who to agree with, so I wrote down their argument as best I could:

Alex: If Block is right, then global workspace theory only explains access consciousness. The whole workspace idea is about broadcasting information so the rest of the system can use it, which is just the functional definition of A-consciousness. So you can totally imagine the workspace doing its thing and the system having A-consciousness but no P-consciousness.

Jordan: But GWT is not supposed to be a theory of mere access. Baars says the global workspace is the mechanism for consciousness itself, including subjective experience. If you say the workspace is running fine but there's no experience, you're not proving A comes apart from P. You're just refusing to accept GWT's main claim.

Alex: No, the point of Block's distinction is that the functional role doesn't settle whether there's any experience. Super-blindsight shows that. A system could have all the access in the world and still be dark inside. So a GWT system with no P-consciousness is a real possibility, not a contradiction.

Jordan: Super-blindsight is a person with a damaged visual system using forced-choice guessing. That's not the same as a fully functioning global workspace. In a normal person, when something is globally broadcast, it is reportable and it also shows up in your experience. If you imagine a GWT system that behaves just like a conscious one but has no experience, you're imagining a different system, not just zapping the P while keeping the A identical.

Alex: That's the thing though. Block would say "different system" is doing too much work. The A-facts are all the functional facts. The P-facts are extra. So two systems can share all the A-facts and differ in P-facts. If GWT only gives you the functional side, it leaves P unexplained.

Jordan: But Baars and Dehaene aren't trying to explain access as Block defines it. They think the global broadcast is what makes a state conscious in the ordinary sense. So your objection is like saying a car engine explains fuel flow but not motion. The whole point is that the broadcast is the motion, or at least the mechanism for it.

Please adjudicate this move by move. Who's right about which specific point, and what are they both missing about Block's distinction or global workspace theory? Also, answer in American English.
