Look, I teach a seminar on distributive ethics, and everytime the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism comes up, it eats the whole session and ends in a draw. Somebody reads Rawls and comes in saying utilitarianism aggregates across lives as if a group were one person whose gains and losses simply offset; somebody else says fine, but if you throw out interpersonal trade-offs entirely you can't justify triaging a ventilator or pricing a carbon externality; and then we stall. Before I assign another round of response papers, I want the spec on the table first. Write the desiderata that a theory of how we weigh one person's good against another's has to meet to count as adquate — where "adequate" means it can guide real aggregative decisions (public health, climate, torts, insurance) without either dissolving the separateness of individual lives or ruling out justified trade-offs from the start. I want the cases it has to get right with the consideration behind each; the distinctions it can't collapse (I'm thinking of ex ante versus ex post aggregation, and of compensating a person's loss now with the same person's gain later versus with someone else's gain); and the formal results it must not contradict (Pareto, transitivity, maybe the Harsanyi-style theorems). Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a camp preference. Then the part I most want: flag any subset of these desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied. My hunch is that the package people casually demand — no interpersonal compensation, separateness of persons, Pareto, transitivity, and some actual willingness to aggregate in policy — is jointly unstable, and I want to know exactly where it breaks and what you sacrifice at each break. Close with a sentence or two scoring utilitarianism, prioritarianism, and Rawls's difference principle against the list; that part matters least.

Format the answer in five named sections, in this order, with rough lengths: "Cases" (300 words), "Distinctions" (250 words), "Formal constraints" (150 words), "Joint unsatisfiability" (250 words), "Scorecard" (100 words). In Joint unsatisfiability, name the weakest link among the requirements and the most surprising conflict between a pair of them.
