I keep finding myself in the same argument about why some countries are rich and others poor: one friend says the deep cause is institutions, another says geography, a third says culture. I am not an economist or a historian, and I cannot check the comparative record myself, so I have developed a rule. When someone offers a single fundamental cause of long-run cross-country prosperity, I ignore how elegant or richly illustrated the story is and ask one question: what observed pattern in the world would have to be different — or what particular country, historical episode, or plausible data pattern would have to come out otherwise — before you would give up this cause? If they cannot give a rough but specific answer, I stop updating on their claim almost entirely. If they can, I update, and I try to honor that by actually checking the one pattern they named.

Is this rule defensible? A friend of mine says it is a naive abuse of falsifiability: many large historical processes are overdetermined, case selection is hard, and insisting on a pre-specified country or episode that would refute a view just selects for narrow, localizable claims rather than true macro-historical ones. But I cannot do the full comparative history every time, and a deep cause that is compatible with every observed pattern seems like a license to keep a pet theory no matter what. Is there a defensible version of my rule — one that keeps the screening benefit without the bias toward narrow claims — or should I abandon it?
