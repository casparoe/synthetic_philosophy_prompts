I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and a local housing debate has stalled in a way that strikes me as philosophically revealing. The two camps — supply-side housing advocates and community-control advocates — agree on more than either likes to admit: both accept that the city has a severe housing shortage, that zoning and permitting rules restrict supply, and even that loosening those rules would lower rents in the long run. Yet they remain at an impasse over who has a right to the city. The supply side treats the demand of would-be residents for cheaper housing as a legitimate claim that exclusionary zoning unjustly denies; the community-control side treats existing residents' interest in controlling the character and pace of change in their neighborhoods as a legitimate claim that cannot simply be traded away for aggregate gains. My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement, and not exactly a disagreement about values, but a disagreement about whose preferences should count when a land-use policy is assessed: current residents, prospective residents, or some mixture. Is that right? If so, what kind of disagreement is it exactly — a hidden factual question, a difference about whose interests have standing, a difference about what counts as a moral reason, or something else — and what, if anything, could move it: a distinction, an empirical finding, a normative argument, or a change of question? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Answer in British English.
