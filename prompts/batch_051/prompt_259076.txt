I'm a linguistics PhD working on word-sense disambiguation, and there's a parallel I keep noticing that I don't know where to place. Natural languages seems weirdly tolerant of homonymy: 'pitch' is tar, a throw, or a frequency, and nobody panics, because context and selectional restrictions do the disambiguating for free — you don't usually measure the tar in hertz and you don't ask whether a sales pitch is sticky. That looks to me a lot like what programming languages do with overloading and ad-hoc polymorphism: the same name resolves to different code depending on the types of its arguments, and type inference supplies the context. So here's the thing: is natural-language disambiguation secretly a species of type-directed overload resolution, or is the only thing they really share the word 'context'? If it's real, does anything transfer from PL theory — the conditions where overloading becomes incoherent, like ambiguous programs or type-class coherence — to predict when a natural language would have to avoid or mark a new homonym? Or does it break down because natural language semantics doesn't type-check in anything like the PL sense?
