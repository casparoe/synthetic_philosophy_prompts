I'm a TA for a political philosophy survey and I'm grading a midterm. Here's the question the students got:

Explain Kant's distinction in Perpetual Peace between the preliminary articles that are strict laws and those that are permissive laws. Identify which of the six preliminary articles fall in each group, say why the permissive ones may be postponed, and then say whether the permissive articles are still genuine requirements of right, or whether they're only prudential advice. Keep your answer under 400 words.

A student wrote:

Kant divides the six preliminary articles into two classes. The first, fifth, and sixth are strict laws: they have to be followed immediately and without exception, because they prohibit states from doing things such as making secret reservations in a peace treaty, intervening in another state's constitution, or using means of war that make trust after the war impossible. The second, third, and fourth are permissive laws. These may be postponed, because a state cannot be expected unilaterally to give up territories it has already acquired, abolish its standing army while its neighbors remain armed, or stop taking on national debt when other states use debt to fund hostile preparations. Kant calls these permissive because they allow a delay in execution, but they are not exceptions to the law. The permissive articles are therefore not morally binding in the strict sense; they are more like prudental suggestions, which a state may ignore if following them would put it at a disadvantage. That is why Kant puts them among the preliminary articles rather than the definitive articles: the definitive articles carry the real moral weight, while the preliminary articles are only instrumental steps toward that ideal.
