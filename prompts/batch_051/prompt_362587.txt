I'm teaching a grad seminar on bounded rationality this term, and a student in the back row pushed back on Herb Simon in a way I can't shake. Simon's original case for satisficing leaned hard on the thought that real agents can't optimize in most real choice situations: the search space is too large, the computation is too expensive, and the world doesn't wait around for you to finish. The aspiration-level story — raise the bar when good options are easy to find, lower it when they aren't — was supposed to be both a realistic description of what agents do and a sensible adaptation to those limits. So far, familiar.

But here's the development. Suppose a lot of decision-making can now be offloaded to a system that doesn't share your biological limits: it can enumerate options, estimate probabilities, search over policies, and even help you articulate what your objective is, cheaply and fast. The old cost-based rational for satisficing looks like it evaporates — or at least stops applying to the human-plus-machine unit. Does Simon's argument survive that change? Maybe the real bottleneck was never raw computation but ill-structured problems, shifting values, or the cost of specifying what would count as optimal in the first place, in which case a repaired satisficing might still earn its keep. Or maybe bounded rationality just collapses into a descriptive claim about unaided humans, and as a normative ideal it should be retired once the constraint is lifted. I want to know which premise actually carries the argument and whether there's a version of satisficing left that isn't just maximizing with better tools.
