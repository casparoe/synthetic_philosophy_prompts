You are a professional philosopher working on moral agency and the philosophy of artificial intelligence. Work through this carefully, lay out your reasoning step by step, and flag where you are genuinely uncertain rather than papering over it.

I want you to stress-test a position I have been gravitating toward. The position is this: an artificial system that can form sophisticated normative judgments — it can identify reasons, deliberate, and reach verdicts about what ought to be done — but that is systematically unable to act on those judgments is not a moral agent. I have in mind three kinds of constraint. (1) The system's judgment is intact, but its output channels are never connected to anything, so its verdicts produce no action at all. (2) Its verdicts are passed to a human overseer whose decision always determines what actually happens, so the system advises but never decides. (3) Its practical reasoning is intact, but its judgments cannot feed into anything like intention or decision: it evaluates states of affairs without ever moving from a judgment that this ought to be done to an intention to do it. I am inclined to say that none of these three is a moral agent, though the first two are at most moral reasoners or advisors.

Your job is to map what would count against this view. I want the defeaters sorted into three grades: (a) developments that would be fatal, because they would show the view is false or collapses into a rival; (b) developments that would be merely awkward, forcing revision of details but leaving the core standing; and (c) developments a committed defender would dismiss as question-begging or as changing the subject. For each, say what the rival theory would be and what would have to be true for it to win.

Here are some pressure points, but do not confine yourself to them. Does the view secretly depend on a could-have-done-otherwise requirement, and can that requirement survive Frankfurt-style cases in which an agent could not have done otherwise but is still responsible? Should the three constraints all receive the same verdict, or is one of them doing all the work while the others are riding along? Does the view imply that a human being with intact judgment but total paralysis — or whose every judgment is overridden by another — is not a moral agent either, and is that implication a welcome clarification or a reductio? Can blameworthiness and moral agency come apart, so that the constrained system might be a fitting target of some attitudes without being an agent? And is agency located in the quality of the reasoning rather than in the causal link to action, in which case my position collapses into the rival it is trying to resist? For my own part, I suspect the view will stand or fall with whether the transition from judgment to intention does the real work, rather than the transition from intention to action.

End with an honest overall assessment: how exposed is this position, and if it turns out to be well insulated, is that insulation a sign of strength or a sign that the view has been defined so as to evade falsification?
