Ok, so I've been having this reccuring argument on a rationalist-ish forum where one side says "we don't live under the rule of law, we live under arbitrary enforcement" and the other side says "no, the rules are all public and prospective, stop complaining." It made me realise that I don't actually have a working definition of the rule of law that can settle the disagreement, just a vague bundle of features (publicity, prospectivity, generality, and so on) that all point the same way in the easy textbook cases but pull apart exactly in the cases I actually care about.

You're a professional philosopher of law. I want you to help me construct a working definition of 'the rule of law' that isn't a dictionary gloss, and that classifies the following cases correctly. My rough, pre-theoretical verdicts: (a) a secret police decree, never published, used to fine people is clearly a violation; (b) a statute passed yesterday criminalising conduct that was legal when done is clearly a violation; (c) a boring, clearly worded, long-standing, fully enforced law against driving without insurance is clearly fine. The ones I flip-flop on, and the reason I'm asking, are: (d) a properly enacted criminal statute nobody has been charged under for seventy years, until a newly elected prosecutor dusts it off and charges someone (the desuetude case); (e) a published tax provision that nearly every business technically violates but which the revenue authority only ever enforces against firms that donate to the wrong political party; (f) an ancient common-law rule whose content can only be pieced together from three centuries of scattered case reports, but which everyone is still expected to obey on pain of prosecution; and (g) a local bylaw, publicly available, that makes something almost everyone does a technical offence but is only invoked when the police want a pretext to move someone on.

Before settling on a definition, brainstorm briefly the candidate features and which of these cases would force you to choose between them. Then give me your best working definition, run it through all seven cases, and flag any verdict that surprises you or where the definition gives no clear answer, then either revise it or defend the surprising answer. I'm particularly interested in whether a good definition makes (d) and (e) come out as departures from the rule of law, and why, without just defining the rule of law as 'whatever prevents arbitrary state power' and then helping ourselves to the conclusion. Please write the answer in British English.
