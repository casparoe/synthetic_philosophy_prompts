I spent my working life in the natural sciences, and now that I am retired I have been reading political philosophy the way I once read difficult papers: slowly, and on the assumption that an apparent contradiction is usually a distinction I have not yet drawn. I would be glad of a careful attempt at one such distinction here, or an honest admission that no good one exists.

In Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince I find what look like two incompatible statements about how much control human virtù has over fortuna. I read him in translation, so I am tracking the claims rather than the exact wording. In chapter 25 he seems willing to grant, with the common opinion, that fortune governs our affairs, but only in part: fortune is, on his reckoning, the arbiter of roughly half of what we do, while the other half — or close to it — is left for us to direct. That sounds like a fixed share, and his image of a river that floods unless one builds dikes in advance suggests a share that is the same for everyone and can at best be mitigated by prudent preparation. But in chapter 6, on men who rose to power through their own arms and virtù, he says in substance that such men owed nothing to fortune except the occasion — the bare opportunity that supplied the matter on which their virtù could impose a form; without the occasion their virtù would have gone to waste, and without their virtù the occasion would have come to nothing. The end of chapter 25 then moves further from a fixed half: fortune is personified as a woman who submits more readily to the impetuous than to the cautiously calculating, so that boldness and audacity, not patient prudence, are what capture her.

As a scientist, I would call a parameter that takes the value one-half in one derivation and near-zero in another a sign that the model has not been stated consistently. I want to know whether these passages can be reconciled, and at what cost. Is the half a concession to a common opinion Machiavelli does not actually accept? Is it a statistical claim about ordinary people, while chapter 6 concerns rare founders of new orders? Is it a claim about outcomes rather than about the preconditions of achievement? Or is there some other distinction I have not seen? And does the closing praise of impetuosity really sit comfortably with the earlier praise of dikes built in advance, or is chapter 25 itself torn between two stances toward fortune? Please set out the candidate reconciliations, say what each one must assume, and give a verdict on which reading fits the text best without special pleading. I am not asking for a scholarly survey or for the secondary literature; I want a reconstruction of the underlying model of fortune and virtù, if there is one.
