I have been thinking about the old puzzle in the philosophy of logic in which a tortoise accepts the premises of a simple deductive argument and accepts the conditional connecting those premises to the conclusion, yet still declines to accept the conclusion; each further conditional offered to bridge the gap only reproduces the same refusal one level up. A colleague and I recast the puzzle as an argument that seems to prove too much, and we cannot agree about where it fails.

(1) If someone accepts the premises of a deductively valid argument and recognizes that those premises entail the conclusion, then they are rationally required to accept the conclusion.
(2) A valid argument has this rational force only because it instantiates a rule of inference — modus ponens, universal instantiation, or the like — that licenses the transition from premises of that form to the conclusion.
(3) But accepting the premises, and even understanding the rule, does not by itself make the person take the step to the conclusion; they must also accept, or be governed by, a further claim to the effect that the rule applies to these particular premises, so that accepting the premises commits them to the conclusion.
(4) If the arguer tries to supply this missing element as an extra premise — for example, by adding the premise that if the original premises are accepted, then the conclusion ought to be accepted — the same gap reopens. The person can accept all the original premises together with this new premise and still fail to move to the conclusion, unless they are governed by yet another claim about how the new premise bears on the conclusion.
(5) The gap therefore can never be closed by any finite list of premises: each added premise calls for a further rule, or a further premise expressing it, and the demand recurs without end.
(6) Hence no deductively valid argument ever rationally compels acceptance of its conclusion. Deduction is either circular, if the required step is simply taken for granted, or inert, if it is not.

We cannot accept (6), since it seems to make proof idle and to strip mathematics and ordinary reasoning of their binding force. We would like to keep (1) and (2), which capture what deduction is and why it matters, yet we cannot see how to reject any later step without leaving the normative authority of logic mysterious. Which premise or step should be given up, and what does rejecting it commit one to? Explain why that step initially looked inescapable, and what an adequate account of inference must look like in order to avoid the regress without abandoning the idea that deduction genuinely compels belief.
