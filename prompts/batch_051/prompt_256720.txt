I've been trying to get a handle on the victors' justice / retroactive law cluster around Nuremberg and Tokyo, and I keep coming back to Stanley Kramer's Judgment at Nuremberg as a kind of philosophical object. The film seems to take the retroactivity objection seriously — Hans Rolfe's whole defence is partly that the judges are being tried under law that didn't exist when they acted, and that the Allies' own conduct (Hiroshima, the bombing of German cities, the eugenics rulings in the US) undermines any claim to stand above the defendants. Then Janning, the most respectable defendant, refuses the excuse and says he knew the verdicts he handed down were unjust, even if they were legal under the statutes. But the film also ends on Haywood visiting Janning in prison, and the warmth of that ending is a choice, not just a detail.

I'd like you to read the film through the lens of the retroactivity and victors' justice debate in legal philosophy. Specifically: does the film think the retroactivity objection is a real legal problem, a moral excuse, or both? Does it treat the tu quoque argument as a reason to acquit, a reason to be uncomfortable, or a distraction? And how does its dramatic resolution — Janning admitting guilt but being treated sympathetically — map onto the philosophical positions that either reject retroactive criminal law altogether, permit it in exceptional cases, or allow it only when it applies the defendants' own pre-existing moral knowledge? I'm especially interested in where the film cheats: does it let the audience have a clean moral ending by making the victors' justice problem go away through Janning's confession, or does it actually leave the problem open underneath?
