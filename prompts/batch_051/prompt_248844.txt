I have been going back and forth with a colleague about whether imaginary numbers, infinitesimals, and non-Euclidean geometries were "there all along" or were brought into being once mathematicians chose to speak a certain way. It has become clear that we were really disagreeing about a prior question: what would an adequate account of mathematical legitimacy — of what makes something a legitimate object of mathematics rather than a heuristic, a useful fiction, or a merely private formalism — actually have to get right? Before I commit to any side, I want the specification rather than the verdict.

Give me the requirements list that a theory of mathematical legitimacy must satisfy to count as minimally adequate, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. It should cover at least the following. (1) The classical construction problems: the straightedge-and-compass restrictions, where a length can be perfectly well-defined yet not constructible, and where famous problems such as squaring the circle were shown to be impossible rather than merely unsolved. (2) The early modern boundary between "geometric" and "mechanical" (transcendental) curves, where a curve like the cycloid had a clear generating procedure and was heavily studied while being denied full geometric standing. (3) The slow admission of negative and imaginary numbers, which were used productively while being called absurd or fictitious and only later received a defensible footing. (4) Infinitesimals, which classical analysis replaced with limit arguments and which nonstandard analysis later revived in a different form. (5) Nonconstructive existence claims, such as those licensed by the axiom of choice, where an object is guaranteed but no rule for exhibiting it is supplied. For each, say what it forces a theory to distinguish. I suspect a great deal of argument here collapses three different questions — whether an object exists, whether we have a construction or rule for producing it, and whether it is admissible as an instrument of proof within a given mathematical practice — and I want to know which of those distinctions are genuine requirements and which, if any, can be collapsed.

For present purposes, treat "adequate" as able to make sense of these historical boundary disputes and of current practice, not merely coherent as metaphysics. Then give me one more pass: which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular positions? Briefly score Platonism, formalism, constructivism, and structuralism against the list, but keep the scorecard short; I care more about which requirements are negotiable and which are just a particular camp's prejudice dressed up as a constraint. A table is fine.
