I am a novelist with a recurring difficulty. In the last days before I deliver a manuscript, I become convinced that the ending is flawed and rewrite it drastically; twice now the published version has been, by my own later judgment and my editor's, worse than the approved draft. For the new book, my agent proposes that I sign a binding letter of instruction: once I submit the penultimate draft, no further revisions may be made without her written approval, and I cannot revoke the instruction for six months. The point is to protect the book from a future version of me who is exhausted, suggestible, and too close to the material. I hesitate because the person who would want to rewrite at the end is also me, with the same long-term goals and fuller knowledge of the finished book. Why should my present judgment overrule my future judgment? Is this self-binding rational, and are there principled limits on when one may tie one's own hands? A parallel puzzle sits at the center of the novel I am researching, whose characters are drafting a constitution and debating whether to entrench certain rights beyond the reach of ordinary legislative amendment. I suspect the political case and my personal case are versions of one difficulty, but I cannot say how they are related or where the analogy fails.
