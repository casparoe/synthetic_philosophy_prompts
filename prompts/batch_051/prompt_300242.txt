I run a small analytics team at a subscription software company. Roughly once a week we finish an experiment or an observational study, and about once a month we hit a result I have learned to dread: the overall effect and the subgroup effects point in different directions. Last month a checkout-flow experiment looked mildly positive overall while looking flat-to-negative within each device class; two weeks earlier, retention looked improved overall while every plan tier looked slightly worse. Each time, different stakeholders walk into the decision meeting having already chosen whichever level of the result supports their preference, and my analysts do not have a shared rule for which level to trust.

I know this is Simpson's-paradox territory, and I know the usual cautionary tale about how an omitted variable can reverse a trend. What I need is a standing procedure my analysts can run before any result reaches a decision meeting: an ordered checklist that tells them when the aggregate is the honest headline, when the stratified result is the honest headline, and when neither is, because the question itself is not answerable from the data we have.

Please write that decision procedure for us, grounded in what is actually known about Simpson's paradox, confounding, conditioning on post-treatment variables, and the difference between a descriptive question (what happened in this population) and a causal question (what will happen if we intervene). Assume the analyst running it has one undergraduate statistics course and no special training in causal inference; the procedure must be runnable in about 30 minutes using only the experiment logs and segmentation fields we already have; and it must end in exactly one of three verdicts: report the aggregate, report the stratified result, or do not report a directional conclusion and escalate. Verdicts that do not escalate go straight into the decision deck, where they will be read by non-statisticians; anything escalated comes to me for a final call. Where the procedure cannot settle the question mechanically, say so and identify the judgment call by name.

A few things the procedure must handle, because they keep recurring. It should distinguish a reversal that is merely about unequal group sizes from a reversal that reflects a genuinely different effect within groups. It should ask, for any grouping variable, whether that variable was fixed before the treatment or outcome, and refuse to treat a post-treatment subgroup, such as users who completed onboarding, as an honest basis for a causal headline. It should force a written answer to the question of what the unexplained confounder would have to look like to produce the reversal. And it should include a stop rule for the common case where the subgroup that reverses is one of many slices someone tried after seeing the data.

Run the finished procedure on these three cases, and show what each verdict ends up being:

1. The checkout-flow experiment: overall conversion is reliably up, but within each of mobile, tablet, and desktop the estimate is slightly negative or indistinguishable from zero, and the design is a randomized experiment with device measured before assignment.
2. The retention study: overall retention is up, but within each plan tier it is down, and the shift coincides with a pricing change that moved many users from one tier to another.
3. The onboarding analysis: the full experiment shows no overall effect on long-term retention, but the effect is large and positive among users who completed the new onboarding flow, and someone wants to release it on the strength of that subgroup.

Please structure the response under these headings, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. The procedure (about 350 words): the ordered steps as a runnable checklist, each step phrased so an analyst can answer it from data already in front of them.
2. The verdict rule (about 120 words): the mapping from step outcomes to the three verdicts, and what each verdict does and does not license.
3. Judgment calls and failure points (about 180 words): which steps cannot be made mechanical, where the procedure will misfire, and what to do when a required number is missing.
4. Worked applications (about 400 words): run the procedure on the three cases above, including at least one where the procedure gives an answer the team will not like, and say why.
5. What the procedure cannot do (about 120 words): the questions this checklist will not answer no matter how carefully it is followed.
