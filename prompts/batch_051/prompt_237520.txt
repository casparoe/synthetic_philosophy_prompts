I have been puzzling over an asymmetry in how we judge decisions about having children, and I want to know whether it can be defended or whether it is just an inconsistency we have grown used to.

Consider cases where a child is born with a serious condition that the parents could have avoided at modest cost. A woman who drinks heavily throughout pregnancy because she does not want to stop is widely blamed, even though she did not intend the harm. A couple who know they are both carriers of a severe recessive disease and conceive without testing are judged harshly if the child is born affected. In these cases most people seem to accept that parents owe a future child something: do not bring it into existence with an avoidable, foreseeable burden when avoiding it would have cost you little.

Now consider cases where parents are in a position to give the child a better start than it would otherwise have had. Suppose a couple is already doing IVF and must choose which of several viable embryos to implant. One embryo is low risk for a later-life disease and another is meaningfully higher risk, though neither is certain to become ill. Or one embryo has a measurable non-disease advantage and the other does not. Most people, I think, do not regard the couple as having wronged the child if they select the less favorable embryo for a trivial or arbitrary reason. Many would even find it repugnant to insist that they should pick the better one. Yet this sits uncomfortably next to the first set of cases. The child born from the higher-risk embryo has had a foreseeable, avoidable disadvantage chosen for it — or at least a foreseeable advantage withheld — even though the better alternative was available at no extra effort. We still do not treat those parents the way we treat the drinking mother.

So the principle we seem to be applying is this: it is a serious wrong to fail to prevent a bad you could have prevented at little cost, but no wrong at all to fail to provide a comparable good you could have provided at little cost. I would like to know whether that distinction bears the weight we put on it. The obvious candidates are that causing is worse than merely allowing, that we are more strictly forbidden to make things worse than we are required to make things better, or that there is a line between preventing disease and pursuing enhancement. But each appears to wobble. If causing versus allowing does the work, then selecting a higher-risk embryo looks like an action — you chose one embryo over another — rather than a mere failure to act. If the strictness of negative over positive requirements does the work, then the pregnant woman is obeying what looks like a positive demand to arrange her own body for the child's sake, not just refraining from harm. And if the disease-versus-enhancement line does the work, it has to explain why a moderately elevated lifetime risk counts as the relevant kind of bad when it comes through maternal drinking but not when it comes through embryo selection.

I am not asking which choices should be legal, or what I myself should do. I am asking whether this asymmetry is tracking something real, or whether it is a line most of us have simply never been forced to test. If it cannot be justified, which way should it fall? Should we accept that, whenever the option is already in front of us, there is an obligation to choose the child expected to have the better life? Or should we abandon the judgment that the drinking mother has done something gravely wrong?
