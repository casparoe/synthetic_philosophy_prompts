In my course on the rule of law and emergency powers, we have been working with the standard liberal-legalist position that emergency measures are legitimate only if they are formally declared, strictly time-limited, and subject to retrospective review by a body other than the one that exercised the power. The underlying idea is that exceptional powers are tolerable only as a temporary suspension of normal legality, never as a routine instrument of government. During the COVID-19 pandemic, however, many states imposed and repeatedly renewed severe restrictions through ordinary public-health statutes and administrative procedures, often without any formal state of emergency, so that emergency-style governance persisted for years while remaining narrowly lawful. Does the standard argument survive this development? Which premise does the pandemic pattern actually put under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the argument that can still distinguish legitimate emergency power from ordinary administration, or does the distinction collapse once exceptional powers can be extended indefinitely through normal channels? I am asking about the fate of the argument itself, not about any particular government's measures.
