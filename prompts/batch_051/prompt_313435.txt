Write a pantoum from the point of view of the Liar sentence—the sentence that says of itself that it is false—as it is evaluated. A pantoum is a poem in quatrains: the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and in the final stanza the first line of the poem returns as the last line while the third line returns as the second. Use this carrying-over structure to stage the Liar's one-step-at-a-time evaluation: one family of repeated lines should assert that what the sentence says is true, the other that it is false, so that each stanza's inherited lines enact the reasoning flipping the verdict once more. In the final quatrain the two claims should end up side by side and inverted, leaving the reader at the root of the paradox rather than resolving it. The poem should work as a poem, not as versified theory. Below it, add a short note that identifies the diagnosis the ending leans toward—truth-value gappiness, dialetheism, or the object-language/metalanguage hierarchy—and separates the shared, uncontroversial backbone of the Liar (the classical T-schema reasoning that forces the flip) from the contested commitments the poem adopts, making clear that the diagnosis is one contested position, not a consensus.
