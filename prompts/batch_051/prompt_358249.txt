I'm in a philosophy of language class and we spent a week on generics—statements like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria." A friend in the class is convinced there's a statistical story underneath all of it, and after I pushed him on the fact that most ducks do not lay eggs (males, chicks, etc.) he came back with this more careful argument, which he says shows I should stop worrying about the majority theory:

(1) A generic of the form "Ks are F" is true only if most Ks are F.
(2) "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is a true generic; ordinary speakers accept it.
(3) Therefore, by (1) and (2), most mosquitoes carry malaria.
(4) This is false: only female Anopheles mosquitoes can transmit malaria, and even among those only a small proportion are infected at any given time, so most mosquitoes do not carry it.
(5) Since (3) and (4) contradict, either the generic is not true after all, or the semantics in (1) is wrong. He says we should reject (2): "mosquitoes carry malaria" is loose but not strictly true.

I think the argument is valid, but I don't want to accept that ordinary speakers are wrong about a sentence they plainly accept. Is the argument valid? If not, where exactly does the inference fail? If it is valid, which premise is false, and what would a better version of (1) look like?
