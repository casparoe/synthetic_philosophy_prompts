I have spent several years writing automated tests for payments software, and I have recently been working through Wittgenstein's private language argument with a colleague. I keep returning to a parallel that I cannot decide about. As I understand the argument, a person who invents a sign for a private sensation cannot establish a distinction between using the sign correctly and its merely seeming correct, because the only criterion available is the very inclination that is supposed to be checked. In software testing, a test is only as good as its oracle, that is, an independent source of expected outputs. A test that checks a component against outputs produced by that same component, or against the same developer's unchallenged expectations, establishes at most that the component is self-consistent, not that it is correct. The two cases look structurally identical: an internal standard cannot certify itself. Yet I suspect the analogy may be loose, because in testing the problem is that the tester is fallible or biased, whereas the private language problem seems to concern whether any correctness condition exists at all. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or merely a loose analogy? State the mapping precisely; identify what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer; and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false in one direction or the other.
