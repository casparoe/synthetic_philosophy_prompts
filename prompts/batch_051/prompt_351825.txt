So I spent forty years writing Fortran models of tidal circulation in shallow estuaries, and now that I'm retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, property especially. I've distilled a rough list of what I think it takes for someone to own a thing: (1) there's an identifiable owner you can point to; (2) that owner can exclude others, set conditions, license it; (3) that owner can transfer it, sell it, give it away, leave it in a will; (4) the owner is who you'd look to if the thing caused harm, since liability has to land somewhere; (5) the thing can be traced to someone's making or acquiring it.

Here's the case that's been nagging me. In the early nineties a colleague and I wrote a package (call it TidalKit) for simulating tidal flows, under a federal grant. My coauthor died in 2007, without a will, and with no surviving relatives we ever found. The little consulting firm we'd set up was dissolved a few years later anyway. I retired and left the code on a lab server. A former postdoc kept a copy alive and patched it over the years, and now a loose group of coastal engineers maintains an unofficial repository of it. Recently a startup wrote to me asking whether they can build a commercial product on top of it. They can't find anyone who clearly holds the rights. I can't either, and I wrote half of it.

So: is this code still owned, in any sense that matters? Take my five criteria one by one and check the case against each. Where a criterion gives an unclear answer, tell me whether you think the criterion is doing real work or whether ownership just stops being a useful category once the owner is gone. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
