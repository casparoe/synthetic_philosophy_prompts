I am the lead programmer for a small media-arts festival that has begun accepting digital games into a juried competition of roughly sixty submissions a year, with a few exhibition slots and a small award at stake; the board approves the slate. Our selection committee keeps invoking a principle in meetings, but we have never turned it into instructions. The principle is that a game is a candidate for artistic evaluation primarily insofar as it designs an agency: a structured repertoire of goals, abilities, constraints, incentives, and modes of attention that the player inhabits, where the aesthetic object is the player's own practical reasoning as it moves through that structure, rather than the game's imagery, music, or story taken separately.

I need you to turn that principle into a reusable screening procedure that my three screeners, none of whom has a philosophy background, can run on a single submission in about two hours and that yields a defensible recommendation to the board. Please do not give me a reading list or a history of the debate; I need an instrument. The procedure should (a) separate the contribution of designed agency from the contributions of audiovisual craft and narrative; (b) specify what features of the agency to attend to, such as what the player is choosing between, what they must learn, and what textures of decision, hesitation, regret, or mastery the action produces; (c) mark the steps that are judgment calls and give the screeners language for defending those judgments; and (d) state where the procedure returns a clear no, where it stops and hands the decision to a human because the question is no longer factual, and why each stopping point exists. I also want a one-page scoring sheet that records the two kinds of contribution separately and forces a final recommendation.

Then run the procedure on three submissions: (1) an austere text-based management game in which the player must triage scarce resources among people with incompatible claims and live with the consequences; (2) a lavish open-world action game whose missions are all variations on going to a map marker and clearing enemies, though its photography and scripted story are excellent; and (3) a widely praised walking simulator with almost no goals, choices, or failure states, built around environmental storytelling. Make sure at least one worked case yields a recommendation the committee will not want to accept, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do—for instance, whether it is entitled to rule that a game is not art or only that it is not a strong case of this particular kind of art. Finally, tell me where the procedure's reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or evidence would lead you to revise it.
