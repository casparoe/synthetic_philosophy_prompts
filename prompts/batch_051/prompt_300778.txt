Last night I had a long argument with a friend about why some choices are hard, and neither of us moved. His side: every hard choice is really a pricing problem in disguise; there is always some exchange rate between the goods at stake — time, money, health, beauty, belonging — and a decision feels hard only because we have not finished the accounting. My side: the accounting could never be finished without falsifying what it is like to weigh, say, an hour of solitude against a friend's need, or the beauty of a place against the pull of living near family. The question we could not settle: when a choice is hard, is that because the options are almost equal on a single hidden scale, or because they are not on a common scale at all?

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on practical reason and the theory of value — sixteen students, ninety minutes. I would like a runnable classroom activity that makes the students feel the force of the second answer before they hear any of its technical vocabulary. The point should emerge from what they do, not from a mini-lecture. They should make genuinely deliberated choices across goods from different value families (health, beauty, justice, craft, family, solitude, community), then a procedural device should force those choices into a single ranking or a single exchange rate, and the device should visibly strain or produce an unexpected pattern. Only in the debrief should the facilitator name the idea and retrace how the students arrived at it.

Please act as a philosopher of practical reason who knows the contemporary debate about incommensurability and hard choices, including the positions associated with Joseph Raz, Ruth Chang, and Elizabeth Anderson, and as someone who has run enough small-group exercises to know how easily they flatline. Think carefully before you commit. First sketch at least three candidate mechanisms for producing the experience — for example a forced single-ranking board, an auction or trading scheme, or a small-improvement probe in which a small benefit is added to one option and participants are asked whether it now beats its rival — and choose the mechanism that makes the phenomenon show up in participants' behavior rather than in the facilitator's explanation. Do not include the rejected sketches in your final answer except insofar as they justify your choice.

The session should have at least two phases: a phase in which students make individual choices and notice their own difficulty, and a phase in which the group is required to impose a single ordering or an explicit exchange rate on those choices. The design should push toward the small-improvement phenomenon: for at least one pair of options that students ranked as very close or could not rank, the group should be asked whether slightly improving the lower option along its weak dimension finally makes it better than the higher one, and the predictable reluctance to flip should become data for the debrief. In the debrief the facilitator should distinguish carefully among: options that are equal on a common scale, options that differ but only imperceptibly, options about which we are merely uncertain, and the further possibility that two options are not ordered by better than at all but are still comparable in a looser way. The debrief should also say what resisting each interpretation costs.

Do not make the session depend on prior reading, and do not include a reading list; the ideas should enter through the exercise itself.

4. Debrief Script (about 350 words): the questions to ask, the moments in the activity to point back to, and the vocabulary to introduce only here.
