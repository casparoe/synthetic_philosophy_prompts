Ive been reading a lot of old mathematics in translation lately (Euclid, a bit of Archimedes, Newton's fluxions when I'm feeling brave) because my partner got me a nice copy of the Elements and I fell down a hole. I keep noticing this move people make, including me, and I want someone to check a distinction I invented.

I call the first move 'translation anachronism': you restate an old result in modern terms because you basically can't say anything about it otherwise. Like saying Euclid's proof that prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude is a proof of the infinitude of primes, or restating his lemma about a prime dividing a product as Euclid's lemma in modern notation. I think this is unavoidable and mostly harmless.

The second move I call 'promotion anachronism': you take the translated result and attach it to a much bigger modern object or theory, so the old author gets credit for the modern object. Like saying Euclid proved a foundational lemma of commutative algebra, or that Archimedes anticipated the integral calculus with the method of exhaustion, or that Newton basically 'had' the modern concept of a function because of fluxions.

My little theory is that promotion is only acceptable when the author would pass a recognition test: if you showed them the modern statement in the modern vocabulary, they'd recognize it as what they were doing, without first having to internalize a whole new background theory. Translation passes by construction; promotion often fails because the new background theory is doing the real work.

I've been using this in an argument with a friend about whether Euclid 'really' proved the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, and it feels like it settles a lot, which made me suspicious of it. What bugs me is this: the recognition test seems to need the very thing its supposed to adjudicate. To decide if Euclid would recognize the ring-theoretic statement as his lemma, I already need a view about which concepts he could hold, which is exactly where the anachronism worry lives. And it might be too conservative—it rules out saying someone was groping toward a result in cases where that's genuinely the right thing to say.

So: is this distinction already a known thing under a better name? And is the recognition test actually coherent, or does it just push the problem one layer back? Where does it break?
