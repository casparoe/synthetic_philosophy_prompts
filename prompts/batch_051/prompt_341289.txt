A friend who teaches tenth-grade world history has asked me to come in for a day on an idea I've been turning over on my own: that the changeover from foraging to farming may have been, for the people who lived through it, a step down — early farmers were shorter and sicker than the foragers they replaced, their diets narrowed, they worked longer, and the surplus they produced fed durable inequality. Her students will arrive assuming farming was plainly progress because it let populations grow. Plan how I should teach the session's argument: what has to be in place before the claim even seems worth taking seriously, the order in which the remaining pieces must come and what depends on what, the one case or comparison the whole session should lean on and what it must have and lack, the objections and misconceptions the students will raise in roughly the order they'll raise them and where in the sequence each should be headed off, and how I can tell by the end that they have grasped the question rather than just picked up a new contrarian slogan. Tell me too what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so.
