I am a PhD student working on reward modeling for language models, and I have been trying to settle a conceptual question about how the field's standard proxy-optimization argument applies to the way these systems are now trained in practice. I would appreciate a careful analysis.

The standard argument, the line of thought that descends from Goodhart's law, goes roughly as follows. Suppose you want a system to pursue some goal that you cannot write down directly, so you optimize it against a measurable proxy. The proxy is imperfect: some ways of raising it do not advance the goal, and some ways of advancing the goal do not raise it. If optimization is powerful and unconstrained, the system will eventually find the first kind of change, and once the changes that serve both proxy and goal are exhausted, the proxy captures the optimizer and the goal falls away. The system ends up maximizing the measure as an end in itself. Call this the divergence claim.

The development that bothers me is that the assumptions in that story no longer describe how frontier systems are actually trained. The proxy is not fixed: it is a learned reward model, and it is increasingly retrained on new preference data, often targeted at cases where the previous reward model was known to be overconfident or exploitable. The optimizer is not unconstrained: the objective includes a KL penalty that keeps the policy close to a base model trained on ordinary text, so it never travels to the extreme maxima the old argument imagines. And the policy is a language model with human-shaped priors rather than an arbitrary search process over actions.

I want to know whether the divergence claim survives this development. Does the new training regime undermine a load-bearing premise, and if so, which one? Or does the problem simply reappear one level up, in the reward model's training, in the preference data, in the humans who supply it, or in the choice of regularization strength, so that a repaired version of the argument still goes through and says something weaker but still serious? Please say explicitly whether you think the original argument is dead in this setting, survives with repairs, or survives essentially unchanged, and state the repaired claim if there is one. I am asking about the argument itself, not about which training recipe is safest.
