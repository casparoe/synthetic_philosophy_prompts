I spent thirty years doing atmospheric chemistry, and since I retired I have been trying to teach myself decision theory properly, mostly because I keep running into expected utility maximization in philosophy of mind and economics. The thing that frustrates me is that the arguments for why you must maximize expected utility, or else count as incoherent or exploitable, are always the same handful. I want to know whether there is one I have been missing, and I want to make sure I have mapped the teritory before I settle on a view.

Here are the ones I already know. I am fairly sure this is close to the standard list, so please dont give me any of these back as your answer:

1. Dutch book for the belief half: if your degrees of belief violate the probability calculus, there is a finite set of bets, each acceptable to you on its own, that guarantees a net loss no matter what happens. This is the original coherence argument, and expected utility theory inherits it through the probability side.

2. Money pump / Dutch strategy: if your preferences are nontransitive or dynamically inconsistent, a trader can walk you through a cycle of trades that each look good at the moment you make them and leave you back where you started, minus a little money on each loop.

3. Representation theorems: von Neumann and Morgenstern showed that preferences satisfying completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence can be represented as maximizing the expectation of a utility function unique up to positive affine transformation; Savage later gave a version that derives both probability and utility from preferences over acts under more primitive postulates. So satisfying the axioms just is behaving as if you maximize expected utility.

4. Complete class results: in statistical decision theory, under standard regularity conditions, the admissible decision rules are Bayes rules or limits of Bayes rules. That means any procedure that is not at least a limit of such rules is dominated by another procedure that does no worse in every possible state and strictly better in some state. Refuse to be an expected utility maximizer of some kind and a competitor beats you.

5. Long-run convergence: for a long sequence of independent choices with stable odds, the rule that maximizes expected utility on each round is the one whose realized average utility converges, by the law of large numbers, to the highest achievable average; a rule that consistently passes up expected utility falls behind in the long run with probability approaching one.

6. Dynamic consistency: if your conditional preferences violate the sure-thing principle, you can prefer a whole plan now and then predictably deviate from it later even with no new information, so you are in conflict with your own earlier self.

7. Accuracy dominance for beliefs: a credence function that violates the probability axioms is dominated in expected accuracy by a coherent one under any proper scoring rule. This is usually presented as the modern replacement for the old Dutch book argument for probabilism, and it is supposed to secure the belief side of expected utility.

I know one and seven overlap in their conclusion; I am counting them separately because the machinery is different. If you think two of them collapse into one another, tell me, but that is not the main task.

If you have more than one candidate, lead with the strongest and mention the others briefly. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good: does it really establish that one ought to maximize expected utility, or does it smuggle in the conclusion? And say how it relates to the seven above.
