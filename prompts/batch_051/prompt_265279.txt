I'm a junior and I edit the "Explainer" section of my school's STEM magazine, where students send in short pieces explaining weird stuff around campus — why the gym wifi dies at lunch, why the bio pond turtles all sit on the same log, why one parking lot floods when it hasn't rained. We get maybe six of these a week, and we publish one explanation per submission under the label "our best current explanation." Me and my co-editor have basically been doing it by vibes, picking whichever explanation sounds coolest, and our faculty advisor says he'll only keep signing off if we get a real procedure. He took a philosophy of science class once and told us to "use inference to the best explanation," but nobody on the team knows the theory, and I can't tell which parts of it can survive being turned into a checklist for sophomores with no science-methods background.

So: write me a decision procedure my editors can actually run before we publish a piece. It should be a list of questions in order, like a flowchart in words, with what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it should say "this part is a judgment call, ask the advisor" instead of pretending the checklist decided it. We have about 20 minutes per submission. Keep it usable by someone who's never heard of IBE.

Two things I already know you should worry about, so don't leave them out. First, I've heard that "best" has to mean best among the explanations actually submitted, not best in some absolute sense — like a "best of a bad lot" problem — so the procedure needs a place where it can say "none of these are worth endorsing" rather than ranking garbage. Second, explanations have to be compared against one another, not just scored one at a time, because a storyline can sound great until you line it up next to a duller explanation that covers the same facts with less stretching. I do not want a rubric that just gives each one points for being simple and scientific-looking and adds them up — I want the comparison built in, espeically where one explanation is more elegant but the other covers more of the evidence.

At the same time I know you can't make it perfectly precise: simplicity vs. completeness, and consistency with what we already know vs. a surprising new idea, feel like tradeoffs a person has to weigh. Mark those spots clearly as places where the editor has to make a call, or where we kick it to the advisor, so nobody treats the checklist as smarter than it is.

Then run the procedure on these three, including one where you should not let me off the hook:

(a) The gym wifi drops every weekday from 12:45 to 1:10. Submission A says the lunch crowd overloads the router. Submission B says the concession-stand microwave runs during lunch and interferes with the signal. The drop also happens on the two days a month the concession stand is closed for inventory, but those days still have a full lunch crowd.

(b) The bio pond turtles all bask on one particular log. Submission A says that log gets the most morning sun, sits in the warmest shallows, and has deep water right next to it for a quick escape. Submission B says turtles are social learners and copy the first turtle that ever found the log.

(c) The B-wing vending machine eats dollar bills. Submission A says an unknown student is running a long-running prank with fake bills or a magnet. Submission B says the bill validator is just worn out because the machine is old. I'll be honest, I definately want to publish A because it's funnier and would get more attention. Show me what the procedure actually says and don't let me squirm out of it.

Finally, say what the procedure can't do — the cases where we'll still be stuck — so I can put a short "limits" note in our editors' handbook.
