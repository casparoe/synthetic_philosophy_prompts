Since retiring from a research career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the simulation argument and its bearing on fanaticism about the far future — the view that the expected value of our actions is dominated by their consequences for the very distant future. I have collected the following considerations for thinking that Bostrom's simulation argument dampens this sort of fanaticism:

(1) Extinction: the argument's first disjunct is that almost all civilizations at our present stage go extinct before reaching a posthuman stage; if that is so, the astronomical far-future population that fanatical calculations require is unlikely to exist.
(2) Termination: if we are simulated, the future of our world is subject to the simulators' continuing or halting the simulation, so the far-future stakes are not robustly available to us.
(3) Computational bounds: even if a simulation is continued, the number of future observers in it is capped by the simulators' finite computing resources, so the expected far-future population may be far smaller than fanatical reasoning assumes.
(4) Insulation: if we are simulated, our actions have consequences only within the simulation and cannot reach the base-level world in which the truly decisive far future unfolds.
(5) Self-sampling: the same indifference reasoning that supports the simulation argument suggests that, conditional on many simulations existing, I am probably simulated myself; this weakens the fanatic's assumption that the far future my actions influence is the one that dominates expected value.

These may all be mistaken, but they are the considerations I keep encountering. I want a further consideration in the same direction — a reason the simulation argument dampens far-future fanaticism — that is not on this list and is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the items above. Then give me an honest appraisal: is the new consideration any good, and does it succeed where the others fail?
