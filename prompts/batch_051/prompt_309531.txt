Here's a puzzle I've been chewing on, and I'd like you to put on your philosopher-of-language hat and think out loud, not just give me a verdict. I have a friend who has very firm, very consistent views about when someone is allowed to assert something—not etiquette, but whether the assertion itself is proper. I've kept a list of her calls on cases over the years, and I can't reverse-engineer the rule she's running. I'm trying to predict what she'll say about a new case.

Her track record:

- Marta glances at a wall clock she has no reason to distrust, says "it's 3:15," and is right. Proper.
- Jonah says "the meeting is in room 210" because the printed schedule handed out that morning says so, but the room was switched thirty minutes later and nobody told him. He's wrong. She calls this improper, even though he had every reason to believe it.
- Nadia reads on the café's own current website that it's open on Sundays, says so, and it's true. Proper.
- Leo says a proof is correct after going through it line by line; it is. Proper.
- Ayesha says "either it will rain tomorrow or it won't." My friend says proper, but waves it off as a special case, not something that tells us much.

The new case: Priya tells her coworker, the day after the lottery drawing but before results are announced, "your ticket lost." It's a fair lottery with fourteen million tickets, and she's going only on the odds. In fact the ticket did lose. So Priya's assertion is true, and she had overwhelming statistical grounds, but she didn't know—couldn't have known—just from the odds. What does my friend say about Priya's assertion, and why?

More than the bare verdict, I want: the plausible rules she could be running that fit all her recorded calls so far, what each of those rules says about the Priya case, which of the old cases is doing the most work ruling out alternatives, and your best guess at her verdict with the reason. If her old cases don't decide the Priya case, say what each possible verdict would commit her to, and give me the cleanest extra case you'd use to pin down the rule. Start by spelling out the candidate rules you're taking seriously, then go case by case.
