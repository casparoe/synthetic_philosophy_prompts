I've been reading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot after a friend pressed it on me, and one of its claims keeps nagging at me: in a formal poem the meaning is carried by the whole constraint system — meter, rhyme, stanza shape, the way sounds sit against each other — so a translator cannot first extract the sense and then dress it up in the target language. As an engineer I'd put it this way: form is not a wrapper around content that can be swapped out; it is more like a protocol that has to be reimplemented in the materials of the target language, and that reimplementation is the translation. I'd like a poem that lives inside that problem rather than explaining it. Could you write a sestina about the attempt to translate a strictly formal poem, using form, sense, sound, word, keep, and turn as its six end-words in the usual permutations and closing with a three-line envoi? The fixed recurrence of those words in shifting positions should do the structural work of showing that a form survives translation only by being rebuilt in a new arrangement, never by being carried over intact. The envoi should, without turning into prose, say what is actually preserved when every word has changed. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who has worked seriously at literary translation would recognize the diagnosis rather than just the mood, and good enough that a reader new to the problem feels it through the form.
