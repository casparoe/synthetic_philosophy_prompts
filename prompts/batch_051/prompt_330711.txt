In The Will to Believe, William James writes: "When we look at certain facts it seems as if our passional and volitional nature lay at the root of all our convictions. When we look at others, it seems as if they could do nothing when the intellect had once said its say."

Draft the joint statement that would come out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two parties in the dispute over the burden of proof for religious belief. One party is a Jamesean, who holds that when a religious option is genuine—live, forced, and momentous—the passional and volitional nature may lawfully settle what the intellect leaves undecided. The other party is an evidentialist in the Cliffordian line, atheist or agnostic, who holds that the burden of proof remains on the person affirming a religious proposition and that to believe on anything short of sufficient evidence is to fail an epistemic duty.

Take this passage as the central text for both sides. Begin by stating, in wording each side would accept, what James is and is not claiming here. Then produce the document itself: the claims about belief, evidence, and religious commitment that both sides can sign; the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Throughout, separate disagreements that are substantive from ones that may be partly verbal, and say where James's own text is ambiguous enough to be read by each side as support for its view.
