I retired from geochemistry a few years ago and have been working through decision theory in the evenings, and I've gotten stuck on a variant of the Allais paradox I haven't seen discussed anywhere. The standard setup, as I understand it: in one choice you pick between a sure $1,000,000 and a gamble that gives 1% chance of nothing, 89% chance of $1,000,000, and 10% chance of $5,000,000. In a second choice you pick between an 11% chance of $1,000,000 with an 89% chance of nothing, and a straight 10% chance of $5,000,000. Most people take the sure million and also the 10% shot at five million. The independence axiom says that pairing is inconsistent, because once you strip off the 89% common branch in each problem the two leftover gambles are the same: a sure million against a 10/11 shot at five million plus a 1/11 shot at nothing. So far so good.

Now here's the variant I keep chewing on. Suppose we resolve both choices with a single random draw instead of two independent ones. Let r be uniform on [0,1] and write both problems on the same ruler. Option A pays $1,000,000 for every r. Option B pays $0 if r<0.01, $1,000,000 if 0.01≤r<0.90, and $5,000,000 if r≥0.90. Option C pays $0 if r<0.89 and $1,000,000 if r≥0.89. Option D pays $5,000,000 if r<0.10 and $0 if r≥0.10. You must choose one option from the first problem (A or B) and one from the second (C or D), the same r settles both, and your payoff is the sum. So the real options are AC, AD, BC, and BD. Work out what each of those four pays as a function of r, and as a plain probability distribution. Then tell me: does the independence axiom still force the same consistency it did in the standard, two-draw version? In particular, if someone prefers A over B in the first problem and D over C in the second, is that pattern still ruled out, or has the shared draw turned it into something else? I cant tell whether the original contradiction survives the change or weather the change just dissolves the whole problem into one big lottery.
