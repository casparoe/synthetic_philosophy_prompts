Consider a country that creates a new constitutional court to hear individual rights claims under a bill of rights drafted in deliberately broad, abstract terms. The court's enabling statute imposes an expressly particularist method, roughly as follows: the court may not formulate, invoke, or decide under any general principle or rule; each case must be resolved by weighing the particular facts of that case alone; and earlier decisions may be cited only as factually described situations that were resolved in a particular way, never as authority for any general proposition. Judges are tenured, opinions are published in full, and the court has final jurisdiction over rights claims. The framers hold a particularist view: they believe the moral significance of any feature of a situation can shift with context, so that general principles distort judgment rather than guide it.

Treat the arrangement as a mechanism rather than a thesis to defend or attack. Give a reasoned prediction of how this court actually behaves over the next thirty years, and make your chain of reasoning explicit. In doing so, address at least these questions. Which elements of the rule genuinely bind, and which get hollowed out or quietly evaded? Do the decisions converge on stable patterns that operate as de facto principles even though none are ever stated, and how could an observer tell the difference between a de facto principle and a real one? How do litigants, lower courts, and the government adapt: through fact-heavy analogy, strategic characterization of the facts, case selection, or something else? Where does the system first come under serious pressure: predictability, equal treatment, workload, the need for people to plan their conduct, or public legitimacy? Which failure modes could be fixed by small amendments, and which fixes would amount to abandoning particularism? Say whether the court would in practice deliver what the framers wanted, namely decisions more sensitive to context and less distorted by overbroad rules, and indicate which of your predictions are robust under a wide range of assumptions and which rest on a single guess. End by identifying the empirical or philosophical assumption your overall verdict most depends on, and what evidence or argument would change your mind.
