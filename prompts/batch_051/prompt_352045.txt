You are a moral philosopher who works on collective action, public goods, and the normative significance of fairness, and you are especially good at separating the persuasive force of an image from the argument it is being used to carry. Think carefully before answering, and where it helps, begin by laying out the structure of the problem before evaluating the analogy.

In a recent argument, someone dismissed free riders as "social parasites," claiming that people who enjoy the benefits of a public radio station, a vaccination scheme, or union-negotiated wages without paying or contributing are simply living off the bodies of those who do contribute. The parasite image was being asked to do three pieces of work at once: (i) establish that free riding is a form of harm, (ii) establish that it is objectionable in roughly the way parasitism is, and (iii) justify social pressure, stigma, or exclusion against free riders. Underlying the charge is a picture on which a cooperative group is like a host organism and the free rider is like a parasite lodged inside it.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds, not a generic discussion of free riding. Do all of the following:

(1) Give an explicit table of correspondences: what is supposed to be the parasite, the host, the extracted nutrients, the harm to the host, and the parasite's dependence in the social case?

(2) Identify the features of biological parasitism that the image quietly imports into the social case, and sort them into those that matter for conclusions (i)–(iii) and those that are dispensable. Be specific. Think at least about the following contrasts: a parasite usually draws resources the host would otherwise use, whereas many public goods are non-rival and have zero marginal cost; a parasite has no choice about being a parasite, whereas a beneficiary of a non-excludable good may have no realistic way to decline the benefit and may even be willing to contribute under different institutional arrangements; a host is a single organism with interests of its own, whereas a cooperative group is a collection of agents whose interests can diverge; and a parasite's survival is typically tied to the host's resources, whereas the free rider's well-being may be largely independent of the cooperative scheme they benefit from.

(3) Go conclusion by conclusion. For each of (i), (ii), and (iii), say whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, what argumentative support remains when the image is removed, and how much of the apparent force came from the image itself.

(4) If any of the conclusions does survive, state the actual wrong of free riding that bears the real justificatory weight. Be precise about whether it is a matter of fairness, exploitation of others' willingness to contribute, the erosion of assurance or trust, free riding on a cooperative practice's preconditions, or something else — and note how the correct account changes what may be done about it.

(5) Finally, propose a better analogy for whatever wrong survives the audit, and indicate its own limits, so I end up with something that tracks the genuine moral issue rather than merely replacing one misleading image with another.
