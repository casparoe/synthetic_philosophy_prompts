I'm a journalist covering philosophy and the logic of uncertainty, and I'm trying to pin down exactly why a familiar ancient argument feels slippery. Aristotle, in chapter 9 of On Interpretation, considers a future sea battle. He reasons that if every assertion is either true or false, then it was already true yesterday that a sea battle would happen tomorrow, or already true yesterday that it would not; yet if one of these was already true, the matter was already settled, and the battle could not still be an open contingency. Here is a reconstruction of the nerve of the argument:

(1) If every assertion is true or false, then the assertion that a sea battle will happen tomorrow is now either true or false.
(2) If that assertion is now true, then the sea battle cannot fail to happen.
(3) What cannot fail to happen happens necessarily.
(4) So if the assertion is now true, the sea battle happens necessarily.
(5) If the assertion is now false, the parallel reasoning yields that the sea battle necessarily does not happen.
(6) Therefore, on the assumption that every assertion is true or false, tomorrow's sea battle is necessary either way; the future is not genuinely open.

I am not asking for a survey of readings. Take a definite position: is this argument valid, and if not, exactly which step or steps fail? Give the most precise version of the error—whether it is an equivocation, a modal fallacy, a scope mistake, or something else—and say whether Aristotle's own discussion needs that same diagnosis or a different one.
