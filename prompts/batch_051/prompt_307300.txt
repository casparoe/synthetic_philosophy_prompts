Right, I need a fresh pair of eyes on this, because I have to write a short statement of where I stand on basic perceptual knowledge for our reading group, and I've got myself into a muddle. Here's where I've ended up, written out as best I can:

(1) You can know the wall is red just by looking at it, even if you havn't already checked that your colour vision is reliable. The looking itself gives you justification; you don't need prior evidence that your eyes generally work.

(2) I accept closure under known entailment: if I know the wall is red, and I know that if it's red then I'm not a brain in a vat being fed a perfect illusion of a red wall, then I can come to know that I'm not the brain in the vat. Maybe only for the particular occasion, but still.

(3) But bootstrapping is clearly rotten. I can't establish that my colour vision is reliable by spending a week going 'it looked red and it was red, it looked green and it was green' and then generalising. That's the source vouching for itself.

(4) I also think knowledge requires reliability: a true belief only counts as knowledge if it was produced by a reliable process.

(5) At the same time I don't think ordinary perceivers need to run anything like a track record before they're allowed to trust their eyes. 'My vision is reliable' is the sort of thing we're entitled to take for granted in everyday enquiry.

(6) I also accept that memory and ordinary enumerative induction can give us knowledge when the sample is large and varied.
