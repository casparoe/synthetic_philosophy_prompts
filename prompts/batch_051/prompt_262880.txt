Got into a long argument with a friend last night about information cascades—specifically whether there's any way to stop one once the basic conditions are in place. His line was that in the standard setup, where people decide in a fixed order between two actions, each has a private signal, and everyone sees all earlier choices but none of the signals behind them, you're basicaly guaranteed to end up in a herd and it will be extraordinarily hard to dislodge. I stayed up trying to write down every standard escape route I could think of, and by the end I had these five: making the raw signals observable instead of just the choices, so there's no hidden information to trigger the herding; enriching the action space, so that a continuous or fine-grained choice lets observers invert the action and recover the signal; endogenous timing, where people can wait or pay to delay and early movers get sorted by confidence; fragmenting the sequence, so different actors see different subsets of earlier choices rather than one shared ordered history; and a later public information release, like an audit or a poll, which can shatter a cascade and sometimes set off a reverse one.

But our actual argument was about the restricted case where none of those are available: the action has to stay binary, the signals stay private, the order is fixed, everyone sees exactly the same public history, and no outside information release is coming. I have the feeling I'm forgeting one more mechanism—something about the decision rule itself, or the payoffs, or asking people to do something other than just choose in sequence—that is not on the list and not a variant of anything on it. What is it? And tell me honestly whether it actually works under those restrictions, or whether my friend was right after all.
