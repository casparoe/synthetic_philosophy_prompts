My advisor summed up a position on manipulation versus persuasion in a workshop last week, and I've been turning it over since, but I can't tell how fair his summary was. His gloss went something like this:

"When you persuade someone you engage their judgment: you give reasons they can evaluate, and you let their own deliberation settle the matter. Manipulation instead steers them through some route that bypasses judgment — by triggering fear, embarrassment, or social pressure, by exploiting biases, or by rigging how the choice is framed. So manipulation is a claim about the mechanism of influence only: it has nothing to do with the truth of what you say, your intentions, or whether the target is better off. From that it follows that an emotional appeal is manipulative even when what you say is true and you mean nothing but goodwill. It also means that nudges like opt-out organ donation are manipulative even when they're transparent and most people would consent to the policy; you're still relying on a bias for the status quo rather than on judgment. By the same token, giving someone true but cherry-picked reasons is not manipulation, because the person can assess each reason, even if the selection hides other relevant facts from them. And this is also why manipulation is wrong in exactly the way lying is wrong: both treat the person's rational will as an obstacle to route around rather than as something to engage."

I'm half-convinced the nudge bit is an overreach, since transparent opt-out defaults don't seem underhanded the way guilt-tripping does, but maybe that's just my intuition. I'd like an accuracy audit: wich bits would someone working on manipulation and non-rational influence nod along with, which bits would they wince at or call an overstatement, and do the three examples (emotional appeals, transparent nudges, cherry-picked reasons) actually follow from the mechanism-only definition he started with? Also, is the wrongness story part of the definition or is it a seperate moral claim being smuggled in.
