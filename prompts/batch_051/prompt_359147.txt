In a constitutional-design seminar I keep returning to Burke’s idea that longstanding institutions can carry accumulated experience that no present committee can fully reconstruct from first principles, and to the Chesterton’s fence heuristic that one should not tear down an old institution until one understands the problem it was built to solve. Suppose a legislature adopted the following rule to make that heuristic explicit and legally binding. Before any statute or administrative regulation more than twenty years old may be repealed or substantially amended, the responsible committee must publish a “fence report.” The report must identify the specific problem the rule was introduced to solve, using the legislative and regulatory record but without assuming the stated rationale was the real one; the groups that currently benefit from the rule, including those who would not appear in official testimony; the full costs the rule imposes; and a six-month suspension trial with pre-registered metrics. Repeal may proceed only if the trial shows either that the original problem no longer arises or that the rule’s costs exceed its benefits according to those metrics.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave over thirty years. What does it incentivize among legislators, regulators, lobbyists, and the civil servants who write fence reports? Where is it gamed first—through strategic choice of metrics, grandfathering existing arrangements, deliberate maladministration during the suspension trial, or collusion among beneficiary groups? Would it tend to protect genuinely useful old institutions, or would it mostly create a market in favorable fence reports and a new vocabulary for justifying decisions already made? Say which failure modes are fixable and whether the fixes would gut the original Burkean intent.
