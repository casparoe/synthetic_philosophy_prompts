I'm a doctoral student working on seventeenth-century rationalism, and I've been trying to get clear on the cosmological argument in Leibniz's 1697 essay On the Ultimate Origination of Things. Leibniz there argues that the whole series of contingent things cannot have its sufficient reason within itself, because each member of the series is contingent and the aggregate of contingents is itself contingent; hence there must be a reason outside the series, and that reason must be a necessary being—God. So far so good. But then he anticipates an objection: if there is a sufficient reason why God chose to create this world rather than some other, does that not make this world necessary? His answer is that the world is necessary only on the hypothesis that God chose it, i.e., hypothetically necessary, not absolutely necessary. He says something like: once God has decreed this world, its existence follows with hypothetical necessity, just as a conclusion follows necessarily once the premises are granted. But I keep feeling that this doesn't get him out of the problem. Here is the argument I reconstruct from his own commitments:

1. For every contingent fact, there must be a sufficient reason why it obtains rather than some alternative. (Leibniz's PSR, as he uses it in the essay.)
2. The fact that this particular world exists is a contingent fact.
3. So there must be a sufficient reason why this world exists.
4. The only candidate for that reason is God's free choice of this world, grounded in God's judgment that this world is the best of all possible worlds.
5. Now, either God's choice has a sufficient reason or it does not.
6. If it has a sufficient reason, then that reason must be such that, given it, God could not have chosen otherwise—otherwise it would not be a sufficient reason, since it would not fully explain why God chose this rather than another.
7. If God could not have chosen otherwise, then the existence of this world follows necessarily, which contradicts the premise that it is contingent (premise 2), and also seems to collapse all modal distinctions.
8. If God's choice does not have a sufficient reason, then Leibniz's PSR is false, and the world's existence rests ultimately on a brute fact.
9. Therefore either Leibniz's PSR leads to the conclusion that this world is absolutely necessary, or his PSR is false. Either way, his position is untenable.

I suspect the weak step is (6), because Leibniz would say a sufficient reason can incline without necessitating, but I can't make that work formally: if the reason genuinely suffices for the choice, how could the choice have been otherwise? My advisor keeps telling me to look at the distinction between absolute and hypothetical necessity, but as far as I can tell, that distinction is exactly what premise (6) is supposed to undermine: once we admit that the hypothesis (God's decree) is itself contingent, the necessity it confers is only conditional, so it doesn't explain why the hypothesis holds rather than another. So where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I have to give up a premise, which one should I give up, and what does that cost for the cosmological argument as Leibniz presents it?
