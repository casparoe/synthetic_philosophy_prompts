I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of death and immortality, and we've been stuck on the same argument for months: whether heaven—or any literally endless life—is even coherent, because of boredom. The Williams-flavored side (his Makropulos case, the centuries-old woman who declines the elixir because everything has long since become tedious) insists that any finite creature with a finite set of desires and projects will eventually run through every combination of them over infinite time. And they've clarified that they don't just mean boredom in the sense of wanting new entertainment; they mean the loss of the sense that one's projects matter, once the limits, urgency, and unfinishedness that give a finite life its shape are gone. So "eternal bliss" either secretly changes the person into someone who no longer cares about the things that made her life hers, or it stipulates away the boredom without explaining how. The other side says this is just a parochial picture of what an endless life could be: the everlasting enjoyment of an infinite object (God), or a life in which new desires and new goods arrive without limit, or a transformation that removes the possibility of boredom without destroying identity. They add that the Williams side is fetishizing scarcity—loving a person or contemplating a truth doesn't need to be able to end in order to matter.

I have a working hypothesis about why we're stuck, but I don't want to bias the answer. It looks to me like the two sides are answering different questions: one is doing psychology about how finite interests habituate across infinite time, and the other is doing conceptual engineering about what counts as heaven or as the same person over time. But it could also be a real disagreement about personal identity, about wether a finite mind can enjoy an infinite object, or about whether the "meaning" the Williams side appeals to is something we should want to preserve. I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, a thought experiment, a formal model of desire-exhaustion, a change of question, or an argument that no one should expect movement? Please also say how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of.
