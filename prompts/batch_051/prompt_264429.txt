I keep returning to a parallel between two ideas from different fields and I want a committed verdict on whether it is a real structural identity or a loose analogy built on the word "prior." In game theory and formal epistemology, the common prior assumption says that rational agents share a prior probability distribution, so any disagreement in their posterior beliefs must trace entirely to differences in their private information; Aumann's agreement theorem then makes the point vivid by showing that common knowledge of posteriors leaves no room to disagree. In algorithmic information theory, Solomonoff induction begins from a canonical universal prior over computable hypotheses, defined via a universal Turing machine, and treats it as the objectively correct starting point for inductive inference. Both read to me as the same move: refusing to let rational belief start from arbitrary subjective priors and replacing them with a single privileged one. But the two priors may be "privileged" for different reasons—one because it is shared, the other because of its simplicity-weighted convergence properties. Is this the same idea in two places, and if so, which results should transfer (for instance, should there be a Solomonoff analogue of Aumann's agreement theorem, or does the common prior assumption inherit any of Solomonoff's incomputability worries)? If not, name at least one specific prediction the parallel makes that fails in one of the settings. I would rather you took a definite position and defended it than surveyed the options.
