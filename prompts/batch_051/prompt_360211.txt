A couple of friends of mine got stuck in a long argument over dinner about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and I'm writing out the moves because I couldn't referee it in my head. Neither of them is a philosopher, but they'd both read enough to be dangerous. (1) Maya opened with the familiar line that guilt is about what you did while shame is about what you are, and argued that guilt is the healthier, more useful moral emotion because it stays attached to the act: if you feel guilt, the repair is still in your hands — apologize, make restitution, change the behavior — whereas shame tells you the whole self is defective, so the natural responses are to hide, deflect, or lash out, not to fix anything. (2) Dan shot back that that's a cartoon of shame. A lot of shame isn't "I'm worthless"; it's the sting of falling short of standards you actually care about, often standards held by people you respect. And guilt, he said, can be just as pathological — scrupulosity, endless self-lacerating over trivialities, no outside check on whether your guilt is tracking anything real. He added that shame does a job guilt can't: it ties you to being seen, and morality is lived with other people, not in a private monologue, so an emotion that tracks your standing in their eyes is tracking something morally real. (3) Maya answered that being seen is exactly the problem. Guilt can fire when nobody else finds out; shame only binds when you might be caught, so someone low in guilt but high in shame just becomes a better concealer. And worse, if your community's norms are corrupt, guilt — being your own verdict on the act — can be the thing that lets you break away, while shame chains you to whatever the audience happens to approve of. Shame, she said, is an emotion for conformists. (4) Dan replied that this makes guilt sound more private than it actually is. Your "own" verdict is usually the internalized voice of other people or of your upbringing, so the difference between the two emotions is smaller than she's admitting; both get their content from somewhere social. And of course shame can track corrupt norms, but so can guilt — people feel guilty about perfectly harmless things their culture taught them to see as wrong. The real question, he said, isn't which emotion can go bad, but which one does more moral work when it goes right; his answer was shame, because it keeps you answerable to an actual moral community instead of making your own conscience the final court of appeal. (5) Maya said fine, but compare the failure modes. When guilt misfires, it's local — "that particular act was wrong" — and you can walk that back. When shame misfires, it indicts the whole self, and the person has nowhere to go; that's the road to depression, withdrawal, or even resentment-driven aggression. Guilt fails safer. (6) Dan, almost rolling his eyes, said that assumes ordinary people maintain the act/self seperation she started with, and they mostly don't. Guilt spirals into "what kind of monster would do that" all the time, and mature shame can be "that was beneath me" rather than "I am rotten." So failing safely isn't a property of the emotion; it's a property of how reflectively someone handles whichever emotion they've got. At that point someone switched the subject.

So I'm left with two questions. First, who's right about what across (1)–(6)? Don't just declare a winner — go move by move and say what each side gets right and wrong, and what both of them are missing. Second, suppose we did reach a verdict about which emotion is better: could a culture actually choose to cultivate one emotional style over the other, or is that sort of thing set too deep — by child-rearing, religion, social structure — for a culture to just decide to change it? Please answer in American English.
