I'm drafting a lecture for an interdisciplinary seminar on risk and decision, and I want to spend one session on Dostoevsky's The Gambler, read not as clinical pathology but as pressure-testing the tools of decision theory. The session's organizing question is what the novel shows about an agent whose attraction to risk cannot be cleanly located in a utility function over money. I want the reading to work with two formal anchors. The first is expected utility theory, which must explain a gambler's choices through the curvature of utility over outcomes: a risk-seeker has convex utility for money, or an unusual taste for some non-monetary consequence of gambling. The second is Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, which separates the evaluation of outcomes from the agent's attitude to the spread of those outcomes. There, a strictly increasing risk function r transforms the cumulative probability of receiving at least a given outcome; an agent whose r inflates those probabilities (r(p) > p) is globally risk-seeking even with a linear utility for money, because better outcomes are weighted as if more probable than they are.

Your deliverable is a reading of the novel that takes both anchors seriously and refuses both reductions: neither treating the fiction as a mere illustration of the theory, nor stretching the theory until it says whatever the fiction says. In particular: is Alexei Ivanovich best read as a risk-weighted expected utility maximizer with an overweighting risk function, as an expected utility maximizer with an odd utility function, or as someone who defeats both models by caring about the gamble itself — the suspended moment at the table — in a way that enters the framework only as an outcome labeled "the thrill" and thereby makes the model formally true and substantively empty? Anchor the reading in the major scenes you can recall, including his obsessive play at the roulette wheel, the grandmother's ruinous streak at the casino, and the fortune he wins for Polina and still cannot walk away from. Say where the risk-weighting machinery genuinely illuminates the psychology, and where it distorts it by translating compulsions into preferences. Do not quote from the text; paraphrase remembered detail and flag any detail whose accuracy matters to the reading but about which your memory is uncertain. End with what the novel adds to the decision-theoretic debate that the formalism alone doesn't, and then mark where your reading is most likely to fail: which alternative interpretation of Alexei, or which stricter reading of RWEU, would most force you to revise the overall verdict.
