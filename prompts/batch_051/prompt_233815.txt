I run a small software studio — about 15 people — and every quarter we drag ourselves into a "go or kill" meeting for the projects that are limping. The pattern is always the same: we've already spent four or five months on something, the original estimate is long gone, and somebody says "we're too far in to stop now." I ended up reading about the dollar auction, where two bidders keep outbidding each other for a dollar and both can end up paying more than the prize because neither wants to eat the bid they already made, and about escalation of commitment, which sounds like the same disease. It felt so familiar I almost laughed: our version is two teams commiting more and more to a feature nobody's sure anyone wants.

What I need isn't a blog post that says "think about opportunity costs." It's a procedure I can actually run in 30 minutes per project, with questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it says kill, where it says keep going, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because no checklist can make it. The room is full of the people who built the thing and really want it to live, so the procedure should catch the moment we're just bidding because we already bid. Then run it on two cases: an internal dashboard tool that's been "two weeks from done" for three months and still has no users, and a client project we knowingly took below cost to keep a relationship, which keeps growing extra requests.
