I've been taking a historiography course and there's a tension I can't get out of my head. We're taught that good historians don't moralize about the distant past: if you write about Roman slavery or the Inquisition, you're supposed to explain what people thought they were doing, what the institutions allowed, what the available alternatives were—but saying that someone was a monster or that a practice was evil is considered a bit amateurish, like you've stopped doing history and started doing something else. At the same time, the discipline seems perfectly comfortable with strong moral language when the subject is closer to us—the Holocaust, Stalin's purges, the Atlantic slave trade as treated in recent scholarship. Nobody tells a historian of Nazi Germany that they're being presentist for writing a book that condemns perpetrators. I'm not crying hypocrisy; I want to know what this pattern commits the discipline to.

In the preface to his History of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples, Ranke says something like this: history has been given the office of judging the past and instructing the present for the benefit of future ages, but the present book will try only to show how things actually happened. I read that as a statement about the proper task of history, not as a claim that historians lack moral commitments. So my question is: does this practice—bracketing moral judgment for most of the past but allowing it for some recent atrocities—presuppose a coherent theory about whether historians can escape the standards of their own time? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where it's underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change in historians' training and writing if we replaced that theory with a straight presentism: the view that all historical writing inevitably evaluates the past by the historian's own moral standards.
