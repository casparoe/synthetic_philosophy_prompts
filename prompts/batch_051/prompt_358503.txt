You are a professional epistemologist who specializes in testimony and social epistemology. Think through the following argument carefully; start by reconstructing each numbered step in its strongest plausible reading before you assess it.

A hearer is told something she cannot check for herself: a mechanic reports which part of an engine is failing, or a stranger gives directions to a station.

(1) If testimonial justification is reducible to the hearer's other sources of justification, then the hearer is justified in believing the report only if she has non-testimonial evidence that the speaker is reliable about the relevant subject matter.
(2) The only non-testimonial way to acquire evidence that a speaker is reliable is to observe a track record in which that speaker's reports, or reports of that type of speaker, match facts the hearer has observed independently.
(3) In the cases described, the hearer has no such track record for the individual speaker and at most a coarse, untested one for the type.
(4) So, if reductionism is true, the hearer is not justified in believing the report.
(5) But the hearer plainly is justified in believing the report, absent defeaters.
(6) Therefore, reductionism about testimonial justification is false; testimony is a basic source of justification.

Is the argument deductively valid? If not, where exactly does it go wrong? If it is valid, state which premise a reductionist should reject and give the strongest corrected version of it that still yields a defensible reductionism. In particular, say whether step (2) smuggles in an overly demanding picture of what it is to have evidence, and whether an inductive justification drawn from general human experience could cover the strangers in step (3).
