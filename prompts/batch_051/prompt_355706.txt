I am the head of research at a small quantitative betting syndicate, and I need help turning a principle I half-understand into a procedure my analysts can actually run. We put on roughly forty positions a week across sports and a few financial props. The edges are small, the bankroll compounds, and every few months a single oversized position on a supposed sure thing gives back a season's gains. I have read that for repeated bets the rational target is not expected winnings but expected log wealth — Kelly betting, geometric growth — and I follow the toy derivation with a known coin-toss probability. What I do not have is a decision procedure for our actual cases, where the probability is an estimate from a model or a tipster, the payoff is not fixed in advance, positions are correlated, several opportunities compete for the same bankroll at once, and we often cannot get the full computed stake down at the quoted price before the line moves. Please write the procedure so that an analyst with one statistics course and an hour per decision can run it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values or risk tolerance rather than arithmetic.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) our model prices a team at 61% against even odds; (2) a tipster with a short but dramatic record insists a 10-to-1 shot is really 7-to-1, though we can only guess at his calibration; and (3) four small-edged positions that all depend heavily on the same underlying outcome, so they are far from independent. Make one of the three a case where the procedure tells us to do something we will not like, and end with a section on what the procedure cannot do — the realistic cases where it will misfire or go silent, and the distinctions the simplified version has to drop in order to stay usable. Please also separate what is settled here, in the sense of mathematics that no competent person disputes, from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this — for instance, whether full Kelly is a norm to follow or merely one point on a larger risk-return frontier — and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
