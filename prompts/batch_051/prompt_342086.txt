In historical writing, non-human causes do not all enjoy the same standing. A historian who says that the Black Death was a demographic shock that loosened the bonds of serfdom and raised the price of labor can make that argument without defending the very idea that a microbe belongs in a historical explanation. A historian who says that the cold, wet decades of the Little Ice Age drove the harvest failures behind the crises of the seventeenth century, or that the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora helped produce the year without a summer and the dearth and unrest that followed it, will be met with a different kind of suspicion: that the argument is environmental determinism, that it flattens human agency, that it mistakes weather or geology for history. The pathogen is allowed into the room of historical causes; the volcano and the climate pattern are asked to wait outside.

Yet the three cases look, on their face, structurally similar. A plague bacterium has no intentions; neither does a late frost, a drought, or a volcanic aerosol veil. Each reaches human affairs through the same intermediate things—bodies, harvests, prices, trade, settlement—and in each case those effects are filtered through human institutions before they become the outcomes we call historical. I want to know whether the asymmetry between the respectable cause and the suspect one tracks a real distinction. Try to construct the strongest principled difference you can, whether it concerns the discreteness of the shock, the directness of the mechanism, the timescale, the exogeneity, the reversibility, or something else. Then stress-test your principle against nearby cases that look mechanistically similar: a crop blight set beside a killing frost, a plague epidemic set beside an eruption that brings famines. Where the principle fails, admit it. In the end, tell me whether historians should keep this asymmetry, reverse it, or discard it.
