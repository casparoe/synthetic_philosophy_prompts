I keep encountering a strong claim about word embeddings, often presented as established: that semantic similarity is encoded as distance in embedding space, that relations like analogy show up as directions or vector offsets, and that the geometry of the space reveals semantic structure itself. I want to know how one could actually test that conceptual claim. Design the best test, or battery of tests, you can for the proposition that embedding geometry reflects semantic structure, as against the deflationary alternative that the geometry is a byproduct of the training objective, the evaluation setup, or the projections through which we inspect the space. Be concrete about the data, controls, and measurements, and for each test say what result would favor the strong reading and what would favor the deflationary reading; then say what a positive result would and would not settle. Finally, identify the residue that no such test can reach even in principle, including whether a fully successful geometry would show that the model represents meaning or only that it has organized distributional information in a geometrically convenient way. Throughout, separate what is already settled from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on these systems, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. I would like the response as continuous prose only, like a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
