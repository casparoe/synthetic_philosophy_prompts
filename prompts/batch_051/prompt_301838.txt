I am a high school student, and in my biology class we were taught that a population is undergoing Darwinian selection only if three conditions hold: individuals vary, some of that variation is inherited, and different variants reproduce at different rates. I have been trying to apply these conditions outside biology, and I keep coming back to a case that seems to sit right at the edge.

Consider what I will call a population of AI systems. Foundation models vary because they are trained on different data and fine-tuned with different architectures, amounts of compute, and alignment methods. Inheritance seems present when a model is copied or distilled and then used as the starting point for a new model, since its weights are passed on, though not always perfectly. Differential reproduction occurs when models are chosen for further investment, deployment, or use as base models because they score well on benchmarks, attract users, or pass safety review. In some cases, later models are trained partly on text or code produced by earlier models, so traits can be passed through training data as well as through weights, even when no human specifically intends to transmit them.

Does this case actually qualify as Darwinian selection? If it does, should we call it natural selection or artificial selection? Go through the three conditions honestly. Where the answer is not a clear yes or no, say whether the difficulty comes from the conditions being too coarse or from the case being under-described. Finally, take and defend a position on whether this way of thinking gives genuine support to the claim that selection will favor AI systems at human expense, or whether it is a metaphor that distracts from the human decisions actually doing the selecting. Use American English.
