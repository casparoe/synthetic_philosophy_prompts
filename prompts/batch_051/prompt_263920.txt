I have been trying to settle a conflict between a principle I find compelling and an intuition I cannot get rid of, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a quick choice of one side over the other. The principle — call it the Source Principle — says that a person is morally responsible for an action only if they are the ultimate source of that action. Being the ultimate source would require being responsible for the character and mental states from which the action flowed, but those reach back to heredity, upbringing, and circumstances no one chose. So, by the Source Principle, no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything, and all judgments of blame and credit are, strictly speaking, false. I find the argument hard to resist because it is so economical: a single, plausible-sounding requirement seems to explain the failure of blame and credit at once. Yet I also cannot shake the intuition that a person who deliberately betrays a friend for a small advantage is blameworthy — not just in the sense that blaming them would be socially useful, but in a deeper sense. I feel indignant, and I cannot bring myself to treat that indignation as nothing more than a useful illusion. I have also come across the idea that our reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, the hurt we feel on a victim's behalf — are natural and inescapable, and that questions about responsibility are really questions about when we may suspend those attitudes, not whether the whole practice rests on a metaphysical mistake. On that view, we drop the reactive stance toward someone only for local reasons, such as that they did not know what they were doing or could not help themselves, not because of the general truth of determinism. I am unsure whether this solves the problem or changes the subject. What I want to know is this: should the Source Principle, as an economical and principled theory, lead me to debunk the intuition that the betrayer is truly blameworthy? Or should the firmness of that intuition lead me to amend or abandon the principle? Is there a reading on which both survive — for instance, one where blame is justified as a reactive attitude even if the Source Principle is true — and would that reading preserve the deep kind of blame, or only a deflated substitute? Give the strongest case for each move, and then say which you would make.
