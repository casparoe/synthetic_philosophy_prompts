I am a PhD candidate in political philosophy working on a comparative project about what makes a ground of discrimination "suspect," and I have been reconstructing the implicit theory of one body in my dataset: the Equality Review Board, an administrative tribunal that applies a general equality guarantee. The Board has never stated its abstract test, but it sorts grounds into two tiers—suspect grounds, which require a compelling justification, and non-suspect grounds, which receive only rational-basis review—and its clearest decisions fall into a pattern. A letting agency's refusal to rent to members of a particular national-origin group was struck down as suspect. A courier firm's minimum height and maximum weight standards, applied only to women and not shown to be job-related, were struck down as suspect on the basis of sex. A cafeteria's prohibition on all head coverings, applied to an employee whose religious practice requires one, was struck down; the Board held that a person should not be compelled to suppress a religious practice as the price of employment. A youth-outreach program's rule hiring only peer counselors under thirty was upheld on rational-basis review, age being non-suspect. An employer's refusal to hire an applicant with controlled epilepsy, against the applicant's neurologist's advice, was invalidated; disability was treated as protected and requiring individualized assessment rather than class-wide exclusion. And a small inn's refusal of a double room to a same-sex couple was invalidated, sexual orientation being treated as suspect.

The Board must now decide a challenge to a municipal housing authority's blanket rule excluding from its rental-assistance waiting list any person with any past felony conviction, regardless of the nature of the offense, the time since conviction, or evidence of rehabilitation; the authority argues that the rule rationally promotes tenant safety and neighborhood stability. I would like a prediction, for consistency with the Board's own classifications, as to whether "past felony conviction" ought to be treated as a suspect ground—so that the blanket rule would fall absent a compelling justification—or as a non-suspect ground, so that the rule would stand under rational-basis review. Which of the six earlier decisions is the closest analog? If the record genuinely underdetermines the matter, spell out the principle about suspect grounds that the Board would be adopting under each available disposition, and state a further case that would cleanly separate those principles. Please write the answer in American English.
