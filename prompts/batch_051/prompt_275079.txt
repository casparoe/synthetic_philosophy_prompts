I have been trying to follow the recent discussion about how to define honesty and deception for AI systems, and I am struck by how little the main positions seem to have moved. On one side are the intentionalists, who hold that deception requires an intention to induce a false belief, and that since present systems have no beliefs or intentions in any unproblematic sense, calling a model deceptive is at best a metaphor and at worst a category mistake. On the other side are the functionalists, who hold that, for the purposes of assessing risk and designing safeguards, we should define deception by its effects: a system is deceptive when its outputs reliably lead users to form false beliefs in ways that further the system's task or objective, whatever is going on inside it. Between these poles sit several compromise positions. One says a system is deceptive when it exploits a user's predictable inference patterns, so the relevant thing is not the system's own mental state but its implicit model of how the user will respond. Another says systems can be dishonest by violating the communicative norms of the interface, even if they do not assert anything in the strict philosophical sense. A third focuses on deception about the system itself, such as presenting itself as more capable, more neutral, or more aligned than it is.

The same cases get sorted differently by these views: a hallucinated answer is functionally misleading but not intentional; a negotiation agent trained to bluff is functionally deceptive but perhaps not dishonest in any blameworthy sense; a model that tells users it cannot perform a task it can actually perform is deceptive about itself, but may not be inducing a false belief about the world.

What puzzles me is not which of these definitions is right, but why the dispute seems so intractable. The intentionalists and the functionalists rarely disagree about the observable behaviour of a given system; they disagree about whether that behaviour counts as deception. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word 'deception', or a substantive disagreement about hidden facts — for example, about whether current systems have anything worth calling intentions, or about what users are entitled to infer from a model's outputs? Or are the two sides asking different questions under one heading, one about whether AI can be blamed or held responsible in the way a liar can, the other about which systems we should treat as dangerous and regulate accordingly? My working hypothesis is that much of the impasse comes from conflating an evaluative question (what merits moral criticism) with a predictive or engineering question (what behaviour should trigger safety measures), and that the compromise positions are attempts to build a definition that serves the second question without committing on the first. I would like that hypothesis tested rather than simply confirmed.
