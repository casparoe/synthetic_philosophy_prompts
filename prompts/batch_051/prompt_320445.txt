I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so forgive the loose vocabularly here. The thing I keep chewing on shows up in my day job: in a strongly typed or constructive setting (I work in a proof assistant and a bit of Haskell), only the things you can actually build count. You can't just invoke the law of excluded middle and declare that a number exists because assuming it doesn't leads to a contradiction; you have to produce the object. But ordinary math is stuffed with objects nobody can actually produce: a well-ordering of the reals that just falls out of the axiom of choice, Banach–Tarski decompositions, uncomputable reals, almost all real numbers in the sense that no finite description singles one out. And the accepted line keeps moving historically. Negative numbers were once treated as bookkeeping tricks until they weren't. Imaginary numbers were dismissed as sophistical until they turned up real roots of cubics. Infinitesimals got kicked out by the rigor people, and now physicists use them constantly and there are formal foundations for them again. Descartes drew the line at algebraic curves and treated the cycloid as merely mechanical, not properly geometric — and now transcendental curves are just part of the furniture. Straightedge-and-compass constructibility isn't the gold standard either; we're fine with objects that Galois theory says can't be built that way.

Before I'd argue about which philosophical theory of math is right, I want the full spec of requirements a theory of what counts as a legitimate mathematical object has to actually meet. I mean the desiderata: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse (useful vs well-defined vs constructible, for instance), the facts from real mathematical practice it can't contradict, and the history it has to accommodate — any theory that retroactively declares Cardano, Euler, or the pioneers of the calculus to have been doing nonsense fails on contact. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the tastes of one camp (constructivists, platonists, formalists, structuralists, whatever). If some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, that tension is what I most want spelled out, because it would explain why this debate never settles. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular positions. I don't need a full scorecard; the criteria are the point. Answer in American English, be explicit about how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
