I'm a PhD student working on cultural evolution, and I've ended up as the resident theory person in a public health communications team. Every few weeks they have to decide which slogans, posters and video formats to put budget behind, and the stakes are genuinely high because the stuff they're trying to shift is hard to message. They've started calling their campaign variants 'memes' and treating them as if they evolve by natural selection: make lots of variants, measure what spreads, kill the losers. I keep telling them that whether that's the right model depends on whether cultural variants actually replicate as discrete, lineage-forming units with copying errors, or whether people just reconstruct the same underlying idea from a stable mental template each time—in which case the 'variants' aren't really competing at all. They've asked me to stop giving them theory and give them something usable. Could you write a decision procedure a non-academic can run in under an hour for deciding whether some recurring cultural item—a slogan, a rhyme, a poster, a video format—is genuinely evolving by selection among variants (high-fidelity copying plus differential spread) rather than being independently re-produced from a shared template? It should spell out the questions to ask in order, what counts as evidence for each, where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a judgement call, and what each outcome implies for how the team manages the item. Then run it on three things currently on their desk: (a) a vaccine symptom-reporting phrase that community workers keep translating and paraphrasing; (b) an antibiotics poster that people keep photographing and redrawing with small alterations; and (c) a school handwashing rhyme that stayed stable for years but is now drifting as it moves between year groups. I suspect one of these will force them to give up the meme framing they're attached to, and I'd rather the procedure be honest about that.
