I have spent the past few months working through the arguments around what some call the singleton hypothesis: the expectation that, in the long run, intelligent life on Earth will come to be coordinated by a single decision-making agency at the highest level — a world government, a global artificial intelligence, or some tightly binding coordination regime. I find the arguments for this surprisingly compelling. They seem to me to have real systematic virtues. They unify a range of otherwise scattered worries about arms races, coordination failures, and existential risk; they explain why decentralised actors who individually prefer safety can nonetheless be driven into destructive competition; and they offer a comparatively tidy account of how a stable, long-lived civilisation could actually persist. The case does not feel like a loose collection of empirical guesses; it feels like a single structural insight about what happens when many powerful agents interact.

And yet I also have a firm intuition that the end state it describes would be a loss rather than a solution. It is not merely that I think a singleton would probably be run badly. My intuition is stronger: the ongoing existence of many independent centres of judgement and decision seems to me to be part of what makes a future worth having, not just a means to better outcomes. The image of one agency settling what will be done, for everyone, at the highest level, strikes me as a defect in the world even if the decisions were good ones. I recognise that this may look like a brute anti-authoritarian sentiment, or a bias in favour of the plurality I happen to be used to.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that is debunked and abandoned — for instance, would it be enough to show that it is an instinct shaped for small-group life, or that a singleton would preserve genuine plurality at every level below the top? What would it take for the hypothesis to be amended or given up — for example, would it fail if stable coordination could be achieved through a plurality of overlapping institutions rather than one apex? And is there a reading of "singleton" on which both the theory and the intuition survive, or do they genuinely contradict one another? How should I think about which side yields, and what would justify each move? Please answer in British English.
