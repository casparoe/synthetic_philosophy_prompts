Since I retired from the natural sciences and took up philosophy, I have been struck by how often a debate that looks empirical turns on what, in my old field, would have been settled by an operational definition. The dispute over psychological egoism seems to me the purest case, and it is a dispute that has visibly stalled. One side holds that every human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest; the other side points to soldiers falling on grenades, anonymous donors, and parents who ruin their health for a child. The egoist replies that these are disguised self-interest — the soldier avoided a life of shame, the donor purchased self-approval, the parent could not bear the guilt. The critic then objects that the thesis has been made unfalsifiable, since any action can be redescribed as serving some interest of the agent. The egoist answers that the theory is not ad hoc: it is a substantive claim about the hidden springs of action, supported by introspection and by the selective pressure to seek one's own survival and reproduction. Each side treats the same behavior as evidence for its own view, and that is where the exchange usually stops.

My hypothesis is that this is really several disagreements held together under one heading, and that the descriptive dispute keeps borrowing heat from a different, normative one — namely ethical egoism, the view that one ought to pursue one's own interest. People often resist psychological egoism because they fear it licenses selfishness, while its defenders sometimes write as though showing altruism to be an illusion would settle what we owe one another. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. Is the core disagreement verbal, a difference about what would count as evidence for a claim about ultimate motives, a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, or a hidden factual question that has never been stated in testable form? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a reframing, a concrete experimental prediction — or is there reason to expect that none is available?
