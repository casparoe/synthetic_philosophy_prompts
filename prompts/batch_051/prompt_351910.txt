I have been turning over what feels like a structural parallel between Dennett's three stances and the main tiers of legal culpability, and I want to know whether it is more than a loose analogy.

As I understand them, Dennett's three stances are increasingly abstract ways of predicting a system's behavior: the physical stance predicts from material structure; the design stance predicts from what the thing is designed or selected to do; the intentional stance predicts by treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. He further holds that all three stances can track real patterns rather than merely useful fictions. The legal tiers seem to ascend in a similar way. Strict liability assigns consequences on the basis of the act and its outcome without requiring any particular mental state, which looks like a refusal to go inside the agent. Negligence applies an objective standard of how a well-functioning agent would have behaved rather than asking what this particular agent actually had in mind, which looks like the design stance's appeal to proper functioning. Intentional wrongdoing asks about the agent's actual beliefs and aims, which looks like the intentional stance.

The proposed mapping, then, is strict liability as law's physical stance, negligence as its design stance, and mens rea as its intentional stance. The analogy is tempting because both progressions move from a stance that refuses to interpret the actor, to one that interprets against a norm of proper functioning, to one that reads off an actual mental life. But there is also an apparent mismatch: the legal categories do not merely predict — they ground blame and punishment — and which category applies is fixed by the offense charged, whereas Dennett's stances are freely adoptable interpretive strategies justified by their predictive yield rather than by any claim to reveal what is really there.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? In particular, does anything from Dennett's instrumentalism about the intentional stance transfer to the law? And is there a point where the parallel predicts something that turns out false — a legal practice the analogy would license but which the law rejects, or a feature of the stances with no legal counterpart? As you answer, please say how confident you are in the overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure of.
