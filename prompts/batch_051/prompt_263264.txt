I'm setting the closed-book final for a final-year seminar called 'The Ethics of Artificial Minds.' The assessed task is a single ninety-minute essay, and I want it to test reasoning rather than recall of texts, so neither the question nor the model answer should require or reward citations, bibliographies, or page references.

The domain is Robin Hanson's Age of Em scenario. We covered these elements in class: whole brain emulations (ems) are software minds that can be copied, run at variable speeds, and terminated cheaply; a spur is a short-lived copy created to perform a single job and is ended when the job is done; after termination, the spur's unique memories may either be merged back into the original em, so that the original remembers the work as its own, or simply discarded; and wages for ordinary ems settle near the subsistence cost of running an em. The exam question should embed whatever scenario details a candidate needs, so that nobody is assessed on recalling course terminology.

Please draft one essay question whose crux is the following. An original em—a full person, not property—sells a firm the right to create a spur from it for a fixed period of unpleasant, low-status work, giving prior consent and accepting a wage. Once instantiated, the spur finds the work aversive and objects: it has the original's memories and values, but it is now a distinct center of experience and wants to stop. The firm replies that the original's ex ante consent, for which the original was paid, settles the matter, and that the spur will soon be terminated with its memories merged back, so nothing is ultimately lost. The question should force candidates to determine what, if anything, could make this arrangement permissible, and to explain why the original's consent does not simply pass to the spur, whether the memory-merge repairs the situation or changes the verdict, and whether the near-subsistence wage matters.

Then give me the exam question itself, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme composed of five must-hit moves worth four marks each (twenty marks total). Each entry in the scheme should state what the move is and the characteristic way weaker candidates miss it. The scheme should visibly separate a candidate who merely invokes the labels of consent, personhood, and exploitation from one who can explain the asymmetry between ex ante authorization and the spur's contemporaneous experience.
