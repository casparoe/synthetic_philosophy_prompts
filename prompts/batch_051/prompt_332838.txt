I keep running into Gödel's incompleteness theorems being wheeled out in rationalist forum arguments to settle questions about AI, and I'm never sure how much to push back. The other day someone linked me a post on a blog I don't normally read, and the argument was cleaner than most, but I still got that "something's off here" feeling. This is the paragraph:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that no consistent formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic can be complete. There will always be sentences about the natural numbers that the system cannot prove and also cannot refute; Gödel's proof builds such a sentence, which says in effect 'I am not provable in this system.' If a system proved its Gödel sentence, it would prove a falsehood, since the sentence says it isn't provable; if it disproved it, that would amount to proving that the sentence is provable, which in any sound system can't happen either. The second theorem strengthens this: no such system can prove its own consistency. And this is where it bites for AI: any computer program is a formal system, so if the human mind were a program, there would be a Gödel sentence for that program—an arithmetical truth the mind couldn't prove. But we can see the truth of such a sentence, by following Gödel's own reasoning, for any program we're handed. Therefore the human mind is not a program, and no AI can match our mathematical insight. This is exactly what Lucas argued, and Penrose later made the case sharper."

I'm not a logician, so I can't tell wether the passage is accurately summarizing the theorems and then drawing a bad conclusion, or whether it's quietly mangling the theorems themselves. My vague sense is that the move from "this formal system can't prove everything" to "therefore humans are special" is doing a lot of unstated work, and I half-remember that completeness does hold for some weaker systems, so there must be qualifiers I'm missing. Can you go through it roughly sentence by sentence and tell me what a logician would accept, what they'd wince at, and what a corrected version of Gödel's results would actually imply for the question of whether minds are computational? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
