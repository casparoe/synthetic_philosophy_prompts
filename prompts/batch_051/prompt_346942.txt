I sit on the living donor review committee at a teaching hospital. Our current case is a directed kidney donation. The proposed donor is a 24-year-old woman with mild intellectual disability; she lives semi-independently with some family support and works part-time at the restaurant her father owns. The proposed recipient, her older sister, has end-stage renal disease but is stable on dialysis; transplantation would likely improve her quality of life substantially, though there is no immediate threat to life. During the consent discussion, the recipient's nephrologist told the family, in the donor's presence, that the sister will die without this kidney. He later clarified that he meant only that the disease is fatal if left untreated, not that dialysis is failing; the donor, however, appears to have taken the remark as an immediate warning that her sister cannot survive on dialysis. Our social worker later met with the donor alone: the donor said she wants to help but is afraid of the surgery, and that her father has described refusing as a betrayal and reminded her that he gave her a job and a home. A psychologist who assessed her found that she can repeat the basic facts about donation but has difficulty weighing longer-term risks and tends to offer the answer she believes the family wants. The committee is divided. Some members hold that the inaccurate impression, the family pressure, and the cognitive limitations together mean there is no valid consent. Others reply that refusing to accept this adult's wish to make a generous, high-benefit decision would be paternalistic, and that her stated preference should be honored with extra safeguards rather than overridden.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee. It should recommend a single actionable course of action — approve, approve with specified conditions, defer pending further evaluation, or deny — and give a clear bottom line plus the reasoning for it. Do not rest the decision solely on what the nephrologist said; the memo should say explicitly how the decision treats each of the three consent problems at stake: incomplete or misleading information, pressure or coercion, and decisional competence. It should also present the strongest argument for the recommendation the committee rejects and explain why it does not prevail, and identify what change in the facts would reverse the recommendation.
