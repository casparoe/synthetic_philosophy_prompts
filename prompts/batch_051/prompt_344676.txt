I'm taking a class that's cross-listed between philosophy and cognitive science, and the last unit paired the body chapters from Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception with some recent embodied cognition work. The Merleau-Ponty argument we focused on is that the body has a kind of pre-reflective motor intentionality: the blind man's cane isn't something he feels but something he feels through, the phantom limb isn't just a memory or a faulty belief, and a habitual action like typing doesn't run through an explicit representation of the keyboard. The prof's version is that this bodily know-how is irreducible to both third-person physiology and intellectual representation, and that the lived body is the absolute here that all spatial orientation and experience is anchored in.

Then in the cog-sci half we actually did the rubber hand illusion in section — two paintbrushes, a fake hand, your real hand hidden — and watched the VR full-body ownership experiments where a first-person view of an avatar can make the avatar feel like your body and shift where you feel located. The obvious upshot seemed to be: if the sense of owning a body can be fooled into jumping to a piece of rubber or a virtual avatar, then the lived body is more like a fragile construction, maybe even a representation that can detach from the actual body. My prof says no, this actually vindicates Merleau-Ponty, because the body schema was always plastic — it already takes up canes and hats and tools — and the lab illusions are just that plasticity shown experimentally.

I cant tell if he's saving the argument by quietly watering it down. Which exact premise is under pressure here: the non-representational character of motor intentionality, the body-as-absolute-anchor part, or something else?
