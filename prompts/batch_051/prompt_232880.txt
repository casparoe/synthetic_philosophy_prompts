I am an economics undergraduate taking a philosophy seminar on play and leisure, and I keep finding that the ideas of the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga and the German philosopher Josef Pieper slide back into the categories I already think in. As I understand them, Huizinga holds that play is not a pause between serious activities but something primary: voluntary, bounded, rule-governed, disinterested yet absorbing, and culturally generative, since forms such as law, contest, poetry, and philosophy arise in and as play. Pieper holds that leisure is not time off from work or recovery for renewed work but a receptive, contemplative, celebratory state of the soul, one that a culture organized around total work systematically fails to understand. I am much more fluent in the tools of economics than in these texts, and I want to restate both positions in the vocabulary of my own discipline: utility, consumption and production, the labor–leisure choice, opportunity cost, human capital investment, and revealed preference. Please do this translation for each author as precisely as you can, marking what translates cleanly (for instance, an activity wanted for its own sake might become an argument that enters the utility function directly rather than one that produces later utility), what can be forced into the economic vocabulary only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in it at all. Then give me your assessment of what does not survive the translation: does the failure of something to translate show that the economic framework is genuinely missing a distinction these authors are trying to draw, or that their talk of play and leisure as ends in themselves is less clear than it appears? I care less about whether an economist could introduce a variable to catch some of this than about whether the translation preserves what Huizinga and Pieper actually mean.
