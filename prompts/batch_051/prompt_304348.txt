I've been trying to work out what principle explains my judgments about blackmail, and they don't fit together as neatly as I expected. I've written down my reactions to a series of cases; the first six concern a classmate, Marcus, whose cheating I could either report or keep quiet about.

1. I learn that Marcus cheated on the math final, and I report him to the teacher. This seems permissible, maybe even the right thing to do.

2. I learn the same thing but stay quiet; later I ask Marcus for $30 because I'm short on cash, without mentioning what I know. I think this is fine, though slightly awkward.

3. I learn the same thing and tell Marcus that if he doesn't pay me $30, I'll report him. This seems clearly wrong.

4. I tell Marcus I'll report him unless he confesses to the teacher by Friday. This seems fine, actually better than reporting him right away.

5. I tell Marcus I'll report him unless he returns the $30 he took from a friend's locker. This also seems fine, perhaps better than reporting him immediately.

6. I tell Marcus I won't report him, but that he could do me a favor sometime. This makes me uneasy, but it doesn't seem as clearly wrong as 3.

7. A different classmate who saw me leaving a therapy appointment says he'll tell the whole grade unless I do his physics homework for a week. That strikes me as wrong in the same way as 3.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? If a clean principle forces me to drop one, which one should go? And what would the best principle say about the case I still can't settle: I tell Marcus I'll keep quiet about the cheating if he lets me have his parking spot for the rest of the semester — different from 3 only in that he'd give me a spot, not cash?
