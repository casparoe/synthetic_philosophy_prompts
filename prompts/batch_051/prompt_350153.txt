It is a familiar view, standardly attributed to Hume, that causation consists in nothing more than constant conjunction: an event of type A is regularly followed by an event of type B, the two are contiguous, the cause precedes the effect, and there is no further necessary connection binding them. The attraction of the view is that it keeps causation out of the occult: we never observe a tie or glue between distinct events, only their regular succession.

Since that view was formulated, a specific development has intervened. Formal causal modeling — structural equations, the notion of an intervention, and the associated do-calculus — together with interventionist accounts of causation, now lets us state precisely several things that constant conjunction alone cannot. It distinguishes a storm from the falling barometer that regularly precedes it by asking what changes when a variable is intervened on rather than merely observed. It treats causal direction as an asymmetry of manipulation rather than an asymmetry of time. And it represents counterfactual dependence that need not show up as regular association in any actual dataset, because interventions can reveal what a variable does when it is set independently of its normal causes.

My question is whether the regularity theory survives this development. I am not asking for Hume exegesis, and I am not asking whether the development refutes what Hume actually believed. I am asking about the metaphysical position itself. Which premise does the development put under pressure: the claim that regularity is sufficient for causation, the claim that regularity is necessary for causation, or the implicit assumption that there is no further structure to represent? Is there a repaired version of the regularity theory that keeps its anti-necessitarian spirit while absorbing the interventionist distinction between genuine causes and spurious regularities, or does any such repair tacitly reintroduce the very modal connection the theory was meant to avoid? If the theory cannot be repaired, should we say it was refuted, or that it was answering an epistemological question — how we learn about causes from experience — rather than a metaphysical one all along?
