Retired chemist here, and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group. Last week two people in the group got into an arguement about heritability, and neither would budge. I'd like a careful point-by-point adjudication of it. We'd been reading that adult height has a heritability around 0.8 in affluent populations. The argument went roughly like this, as best as I can reconstruct it.

Dan: 'So 0.8 means 80% of height differences are down to genes, so environment can only account for about 20% of the causal story, even at the level of individuals.'

Priya: 'No. Heritability is a population parameter. It says what fraction of the variance in the sample is associated with genetic variance, given the range of environments those people actually experienced. It doesn't partition an individual into 80% genetic and 20% environmental, and it doesn't tell you what would happen under a new environment. Height shot up over the 20th century while its heritability stayed high, so a high heritability clearly doesn't mean environment can't move the trait.'

Dan: 'But in a given population at a given time, if genetic differences account for 80% of the spread, then environmental differences among those people account for less. That has causal content; it isn't just a statistical artifact.'

Priya: 'It only has causal content under strong assumptions. Genes and environments are correlated—taller parents provide different food, encourage different sports, and so on, partly because of their own genotypes—so you can't apportion the variance into independent genetic and environmental bins. And even a high estimate says nothing about a novel environmental intervention, because the number is indexed to the existing range of environments.'

Dan: 'Now you're overstating it. Heritability isn't meaningless. If I pick two kids at opposite ends of the height distribution in that same population, with the environmental range what it is, my best bet is that a large share of the gap traces to genetic differences. That's real predictive work. What's the point of estimating the number if it carries no information?'

Priya: 'It carries information about the population under current conditions, fine. But you opened by saying environment can only account for 20% of the causal story even at the level of individuals. That is exactly the step the statistic doesn't license. Heritability can be 0.8 while a particular short person's shortfall is entirely environmental—illness, poor nutrition, whatever. And it can be 0.8 while a new factor shifts the whole distribution.'

Dan: 'Maybe the individual-level phrasing was loose. But my deeper point is that people hide behind it doesn't apply to individuals to avoid admitting that, in the actual population, genes are doing most of the work of differentiating people. If a polygenic score can predict height from DNA, then genes are telling you about individual differences too.'

Priya: 'Prediction isn't apportionment. The score can predict individual height while an environmental change still matters far more than the heritability would suggest, especially outside the sampled conditions. You keep sliding between prediction, variation, and causation.'

I'd like to know, for each round, who is right about which specific point and what both of them are missing. And since I'm trying to learn rather than just get a ruling: tell me how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the specific point you're least sure of.
