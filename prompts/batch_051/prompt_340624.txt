I'm the programming director at a city parks and rec department, and I get maybe a dozen proposals a month for what to do with our community centers' open hours. A couple years ago the city council carved out a line item for what the charter loosely calls "play and leisure as ends in themselves," and now every proposal tries to fit under it. In practice that means a gamified steps challenge, a craft night whose output gets sold to fund a food pantry, a "financial literacy through board games" night for kids, and coached youth soccer that everyone calls play but is obviously skill development with a ball. I want a decision procedure my front-desk staff can actually run in about twenty minutes per proposal — they don't know the theory and I don't have the time to teach it — that sorts genuinely autotelic play and leisure, in the Huizinga/Pieper sense (the magic circle, voluntary, bounded, done for its own sake, not recuperation so you can work more), from disguised work, enrichment, fundraising, and therapy. Give me the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and which step a sympathetic staffer who likes a proposal is most likely to fudge. Then run the procedure on three proposals: an unstructured drop-in open gym; a board-game night pitched as building kids' executive function; and a monthly potluck-and-dance run by a neighborhood association who swears it's just about joy. Be honest about wich one the procedure can't settle and whether the procedure ends up saying no to something it probably shouldn't.
