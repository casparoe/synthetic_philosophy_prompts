My ethics class has been studying the difference between guilt and shame, and my teacher, Ms. Alvarez, has a practice of announcing, for each case we read, which of the two emotions is the fitting one for the person to feel—not necessarily the one the person would in fact feel. I have kept a list of her verdicts and have been trying to infer the rule behind them. The problem is that at least two rules fit the record equally well, and the case she has assigned for next week will force me to choose.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A student cheats on a chemistry final and, without being caught, receives a scholarship that would otherwise have gone to another student. Fitting emotion: guilt.
2. A varsity tennis player stops practicing, and much of the school notices; he then loses badly in the first round of the regional tournament. Fitting emotion: shame.
3. A student borrows a friend's camera, breaks it, and never tells the friend, who concludes it broke in her bag. Fitting emotion: guilt.
4. A member of the school orchestra stops practicing and performs badly at the spring concert, before a silent audience. Fitting emotion: shame.
5. A student lies to her parents about where she was at night; they never find out, although she knows she abused their trust. Fitting emotion: guilt.
6. A newly elected class president gives an incoherent first address to the entire school because he did not prepare. Fitting emotion: shame.

Next week's case is this: a student posts a cruel and mocking rumor about a classmate in the school group chat; the post spreads through the whole school before it is traced back to him, and the target is visibly hurt and humiliated.

One rule I can extract says that the fitting emotion tracks harm: guilt when a specific person has been wronged, shame when the person has failed to meet a standard and no one else is directly harmed. Another rule says it tracks exposure: guilt for private wrongdoing, shame for failure that has become public or damaging to one's standing. These two rules agree on all six of her past cases, since every case so far combines either a private wrong with a victim or a public failure without one. The new case is the missing combination: a public wrong with a victim. I am less interested in the one-word prediction than in the rule behind it.

Predict her verdict on the new case; explain which rule—or which other rule that fits the six cases equally well—best accounts for her judgments and why; identify the closest analog among the earlier cases; and describe a further case that would cleanly separate the candidate rules, including what each would predict about it.
