I'm trying to work out what I actually think about proof, by going with my gut on a handful of cases, and I'd like you to tell me whether there's one principle behind it. Here are my verdicts.

The blue bus. A woman is hit by a bus on a road where the only buses are run by two companies: Blue Bus operates 80% of them that hour, Red Bus 20%. No one saw the colour. She sues Blue Bus on the balance of probabilities. My gut says she loses, even though it's more likely than not, on the evidence, that the bus was Blue's.

The eyewitness. A woman is hit by a bus on a road served by the same two firms, and the only evidence linking the crash to Blue Bus is a passer-by who says the bus was blue. The passer-by is tested and gets such sightings right 80% of the time, and there's no other evidence either way. My gut says she wins. On the evidence, the probability it was Blue Bus is .8 — the same as in the first case. So whatever is doing the work for me, it isn't the number alone. Though then I notice the witness's 80% is itself a rate over a class of sightings, so my easy distinction is already wobbling.

The gatecrasher. A thousand people attend a rodeo; 499 paid and 501 didn't. The organiser points at a random attendee and sues him for the price of entry. My gut says the organiser loses, even though the probability the man didn't pay is .501.

The prison yard. A guard is killed in a yard by an attack. It's proved that 99 of the 100 prisoners present took part, but the CCTV can't distinguish faces. One prisoner is prosecuted, and the only evidence against him is that he was in the yard. My gut says acquit, beyond reasonable doubt, even though it's .99 that he took part.

The cold hit. A DNA profile from a burglary matches a man identified only because his profile was sitting in a national database of several million people; the match probability is given as one in ten million. No other connection to the scene. I'd convict, though I can't say why this doesn't bother me the way the buses do.

The fingerprint. Same kind of scene; an examiner says the latent print matches the defendant and the chance of a false identification is negligible. I'd also convict.

I keep reaching for the thought that proof has to be 'individual' — about this defendant and this event — rather than a bare base rate about a class they fall into. But I can't make that precise, and the cold hit looks on its face exactly like the base-rate reasoning I reject in the blue bus case. Is there one principle, stated precisely enough to test, that gets all my verdicts? If not, which should I drop and why? And apply whatever you settle on to the case I haven't made my mind up about: the blue bus facts, but Blue Bus operated 99% of the buses that hour. Should she win?

Please also be clear about confidence: seperate what's actually settled — in the law, or among people who work on this — from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested answer as if it were the consensus.
