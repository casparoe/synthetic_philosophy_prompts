Here's a thing I can't get straight. If a delivery driver runs a red light and kills a pedestrian, we know what to do: the driver is prosecuted, blamed, punished. But if an autonomous truck runs the same red light because its vision system misjudged the light's timing, the same death suddenly has no obvious human author. We talk about the manufacturer, the software vendor, the fleet operator, the regulator who approved it — and yet most people are deeply unsatisfied with the conclusion that nobody in particular is blameworthy. People who would never call the human driver's crash "just one of those things" will also reject "no one is responsible" in the machine case. So the same event produces focused individual blame when a human was driving, and a scramble to find someone, anyone, to blame when the machine was driving — even though the whole point of removing the driver was to remove the one place we knew how to locate blame. This is the so-called responsibilty gap, and I can't tell whether it's a real moral finding or just our inability to tolerate unowned harms.

Is the asymmetry justified? Give me the strongest principle you can find that either generates both judgments or shows that one of them should be revised. Then test it on these: (1) a human driver who kills a pedestrian because of an unforeseeable seizure — we mostly don't punish; (2) a programmer who wrote one specific line of code with a known but low-probability failure mode that later kills someone; (3) a company board that cut the safety-testing budget to ship on schedule. If you'd keep the asymmetry, say exactly where the line falls in those cases and why; if not, say which judgment should be revised.
