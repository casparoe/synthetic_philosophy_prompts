You're a philosopher of science who works on reduction, emergence, and mechanistic explanation, and I want you to think out loud before giving a verdict — maybe start by steelmanning my policy.

I've developed a habit in my own reading, mostly biology and neuroscience. Whenever a paper or a talk says that some property emerges from lower-level parts — a behavior from neural activity, a cell type from a gene network, whatever — I treat the word emergent as a promissory note, not an explanation. I don't update on the claim until someone can give me at least three things: the lower-level parts and their interaction rules, the higher-level pattern, and an account of why the pattern isn't obvious from the rules or what it adds beyond them. Absent that, emergent goes in the same bucket as vital force or it just is. I can't chase every mechanism to the ground, so the rule does a lot of filtering for me.

But I've noticed I don't hold reduces-to claims to the same standard. If a paper says a capacity reduces to a set of ion channels, I usually nod along without asking for the actual derivation. I'm probly being asymmetrical, and I can't tell whether the asymmetry is good sense or reductionist bias dressed up as rigor.

So: is this policy defensible? When does treating emergent as a placeholder protect me from empty explanation, and when does it blind me to real higher-level structure? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the skepticism without the asymmetry — something like taxing emerges-from and reduces-to at the same rate — and what would it look like? And how would I tell from the inside whether I'm using it as a filter or a shield?
