I'm taking a philosophy of law course this semester and we spent a chunk of last week on sovereign immunity, and I can't shake the feeling that the doctrine is just an inconsistency everyone has agreed to call a principle. The way it was put to us is that the government can't be sued without its consent, even for things that would plainly be torts if a private person did them. The example that got me: a state agency builds and maintains a levee that fails and floods a family's basement, and the state escapes liability on the ground that flood control was some kind of governmental function or discretionary policy call; but if a neighbor builds a retaining wall that fails and causes the same flooding, the neighbor is obviously on the hook. So the same harm, same causal story, flips from compensable to not depending entirely on whether the defendant happens to be the state.

I get the standard reasons—protecting the public treasury, avoiding a flood of litigation, not letting tort suits scramble policy choices, seperation of powers, all of that. And my roommate keeps saying the doctrine is mostly a technical backstop nowadays because governments have consented to being sued in lots of areas. But those all sound like reasons to cap damages, or set up a special claims process, or give deference to policy calls. They don't sound like reasons to say the state didn't wrong you, or that its wrong isn't the kind of wrong you can complain about in court. Meanwhile in ordinary life we treat the state as exactly the kind of thing that can wrong people: we say the government wronged those homeowners, and we don't think we're being metaphorical.

So here's the asymmetry I want tested. A private defendant and the state do the same damage, but only one is subject to ordinary liability, ordinary judgment, ordinary remedy. Is there an actual principle that justifies that asymmetry, or is it a historical leftover that survives because its useful to governments? I want the strongest defense you can build, tested against nearby cases—a state employee who personally floods the basement, a suit seeking an injunction rather than money, gross negligence versus ordinary neglect. And an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept as-is, narrowed, or dropped. Please write in American English, and as plain continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just the way you'd write an email.
