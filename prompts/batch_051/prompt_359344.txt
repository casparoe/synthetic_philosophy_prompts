I've been running a series of thought experiments past a friend to triangulate her tacit principle about how far everyday local conventions bind outsiders, since she refuses to state the principle outright. I'd like a prediction about how she'll answer the next case.

Her track record so far:

1. A traveler from a no-tipping country sits down at a standard full-service restaurant in the United States, where waitstaff are paid a tipped minimum wage well below the ordinary minimum. She says a 20 percent tip is a genuine obligation, not a charitable extra, and it binds the visitor exactly as much as a local.

2. An American diner at a mid-range restaurant in Tokyo, where there is no tipping slot in the payment system and the server would be mildly nonplussed by a tip. She says adding a tip is a small unkindness, and not tipping is not merely permissible but correct.

3. At a busy London bus stop, locals have formed a single orderly queue. A traveler could board through the front and make the bus to the airport, or join the back and miss it. She says joining the back and missing the bus is required, and board-front queue-jumping would be wrong even under real time pressure.

4. In a large city where pedestrians routinely cross against the signal whenever the street is empty and drivers expect this, a visitor asks whether she may cross with the locals. She says yes; waiting for the signal is permissible but pointless. She denies that the visitor must follow the local law simply because there happens to be one.

5. In a New York restaurant that adds a 20 percent service charge and states that no further gratuity is expected, she says no further tip is required, because there is no hidden tipped worker depending on it.

Now the case I care about. A traveler books a small private guided tour in a country with no general tipping custom. Local clients do not tip and pay the posted price. But over the past thirty years, the small set of English-speaking guides in that city has come to expect 10 to 15 percent from Western tourists; tour operators now describe tipping as customary for this service, and the guides quietly set prices and plan around it, though they never request a tip and would deliver the same tour either way. Does my friend say the traveler should tip?

I can see at least two generalizations that fit everything she has said so far. One says a convention obligates only when it creates expectations or reliance in the specific people one interacts with, so local custom by itself has no force. The other says a visitor should adopt the convention that actually governs the practice in that place, because preserving a local equilibrium matters even when no particular person is relying on you. The two come apart on this new case. Predict which rule she is running, or reconstruct a better one if neither is right; say what each leading rule implies about the guided-tour case; give your best guess about her verdict and why; and supply one further case that would cleanly separate the leading rules if this one does not.
