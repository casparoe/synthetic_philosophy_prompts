In my philosophy of biology reading group we have spent the last two weeks on what counts as a biological individual, and one member keeps reaching for the old superorganism metaphor: an ant colony (or a termite mound) is literally a single organism, just one whose body happens to be made of ants rather than cells. Her analogy runs something like this. Workers are somatic cells, the queen is the germline, the nest architecture is anatomy, pheromone trails and trophallaxis (the mouth-to-mouth food exchange) are a sort of circulation and nervous system, and the colony's defense of its boundary is like a skin or immune response. She then extends the picture outward: a Portuguese man o' war is already treated as one animal even though it is a colony of zooids, and a coral plus its zooxanthellae or a person plus their gut microbiome is a holobiont, so being an organism is just a matter of how tightly integrated the parts are rather than some special threshold. From this she concludes that organismality is graded and partly a convention, and that we should stop hunting for the real unit of selection because the colony or holobiont can be one whenever integration is tight enough.

A few of us find this persuasive, but I have a nagging feeling the body analogy is doing a lot of quiet work, especially around reproduction, conflict, and where the boundary of the body is supposed to sit. I would like you to audit the analogy the way one might audit the brain-as-computer analogy: lay out the correspondence table in words (what maps onto what), then the places where the mapping breaks, and importantly seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for her conclusion from the ones that are just superficial. What does the body analogy smuggle in about common fate, cooperation, and life cycles that a colony or a holobiont does not automatically have? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion that organismality is graded and partly conventional survive anyway, or was it being carried by the analogy the whole time? If the body comparison is doing more harm than good, say what a better comparison would be. And please write this out as continuous prose, like an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
