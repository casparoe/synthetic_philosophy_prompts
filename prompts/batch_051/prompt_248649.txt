I spent my career as a research scientist in atmospheric physics, most of it running regional flood-forecast and climate-model chains at a government laboratory, and since retiring I have been taking up formal epistemology. I am auditing a graduate seminar whose current unit is imprecise probability, and I am trying to reverse-engineer the seminar leader's position from her rulings on cases, because they depart systematically from the single-number forecasting habits of my old discipline.

She records a verdict for each case as either a precise credence or a lower/upper interval. Here are seven of her rulings:

1. A fair coin, before one toss: precise 0.5 for heads.
2. A die known to be fair, before one roll: precise 1/6 for a six.
3. The Ellsberg urn: 60 balls are red, and the remaining 40 are black or yellow in an unknown proportion; one ball is drawn. For the proposition that the ball is red she gives precise 0.6; for the proposition that it is black she gives imprecise, lower 0, upper 0.4.
4. A coin selected uniformly from a bag of one hundred coins of unknown individual biases, before any flips: imprecise, lower 0, upper 1, for heads on the first toss.
5. The same coin after one hundred flips, of which sixty were heads: imprecise, lower 0.50, upper 0.70, for heads on the next toss.
6. A river-gauge record based on 120 years of data and a well-calibrated hydrological model, for the proposition that the river will exceed its levee next year: precise 0.30.
7. Two equally validated, equally well-calibrated climate models disagree about an ice-free Arctic September in 2040, one giving 0.15 and the other 0.85: imprecise, lower 0.15, upper 0.85.

The case now before the seminar is this. A single, carefully specified mechanistic model of a new alloy predicts that the alloy will fracture within its first year of service with probability 0.65. The model is built from first principles; there is no historical frequency for this alloy, no accepted reference class of similar alloys, and no rival model on offer. What will she say about this case?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the principle behind it. Please do the following: (a) state the rule or rules about when imprecision is warranted that are consistent with all seven earlier verdicts, and I suspect there are at least two genuinely different ones that fit; (b) say what each such rule predicts for the alloy case; (c) give your best guess about her verdict, with the reasons that would lead a careful person to prefer one rule over the other on this record; and (d) name one further case, not among the seven and not the alloy case, whose verdict would most cleanly tell the competing rules apart.

Please keep the answer formal, and end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states your predicted verdict for the alloy case and the rule you favor, in a short machine-readable phrase, for example "Verdict: precise 0.65; rule: single-model determinacy" or "Verdict: imprecise, lower 0, upper 1; rule: no reference class".
