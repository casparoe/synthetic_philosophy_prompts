I’ve been defending a position in a reading group on Isaiah Berlin’s Two Concepts of Liberty, and I’d like you to attack it as seriously as possible. The position I hold is that the only analytically clean concept of political liberty is negative liberty in something like Berlin’s sense: a person is free to the extent that other people or institutions do not deliberately interfere with what she would otherwise be able to do. The crucial feature of my view is the word other. I insist that internal obstacles—compulsion, addiction, phobia, a preference formed under threat or indoctrination, a failure of nerve—are not restrictions on liberty but restrictions on capacity or self-mastery, and that collapsing the two is exactly the slide Berlin warns against. He makes the point, roughly, that when a desire is one I cannot resist, the question of whether it is mine or an external force cannot be settled without positing a split within the self; and once one speaks of a real or higher self that must govern the empirical self, one has left the plain sense of liberty behind and opened the door to coercion in freedom’s name. That argument strikes me as right, and I want to know what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

What would count against the position that negative liberty, with internal constraints excluded by definition, is the only defensible concept of political liberty? I don’t just want the standard objections—the contented slave, the prisoner who has adjusted his desires—but the cases and arguments that would actually force me to weaken the position or abandon the internal/external line. In particular: are there situations in which a person’s own psychology does the same work as a jailer, and why exactly should that not count? Can the line between external interference and internal incapacity be drawn in a principled way, or does it quietly depend on background judgments about causation, responsibility, or normal psychological functioning that the theory has not earned? Which of these challenges would a committed defender of the position concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss as category mistakes? I’d like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus an honest verdict on whether my willingness to reclassify inconvenient cases as “not really liberty” has insulated the view into unfalsifiability—and whether that would even be a problem for a conceptual claim of this kind.
