I am an undergraduate taking a seminar called Dreams, Illusions, and the Self, and we have spent the past few weeks on the passage in the Zhuangzi in which Zhuang Zhou wakes after dreaming he is a butterfly and finds he cannot decide whether he is a human being who dreamed he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is human. Our instructor has framed the passage as posing a boundary problem rather than a story about reincarnation: the same experience is compatible with two very different accounts of what one is, and the waking state supplies no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between them.

For my final paper I want to place this puzzle in a concrete near-future setting, and I would like help working through the social consequences instead of only laying out interpretive positions. Suppose that next year a safe, inexpensive compound becomes legal and widely available to adults in liberal democracies. Taken before sleep, it reliably induces a dream in which the user is a butterfly: the dream is not lucid, the user has no memory of ever having been human, and the sensory world of the dream is exactly as vivid as waking life. From the inside the dream lasts about a single day, and upon waking the user remembers it as vividly as the previous human day, so that for some hours she cannot tell by feel alone which of the two lives is the dream. Each dream is a fresh episode with no continuity from one butterfly night to the next, though the human remembers all of them over months and years.

Given those conditions, I am asking for an argued prediction of how regular use of such a compound would play out over a generation in individual psychology, intimate relationships, work, medicine, law, and religious or philosophical communities. I am less interested in whether you are optimistic or pessimistic than in the causal chain: which effects follow robustly from the features of the compound, which predictions are guesses, what would have to be true for each major prediction to fail, and where the strongest incentives or constraints would push back. Finally, I want to connect the scenario back to the text: would widespread access to such an experience sharpen Zhuang Zhou's puzzle into an ordinary practical problem that people have to live with, or would it change the subject and leave the skeptical worry largely untouched?
