I run a small reading group for people who came up through LessWrong and the EA Forum, and we are planning a session on Yudkowsky's torture-versus-dust-specks problem and, more generally, on whether and how harms to different people can be added together. The published discussions tend to force a crude binary between biting the dust-specks bullet and accepting a lexical pain threshold, but in the group the real disagreements are finer-grained and mostly show up in how people answer concrete tradeoffs rather than in the labels they choose for themselves. I would like a diagnostic instrument that sorts people by what they actually say about cases.

Please write a "which view of harm aggregation do you actually hold?" quiz. It should have eight to ten forced-choice questions, each one presenting a concrete tradeoff that varies at least one of the following: the number of people affected, the intensity and duration of the harms, whether the harms are spread across many people or concentrated in a single person, whether the choice is a one-off forced act or an iterated policy, and whether the harms fall within one person's life or across distinct persons. The scoring key should map response patterns onto at least these positions: strict lexicality (no number of small pains can outweigh one severe pain), bounded aggregation (small harms do sum, but their marginal contribution tapers off so that no amount of them overtakes a finite severe harm), plain additive aggregation, a separateness-of-persons view that permits aggregation within a single life but not across different lives, and a deontological side-constraint view that refuses to treat the torture option as eligible at all. No question may simply ask whether the respondent believes small harms aggregate; every question should be answerable by someone who has never encountered any of these labels. After the scoring key, add a short taxonomy note that diagnoses which combinations of responses are empty or incoherent, and for each empty combination say whether nobody holds it because it is unstable under iteration, unattractive on reflection, or merely underexplored. Finally, flag any two positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate with these cases, rather than papering over the boundary between them.
