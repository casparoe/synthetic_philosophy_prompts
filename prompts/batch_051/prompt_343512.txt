I'm running a small reading group on Warren Quinn's 1990 paper "The Puzzle of the Self-Torturer," and we've gotten stuck on one specific spot. The set-up is a person with a medical device that has 1,000 settings, each step up adding a barely noticeable amount of pain and a small amount of money. The person prefers each adjacent step n+1 to step n, but also strongly prefers setting 0 to setting 1,000. Quinn goes out of his way to say the stepwise preferences are stable and well-informed, not changing like an addict's. Then he writes, "He wants to know how he should act on the ones he actually has." That one line has become the sticking point for us. I'm attracted to the standard view that rational preferences must be transitive, so the self-torturer's pattern is irrational and he needs to revise it. But I also have a firm intuition that there's nothing obviously irrational about preferring each small step while preferring the start to the end—and Quinn's line seems to insist the interesting question isn't whether he's rational, but how he should act given the preferences he actually has. I don't want to abandon transitivity just because a thought experiment makes me uneasy, but I also don't want to dodge the practical problem by saying "just go get new preferences." Which should yield: the theory, the intuition, or my reading of that line?
