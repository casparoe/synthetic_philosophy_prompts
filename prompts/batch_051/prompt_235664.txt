I'm in a philosophy of law seminar on evidence and proof this term, and after the last few sessions I've realized I don't have a working definition of 'naked statistical evidence' beyond 'the stuff juries aren't allowed to convict on,' which is useless for new cases. The class keeps saying naked statistical evidence can't meet proof beyond a reasonable doubt no matter how high the probability gets, but nobody can say what property is actually missing. My study group went through a bunch of examples and we all agree on the easy ones, but no one can state the line. Could you give me a definition that sorts my list below, or at least tell me which cases it can't sort and what a revision would have to pay?

(1) Blue bus: 80% of buses on the route are owned by the Blue Bus Company, and the only evidence against them is that percentage. Everyone calls this naked.
(2) Gatecrasher: 1,000 people attended a rodeo and only 499 bought tickets, so at least 501 gatecrashed; the state randomly selects one attendee and prosecutes. Also naked, even though it's more likely than not that she didn't pay.
(3) Prison yard: 100 prisoners are in a yard when a guard is beaten; the state can prove 99 participated but not which 99, and charges one. Feels naked even though the odds are 99%.
(4) Eyewitness: a witness says she saw the defendant do it and holds up under cross-examination. Not naked, everybody agrees.
(5) Fingerprint: a full latent print from the weapon matches the defendant's left thumb, per a trained examiner. Not naked.
(6) DNA cold hit: the defendant's profile in a database matches crime-scene DNA with a random-match probability around 1 in 10 million. This is where we split and I genuinely don't know.
(7) Facial recognition: a store camera gives a 94% similarity score against the defendant's driver's-license photo. Also splits us.
(8) Tire tread: a distinctive tread at the scene matches something like 1 in 25 vehicles in the city, and the defendant owns one. Some call it naked, some say car ownership makes it individualized.

If your answer can lead with the definition in the first sentence and then run it through the cases, that'd be ideal; no preamble or restating the question.
