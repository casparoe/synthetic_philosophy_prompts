William James, in *The Principles of Psychology* (chapter 4, "Habit"), writes: "Plasticity, then, in the wide sense of the word, means the possession of a structure weak enough to yield to an influence, but strong enough not to yield all at once. Each relatively stable phase of equilibrium in such a structure is marked by what we may call a new set of habits."

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and this passage has been stuck in my head ever since I read it. I work on a mobile app that uses an infinite scroll of personalized recommendations, autoplay, and a streak counter. James obviously wasn't imagining anything like a smartphone feed when he wrote about habit as a physical property of nervous tissue. But if plasticity means the structure yields to an influence without yielding all at once, then a feed that removes every bit of friction seems like an extremely efficient way to make the structure yield as much as possible—to install a new set of habits, as he'd put it. The thing is, the same description seems to apply to practicing an instrument or going to the gym, and we don't normally call those manipulative. I care less about the practical policy answer than about what James's principle actually commits us to: does it give any principled way to distinguish between building a habit and bypassing the person's control? Or is that distinction something his account just can't draw?
