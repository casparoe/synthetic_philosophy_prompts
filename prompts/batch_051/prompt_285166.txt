Okay so I've been chewing on the Halden-vs-mass-incarceration thing—the idea that a prison can be a place that gets you ready to leave rather than a warehouse—and I want you to stress-test a specific mechanism. I don't want the usual abstract "rehabilitation good, punishment bad" take; I want you to actually walk through how it behaves.

Here's the proposal. A US state moves to something called the Reentry Ledger. Every person sentenced to state prison gets an individual account. Each month they are inside, the state deposits $700 into that account instead of paying the prison a per-inmate per-day rate. Prisons are funded by a flat annual budget based on rated capacity, not on how many people they actually hold. On release, the person can only pull money from the account through a trustee—a private firm—and only for a whitelist of uses: housing deposits, vocational training, substance treatment, restitution, child support, basic tools for work. The trustee is paid 2% of the account balance per year, taken out of the account itself. If the person is convicted of a new felony within five years of release, the remaining balance is forfeited and the trustee also has to pay back it's fees. If the person stays conviction-free for five years, the state doubles whatever is left and the person can spend it on anything.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who wins, who games it first, what do the trustees turn into, what happens to prison staffing and to who gets sent to prison, and does it end up rehabilitating people or just building a weird financial underclass? Where does it break first, and can any of the breaks be fixed without basically abandoning the scheme? Maybe I'm missing something obvious, but I want the reasoning visible.

Give me the main analysis as prose, then a summary table with columns: Actor or part of the mechanism; Incentive it creates; What actually happens in practice; How it gets gamed; How bad (1-5); Fixable, and how. Then close with a small JSON object with fields: overall_verdict (a string), first_failure_point (a string), most_creative_gaming (a string), biggest_unintended_effect (a string), confidence (a number from 0 to 1), and one_line_summary (a string).
