The episode I would like analysed is the removal of homosexuality from the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders in the 1970s, together with the residual diagnoses that succeeded it. The salient facts, treated as common ground for the analysis, are these. In December 1973 the Association's Board of Trustees voted to remove homosexuality from the DSM-II, and the 1974 printing replaced it with sexual orientation disturbance, applicable only to people distressed by, in conflict with, or wishing to change their same-sex orientation. DSM-III (1980) then carried a renamed and narrowed category, ego-dystonic homosexuality, before the DSM-III-R in 1987 dropped that named diagnosis, though broad residual categories could still capture distress about sexual orientation for some time afterwards. The theoretical lens is Ian Hacking's account of the looping effects of human kinds — the idea that classifications of people do not merely label kinds that were already there, but interact with the people classified, who take up, resist, or reshape the labels, and thereby feed back into the classifications themselves, so that human kinds are interactive, whereas the kinds studied by the natural sciences are largely indifferent to being classified.

I want the analysis to run in both directions and to be disciplined about the mapping. First, set out the episode in Hacking's terms: who classified whom, through what institution and with what authority; what sort of person the diagnosis helped to make available or consolidate; where the feedback entered; and, in particular, whether the organised refusal of gay people to accept the diagnosis should be read as a looping effect, as an attempt to break a loop, or as something the framework did not anticipate because it treats the classified too much as individuals responding to a label rather than as collective agents. Second, say what the looping-effects story explains that a purely scientific story and a purely political story each miss. Third, say what it does not explain: the empirical studies that undermined the pathology view, the internal politics and procedural oddities of the Association's decision, the compromise character of the residual categories, and the mixed motives behind their eventual removal. Finally, give a verdict on what the episode shows about the theory — whether it confirms it, exposes an anomaly, or forces a refinement. Attend in particular to the fine grain: ego-dystonic homosexuality looks like an uneasy compromise, but it also created a new target for looping, one organised around distress and the wish to change rather than around identity. Does the deletion of that category in 1987 display the same looping structure at a second order, or does it show that a classification can be deliberately wound down by the people it classified — and, if so, does Hacking's framework have a place for that?
