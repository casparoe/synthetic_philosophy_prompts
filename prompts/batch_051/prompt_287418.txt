In debates about Christian worship, one side holds that whatever is not commanded in Scripture is forbidden, while the other holds that whatever is not forbidden is permitted. I keep coming back to a sentence in John Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book IV, chapter 10, section 1, where he writes: "In ordinary language, the name of human traditions is given to all decrees concerning the worship of God, which men have issued without the authority of his word." Yet in that same chapter Calvin insists he is not attacking church constitutions that preserve discipline, decency, or peace. That suggests the later regulative-principle debate is already present inside Calvin, and I want a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled rather than another defense of either side. Is this a substantive disagreement about what counts as worship, about how to infer God's will from scriptural silence, about who has authority to order public practice, or about the burden of proof when Scripture is silent? Be concrete about which moves on each side depend on which assumption, and use a familiar test case such as vestments or musical instruments only if it sharpens the diagnosis. Then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, a historical finding, a better reading of Calvin, or a change of question—or explain why no progress should be expected.
