Write a dialogue between two AI policy researchers, Priya and Marcus, who disagree about why AI labs keep failing to coordinate on slowing down even when most of the people involved say they'd prefer a slower race. Use the following case as their starting point: one lab has just announced a training run an order of magnitude bigger than anything discussed in the coordination talks, and the other labs immediately announce matching runs.

Both characters accept the standard rationalist framework from international relations—that rational actors can end up in conflict despite a mutually preferred bargain because of private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issue indivisibility—but they disagree about which mechanism is doing the work in the AI case.

Priya argues that the main mechanism is a commitment problem: a slow-down deal would make everyone better off, but no lab can credibly commit to stay slow after another lab breaks the deal, especially given winner-take-all dynamics, so preemptive sprinting is individually rational even under full information. Marcus argues the main mechanism is private information with incentives to misrepresent: each lab has private knowledge of its capabilities and timelines and strong incentives to bluff or sandbag for funding and strategic reasons, so the race is a failure of credible information-sharing more than a failure of credible commitment.

Make both characters equally competent and fair to the other's view; no strawmen. They should respond to each other's actual points, not talk past each other. At some point have them confront the worry that these two explanations are hard to tell apart, and have them try to identify at least one observation that would seperate them. End the dialogue with each character stating the strongest point the other one made. Aim for about 800 words.
