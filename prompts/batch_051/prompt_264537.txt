I'm a novelist, and I'm three drafts into a book about a small-town school board meeting that goes viral on a live feed; it's really about the viewers, the ones who watch and comment and feel involved but never actually do anything. That's what sent me back to Kierkegaard's writing on the daily press and what he calls the public. The way I take him, the press manufactures a kind of ghostly public — an abstraction made of spectators rather than actors — and membership in that public lets actual people hide from responsibility: everyone is watching, so no one has to act, and eventually the crowd levels everything down into a blur of commentary and opinion. I keep thinking The Truman Show is the best available dramatization of this, maybe better than his own examples, but I want someone to pressure-test that against the film instead of just nodding along with me.

So read The Truman Show through that Kierkegaardian lens. Where does the film really show the public as an irresponsible spectator-audience — the guy in the bathtub, the two security guards eating their lunches, the patrons in the bar who cheer Truman on but wouldn't lift a finger — and where does the film resist or complicate the theory? Is Christof the press in the relevant sense, or is he more like an author-god figure that Kierkegaard's picture can't quite handle? What should I make of the ending: does the audience's final cheer mean they've become something other than a public, or is it just one more channel change? Be honest where the analogy strains; I don't want to build a novel on a forced reading and loose the actual movie in the process. And end by saying what the film adds to the philosophical picture that the arguments alone leave out, because I'm trying to write it into fiction rather than turn a character into a mouthpiece for a concept.
