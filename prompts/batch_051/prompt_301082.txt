Im in a philosophy of cosmology seminar and the fine-tuning argument keeps unraveling because everyone means something different by "fine-tuned." The design-friendly people mean the universe's constants look arranged to allow life; my physics friend says that's just drawing the target after the arrow lands; my roommate uses it for his coffee grinder, which he honestly did adjust by taste. Before I even take a side on whether the universe supports design, I want a definition that does actual work.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

1. A physical constant (say the cosmological constant or the strength of the strong force) that has to be set within a very narrow range for stars or nuclei to form, as far as current physics can tell.
2. A lottery winner reflecting that the chance of their exact numbers was one in several million; someone had to win, but it still feels like something happened to them.
3. A puddle that wakes up, notices the hole in the ground fits it with astonishing exactness, and concludes the hole must have been made for it.
4. A chef who tastes and adjusts the salt over and over until a dish comes out exactly right, and can do it again tomorrow.
5. A trading algorithm that was fine-tuned on ten years of historical data and nails the backtest but loses money the moment it goes live.
6. A shuffled deck of cards inspected afterward: the exact order is one in 52! and almost certainly unique in history, but nobody selected it.

I think the chef and the overfitted algorithm are clear cases of fine-tuning and the shuffled deck is a clear case of something else, but I honestly don't know whether the puddle or the physical constant should count, and the lottery could go either way. Give me a definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it gives a verdict I didn't expect — and if it does, whether that's a problem with the definition or with my hunches.
