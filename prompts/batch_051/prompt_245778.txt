I'm in a reading group on Augustine's ethics, and we've been stuck on On Lying for a couple weeks now. Early in the treatise he defines a lie like this: "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." Then he says, a little later, that the fault of the liar is "the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind." I think I get the basic contrast with error: if I say the car is in the garage because I believe it is, and it's actually been stolen, I'm not lying. And if I say it's in the garage while believing it isn't, and it turns out to be there anyway, I'm still lying because my mind and words were out of step. But I keep going in circles on what the definition is actually anchored to. Is it purely a matter of the speaker's inner state? If so, then a true statement could be a lie and a false one could fail to be a lie, which Augustine seems to accept, but then why keep using the language of "falsehood" and "truth" at all? It also seems like desire to deceive must do some work, because otherwise a person who utters what they believe false as a joke or in sarcasm would count as a liar, which Augustine says is not so. But if the desire to deceive is necessary, then what about a person who knows the hearer won't believe them (like a prisoner lying to an interrogator who he knows will not buy it)? Does that person fail to lie because he has no realistic expectation of deceiving? Or is the desire still there even if the outer effect is absent? I'm also trying to connect this to Augustine's blanket refusal of well-motivated lies, like the classic case of lying to a murderer about where your friend is. Is that supposed to be wrong because it still involves a split mind, or because the desire to deceive is itself corrupting, or because falsehood uttered knowingly always misuses the faculty of speech, regardless of motive? I'd be grateful for a careful unpacking of how the two conditions in the definition — the mismatch between mind and utterance, and the will to deceive — fit together, and whether either one alone is sufficient for a lie on Augustine's account.
