In my church group someone always reaches for the same move when science comes up, and I used to find it calming: scripture and nature are like two books written by the same author, so if they seem to contradict each other, at least one of them has been misread. The conclusion people draw is that a rightly read Bible and rightly done science can't genuinely conflict — they just answer different questions — and I gather this is basically the line Galileo took when he got in trouble with the church. My brother-in-law is a field geologist and lost his patience with it: books don't erode, don't have strata, don't need to be dated, and don't get read with seismometers, so he says its just a flattering metaphor. I don't think either of us has actually worked out what the analogy can carry, and I'd rather have it audited than have a verdict on who wins.

Walk me through what corresponds to what: in the target, what plays the role of the author, the language, the pages, the genre, the translator, and the act of reading well. Then show where the mapping starts to break, and separate the breaks that actually threaten the no-conflict conclusion from the ones that are just decorative. Finally, say whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't survive in this form, whether a better analogy would carry it or the analogy was doing the work the whole time.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
