I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on an article about democratic theory. Specifically, I want to help readers think through what political theorists call the boundary problem: before any democratic decision can be made, someone has to decide whose voices count — who belongs to the demos. I would like you to provide a working definition of a member of the demos, meaning someone properly entitled to participate in a polity's binding decisions, whether directly or through representatives. I am interested in the normative concept against which an actual franchise or voter roll could be judged, not in restating what any particular legal system happens to do.

Please test your definition against the following cases:

1. A native-born adult citizen who has lived in the country all her life, works there, and votes in every election. (I take this to be a clear inclusion.)
2. A tourist attending a two-week business convention in the country. (I take this to be a clear exclusion.)
3. A permanent resident non-citizen who has lived in the country for twenty years, owns a small business, pays the same taxes as citizens, and is fully subject to its civil and criminal law, but cannot vote in national elections.
4. A citizen who has lived abroad for twenty-five years, has no property or income in the country, and is subject to almost none of its laws, but retains the right to vote by mail.
5. A sixteen-year-old citizen who works part-time, pays sales and income taxes, and is subject to the same criminal law as adults, but cannot vote.
6. A citizen serving a long prison sentence in a jurisdiction that denies the franchise to people while they are incarcerated for serious offenses.
7. A foreigner living in a neighboring country downstream of a dam whose construction was approved by a national referendum in the first country, where the dam's operations substantially affect the foreigner's water supply and livelihood.
8. A member of a future generation who will be born in the country and whose welfare will be shaped by a current referendum on long-lived climate and infrastructure policy.

I am confident about the first two cases and genuinely uncertain about several of the others, so please do not simply ratify current electoral law; the point is to test it. I have encountered two rough guiding slogans in this debate: that standing should follow from having one's interests affected, and that it should follow from being subject to the state's laws or coercion. If you use either as a starting point, show where it breaks down on the cases above, especially the downstream foreigner and the future generation.

Please propose the definition, run it through each case in turn, state explicitly where it gives what I would regard as the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition to fix the worst failures or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, tell me which of your verdicts is the least secure. Use American English throughout.
