Consider a variant of Putnam's Twin Earth. In the original case, Earth's water is H2O, Twin Earth's "water" is a distinct compound XYZ, and the two liquids are indistinguishable in ordinary use; the case is taken to show that what "water" means depends on the speaker's environment, not only on her internal physical state. Now replace the two planets with a continuum: for every real number t between 0 and 1, there is a planet whose clear, drinkable, water-like liquid has a chemical composition that varies continuously with t, with H2O at t = 0 and XYZ at t = 1. The inhabitants of every planet are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of Oscar, and each community's word "water" was acquired through ordinary causal contact with its local liquid. Consider two planets whose t-values differ by an arbitrarily small amount. The externalist seems forced to say either that the two communities' words differ in extension for every nonzero difference in t, however tiny, or that there is a sharp threshold in t at which the extension changes discontinuously; both options look strange. What should a semantic externalist say here, and what hidden assumption of the original Twin Earth argument does this variant expose?
