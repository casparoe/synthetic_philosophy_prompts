Consider two features of the modern law of armed conflict. First, a regular soldier who fights for an aggressor state and a regular soldier who fights for a state acting in self-defense are both entitled to prisoner-of-war status if captured, and neither may be tried simply for having fought. Second, the same symmetry is written into combatant immunity: in an international armed conflict, a soldier's killing of an enemy soldier is not treated as murder or as a crime in itself, regardless of whether the soldier's side is fighting a just or an unjust war. Yet the same legal order strips this immunity from fighters in non-international armed conflicts and from members of armed groups that do not satisfy the criteria of regular armed forces, who may be prosecuted under ordinary domestic law for the same acts of killing.

Reconstruct the theory of the moral equality of combatants that this arrangement presupposes. Is the symmetry in the treatment of regular soldiers best explained by a substantive moral thesis—that unjust combatants do not wrong their adversaries merely by fighting—or by a pragmatic, convention-based thesis that the law of war must be symmetrical if it is to be enforceable and humane? Distinguish what the institution strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say what would have to change in the law's treatment of prisoners and fighters—and in the legal categories that determine who counts as a combatant—if the law were instead built on a revisionist just-war theory according to which only those fighting for a just cause have a moral license to kill.
