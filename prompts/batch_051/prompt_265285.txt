Mill gets revived tomorrow and someone hands him the last sixty years of debate about embryo selection and liberal eugenics. The procreative beneficence side says that if you're already doing IVF and you've got several embryos to choose from, you should do more than just avoid serious disease: you should pick the one that, on the available evidence, is likely to have the best life overall, even if the predicted differences are modest things like temperament or long-term health risks. Critics say this collapses into treating children as products and brings eugenics back through the back door. Where do you think Mill would land, given that he was a utilitarian but also spent a lot of time in On Liberty insisting that self-regarding choices should be protected from social coercion? I'm especially curious whether he'd read the should in procreative beneficence as a real moral obligation or just as good advice, and whether the harm principle would do any work in his answer.
