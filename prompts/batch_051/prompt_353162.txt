Essay question:

Assess the relative merits of institutional, geographical, and cultural explanations for why some nations are rich and others poor, paying particular attention to the difficulties of causal inference from historical evidence.

Student's answer:

The institutional explanation is in my view the most convincing of the three, although the limits of historical evidence are such that no resolution is likely to be decisive.

Institutional explanations hold that the rules governing property rights, contracts, and state predation determine whether investment and innovation are rewarded. The most influential contemporary version is that of Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson. In their 2001 study of former European colonies, they use the mortality rates of early settlers as an instrument for institutional quality: where settlers could survive in sufficient numbers, colonial powers created property-protecting institutions, whereas where disease made settlement hazardous, they created extractive institutions geared to the rapid extraction of resources. Their later argument about a reversal of fortune (2002) shows that, among the territories colonised by Europeans, regions that were relatively urbanised and prosperous around 1500 had become relatively poor by the late twentieth century, a pattern that is difficult for a purely geographical account to explain.

Geographical explanations emphasise constraints and opportunities set by the physical environment. Diamond argues in Guns, Germs, and Steel that continents differed in their stocks of domesticable plants and animals, which determined the timing of the transition to agriculture and thereby shaped the later distribution of technology and state power. Sachs and others stress the direct productivity costs of tropical disease and poor soils. Sokoloff and Engerman add a further channel: in the New World, factor endowments suited to plantation agriculture produced highly unequal colonial societies, and the extractive institutions built on those endowments long outlasted the original crops.

Cultural explanations trace divergence to values and beliefs. Weber's Protestant ethic thesis held that early modern Protestantism encouraged methodical saving and the rational pursuit of profit; more recent cultural arguments appeal to trust, impersonal cooperation, or attitudes towards entrepreneurship. The difficulty with cultural explanations is that culture is diffuse, slow-moving, and itself plausibly shaped by institutions.

On the institutional account, inclusive institutions are in effect competitive elections and broad political participation. Botswana and South Korea are the standard illustrations, since both began sustained growth once such institutions were in place.

The deeper problem is causal inference. No historical process randomly assigned institutions, geography, or culture to countries. Wealthy countries tend to have secure property rights and impartial courts, but that may be reverse causation: prosperity itself makes such institutions affordable. Omitted variables, such as human capital or administrative capacity, may drive both. The Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson instrument is an attempt to break this circle. Because settler mortality is a predetermined feature of the disease environment, it is not caused by later income and is therefore exogenous; the resulting estimates identify the causal effect of institutions, and they thereby show that geography has no direct effect on present-day incomes. The identification assumptions behind this evidence are, however, strong enough that the question is unlikely ever to be settled decisively.
