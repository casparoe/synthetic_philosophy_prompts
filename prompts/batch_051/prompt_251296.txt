Suppose that in 2040 a process is invented, and within a few years becomes available worldwide, that can produce a duplicate of any painting or sculpture that is indistinguishable from the original by every physical and chemical test we have: the same pigments, canvas weave, craquelure, isotopic profile, accumulated grime, and even the same later restorations. The only asymmetry is causal history, and it is made publicly legible: a tamper-proof international registry, accepted by courts and auction houses as authoritative, records which object is the one the artist actually made or had made in their workshop, and every duplicate is automatically logged as such at creation. Duplicates can themselves be duplicated without limit, and the process costs roughly what a high-quality reproduction costs today. Assume the technology works exactly as described and the registry cannot be fooled or hacked during the period you consider.

Predict, with reasoning, how the art world reorganizes itself over the next forty years. I am less interested in a simple verdict about whether registered originals stay expensive than in the mechanisms: what exactly does a premium on an original track once perceptible features no longer distinguish it, and does that premium reflect a rationally defensible value or a bare attachment to causal history? What happens to museums, to the authentication and forgery trade, to art law and insurance, and to the standing contrast between autographic arts, where the object's history is part of the work's identity, and allographic arts, where a faithful copy is the same work? Be explicit about which predictions you are confident in, which are guesses, and what assumption each fragile prediction depends on.

Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five predictions, each one or two sentences stating the effect and the main mechanism behind it, with no introduction or conclusion.
