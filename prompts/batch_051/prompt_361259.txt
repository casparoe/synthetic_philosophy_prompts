I work in the preliminary-examination unit of a national patent office, screening applications that claim to turn information, fluctuations, or very small temperature differences into useful work. Several times a year a descendant of Maxwell's demon reaches my desk: a gate that sorts fast from slow molecules, a Brownian ratchet, a one-bit memory said to make measurement free of energy cost. I am not a physicist, I have one working day per application, and a supervising examiner signs off on every rejection, so each refusal needs a one-sentence failure reason. I can send genuinely ambiguous cases to a staff physicist, but that channel is scarce. The stakes run both ways: a wrongly granted patent protects a machine that cannot work, misleads investors, and clogs the system; a wrongly rejected application blocks a valid, ordinary heat engine.

Please write me a reusable screening procedure, grounded in what is actually settled about Maxwell's demon, the Szilard engine, and the thermodynamics of information, that sorts such applications into three bins: reject as claiming a physical impossibility; refer to the physicist; or treat as a conventional thermal-engine application with physics-flavored marketing. The procedure should be an ordered list of questions a non-physicist can answer from the application's text and diagrams, each with pass, stop, or escalate outcomes, and it must not beg the question by assuming the second law at the first step; it should earn its rejections. Mark the places where judgment survives, for example when an applicant claims erasure is avoided or counts the cost of measurement but not the cost of resetting the measuring apparatus. Keep the distinction between measurement and erasure in view, and treat Landauer's principle as a lower bound, not as a guarantee about any particular device.

Then run the procedure on three applications, saying which bin each lands in and why: (1) a demonic gate that measures each molecule's velocity, admits the fast ones, and claims net work from the resulting pressure difference, the measuring circuit being powered by a battery the applicant counts as measurement cost rather than erasure cost; (2) a Feynman-style Brownian ratchet with a pawl and spring, no explicit memory element, claimed to convert ambient heat into continuous rotation; (3) a small Stirling engine running on a two-degree temperature difference between two reservoirs, nowhere claiming to beat Carnot, but whose abstract advertises it as an implementation of a Maxwell demon. Tell me which of these outcomes I will dislike, whether I should override it, and how to notice that I want to.
