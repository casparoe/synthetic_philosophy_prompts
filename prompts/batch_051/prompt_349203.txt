In a general-audience essay I was reading, the author defends something close to autarky for essential goods by analogy. The author's picture is this: we admire, perhaps even envy, the person who grows much of her own food, keeps a well-stocked pantry, can repair what she owns, and does not depend on others for her daily necessities. Such a person, the argument runs, has a kind of dignity and security that a dependent person lacks; she cannot easily be pressured through her needs. A nation that imports its staple foods, energy, and critical manufactured inputs is in the same condition, so it should pursue self-sufficiency in those essentials even at a real cost in efficiency and convenience. Another reader objected that states are not persons and that the analogy is simply a category mistake.

I want a rigorous audit of this analogy rather than a quick dismissal. Lay out the intended mapping between individual self-sufficiency and national self-sufficiency: what corresponds to what — personal skills and the household to national industry and infrastructure, personal vulnerability to supply-chain exposure, personal dependence to import dependence, dignity and non-coercion to sovereignty and political autonomy, and any other pairings the argument needs. Then identify the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the author's conclusion from the ones that are merely incidental. In particular, say what the analogy quietly assumes about whether individual self-sufficiency really is a virtue and about what kind of dependence is doing the normative work; consider how the step from a person to a collectivity with internal divisions, borders, and distributional choices changes the picture; and state what, if anything, of the policy conclusion survives once the disanalogies are made explicit. Then say whether a better analogy would do the author's work, or whether the point would be better argued without an analogy. Finally, flag any part of this debate that the analogy cannot settle and that should be handled on empirical or strategic grounds instead.
