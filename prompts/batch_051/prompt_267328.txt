I run a reading group that pairs novels with themes from a philosophy of sociality course, and next month we are pairing William Golding's Lord of the Flies with a Lewisian account of convention — roughly, a convention is an arbitrary solution to a recurring coordination problem, sustained by common knowledge, mutual expectation, and each person's conditional preference to conform because the others do, where some alternative regularity could have served just as well. I don't want the discussion to reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory, and I don't want the theory flattened into "people follow rules." Give me a reading that keeps the book's psychology and the theory's structure both in view. Walk through which arrangements on the island — the conch as a claim to speak, the assembly, the signal fire, the hunters' chant, the taboo around the Beast — behave like Lewisian conventions at which moments, and which are better described as imposed rules, mere shared habits, or something the account cannot classify. Then diagnose the collapse: when the boys' fragile system of expectations unravels, does it fail for the reasons a Lewisian would predict (common knowledge shatters, the two factions become rival coordination points, the conditional preference for conformity is withdrawn) or for reasons the account underplays — violence, charisma, fear, the absence of third-party enforcement? Which specific scenes should I put in front of the group to make each point land? And what would you say the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about how norms emerge and die that the theory alone cannot? If the premise of reading the novel through this lens is strained, or the lens distorts the book more than it reveals, tell me that plainly and say where the fit fails.
