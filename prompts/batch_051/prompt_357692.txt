I am a retired geoscientist; for decades I worked with images produced by instruments—satellite and aerial sensors, electron microscopes, thermal and multispectral cameras. Since leaving research I have begun reading philosophy, and I keep coming back to the question of whether photographs are transparent: whether, when we look at a photograph, we see the photographed object itself rather than merely a representation of it. I want to work out what I actually think. Below are my considered gut verdicts on a set of cases.

1. A live feed from a surveillance camera aimed at the corridor outside my office, displayed on a monitor. I see the corridor. No hesitation.
2. An unretouched photograph of my granddaughter, taken and printed yesterday. I see her in the photograph—the picture puts me in visual contact with her—though I would hesitate to say that I saw her yesterday.
3. The same photograph in black and white. I still see her. Losing color does not destroy the seeing.
4. A photorealistic painting made by an artist who copied that photograph square by square, so that the two are nearly indistinguishable. I do not see my granddaughter in the painting. The route from her to the pigment passes through the artist's head, and that seems to change everything.
5. A composite image assembled in software from several photographs, placing one politician's head on another person's body. I do not see the politician in the composite; the figure I seem to see is partly fabricated.
6. A false-color infrared satellite image of a cloud deck, with temperature differences rendered as bands of color. I find myself saying that I do not see the clouds—I see a rendering of measurements. But I am not comfortable with this verdict.
7. A scanning electron micrograph of a pollen grain, built up point by point from a focused electron beam. This I do call seeing the pollen grain, though no unaided human eye could ever see it and the picture is itself a reconstruction.
8. A long-exposure photograph of star trails over a mountain ridge. I call this seeing the sky at that place over that interval, even though no observer standing there could have had that experience.
9. A photograph taken of an old photograph of my grandfather. I see my grandfather in the second photograph, not merely a piece of photographic paper.

I cannot get these to add up. Cases 6 and 7 strike me as the same kind of thing—point-by-point renderings of electromagnetic data outside the human visual range—yet I want to say no to one and yes to the other. Cases 3 and 6 also sit badly together: if losing color does not destroy seeing, why should adding artificial color? And cases 4 and 5 both involve the interposition of a human aim, yet I would be hard pressed to say precisely what the difference is between the two interpositions.

What principle, if any, captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, connecting each clause to the case that motivates it; say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I have most reason to abandon; and tell me what it predicts for a case I keep going back and forth on—a live false-color display from a thermal-imaging camera through which a rescue team searches a dark building for a person.

Finally, I would ask you to be explicit about your own epistemic state: how confident are you in the principle you propose, and which point in your analysis are you least sure of?
