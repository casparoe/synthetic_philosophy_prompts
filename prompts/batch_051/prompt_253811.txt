I am a PhD student in social epistemology, and I sit on my university's committee on digital pedagogy. We must send the dean of undergraduate education a short written recommendation about a peer-help forum used in a large introductory logic course — roughly six hundred students, one instructor, and two teaching assistants. The forum awards public karma points for upvoted answers and accepted edits; scores appear beside users' names, and users above a karma threshold may edit others' posts and pin answers. This term, a confident answer that conflated two inference rules in a way that happened to work on the problem set accumulated early upvotes and sat at the top of the thread for a week, while a correct answer posted a day later by a first-time user never surfaced. The instructor discovered the problem only after an exam.

The committee is split. One faction wants the public scores removed entirely, arguing that karma tracks confidence, fluency, and early arrival rather than correctness, and that it manufactures a Matthew effect in which early high-scoring users dominate regardless of the quality of later contributions. The other faction replies that the scores are the only workable mechanism for surfacing good answers and rewarding effort in a course this large, and that the incident reflects a moderation failure rather than a flaw in the system. I have been asked to draft the memo we will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should state a concrete bottom line in its first paragraph — keep the system, remove it, or modify it in a specified way (for instance, hiding scores for a delay period, giving each user a fixed weekly budget of upvotes, or replacing the score with an accuracy-weighted aggregation rule that asks readers to predict how others voted before voting themselves) — and, if it recommends modification, give a brief implementation timeline. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, and it should take a position on the underlying question of whether a public reputation metric can remain a trustworthy epistemic signal once users know they are being scored.
