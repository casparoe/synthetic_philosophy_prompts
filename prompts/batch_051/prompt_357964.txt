I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to think through a position associated with Wang Yangming, often summarized as the unity of knowledge and action. As I understand him, the position is primarily a claim about practical and moral knowing: knowing that one should care for a parent, or knowing another person's pain. He denies that this knowledge is a self-contained mental state that may or may not later issue in conduct. Someone who can state the right principle but does not act on it does not yet know it in the relevant sense, because the action is what completes or proves the knowledge. The familiar gap between knowing and doing is, on this view, a symptom of incomplete or self-deceived knowledge rather than a neutral interval between two separate stages. I take this to be a strong claim about the identity of knowledge and action, not the weaker claim that they are often correlated.

My problem is that the systems I build seem engineered to pry the two apart. I can train a model until it has a highly accurate representation of a domain: it classifies, predicts, and answers questions about that domain, but behind an API it does nothing in the world except emit tokens in response to requests. That looks like something worth calling knowledge with no corresponding action. Conversely, I can deploy a learned policy that acts competently in a changing environment while its parameters encode nothing we would naturally call knowledge of what it is doing; it acts without anything worth calling understanding. In my work, training and deployment are deliberately separate stages, and either can exist without the other. The older picture of an agent who first knows and then chooses whether to act does not describe this architecture.

So my question is whether Wang Yangming's argument survives this development, whether it needs repair, or whether it becomes inapplicable once knowing and acting can be engineered as separate stages. Which premise does the development bear on: the premise that knowledge of this kind can be possessed only by an agent who also acts, the premise that a knowledge-action gap is always a defect in the knowledge rather than a feature of the system's architecture, or some other premise I have not isolated? Is there a repaired version of the unity claim that remains true and still interesting, perhaps one restricted to agents with a practical self-conception, or one that concedes that machine systems have representational accuracy without action while denying that this is the kind of knowledge Wang Yangming meant? If the repair requires drawing a line between representational knowledge and practical-moral knowledge, I want to know whether that line can be drawn without surrendering the unity thesis, and what is left of the argument once it is. I am asking what becomes of the philosophical argument, not whether current systems are conscious or have minds.
