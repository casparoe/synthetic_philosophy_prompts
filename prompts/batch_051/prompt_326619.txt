One thing I keep tripping over: the Bayesian convergence theorems (merging of opinions, Blackwell–Dubins, that family) seem to say that if two agents' priors aren't mutually singular — if neither starts out ruling out hypotheses the other allows — then as the evidence piles up their posteriors will get arbitrarily close to each other with probability 1. So suppose two scientists know their priors overlap in the right way. Then they know now that they'll end up agreeing in the limit. Doesn't that make their current disagreement just a scheduling problem, like the data just hasn't finished arriving yet? And if they both know they'll converge eventually, why is either of them rationally entitled to keep defending their current view instead of flipping a coin and waiting?
