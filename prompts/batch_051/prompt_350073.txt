Design a classroom activity for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of AI, about 16 students, one 75-minute session. The topic is: given only a record of an optimizer's behavior, what can be inferred about the objective it is maximizing and the space over which it is searching?

The activity should give participants a concrete sequence of observed choices—for example, moves in a small game, strings selected from a finite alphabet, parameter settings, or foraging paths—and ask them to infer the hidden objective and search process. Structure it so that participants first commit to a prediction or a fitted objective, then discover that two or more sharply different objectives, each paired with different assumptions about the search space or search cost, account for the observed behavior equally well. Only afterwards should they see which interventions or further assumptions would break the tie: changing the choice set, varying time pressure or step limits, observing responses to new starting points, and so on.

Please specify the rules, materials, and a scoring scheme that requires no real money, plus a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the formal ideas that behavior underdetermines the objective absent assumptions about the search space and the optimizer, that a change in policy can reflect a change in search rather than a change in values, and that targeted interventions reveal more than passive observation. Do not lecture up front; the participants should run into the underdetermination themselves before the idea is named. Conclude with a list of things that will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle each, and be explicit about which aspect of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply directly.
