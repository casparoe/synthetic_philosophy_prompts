I am a novelist, and I use a large language model daily in three parts of my work: drafting background description and period detail, continuing scenes in a character's voice when I am blocked, and answering factual questions about the settings I write about. I have no stable policy for handling what it produces; in a single day I treat it as a dumb tool, a collaborator, and something like an improvising actor. Please turn the philosophical debate over whether LLMs are best understood as agents with beliefs and intentions, as simulators that produce roles and voices without committing to any of them, or as mere instruments like a thesaurus with a very large memory into a reusable decision procedure for me. The procedure must tell me, for each use of the model: which questions to ask and in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer at each question; and where it directs me to trust, verify, revise, discard, or hand the decision to myself because the question is one of authorship, responsibility, or taste rather than fact. It must be runnable in under ten minutes, by someone who does not know this literature, for outputs that will appear in novels published under my name—where a fact-checker sees the manuscript but my process is invisible. Then run the procedure on three cases from my practice: (1) the model invents a plausible-sounding nineteenth-century dialect word I want to keep for texture; (2) the model continues a scene in a character's voice and writes a better line than mine but assigns the character a motive I had not decided on; (3) the model states as fact a point of nineteenth-century law I cannot check from memory. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I will not like, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
