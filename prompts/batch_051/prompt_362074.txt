I was discussing trade-offs with an economist friend while working on a piece about how people evaluate incremental changes, and I ended up stuck on a puzzle that feels like it should be harmless. It's about whether "better than" is transitive — you know, if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A is better than C. That seems almost too obvious to question, but here's an argument I've been chewing on that seems to force a choice.

Imagine a sequence of possible lives, L1 through L10,000. As you move along the sequence, each life is exactly like the previous one except that it lasts two days longer and involves a minuscule increase in everyday pain, so small you could not distinguish it subjectively. At any single step the trade seems clearly good: a couple of extra days of life in exchange for an imperceptible downgrade in comfort is an improvement. So:

(1) For every n from 1 to 9,999, L(n+1) is better than L(n).
(2) If something is better than a second thing, and the second thing is better than a third, then the first is better than the third — transitivity.
(3) Therefore, by applying transitivity repeatedly along the chain, L10,000 is better than L1.

But L1 is a year of pain-free flourishing, and L10,000 is 80 years of constant severe pain. That conclusion seems obviously wrong — in fact L1 seems much better than L10,000. If I reject the conclusion, I have to reject either (1) or (2), but the only way to reject (1) seems to be to name a particular n where the next life stops being better, and that means one imperceptible extra twinge of daily pain turns a life from better to not-better, which feels arbitrary and insane. So: is this argument actually sound, and should I bite the bullet that "better than" is not transitive? If not, which numbered step fails, and what is the corrected version of that premise? Also, does your answer change if we're talking about what a person has most reason to choose versus what is genuinely better for her?
