One of my students in the theodicy seminar asked something last week that I keep turning over, and I'd like you to take a proper run at it. Suppose Augustine were shown the modern picture: predation, parasitism, disease, starvation, and whole mass extinctions baked into the history of life for hundreds of millions of years before any human being showed up, with our own species formed by the same bloody process. He can't wave this away as a misreading of the fossils; grant him full acceptance of the science. What do you think he would do with it? I don't just mean guess his psychology—I mean construct the best position someone with his actual committments could take. He held, roughly, that creation as God made it was good and that death and suffering are tied in some deep way to the Fall. If that's his starting point, how does he keep the doctrine of a good creation intact while conceding that animal suffering long predates Adam? Would he say prehuman animal death isn't really evil in the relevant sense, that it's a consequence of some earlier cosmic fall, that the goodness of creation lies at the level of the whole rather than each organism, or something else?

And after you've built the strongest version of his view, tell me where it strains hardest—what observation or objection would most pressure it. I'm less interested in the historical question of exactly what Augustine himself would have said than in treating his theological commitments as a constraint: given those constraints, give me the most intellectually serious Augustinian account of animal suffering before the Fall, and then show me the crack in it.
