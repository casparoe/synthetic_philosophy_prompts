I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently preparing a feature about a city that eliminated fares on its public bus network. The question at the center of the piece is whether that policy caused a decline in private-car commuting. The researchers I am profiling did not run a randomized experiment; they compared the city with otherwise similar cities and tracked commuting before and after the fare change. They write that the policy caused the decline. My editor disagrees: without random assignment, he says, all we can claim is association, and he wants me to soften every causal sentence. One of the researchers pushes back. The right standard for causal language, she says, is not randomization but whether the relationship would hold up if the policy itself were changed—set the fare to zero, then reinstate it—while everything else is kept fixed, and whether it does so across different places and periods. A second researcher I consulted calls this test circular. To say what it means to change the policy itself while keeping everything else fixed, he argues, we must already know what the policy causally influences; otherwise we cannot distinguish changing the policy from changing whatever happens to travel with it. I now have a draft due, and I do not know whether the circularity objection undermines the first researcher's standard, or whether either of them gives the right rule for my piece. How should I assess this disagreement, and what should govern my causal language?
