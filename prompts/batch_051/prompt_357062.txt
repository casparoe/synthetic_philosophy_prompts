I've been thinking way too much about a checkbox. Last week I signed up for a new phone plan online, and at the end I checked the little box next to "I have read and agree to the terms and conditions." I hadn't read a word of the 30-page document, and I know the company know that basically nobody does. It got me reading an old translation of Augustine's On Lying, not for a class or anything, just because the subject was already in my head. Two passages got stuck in my mind. Early on he says that jokes have never been counted as lies, since "they bear with them in the tone of voice, and in the very mood of the joker a most evident indication that he means no deceit, although the thing he utters be not true." Then, a little later, he gives his definition: "Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind."

Here's what I keep turning over. The checkbox feels a lot like a joke: nobody expects the statement to be true, and nobody is trying to trick anybody. Its a ritual that only works because everyone knows everyone else is probably fibbing about having read the thing. But Augustine's definition isn't about whether someone is actually deceived. It's about my mind and the sign I put out. I clicked a box that said "I have read" while my mind contained "I have not read," so those two are pointing in opposite directions. Does Augustine's definition make me a liar, or does his joke exception cover the checkbox? And does it change things if the company later uses that checkbox against me, say in an arbitration, as proof that I read and agreed? I want to know how you'd actually apply his line of thought to this, and whether his way of drawing the line holds up for this kind of online ritual.
