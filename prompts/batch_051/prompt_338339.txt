I'm fluent enough in this machinery that you can use it without explaining the basics, including the distinction between updating on a person's report and updating on the fact that they hold some belief.

In aesthetics there is a running dispute about aesthetic testimony: whether you can legitimately acquire aesthetic knowledge from what other people tell you about a work. The pessimist says no. On this view, a critic's description, a friend's enthusiasm, a work's canonical reputation can transmit ordinary facts — the sonata is in C minor, the plot is predictable, it was made in 1963 — but not the knowledge that matters aesthetically, such as whether the work is good, beautiful, or moving. That knowledge, the pessimist claims, requires firsthand acquaintance with the object; without it you can end up with a borrowed opinion, but not knowledge. The optimist answers that this mystifies one domain for no good reason. Testimony is a normal knowledge channel nearly everywhere else, and aesthetic informants — critics, experienced friends — reliably track real features of works, so deferring to them can leave you better placed than your own unformed first impression. A moderate pessimist variant concedes that testimony can transmit the bare belief and perhaps some justification, but not the understanding or appreciation that makes aesthetic judgment worth having.
