I keep noticing a weird asymmetry in how I think about objective chance, and I can't tell if it's defensible or just a psychological hang-up. Before someone flips a fair coin, I'm perfectly happy to say the probability it lands heads is exactly 1/2, and I treat that as a real feature of the coin-plus-toss, not just my ignorance — I'd bet accordingly. But once the coin has been tossed and is sitting under a cup where neither of us has looked, even though my information about which side is up is identical to what it was before the toss, I start feeling like saying "the probability it's heads is 1/2" is no longer right, or at least isn't the same kind of claim anymore. Before, 1/2 felt like a fact about the world; after, it feels like pure ignorance on my part. Is there any real priniciple that justifies treating the pre-toss and post-toss cases differently, or should a rational person have exactly the same 1/2 credence in both and just get over the feeling? If you think there is a difference, give me the strongest version of the principle that draws the line. Answer in American English.
