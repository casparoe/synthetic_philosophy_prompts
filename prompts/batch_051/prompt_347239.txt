I teach an upper-division applied ethics seminar with about 18 students and a single 75-minute session, and I want to run a classroom activity on Jane English's friendship model of filial obligation. The view, as I understand it, is that what adult children owe their parents is not a debt incurred by past sacrifices that has to be repaid on some schedule; rather, it is whatever the actual, ongoing relationship generates — obligations of roughly the kind friends have, rooted in reciprocity, voluntary maintenance of the relationship, and care for the person, and weak or absent when the relationship itself is estranged. The trap I want to avoid is students walking out thinking either that the view simply lets grown children off the hook, or that it is just "helping out of love" with no structure.

Please design a runnable activity for this group — a structured role-play or small-stakes forced-choice exercise, no real money — in which the difference between the debt model and the friendship model emerges from the decisions students actually make before it is named. Commit to one definite design: a concrete scenario (I am considering two adult siblings, one close to their mother and one long estranged, who must decide how to divide responsibility for her care under tight money and competing obligations, but change this if it does not serve the point), specified roles, decision points, constraints, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto particular components of English's view. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, how to handle that in the debrief, and which features of the friendship model the exercise cannot itself produce and the facilitator must supply. Defend the pedagogical choices you made rather than laying out alternatives.
