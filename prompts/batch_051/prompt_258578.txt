I'm a retired population geneticist — thirty years in evolutionary biology, then a decade teaching — and I've started dropping in on a philosophy-of-science reading group that's mostly machine-learning people. Last week we were arguing about whether it's useful to think of natural selection as an optimizer. The ML folks kept caling evolution a "stupid optimizer": it doesn't compute gradients, it can't do credit assignment, it gets stuck, all that. A philosopher in the room kept answering with "nature knows best," as if that disposed of the point. I've heard that line my whole carreer — from ecologists, from the anti-GMO crowd, once from a drug-discovery person defending natural products — and I've never pinned down what it's actually claiming. I suspect it can mean anything from a banal point about organisms having a history to a false claim that evolution finds the globally best design, with a few interesting claims in between about stochastic search versus gradient-based search.

So, taking the context as given (is natural selection a "stupid optimizer" compared to gradient descent?), I want you to do a slogan autopsy on "nature knows best." Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, as a numbered menu. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and — the part I care about — whether that reading would actually block the ML people's claim about evolution being a stupid optimizer. I especially want you to catch the readings that are true but don't do the work the philosopher was using the slogan to do.

For format, give me four titled sections in this order, with rough lengths: "The menu of readings" (numbered, 300–400 words), "Verdicts" (one short paragraph per reading, 350 words), "What the argument needed" (200 words), and "A remaining question" (100 words, the one follow-up I should press next).
