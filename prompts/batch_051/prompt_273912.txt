My reading group is working through John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty*, and for our next meeting each of us has been asked to present the commitments about individuality and self-expression that we actually hold, so the others can press on them. I have been building my position around the following passage, from Chapter III, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being":

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them. It is desirable, in short, that in things which do not primarily concern others, individuality should assert itself. Where, not the person’s own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress."

The set of commitments I find myself holding is this:

1. I accept the harm principle in something close to Mill's formulation: the only purpose for which power may be rightfully exercised over a competent adult against his will is to prevent harm to others; his own good, whether physical or moral, is never a sufficient warrant.

2. I regard the choice of a plan of life as self-regarding in the fullest sense. No one else's judgment about which way of life would be best for me has authority over that choice, and the fact that someone thinks another mode of life would be better for me can never justify coercing me away from it.

3. Experiments in living are to be defended chiefly because they are informative: they put different modes of life to a practical test, so their social value depends on their being serious enough and legible enough for observers to learn something from them.

4. A person whose desires and impulses are his own has a value that is not exhausted by whatever lesson others can extract from his example. A life that merely reproduces custom is diminished even if it is contented and harms no one.

5. Private persons may think worse of others for their self-regarding choices, say so plainly, avoid them, and decline to associate or do business with them, and none of this violates the harm principle.

6. The state may use non-coercive means—public education, subsidies, official commendation—to favor some ways of life over others, including self-regarding ones, provided it neither forbids nor punishes.

I can feel that there are strains somewhere in this set, but I am not sure where, and I would rather not bias the group by saying which commitment I would be most willing to revise. Treat the list as an object for audit rather than a position to be defended. Go through the commitments and identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be; for each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what would have to give, and what the cheapest fix would cost; for each apparent one, say why it dissolves on inspection. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set cohere overall. Where Mill's passage bears on a dispute, use it as the reference point and say whether it cuts for or against the relevant commitments.
