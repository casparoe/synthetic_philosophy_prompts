In our epistemology course we have been contrasting reductionist and anti-reductionist accounts of testimony. Reductionists hold that a hearer is justified in believing a report only if she has positive reasons to take the speaker to be reliable; anti-reductionists hold that testimony is a basic source of justification, so that in the absence of defeaters a hearer is entitled to believe what she is told. I have been trying to think through a less-discussed complication. Within reductionism one can distinguish a global requirement, according to which the hearer needs reason to think testimony in general is reliable, from a local requirement, according to which the hearer needs reason to think this particular speaker is reliable. Consider a hearer who has no evidence about an individual university registrar's track record but has strong evidence that the registrar's office is reliable; moreover, that evidence is itself entirely testimonial, since she has only been told this by other students and has never independently checked the records. Compare reductionism and anti-reductionism on this case. In particular, assess whether a local reductionist can count this second-order testimony as satisfying the demand for positive speaker-specific reasons without falling back into the circularity that usually threatens global reductionism, and whether doing so collapses the view into anti-reductionism.
