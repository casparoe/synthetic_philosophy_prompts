I work in philosophy of language and moral philosophy, on deceptive speech. I have a distinction I keep leaning on and want it pressure-tested. The familiar thought is that misleading is less serious partly because the speaker can always retreat to the literal truth. I want to run that same observation the other way. My proposal is the trace view: a lie attaches the deception to a definite asserted falsehood, which the hearer can later identify, contest, and demand a correction for; misleading speech works through true statements arranged so that the hearer draws the false conclusion, and it leaves no false claim to anchor the grievance in. The very deniability that is supposed to make misleading milder is, on this view, what makes it more insidious: it protects the speaker by depriving the deceived of a corrigible object. That pushes me toward a conclusion I find slightly perverse: along the dimension of repair, the lie is the more honest form of deception, because it at least leaves a trace that can be corrected. Before I build on this, give me an honest assessment. Is the trace view doing real work, or is it just the deniability point with different packaging? Where does it break, for example, with hedged or deniable lies, or with misleading statements whose implied falsehood is exactly as determinate as an assertion? And should I accept my conclusion that misleading can be worse than lying along this dimension, or is the right lesson just that lying and misleading are distinct wrongs with different repair conditions?
