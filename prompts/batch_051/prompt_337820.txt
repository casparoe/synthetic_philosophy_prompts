Okay here's one that's been eating me. My flatmate has an argument that blackmail is morally fine, and I can't find the hole in it. To keep things clean, forget the cases everyone immediately reaches for -- no nudes, no lies, no info obtained by hacking or by a therapist breaking confidentiality. The core case: I truthfully know something real and damaging about you (say, an affair), I came by it honestly, and I say "pay me X or I tell your spouse." Flatmate's argument runs:

(1) If you are permitted to tell a truth, you are permitted to threaten to tell it. A threat to do a permissible thing is just a statement about your future conduct with an if attached; if the underlying act is permitted, the threat is permitted.

(2) You are permitted to ask for money in exchange for not doing something you had no duty to do. If I'm allowed to speak, I'm allowed to stay silent, and if staying silent is allowed, I can charge for it -- people sell confidentiality all the time.

(3) If two acts are each permissible, then performing both together is permissible.

(4) Blackmail is nothing over and above the two acts in (1) and (2) run together: the blackmailer performs a conditional threat to reveal a truth, and simultaneously offers not to reveal it for a price. The case involves no lie, no force, and no breach of confidentiality.

(5) So blackmail is morally permissible.

But that conclusion seems flatly wrong -- blackmail isn't just a sale, it's extortion, and the law pretty much everyhwere treats it as a crime. What worries me most is that the same moves seem to license every conditional threat whose threatened act is permissible: post the honest bad review unless the owner pays, tell the truth about your coworker unless she covers your shift, that sort of thing. But normal life is full of permissible conditional threats -- "give me a raise or I quit" is just bargaining, not extortion. So I don't think we can reject the argument with a blanket rule against conditional threats either. Something has to give, but each premise looks solid on its own. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is actually sound, then tell me why the bad-review case doesn't follow -- or why I should accept that too.
