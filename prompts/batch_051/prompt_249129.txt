A while back I read a blog post explaining Aumann's agreement theorem, and it's been bugging me because it sounded simultaneuosly obvious and way too strong. The post said something like: "In the 1970s Robert Aumann proved that rational Bayesians can't agree to disagree. Suppose two people start out with the same prior, then each gets a different piece of private evidence about some claim. If they each honestly announce their posterior probability for that claim, and each knows the other announced honestly, then their two announced probabilities must match. So in practice, a frank exchange of opinions between reasonable people should force them to converge, and if they still disagree afterwards, someone is either being irrational or holding back information."

I brought this up in an epistemology seminar and the instructor said the theorem gets mangled in popular writeups but didn't say where. My hunch is that the "same prior plus honest announcement" part is doing a lot of work, that the "announced probabilities must match" bit is closer to the real result, and that the "forces them to converge" line is the mangled part — but I can't tell whether the last moral about real disagreements follows from anything in the theorem. Could you go through the blog's version piece by piece, say what a careful statement of the theorem would have to spell out, and then tell me how much of the "disagreement is irrational" conclusion actually survives?
