I've come to think that the propensity interpretation of fitness is broadly right: the fitness of a type or an organism is not its actual reproductive output but its expected reproductive output, a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce relative to a specified range of environments. I know the standard story, that this is what dissolves the old tautology objection — the worry that if fitness is defined as actual reproductive success, then natural selection becomes the vacuous claim that the better reproducers reproduce better. But I suspect the view is doing more work than that story lets on, and I want to know where it is genuinely exposed.

Tell me what would count against the propensity interpretation. I am not only interested in the familiar charge that it merely relocates the tautology, since a disposition to survive and reproduce is still characterized in terms of surviving and reproducing. I also want the less obvious defeaters: cases in which two types have the same expected number of offspring but different variances in reproductive output, so that the expectation alone leaves out something that matters to long-term success; the problem of specifying the relevant environment when fitness is sensitive to environmental detail at a finer grain than any manageable description; the worry that a propensity is supposed to be a causal property of an organism or type, while fitness values are in practice estimated from population statistics and can look like model-relative summaries; the treatment of drift, which the propensity view tends to relegate to a residual deviation from expectation rather than a component of evolutionary change; and the question whether probabilistic propensities explain actual outcomes or only redescribe them probabilistically.

For each of these, tell me whether a committed defender of the propensity interpretation would concede that it counts against the view, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I would also like your own verdict on whether the interpretation has insulated itself with its qualifications — the relativization to environment, population, and life cycle — and whether that insulation is a problem or just what a good dispositional account looks like. Where the considerations are genuinely unsettled, say how confident you are in each assessment rather than hedging vaguely. Personally I find the drift worry the most troubling, because it makes me doubt whether propensity fitness is picking out a cause rather than a bookkeeping device. If you think the question is misframed — say, because the tautology objection was never really aimed at the propensity interpretation, or because fitness talk in biology is too heterogeneous to receive a single interpretation — push back plainly instead of playing along.
