I keep getting dragged into the same argument with my brother about whether ASL counts as a language, and I've realized I don't actually have a definition of "language" I can defend. His line is that ASL is not a *full* language because it has no written form and (he says) you can't do abstract reasoning in it, which I'm pretty sure is just false, but I keep not being able to say exactly why. So give me a working defintion of language that gets these cases right:

- a spoken conversation in English — clearly a language
- a conversation in ASL between two Deaf signers — clearly a language, and any definition that kicks this one out is dead on arrival
- a novel in English — clearly a language too, so the definition better not require speech
- a honeybee's waggle dance, wich encodes distance and direction to flowers — that's communication but I'd say not a language
- Python — people call it a programming language, but I suspect that "language" is a metaphor; still, I can't say why without a definition
- a parrot that says "hello" and "good morning" on cue — clearly not a language
- a deaf kid with no access to an established sign language who invents a systematic gesture system to talk with their hearing family (I think people call this home sign) — this one really trips me up. It's structured and referential, but it's only used by one family.
- a village where a big chunk of people are deaf and everyone, hearing and deaf, grows up using the local sign system — not sure whether this should count the same as a language or differently
- a two-year-old saying "me want cookie" — language? on the way to language? something else?

Propose the definition, run it through all of those, and tell me where it surprises you or where you'd have to bite a bullet. If it puts the parrot and the bee dance in the same bucket as the ASL conversation, or kicks out the two-year-old or the novel, I want to hear that explicitly.
