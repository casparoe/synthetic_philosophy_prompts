Consider a familiar asymmetry in our judgments of self-control. Two people both decline a third drink at every party, and both keep their work, spending, and health in order. One is a recovering alcoholic who still feels a strong pull toward drinking and must actively resist it; the other has simply never wanted more than two drinks. We are inclined to call the first person "genuinely self-controlled" and to admire the struggle, while we give the second little or no credit, treating their conduct as easy, lucky, or a matter of temperament. Aristotle, however, treats the second person's condition as the more perfect one rather than as a defect. In Nicomachean Ethics VII.9 (Rackham translation), he writes:

"Many terms are used in an analogical sense, and so we have come to speak by analogy of the 'self-restraint' of the temperate man, because the temperate man, as well as the self-restrained, is so constituted as never to be led by the pleasures of the body to act against principle. But whereas the self-restrained man has evil desires, the temperate man has none; he is so constituted as to take no pleasure in things that are contrary to principle, whereas the self-restrained man does feel pleasure in such things, but does not yield to it."

Is our asymmetrical admiration justified, or is it something we should give up? Identify the best candidate justifications—for example, that effort reveals stronger commitment, that the untempted person's conduct is not really voluntary or praiseworthy, or that becoming temperate usually requires having first struggled—and test them against variations: a struggle that leaves the person miserable and hard to be around; a calm temperament that was itself built through decades of earlier discipline; someone who resists only out of fear of social shame; and the person who struggles and eventually slips. Should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it?
