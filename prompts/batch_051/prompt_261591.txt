I'm working through the opening of Ibn Rushd's *Decisive Treatise* (the Hourani translation), and I keep getting stuck on a particular move in his argument. Here is the passage:

"If the activity of 'philosophy' is nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan, i.e. inasmuch as they are products of art (for beings only indicate the Artisan through our knowledge of the art in them, and the more perfect this knowledge is, the more perfect the knowledge of the Artisan becomes), and if the Law has encouraged and urged reflection on beings, then it is clear that what this name signifies is either obligatory or recommended by the Law."

I want to understand what this argument is actually doing, in American English, without a general summary of the treatise. Specifically, how much weight rests on the phrase "nothing more than"? What does he mean by "indications of the Artisan," and why does he think the Law's encouragement of reflection is enough to license the study of philosophy in the technical Greek sense, not just everyday reflection on nature? Then take the obvious objection that philosophy for Ibn Rushd meant something like the Aristotelian demonstrative tradition, which is a lot more specific than just studying existing beings, and say where the argument can be defended and where it starts to strain.
