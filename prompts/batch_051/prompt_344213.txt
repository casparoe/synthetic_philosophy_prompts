Every few years I redo my unit on the knowledge argument, and I always end up presenting the standard replies as a menu without ever committing to how much each one gives away. This year I'd rather actually order them, partly because my seminar is split between students who think Jackson's argument is obviously sound and students who think it's obviously a cheat.

Suppose we grant, for the sake of argument, that pre-release Mary genuinely knows every physical fact about color. Here are the four replies I have in mind: (i) the ability hypothesis, on which she only lacked color-related know-how; (ii) the acquaintance hypothesis, on which she lacked direct acquaintance with color experiences rather than any fact; (iii) the phenomenal concept strategy, on which she acquires a new concept or mode of presentation and thereby comes to think an old physical fact under a new guise; and (iv) the intuition-denial reply, on which there was nothing real she lacked and the 'aha' on release is an illusion driven by a bad picture of experience. Rank those four from most concessive to the anti-physicalist to least concessive — that is, by how much each one admits that somthing real and non-trivial was missing from Mary's pre-release understanding. Give me a sentence for each saying exactly what it concedes and why it lands where it does. Then give each reply a damage score out of 10 for how much it weakens the knowledge argument as an argument against physicalism. Finally, which single reply would you put in front of an undergrad who finds Jackson's argument completely convincing, to make them squirm the most, and why?
