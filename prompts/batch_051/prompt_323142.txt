I've got a pet account of how thought experiments — the things Dennett calls intuition pumps — actually do their persuading, and I want it stress-tested. I call it the staging account. The claim is that a thought experiment gets most of its force from what it stages as fixed background, not from the choice it seems to put in front of you. The displayed part — pull the lever or don't, unplug the violinist or don't — is never where the real work happens. The real work is in the stage setting: in Thomson's violinist you wake up already hooked up; in the trolley problem the five are already tied to the track, so the only live question is whether you intervene. My stronger claim is that people are much worse at noticing load-bearing assumptions when they arrive as scenery, already in place and not up for discussion, than when they arrive as explicit premises — and that's exactly why the device works. It also predicts you can defuse most intuition pumps by asking why the scenario begins where it begins. So: is the staging account actually different from the old point that thought experiments have hidden premises, or have I just re-named that? Where does it fail — can you point to a thought experiment that persuades without a loaded starting point? And whats the strongest objection to the stronger claim about scenery being harder to scrutinize?
