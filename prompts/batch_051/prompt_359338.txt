In his later writings on religion, most explicitly in The Future of an Illusion and in his discussion of the oceanic feeling in Civilization and Its Discontents, Freud treats religious ideas as illusions in a special sense: not simply false beliefs, but beliefs whose grip on us is explained by the wishes they fulfill rather than by the evidence for them. Behind that analysis sits a developmental story about childish helplessness, the longing for a protective father, and a lingering belief in what he called the omnipotence of thoughts—the animistic intuition that wanting something to be so is the first step toward making it so. But Freud worked this out against the background of the European religiosity he knew: a personal God, a church, a congregation, and a body of doctrine. He died in 1939 and never saw the post-institutional spiritualities that have since flourished, and in particular he never saw the contemporary manifestation culture—the increasingly secularized, self-help practice of visualizing and affirming desired outcomes such as wealth, health, and relationships into existence, now spread through bestselling books and social media.

I am trying to reconstruct, not what Freud did say, but what his mature theory commits him to saying about this kind of practice. Would he classify manifestation as religion continuing by other means—a wish-fulfilling illusion that has survived the death of the personal God by relocating the sense of omnipotence from a deity to the self, in effect a return of the repressed in therapeutic vocabulary? Or would he have to concede that the phenomenon escapes his account, because it posits no deity, promises no consolation from a father-figure, sponsors no collective ritual, and presents itself as a technique for self-management rather than a creed? Give me the strongest version of both readings, then say which one you think follows most naturally from Freud's mature commitments, and finish by identifying the one point on which a thoughtful practitioner of manifestation would be most right to object that Freud's framework distorts what she is doing.
