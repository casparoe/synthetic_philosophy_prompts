I’m an undergraduate taking a philosophy of animal minds course this term, and we’ve been spending a lot of time on C. Lloyd Morgan’s canon. Our lecturer gave us the standard formulation as: “In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development.” She also mentioned that Morgan later qualified this by saying, in effect, that there is nothing really wrong with complex interpretations if the species has provided independent signs of high intelligence. I understand the canon as a kind of parsimony principle, but I’m struggling with how to apply it when the evidence is genuinely mixed, especially for animals where the “scale” is not obvious because they are very far from us phylogenetically.

Here is the case that has been bothering me. An octopus in a research aquarium is presented with a transparent box containing a crab. The box has a lid secured by a simple rotating latch. Over several sessions the octopus solves it by trial and error, taking many minutes and producing a great deal of apparently random manipulation. Later, the same octopus is allowed to watch, for thirty seconds, while a conspecific in an adjacent tank opens the identical box smoothly and eats a crab. On its next trial, the observer octopus opens the box on its first attempt in under ten seconds. The researchers then present it with a new box whose lid is secured by a different mechanism—a push-and-turn childproof cap, which the octopus has never encountered. The octopus opens that too on the first attempt. Some researchers describe this as observational learning plus flexible, goal-directed problem-solving. Others say the first success is explained by stimulus enhancement and previously acquired motor patterns, and the second success is just a lucky extension of the same manipulation, reinforced by the first success: the octopus learned that lids of this general kind yield food, without representing the mechanism.

Given Morgan’s canon, are we required to prefer the lower explanation here, or does the later qualification permit—or even encourage—the higher one? Go through the case under both readings of the canon, and say where the principle itself fails to settle the question. In particular, I want to know whether “fairly interpreted” means merely possible or genuinely plausible, and what would count as independent signs of high intelligence in an octopus that would license the complex interpretation. Is the problem here that the canon needs to be refined, or that the case is simply under-described?
