I'm taking a philosophy of law module this term and we've spent the last couple of weeks on naked statistical evidence, which I'm finding properly strange. The stock examples are the blue bus hypothetical, where a bus company runs 80% of the buses on a route and a pedestrian is hit but no one can identify the bus, and the gatecrasher case, where you can prove that 501 of 1000 people at an event never paid but not which ones, so any random attendee is 50.1 per cent likely to have gatecrashed. Courts supposedly refuse liability in both cases, even though the probability in the blue bus case sits comfortably above the civil standard. My lecturer insists this shows that legal proof requires the right kind of evidence, not just a high probability: something that attaches to this particular defendant, like a witness or a trace, rather than a bare base rate. So the deeper claim is meant to be conceptual — that standards like 'balance of probabilities' and 'beyond reasonable doubt' are about individualising evidence, not merely credences.

I'm not convinced that's conceptual rather than just a fact about how judges and jurors get queasy around numbers. Can this actually be tested?
