The usual line in math education is that you can be procedurally fluent without understanding: you can check each step of a proof and still have no sense of why the theorem is true, and without that sense you don't actually understand it. That argument assumed a proof was the kind of thing one person could in principle survey. But a lot of contemporary mathematics now goes through proof assistants — the computer verifies every inference and can certifiy the result, while no single person may have a grip on how the whole proof hangs together. Does the old argument, that understanding is the thing that matters and not mere verification, survive this? Or does certification without understanding become a fine way to know a theorem once proofs outrun any one mind? I want to know which premise the proof-assistant development puts pressure on, and whether some weaker version of the argument is still worth keeping.
