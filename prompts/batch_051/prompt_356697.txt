I'm in a small betting syndicate, and Article IV of our charter says: 'In any choice between a wager and declining it, a member shall calculate the expected value of the wager and shall take it only if that expected value is greater than zero.' Someone has offered us a game where a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads is on flip n, the player receives (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars — a gain on odd n and a loss on even n. If you add the expected-value terms in the order the outcomes naturally occur, the series converges to ln 2, so by the letter of the rule the bet looks good. But the gain states alone have infinite expected value, and the loss states alone have infinite expected value, so the expectation is really an ∞ − ∞ form and is undefined in the standard mathematical sense. The people who wrote Article IV were thinking about ordinary bounded gambles and plainly never imagined this. Does Article IV require us to take the bet, forbid it, or fall silent? Give me a committed answer: work through what the words, the purpose of the rule, and what the drafters would have said if asked each support, then say which approach should control and what we should actually do.
