I'm a philosophy professor, but I've written enough Haskell over the years that I basically reach for pure functions, referential transparency, and types whenever I want to make an argument precise. I want you to translate Hofstadter's superrationality into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Start from a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: two agents, symmetric payoffs, no communication, no binding commitments, and common knowledge of rationality. In Haskell-ish terms, is the superrationalist saying the two players should be modeled as the same function evaluated in two environments, rather than as two separately written programs that happen to be extensionally equivalent? If so, what follows in the translated version, and were does it stop being faithful? Then give me a residue report: what parts of superrationality translate cleanly, what parts only go through with distortion, and what parts have no counterpart at all. I'm especially interested in whether the idea that the players are similar reasoners with common knowledge of their similarity becomes something crisp, like deciding you'll write one program and run it twice, or whether it smuggles in an epistemic claim the type-level story is to coarse to capture. And finally: is that residue a limitation of the Haskell vocabulary, or evidence that part of Hofstadter's argument was doing its real work by being left vague?
