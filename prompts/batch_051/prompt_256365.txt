I am a novelist drafting a scene in which two old friends argue over a town's proposal to end its annual public commemoration of a massacre and replace it with a small, private observance. One friend holds that the community owes the dead a continuing, particular remembrance—naming the victims, not merely marking the event—and that letting the public ritual lapse would wrong the dead a second time. The other holds that the ritual has become a self-congratulatory performance, that the particular remembrance of old wrongs now mainly binds present identities to past grievances, and that there are times when deliberately forgetting is the more honest and merciful course. Write their dialogue. Have them restate and answer each other's strongest points rather than talking past one another, and have them press the hard cases: a survivor who asks for the ceremony to end, a peace settlement that depended on amnesty, a perpetrator community that would be relieved by forgetting, and a younger generation that feels no connection to the dead. Neither should be a strawman, and neither should plainly win. Please write in British English.
