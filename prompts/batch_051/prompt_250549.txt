I am a systems engineer by training and habit: I think naturally in terms of processes, threads, virtual machines, snapshots, live migration, hardware abstraction, and the distinction between a program's specification and its execution on particular hardware. I can be precise in that vocabulary, but I have only an outsider's grasp of philosophy of mind.

I want you to take a specific position from the whole-brain emulation debate and restate it in that vocabulary. The position has three parts. First, the mind is, in the respects that matter, a computational process realized by the brain. Second, whole-brain emulation is feasible in principle: you do not need to replicate every physical detail of the brain, only its causally relevant dynamics at the right level of description — say, synaptic connectivity and activity rather than molecular chemistry. Third, if an emulation is built with sufficient fidelity, it is the same mind as the original: not a distinct copy or a mere simulation of that mind, but a continuation of the original mind, such that the original person could in principle wake up as the emulation.

Translate each of the three parts into systems-engineering vocabulary, one at a time. For each part, tell me what carries over cleanly, what can be expressed only with distortion or by silently adding an engineering assumption, and what has no expression in the vocabulary at all. Then give a residue report: does what fails to translate point to something the systems vocabulary is blind to but the position needs — phenomenal experience, the first-person perspective, the difference between numerical identity and continuity — or does it point to something the original position left under-described or question-begging? Keep the translation honest. Do not settle the identity question by choosing one engineering analogue over another — live migration rather than forking a process or restoring from a snapshot — unless you say explicitly that you are doing so and why that analogue is the right one.
