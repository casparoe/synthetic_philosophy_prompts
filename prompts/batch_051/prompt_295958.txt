In Arrival, Louise Banks is basically doing radical translation: no shared language, no shared history, barely any shared biology, and she has to build up a whole manual for the heptapods' inkblot logograms from pointing, gesture, and trial and error. That part is pretty Quinean. Quine's point is that in radical translation the totality of behavioral evidence can't settle whether the native's word picks out rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, rabbit-stages, or some other goofy slicing of the world, and there's no hidden fact of the matter that makes exactly one of those right. But then the film does the time thing: once Louise really internalizes Heptapod B, she's supposed to be able to see time the way the heptapods do, remember her own future, and act on it. That sure looks like the movie is saying there is a correct, determinate interpretation of the alien language after all — one that plugs her into their actual physics, not just one empirically adequate translation among many. So I want to know where the film is doing good Quine, where it quietly smuggles back in the idea of a uniquely true translation, and whether it even knows the difference between epistemic underdetermination (we can't tell which is right) and indeterminacy (there's nothing there to be right or wrong about).
