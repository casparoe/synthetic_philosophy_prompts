I'm a high school student who co-founded an after-school club on rational thinking, and I've been asked to write the opening essay for a small student conference. Our club has spent the year doing decontextualized reasoning drills: premortems on planned projects, red-team/blue-team exchanges, daily updating logs, and something we call belief katas, in which we repeat a structured move—such as stating the observation that would most change our minds about a held belief—until it starts to feel automatic. I'm starting to suspect that we have gotten better at performing the drills while staying just as overconfident and confirmation-prone in real disagreements, which is exactly the criticism martial artists sometimes make of kata training. Please write a formal essay that takes up this question directly: can rationality be trained like a martial art, through repeated, formalized exercises whose value depends on transfer to live reasoning? Weigh the strongest arguments for thinking yes, such as automaticity, lowered inhibition, and the value of isolating failure modes, and the strongest arguments for thinking no, such as the decontextualization problem, the illusion of competence, and dojo blindness. But do not end non-committally: defend a definite verdict. If your verdict is conditional—for example, that drills work only if they meet certain design standards—make those conditions specific enough that our club could use them to decide whether to keep, modify, or abandon the katas.
