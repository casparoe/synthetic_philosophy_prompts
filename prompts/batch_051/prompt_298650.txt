Act as a decision theorist who works on commitment devices and rational irrationality. Think carefully, maybe jot down the moving parts first, but give me a clean writeup.

A startup proposes what it calls Parfit contracts. You enroll, pay a fee, and designate a class of "governed" promises — roughly, promises made while you're in serious need, like Parfit's hitchhiker promising a driver he'll pay after being taken home. Once one of those promises is made, the firm's agent takes over the decision of whether you keep it. The agent is instructed not to re-run your current expected utilities at all: it just enforces the promise, even if paying now looks like a bad deal. The contract makes revocation or sabotage trigger a larger penalty, paid to a charity you dislike.

How would a market for these actually behave? I want the analysis, not just a verdict on weather it's a good idea. Give me:
- "The pitch" (150-250 words): the real benefit a rational person could get by signing up, and why Parfit's hitchhiker specifically can't get it from ordinary escrow.
- "How it actually works" (200-300 words): who gets screened in or out, what the agent can and can't actually enforce, and whether "ignoring the utilities" is a real property of the system or just theater.
- "Gaming and failure modes" (300-450 words): moral hazard, disputes over what counts as a governed promise, collusion and extraction, and people routing around the agent.
- "Can it be fixed?" (150-250 words): which failure modes could be fixed by contract design and whether the fixes would reintroduce the original problem.
- "Confidence" (100-150 words): how confident you are in the overall analysis, and the single point you're least sure about.
