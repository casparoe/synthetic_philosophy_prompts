I am a novelist working on a novel about a mathematician with unusually strict standards about which arguments are allowed to count as proofs. In drafting her, I have found that I now share her scruples, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures them.

1. I accept the proof that every integer between 3 and 1000 has a prime factorization, because one can actually exhibit the factorizations.

2. I accept the proof that the square root of 2 is irrational, even though it assumes rationality and derives a contradiction, because what it establishes is negative: no rational number squares to 2.

3. I reject the standard proof that there exist irrational numbers x and y such that x^y is rational — the proof that splits on whether (√2)^(√2) is rational and is done either way. It never settles which alternative obtains and never produces a pair. Yet I accept the same theorem unreservedly when given the explicit pair x = √2 and y = 2 log₂ 3, both irrational, with the computation that x^y = 3.

4. I accept that if a computer program is searching the natural numbers for the least number passing a definite test, and it is impossible that it never finds one, then it will eventually find one. I regard this as a fact about computations.

5. I reject, in general, the move from "not every natural number fails to have P" to "some natural number has P."

Is there one principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that delivers all five verdicts? If no simple principle delivers all of them, which verdict is the least costly to surrender, and why? Please also test the principle you recommend against cases I have not yet ruled on: (a) Euclid's proof that for every n there is a prime greater than n, which turns on a division into cases that can be settled by finite trial; (b) an existence proof that splits on the truth of the Riemann hypothesis, where neither branch supplies an explicit witness; and (c) the claim that some digit occurs infinitely often in the decimal expansion of π. I would like the principle stated precisely, with each clause matched to the verdict it explains, its predictions for cases (a) through (c), and an honest statement of which of my five verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate.
