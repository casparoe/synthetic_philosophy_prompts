For the past several years, as part of my science-and-ideas beat, I have been covering the rulings of an expert body I will call the Data Practices Review Board. Its opinions are advisory, but regulators and the large technology companies generally treat them as de facto guidance, and I am now writing about a case that seems likely to strain its precedents. My notes on the Board's recent rulings, simplified, are these:

1. The Board approved a hospital's use of its own patients' admission records to train a model that flags early signs of sepsis. Patients had signed a general admissions notice rather than consent specific to this use.

2. The Board approved a supermarket's use of loyalty-card purchase histories to personalize discount coupons. It disapproved the same supermarket's plan to sell individual-level purchase histories to a data broker for targeted advertising.

3. The Board approved a health researcher's project that collected public posts from a chronic-illness forum and published only aggregate statistics, with no usernames included.

4. The Board told a bank that it had to disclose the algorithmic creditworthiness and churn scores it assigned to customers, along with the material factors behind those scores, even though the scores were the bank's own analytical products.

5. The Board disapproved a consumer-genetics company's plan to license customers' genetic data to a pharmaceutical firm. The company's terms mentioned sharing de-identified data with partners, but the Board held that this was not specific consent for a commercial transfer of that kind.

6. The Board approved a data broker's sale to lenders of individual reputation profiles assembled entirely from public property records and business-licensing filings, on the condition that the information be accurate and not used in prohibited discrimination.

The case I am writing about now is this. A voice-analytics firm records customer-service calls after a short notice that calls may be recorded for quality assurance. From those recordings it infers each caller's emotional state and builds a stress-susceptibility score, which it wants to sell to life insurers for use in underwriting. A caller whose insurance renewal was affected objects.

If the Board wants to be consistent with the pattern above, should it approve or disapprove this use? Which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and what principle would the Board be adopting under each possible answer? I am less interested in a confident conclusion than in the rival rules that fit all six precedents — a consent-for-transfer rule, a sensitivity-or-harm rule, a derived-product rule, or something else — what each predicts for this case, which one best explains the whole record, and the single further case I should press the Board on to separate them.
