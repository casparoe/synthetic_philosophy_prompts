I have been developing a distinction that I call "inside reasons" and "outside reasons," and I would like an honest assessment of it. An outside reason is a consideration that counts in favor of having an attitude because of what having that attitude would do for you. For example, if someone offered me a large amount of money to believe that the moon is made of cheese, I would have an outside reason to believe it: the belief would benefit me, and it might be rational to hire a hypnotist to bring the belief about. An inside reason is a consideration about what the attitude is about. For example, the fact that the moon is made of rock is an inside reason to believe the moon is made of rock, and a person's kindness is an inside reason to admire that person. My central claim is that only inside reasons can make an attitude justified or fitting. Outside reasons can make it rational to try to get yourself into an attitude, but they never show that the attitude is the right one to have. I know this resembles the distinction between pragmatic and epistemic reasons, but I intend it to cover emotions and desires as well as beliefs, and I do not think pragmatic reasons fail in exactly the same way in every case. Is my distinction coherent? Does it already exist under another name? And where does it break — for instance, are there cases where an outside reason does make an attitude fitting, or where the line between inside and outside cannot be drawn without assuming my conclusion? Please answer in American English.
