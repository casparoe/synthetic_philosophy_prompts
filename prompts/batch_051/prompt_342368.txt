For the past several years I have been part of a reading group on interpretability research, and a single impasse keeps returning no matter how much new empirical work we read. Both camps appear to accept the same findings. On one side: large language models contain directions in activation space, often surfaced by sparse autoencoders or linear probes, that line up surprisingly well with humanly describable features—a direction that tracks truthfulness, one that tracks sycophantic flattery, one that separates scripts, and so on. Steering along such a direction changes model behavior in roughly the way the content ascription predicts. In small networks trained on tasks with a known underlying variable, probes recover that variable, and intervening on it degrades or redirects performance in the expected way. On the other side: the deflationists accept all of this and deny that it settles whether the network has representations. Decodability, they insist, is not use; a weakly trained or randomly initialized probe can recover, with above-chance success, a variable whose downstream role is nowhere to be found. Sparse autoencoders and probes impose a decomposition as much as they discover one, and the same network supports many predictively adequate redescriptions of its internals. Causal manipulability is cheap: a thermostat's bimetallic strip causally mediates the furnace's behavior without, in any substantive sense, representing the room's temperature. And insofar as a "feature" exists only relative to a choice of basis, training objective, and level of granularity, the realist's vocabulary looks like a modeling convenience rather than a discovery about the network itself. The realists reply that the deflationists' alternative descriptions are predictively cheap precisely because they refuse to commit to any content, and that the content-laden features are the ones that let us steer and debug the network—but this reply, too, leaves the deflationists unmoved.

The frustrating thing is that this is not a disagreement over facts; it is a disagreement that outlives every new fact. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are using "representation" with different success conditions—one treating a causally relevant, intervenable, contentful variable as sufficient; the other treating it as necessary to rule out every non-representational redescription—which would make the dispute at least partly verbal. But I can imagine other diagnoses: a hidden formal question about whether there is any privileged decomposition of a network's weights and activations; a methodological difference about how high the bar for content ascription ought to be; a bedrock difference about whether intentional vocabulary describes the network's furniture or merely summarizes its input–output profile; or an ill-posed question that cannot be settled because "representation" has no univocal sense.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who is right. What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom—verbal, factual, methodological, about standards of evidence, or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal criterion, a kind of experiment, a change of question, or a reason to expect none? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey; I want to know why the impasse has the shape it does.
