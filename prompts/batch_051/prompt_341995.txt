Over the past few months I've been trying to work out what actually separates pseudohistory and pseudoarchaeology from legitimate historical and archaeological inquiry, and I've found that my reactions to individual cases are much clearer than any general criterion I can state. I want you to help me extract the principle behind my verdicts—or tell me that no single principle can do the job and that some of my verdicts have to go. My verdicts are these. (i) I regard Atlantis-taken-as-a-real-lost-continent as pseudoarchaeology, but I do not regard it as pseudoarchaeology to ask whether Plato's story encodes a memory of the Thera eruption and Minoan Crete; that hypothesis may well be false, but it seems to me respectable. (ii) I regard ancient-astronaut explanations of the pyramids and similar monuments as pseudohistory, not because they are popular, but because they can explain any monument whatsoever by an invisible intervention and are never revised when a mundane explanation turns up. (iii) I regard Fomenko's New Chronology as pseudohistory: it does not weigh the evidence but reverses the burden of proof, treating the entire documentary and archaeological record as a forgery to be explained away while treating accidental verbal and numerical similarities as positive evidence. (iv) I regard the Kensington Runestone, the purported medieval Norse inscription from Minnesota, as a hoax, and I regard using it as evidence of medieval Norse presence in the American Midwest as pseudohistory; yet I fully accept the medieval Norse settlement in Newfoundland. The difference for me does not reduce to "one is accepted and one is not." (v) I think Schliemann's excavations at Hisarlık in the 1870s were legitimate archaeology even though he was guided by Homer; using a myth to locate a real site seems to me a reasonable heuristic. But I resist the same move when Atlantis enthusiasts use Plato to locate a lost continent, and I need to know what makes the two cases different. (vi) I think the pre-Clovis research program was legitimate archaeology even when it was a minority position against the Clovis-first consensus, and I now accept that people reached the Americas before Clovis. So my principle cannot simply be "respect the consensus." (vii) I regard Graham Hancock's lost-civilization thesis as pseudohistory even though he points to genuinely puzzling sites such as Göbekli Tepe. This worries me, because I cannot dismiss him merely for opposing consensus, and I cannot dismiss him merely for proposing a grand unifying story, since the pre-Clovis case shows I sometimes admire the underdog.

I am tempted by the thought that the difference lies in epistemic conduct: genuine inquiry specifies what would count against it and revises when evidence demands, while pseudohistory protects a favored conclusion with indefinitely flexible auxiliary hypotheses. But that slogan does not obviously explain my Atlantis/Thera verdict, my Troy/Atlantis asymmetry, or why I am so confident about Hancock without having first specified how his thesis would handle particular failures.

I would like you to reconstruct the best principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely and tied to the cases that force each clause. Then apply it to cases I have not settled: the hypothesis of pre-Columbian Polynesian contact with South America, the Vinland map, and the view that the Homeric Trojan War is entirely fictional with no historical core at all. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and tell me which one or two verdicts I should revise in order to make the rest principled rather than ad hoc.
