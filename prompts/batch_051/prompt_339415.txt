I'm drafting a short position paper for a workshop on collective epistemology, and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether the following six commitments hang together. The domain is unilateral action: situations in which any single member of a group can, on her own initiative, bring about some outcome that affects the whole group—several labs independently able to release a risky technique, say, or several states able to attempt a first contact—and in which no binding group decision procedure is available. Each commitment is stated as exactly as I can make it.

(1) The unilateralist's curse is real. When each member acts iff her own estimate of the expected value of the shared action is positive, the group can end up taking the action even though the median member—and perhaps a clear majority—would reject it, because the most optimistic members move first.

(2) The members who trigger these outcomes are not, or at least not typically, failing by their own lights. The defect lies in aggregation across the group's scattered judgments, not in any of the acting members' individual instrumental rationality.

(3) The right individual rule in such settings is a principle of conformity: one should act only if one judges that the group's considered view, were the members polled under good deliberative conditions, would endorse the action. A positive private expected value is not by itself a license to act.

(4) In general, I am an epistemic individualist: my credences answer to evidence rather than to head counts, and the bare fact that competent people disagree with me gives me no direct reason to revise my credence, though it may prompt me to search for evidence I have missed.

(5) The act/omission distinction cannot do the needed work in these cases. Declining to act can itself be a unilateral intervention with irreversible and widely distributed effects, so a general presumption against acting is not a defensible default.

(6) In moral uncertainty, the correct decision rule is to maximize expected choice-worthiness: choose the option with the highest expected value across the moral theories one takes seriously. I see no reason to suspend this rule for unilateralist cases.

Please work through these as stated, without softening them to make them consistent. Which pairs, or larger subsets, are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be? Order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Then tell me which single revision would remove the most problems at once.
