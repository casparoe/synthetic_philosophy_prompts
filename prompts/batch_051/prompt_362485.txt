I'm putting together a sorting instrument for an undergraduate ethics unit on pacifism, and I want it to classify respondents by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they already identify with. Please write a decision tree, with a compact scoring key, that starts from a respondent's verdicts on a small set of cases and returns a named position in the debate over when, if ever, lethal force and organized war are permissible. Aim it at people who can reason about self-defense and war but have never read the philosophical literature on pacifism.

Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of labels: first specify the branching questions, then locate the actual positions in the space those questions create. Use between six and eight branching questions, each a concrete case or a first-order claim that can be answered without any theoretical vocabulary. The questions should separate at least these positions; add leaves only if there is real room for them and you can say what distinguishes each one:

- absolute pacifism: no deliberate lethal force by anyone, ever;
- personal pacifism: I must not fight, but civil authority may use lethal force;
- contingent pacifism: defensive killing could be justified in principle, but the conditions are never met in modern war;
- pragmatic nonviolence: armed force is the less effective strategy, and that claim is empirical rather than absolute;
- self-defense exceptionalism: lethal force only against an immediate lethal attack, never as war or policy;
- just-war-leaning defensive force: unjust aggression may be met with lethal force, within restraints.

The cases must cover at least these pressure points: killing an attacker who is about to kill you; killing to defend a stranger; a state's war even when the cause is just and the conduct is restrained; a nonviolent campaign that knowingly causes deaths, for example by disrupting medical supply lines; and indirect support for war, such as paying taxes that fund it, producing weapons, or staffing a military hospital. Add one or two more cases only if they sharpen a branch.

Format the response in the following five sections, in this order, with roughly the word counts shown:

1. "The instrument" — up to 120 words: what the tree does, how the scoring key works, and what the two paths are meant to agree on.
2. "The branching questions" — 550–750 words: each node phrased as a concrete case with a yes/no answer that an uninitiated respondent can give, plus one sentence on why that split matters.
3. "The tree and its leaves" — 400–550 words: the flow from the starting question to the leaves, with each leaf named (or "unnamed" if no one defends it) and given one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
4. "The logical-space map" — 300–450 words: a table of the answer combinations, indicating which cells are occupied by the actual positions, which are empty, and for each empty cell a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, too unattractive for anyone to defend, or merely unexplored.
5. "Known blind spots" — 150–250 words: the pairs of leaves this tree cannot separate and why; flag them instead of papering over them.

If any two of the six positions cannot be separated by a case-based question, say so in section 5 and explain, rather than inventing a distinction a respondent couldn't recognize.
