I'm putting together a political theory unit on Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry, and I want to screen 12 Angry Men next week. Read the jury deliberation through Dewey's idea that democracy isn't mainly a voting procedure but an experimental method for forming and correcting shared judgments under uncertainty: you treat a position as a hypothesis, test it against the evidence, revise, and what you get is warranted assertibility rather than certainty. I want both directions: the places where the jury room actually works like a Deweyan community of inquiry (the early straw poll, the replaying of the old man's walk, the switchblade experiment, the way the holdout keeps reopening the case), and the places where the film quietly resists the picture — the lone dissenter is a pretty undemocratic hero, the verdict is acquittal by doubt rather than a settled positive account of what happened, unanimity looks like forced consensus, and a criminal jury is reconstructing a one-off event instead of testing policies by their consequences. Does the film know the difference between reasonable doubt and warranted assertibility, or does it blur the two into one mood? I dont want my students to come away thinking Dewey is just groupthink done politely, so be honest about the mismatch. Structure it as: 'The lens' (~150 words, the Dewey view I'll lean on), 'The film as inquiry' (~400 words, close scene-level reading), 'Where the film resists the reading' (~300 words), and 'What the film adds for my students' (~150 words).
