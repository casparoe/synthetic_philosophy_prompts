For the past several years I have followed a deliberate rule about how much to discount my conclusions in light of facts about myself, and I want to know whether it is rationally defensible or an overcorrection wearing the costume of a principle. The rule is this: once I have finished an episode of first-order reasoning and arrived at a credence, a fact about me — fatigue, mood, a known bias in the domain, my historical error rate on similar problems, my blood sugar, whether I was hungry — may move that credence only if I can identify the specific premise, inferential step, or weighting that the fact should change. If I cannot point to the flaw, I treat the fact as already priced in and leave the credence untouched. The rule applies even when I learn the fact about myself only after I have reached the conclusion.

An example from last month. I spent the late evening analyzing whether our organization should accept a proposal and concluded with 0.85 confidence that it should. The next morning I learned that my blood sugar had been very low throughout the analysis. Under my rule I did not lower the 0.85. My reasoning was that the low blood sugar either produced an identifiable error, in which case I should correct that specific error, or it produced none, in which case there was nothing to revise. My partner says this is a refusal to update on higher-order evidence: the fact about my condition is evidence that some error has occurred even when I cannot locate it, and a rule that forbids me from moving until I can locate the error makes my conclusions unresponsive to exactly the information that predicts error.

I adopted the rule because I kept double-counting. When I knew in advance that conditions were poor, I would often reason more conservatively and then, on top of that, lower my final confidence because conditions were poor, taxing the conclusion twice. The rule was meant to eliminate that second tax. But I have started to suspect that it might instead eliminate legitimate updates, and that it may commit me to a form of epistemic akrasia: retaining a credence even though my total evidence — which now includes higher-order evidence that the process behind it may have misfired — supports a different one.

I would like an honest audit of the rule, not a defense of it.

1. Is the rule defensible as stated, or is it an overcorrection for the double-counting problem?
2. Can the distinction between being able to identify a specific error and merely having evidence that some error exists bear the weight the rule places on it? When is each kind of evidence rationally relevant to revising a concluded credence?
3. Under what conditions would the rule coincide with sound updating, and under what conditions would it diverge from it?
4. Is there a revised version of the rule that keeps the protection against double-counting without making my conclusions unrevisable on the basis of facts about my own reliability?
5. Is the state the rule leaves me in best described as epistemically akratic, and does that description settle anything about whether the rule is defensible?

Concrete cases are welcome if they make the points precise, but the central question is whether this is a defensible division of labor between first-order and higher-order evidence.
