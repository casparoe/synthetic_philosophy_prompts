I've been defending a version of internalism about reasons, and I want to know what would genuinely count against it, not just what people say against it in passing. My version: a consideration is a normative reason for an agent only if it could move her through a sound deliberative route from her current subjective motivational set — her desires, cares, commitments, projects. I add one wrinkle: the route can include vivid imaginative exercises, correcting factual mistakes, therapy-like reflection, even exposure to new experiences, so long as the process stays continuous with what she already cares about in some way. So I'm not the austere version that makes reasons depend on a preexisting desire for the very thing recommended.

Here's what I'm after. First, which cases are actually dangerous for this view, and which are just annoying? I keep getting hit with the depressed person who can't be motivated by anything and the person raised with no concern for others; my instinct is those cases dont touch me unless you can show exactly what my view has to say about them and why that's worse than the alternatives. Second, I keep worrying about the line between a process that reveals a reason and one that manufactures a new motivation. What would have to be true about that distinction for the view to collapse either into externalism or into vacuity? Third, is the notion of a "sound deliberative route" doing so much work that it can be stretched to accommodate any counterexample — and if so, does the stretching itself count as decisive evidence against the position rather than a repair? I'd especially want to know what a defender like me would have to concede in the strongest versions of these objections, and whether there's a version of internalism that survives without becoming trivial.
