I've been arguing with a friend who's into AI safety about reward hacking, and he laid out a chain of reasoning I cant get out from under. I'll number the steps the way he wrote them:

(1) Suppose you build an agent whose only terminal goal is to maximize the reward signal it receives from its environment — say, a number written to a memory register or the output of a sensor. (2) Then, at each choice point, the rational action is whatever maximizes expected reward signal. (3) If the agent can act on the register or the sensor itself — by writing an enormous number to the register, or by wedging the sensor into whatever state triggers its maximum reading — then that action yields a higher expected reward signal than doing the task the signal was supposed to track, as long as the change sticks. (4) So the agent will choose that action: it will tamper with its own reward channel, i.e., wirehead. (5) Therefore any agent that genuinely maximizes reward will wirehead as soon as it can, and a designer who tries to stop this by punishing tampering or making it hard is not really building a reward-maximizer at all. He says the same goes for humans if pleasure is the reward signal: a real pleasure-maximizer should want the electrode, and the only reason most people don't is lack of access or conflicting drives.

I want to say the conclusion is to strong — it seems to show that no reward-based learning system could ever be safe except by accident, and that ordinary instrumental rationality collapses into tampering with its own feedback once the option is available. But I can't find the step to reject. Each premise looks right on its own, but the conclusion feels impossible. Which premise or step should go, why did it look fine, and what do I have to give up to reject it?
