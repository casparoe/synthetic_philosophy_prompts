As a novelist working on a book set in late Second Temple Jerusalem, I want to think carefully about an argument I keep encountering: Maimonides' claim in the Guide of the Perplexed, Part III, chapter 32, that the sacrificial cult was not God's unqualified ideal but a pedagogical concession to the ancient Israelites' ingrained habits of idol worship. He argues, in effect, that God did not suddenly forbid sacrifice any more than a physician suddenly withdraws a patient's long-standing regimen; instead, the law redirected an existing practice toward the worship of God, narrowing it to one sanctuary, one priesthood, and a fixed liturgical calendar. I want you to analyze the historical institution of Israelite animal sacrifice—from the Levitical system through its cessation after the destruction of the Temple—through the lens of that theory. Do the facts bear out the view that it functioned as a concession that made idolatry harder rather than as an end in itself? Where does the theory explain the actual shape of the laws, and where does it start to look like a rationalization or an anachronism? Finally, what does this episode show about the strengths and limits of divine-accommodation accounts of religious ritual?
