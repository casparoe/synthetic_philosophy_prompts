He took this to support a distinction between risk, where probabilities can be measured and the relevant outcomes are well defined, and uncertainty, where they cannot. Frank Knight, in Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, which appeared the same year, drew essentially the same distinction, which is why Keynes and Knight are usually discussed together. In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money Keynes applies this distinction to long-term investment: the prospective yield of a capital asset over its whole life depends on so many future conditions that there is no scientific basis for calculating it, so the investor faces uncertainty rather than risk. Faced with this, investors fall back on convention, projecting the present into the future, and on animal spirits, by which Keynes meant waves of irrational optimism and pessimism rather than the result of rational calculation. Keynes's earlier idea of the weight of argument is relevant here: it is his name for the degree of confidence a rational person is entitled to feel in a probability judgement, and it explains why an investor can be less confident about a judgement about the distant future than about a calculable chance such as a spin of a roulette wheel even when the two carry the same probability.
