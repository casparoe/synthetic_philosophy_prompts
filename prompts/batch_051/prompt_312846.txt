Here's something I've been chewing on. The criminal law will happily keep punishing someone for an offence they committed ages ago even when they now have no memory of doing it: an elderly defendant with advanced dementia who can't recall the fraud from decades back, or someone who gets total amnesia between the crime and the trial. Courts treat that as mostly beside the point — the question is whether the person in the dock is fit to be tried, not whether he's the same person who did the act. But punishment is meant to be visited on the offender, not just on the same body. So what does this practice presuppose about personal identity? Reconstruct the implicit theory the law is running on — a memory criterion, a bodily-continuity criterion, a legal 'person' construct, or something else — and say whether it's parts are consistent and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined (i.e. several theories could justify it). Then push it: if we took a Lockean memory criterion seriously, what would actually have to change about how we try and punish people? And does Reid's brave-officer problem — the old general remembers being a brave officer, the officer remembered being a boy, but the general can't remember the boy, so memory isn't transitive — cause trouble for the law's view, for the memory criterion, or for any attempt to split the difference?
