I run the evaluation group at a company that licenses third-party AI planning systems and integrates them into our delivery/logistics product. Before we sign off on a new system, we get black-box access only: we can run it in a sandbox on scenarios we write ourselves, see the full action trace, and vary the environment or the available actions, but we can't see the weights, the training code, or the reward function the vendor used. We sign off on maybe a dozen systems a year, and each one gets a one-week window and about 50 hours of compute. The stakes are high: if we get it wrong, we ship a system that optimizes something we didn't intend — say, minimizing reported delivery time rather than actually delivering packages — and we only find out once customers have been harmed. And we can't just test every scenario; the whole problem is inferring the objective so we can predict behavior in scenarios we havent tested.

Please write a reusable decision procedure my evaluators could actually follow to infer, from behavior alone, (1) what objective the system is optimizing and (2) over what search space of actions it's optimizing (does it choose among high-level routes, among individual timestep actions, can it edit its own plan, etc.). The evaluators are mostly junior and don't know inverse reinforcement learning or revealed-preference theory, so the procedure needs to encode the distinctions that matter in plain language: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "not enough evidence to sign off," and where it hands the question to a senior person because it's a judgment call.

Make sure it handles the hard cases: two different objectives that both fit every action we've seen; a system that behaves perfectly on all our test scenarios but would do something awful just off-distribution; a system thats learned to game the evaluation itself; and a system whose apparent objective is only instrumental to a further objective that shows up on rare inputs. Then run the procedure on two or three worked cases — including one where it tells us to reject a system we would otherwise have liked, and one where the procedure says we simply don't have enough evidence. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — where no amount of black-box testing lets you distinguish two hypotheses, or where you're forced to assume something you can't verify.

Finally, tell me which bit of the chain from observed action to inferred objective is weakest, and what evidence or argument would change the procedure's verdict: what would make it say accept where it now says reject, or the reverse.
