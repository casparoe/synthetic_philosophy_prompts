I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether driving is worth the risk. He kept saying that the chance of dying on any given car trip is so small that refusing to drive would be irrational, while I kept saying that small risks do not remain small when you take them twice a day for forty years, and that people are wildly inconsistent about which small risks they accept. We never escaped the standoff. I teach a first-year college course called Risk and Everyday Life, with twenty students and a ninety-minute session. Design a classroom activity that makes students feel the structure of that disagreement before I name it.

The activity should be a runnable game or simulation, not a lecture. It should put students in the position of making routine transport choices and occasional one-off risk choices under constraints, using the micromort framework associated with Ronald A. Howard: a micromort representing a one-in-a-million probability of death. You may provide simplified illustrative values for different activities, but state them as assumptions for the exercise rather than settled data.

The students should come away seeing three things: first, how a per-trip risk small enough to ignore becomes substantial when repeated across a population and a lifetime; second, that people often accept a large cumulative risk when it is divided into tiny, familiar portions while refusing a single unfamiliar event with the same expected cost; and third, that the question whether driving is worth it cannot be answered by the number alone, because it turns on what the alternatives cost in time, money, convenience, and other values.

Give the activity clear roles or groups, rules, incentives, a timeline, and materials. The idea should emerge from what students do—from the ledger they fill out, the trades they make, the deaths that occur in the simulation, or the choices they defend—before it is named. Include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three takeaways, predicts what will go wrong when the activity is run, and is honest about which feature of the real-world disagreement the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.
