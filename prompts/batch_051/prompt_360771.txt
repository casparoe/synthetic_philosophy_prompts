I run the clinical policy committee for a medium-sized health plan, and every quarter we update the list of conditions that qualify a patient for our chronic-disease management program. It matters a lot: being on the list means a care coordinator, medication reminders, and extra coverage for certain services. We get petitions from patient groups for things like fibromyalgia, opioid-use disorder, age-related hearing loss, metabolic syndrome, 'internet gaming disorder,' and last quater someone wanted 'loneliness' added. My committee is not a philosophy seminar, but we've been going back and forth on what actually makes something a disease, and two of my doctors keep talking past each other: one says a condition is a disease only if a bodily mechanism is malfunctioning relative to how it's supposed to work; the other says that's not enough, because it also has to be bad for the person — otherwise we'd call benign anatomical variations or harmless quirks diseases, and we'd pathologize people who are just statistically unusual. Meanwhile the 'functioning' doctor says the harm view is hopeless because 'bad for you' depends on values and social context and would make everything political.

I don't need more debates. I need a procedure our nurse reviewers can actually use, with modest training, to go through each petition and come out with a recommendation to include or exclude. It should be a checklist or decision tree, in order, with the points where the two frameworks genuine force different answers flagged, and the irreducible judgment calls marked as such. Then apply it to (a) obesity, (b) adult ADHD, and (c) short stature in an otherwise healthy kid, and walk through the logic of each. Please include at least one worked case where the procedure tells us to exclude something my committee would otherwise rubber-stamp, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle.
