Consider the standard money-pump argument against intransitive preferences. An agent strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and is willing to pay a small amount at each step to move to a preferred option. Offered a suitable sequence of exchanges, she returns to her original holding after paying three small fees. The argument concludes that the preference cycle itself is irrational, because the cycle makes the agent exploitable no matter where she begins. Now alter one assumption: the agent is not a myopic chooser who decides each exchange as it is offered, but a temporally sophisticated planner who sees the whole sequence in advance, recognizes that it is a round trip, and adopts a policy of refusing the first trade. Does the money-pump argument survive this change? Identify the premise of the original argument that is put under pressure, then either state a repaired version that still condemns the preference cycle or explain why the argument can no longer reach that conclusion. In particular, say whether any residual defect lies in the intransitive preferences themselves or in a pattern of sequential choices.
