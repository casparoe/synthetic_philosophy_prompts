In my seminar on business ethics and social ontology, we spent the last session on whether a corporation can itself be blameworthy, as opposed to merely blaming the individual employees, executives, and shareholders who act for it or benefit from it. Two classmates continued the argument after class, and while I took notes, I could not referee the exchange as carefully as I would like. I would appreciate a move-by-move adjudication of what was actually said.

(1) Daniel opened by arguing that only individual persons can be blameworthy. Blame, he said, requires the capacity to form intentions, to grasp that an action is wrong, and to feel remorse, and a corporation is a legal fiction with no mind of its own. Saying "the company is to blame" is shorthand for saying that some identifiable people inside it are to blame. If we pretend otherwise, he argued, we let culpable managers disappear behind the org chart while the punishment lands on shareholders and low-level employees who made no relevant choice.

(2) Mara replied that this misdescribes what a corporation is. A firm is not just a set of people; it includes decision procedures, lines of authority, incentive structures, and compliance systems that persist as individuals rotate in and out. Through those structures, she said, a company can act on an intention that no single person formed. A defect can be kept quiet by distributed routines in which each employee's small contribution is individually unremarkable while the organization's overall pattern is deliberate. If blame could attach only to individuals, she argued, some of the most serious corporate wrongs would become unblameable, because no one person ever formed the whole intention.

(3) Daniel answered that all of this can be paraphrased away. The procedures were designed and operated by persons, and they merely aggregate individual actions. Saying that the corporation intended the outcome, he said, is like saying "the market panicked": a useful summary, not a report about a mind. Blame, unlike causal explanation, requires reactive attitudes such as resentment and indignation, and those can be held only toward a subject capable of feeling them. One can resent a person, but resenting a corporation is a figure of speech.

(4) Mara replied that this standard proves too much, since it would also rule out blaming governments, committees, universities, or other collectives that we routinely hold responsible in ways not aimed at any one member. The reactive-attitude point, she added, is overstated: public resentment of a company's safety record is not secretly aimed at some particular employee, but at the organization as something that can acknowledge criticism, change policy, and offer redress. If a corporate decision structure can act intentionally and respond to reasons, she argued, it has the features that matter for blame; insisting on a biological mind is a prejudice, not an argument.

(5) Daniel shifted to a different line: even if we grant the decision-structure story, corporate agency is epiphenomenal. A company's choices are largely an equilibrium produced by market and profit incentives, and putting a different group of people into the same structure would produce much the same behavior. So blaming the corporation adds nothing over blaming the incentives or the individuals who set them, and talk of a "corporate agent" is explanatory wheel-spinning.

(6) Mara said this last point concedes more than Daniel realizes. It treats "the same structure" as a real locus of explanation, and it forces him to identify who set the incentives, which will often be a board, a committee, or a management team rather than any single person. What still needs argument, she concluded, is why an entity that acts, persists over time, and revises its conduct in response to reasons should be exempt from blame merely because it lacks a private inner life.

I honestly could not tell which side was winning at each step. Please adjudicate move by move: assess what is right and what is questionable in each numbered move, then give an overall verdict on who is more right, and say whether the frame of the debate is missing anything important.
