Consider the following argument, which I have been developing for a paper. It is meant to establish that the endowment effect, and the loss aversion usually thought to underlie it, are not rational defects but legitimate expressions of the fact that we properly value the particular things that are already ours. The problem is that the argument appears to prove far more than I want, and I cannot locate the step at which the overreach enters.

(1) A preference for the particular actual things in one's life over qualitatively identical substitutes is not generally a bias. I may reasonably refuse an exchange of my actual hand for a functionally equivalent transplant, or an exchange of my actual child for a replacement child indistinguishable in every observable respect. We would find something wrong with someone who was perfectly indifferent between these options.

(2) The natural explanation of these acceptable preferences is that the actual tokens — this hand, this child — are embedded in my history, relationships, and projects in a way that the substitutes would not be.

(3) Now consider the standard endowment effect. A subject is randomly given an ordinary mug or pen; afterwards she demands substantially more to give it up than she would pay to acquire an identical one. The effect survives controls for transaction costs, income effects, and information differences, which suggests that it is a direct manifestation of loss aversion.

(4) If the asymmetry in (3) is a rational defect while the asymmetry in (1) is not, then there must be some difference between the two cases that carries the normative weight — something that makes valuing the incidental mug irrational while valuing the actual hand or child is rational.

(5) The only candidate differences are differences of degree: how long the item has been yours, how deeply it figures in your plans, how strong your attachment to it is. But no principled cutoff appears along this continuum. The mug handed to you minutes ago and the hand you have lived with for decades differ in depth of integration, yet there is no point on that continuum at which an attachment flips from irrational to rational. A cheap pen used for a decade may reasonably be valued above an identical new pen, even though it began as a fungible commodity.

(6) So no principled difference separates the cases, and the randomly assigned mug is valued as legitimately as the hand or the child.

(7) But by the same reasoning, no status quo preference can be written off as a bias. Any preference for what one already has is legitimate, however arbitrary and however harmful; the exploited employee's attachment to the familiar job, the patient's refusal of a beneficial change because the current state is his own, and the sheer fact of prior possession as a reason to keep anything would all be licensed.

That conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I take it the argument therefore proves too much. Either some step is defective, or I must accept (7). I would most like to hold onto (1) while continuing to reject (7). Which step should I reject or repair, and what exactly is the cost of holding that combination?
