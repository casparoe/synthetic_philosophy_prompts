In my biology class, I was taught a picture of health and disease that I found very compelling. On this picture, health is simply the absence of disease, and disease is an objective departure from statistically typical functioning for members of the human species: a part or process is diseased when it falls outside the normal range and impairs the organism's ability to survive and reproduce. I later learned that this view is called naturalism, and its attractions are real. It offers a tidy, value-free procedure—measure the function, compare it with the species norm, and the answer follows. It keeps moral judgments out of medicine, since whether someone is sick becomes an empirical question rather than a contest of competing values. And it draws a clean distinction between being diseased and being merely different, disabled by an unaccommodating environment, or socially disadvantaged.

But I cannot shake the intuition that the concept of disease is not that clean. Homosexuality was once classified as a psychiatric disorder and then removed, even though the underlying biology had not changed; that looks like a change in social values rather than a discovery about normal function. In mental health, deciding how much sadness, restlessness, or inattention counts as a disorder seems to require a background view of what kind of life a person should be able to lead, which a statistical norm does not supply. Even the choice of which population supplies the norm seems to matter: compare a person's blood sugar or hearing to all humans, and the classification may go one way; compare it to a narrower subgroup, and it may go another, with nothing in biology alone fixing the right comparison class. Some members of the Deaf community argue that deafness is better understood as a way of being human than as a defect, which makes me doubt that harm and normal function are as value-free as naturalism claims.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I would like you to present the strongest version of naturalism and the strongest version of my intuition, which I gather is close to what philosophers call normativism, and then offer a negotiated verdict. Should I debunk and drop the intuition—and if so, how should a naturalist explain the examples above? Should I amend or abandon naturalism by adding a value component? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible, for instance because biological facts fix the boundaries only after we select value-laden goals and reference classes? Please say what would justify each of these moves rather than simply choose among them. Finally, please tell me which consideration would most likely change your verdict and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
