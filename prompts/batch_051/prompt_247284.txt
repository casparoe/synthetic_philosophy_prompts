I have been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial service for my aunt, a physical therapist who spent nearly forty years helping people relearn how to walk after strokes, stand after spinal injuries, and grip a fork again after nerve damage. She had no philosophical training, but during her last decade a small circle of researchers working on Merleau-Ponty and embodied cognition adopted her as a kind of exemplary figure: they came to see her clinic as a living argument for motor intentionality — the way the lived body grasps situations as invitations to movement before anything is represented or calculated. A staircase solicits climbing, a cup solicits a certain grip, and a practiced gait is not a sequence of positions steered by the mind but a bodily style of inhabiting the world.

The room will be split between those researchers, who will catch every mistake, and her family and former patients, who will not tolerate being lectured. The researchers will wince if I describe the body as a tool driven by a mind or reduce skill to internal representation plus computation; at least one relative will bristle at anything that sounds like mysticism about bodies; the patients will remember the woman who coaxed a paralyzed hand back to life and will want grief, not jargon.

Please write the eulogy itself, in my voice, about five minutes spoken, and let motor intentionality do the real structural work: it should decide which memories are tellable, how her patients' recoveries are described, and what the speech refuses to say about the body. The speech should not pause to define the concept or to explain Merleau-Ponty; it should perform the idea by moving the way she taught. One further constraint: during her final year her own movement became effortful and unreliable, so the eulogy cannot sentimentalize the lived body as an invincible source of grace — it must leave room for the thought that the very powers she helped others recover can themselves be lost, without dissolving that loss into a consoling platitude.
