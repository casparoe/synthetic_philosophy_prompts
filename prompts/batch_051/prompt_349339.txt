I'm in an upper-level seminar on causation and scientific explanation, and our instructor has a way of ending arguments by repeating the slogan "No causation without manipulation." It came up most recently when I asked whether the Moon's gravitational pull causes the ocean tides, and whether the asteroid impact at the end of the Cretaceous caused the mass extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs. He answered, "No causation without manipulation," and moved on, as though the slogan had settled both questions. That bothers me, because in the same course we have read Woodward on interventions, invariance, and modularity, and Woodward seems to allow causal claims about systems we could never actually manipulate — his interventions are idealized, not human feats. So I suspect the slogan is doing several quite different jobs at once.

I would like you to lay out the distinct precise principles that "No causation without manipulation" could be used to assert. At least consider these readings: actual manipulation by human agents; the in-principle possibility of an idealized intervention; the existence of an invariant relationship between variables that could in principle be exploited for control; and a purely epistemological rule about when we are entitled to assert a causal claim. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it incurs the circularity worry that arises for Woodward's account because "intervention" and "manipulation" look like causal notions themselves. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license my instructor's dismissal of the tides and mass-extinction cases, which reading sits best with Woodward's actual interventionist view, and which one someone in his position most likely needs if the slogan is to do the work he is using it to do.
