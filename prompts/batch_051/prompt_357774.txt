I am a doctoral candidate working at the intersection of philosophy of action and behavioural economics, and I keep going back and forth on whether weakness of will should be treated as a distinctive failure of agency or as a redescription of time-inconsistent choice. I would like you to write a dialogue that forces the issue as sharply as possible.

Please write a dialogue between two equally able interlocutors, a philosopher and a behavioural economist, who defend opposing views. The philosopher maintains that akrasia is genuine and irreducible: at the moment of action, an agent can judge, all things considered, that she should do X, yet do Y anyway while still holding the judgement that X is what she should do, and this is not merely the expression of a different preference. The economist maintains that the same material is more parsimoniously explained by hyperbolic or quasi-hyperbolic discounting: outcomes lose subjective value steeply at short delays and more gently at long delays, so preferences reverse as temptation becomes imminent. On the economist's view, what the philosopher calls 'the agent's better judgement' is the judgement of an earlier time-slice, and what looks like weakness of will is preference reversal across temporally distinct selves.

The exchange should be grounded in at least one worked example, such as a person who sincerely resolves on Sunday not to drink at the conference dinner, and then, with the wine in front of her on Tuesday, drinks while simultaneously thinking that this is a bad idea. Both characters should test their account against this example rather than merely invoking it.

The dialogue must be a genuine back-and-forth, not two alternating position statements. Each character should understand the other's framework well enough to criticise it from the inside. The philosopher should ask, for instance, why any particular temporal perspective is authoritative, and whether the economist is not covertly treating the earlier self as the 'real' one when the demand for commitment devices is taken as evidence of what the agent truly wants. The economist should press the philosopher to explain why akratic choice is so sensitive to delay, immediacy and precommitment—exactly the signature of discounting—if a single unified judgement is running the show. Each side should concede at least one strong point made by the other and then attempt to answer it.

Conclude at a point of unresolved but precisely stated disagreement, with each character saying what evidence or argument would most shift their view. Please write the dialogue in British English.
