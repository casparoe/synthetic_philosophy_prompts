I help run a monthly civics discussion group at a public library — around eighteen adults, mostly non-specialists — and our sessions run ninety minutes. For some time I have been turning over a problem I do not have a clean name for. Every organized group steers itself with rules: a club has bylaws, a board has procedures, a country has a constitution, and those rules tell us who may decide what, and by what procedure, when something is contested. But sometimes the rules themselves run out. The bylaws replace a resigning officer by pointing to another officer whose position no longer exists; two clauses of a constitution each say the other one governs; an emergency can only be declared by a body that can only be convened by the person the emergency concerns. And yet someone still acts — meetings are held, decisions are made — and it becomes hard to tell whether that person is following the rules, breaking them, or quietly making a new rule while claiming only to apply the old one.

Could you design a ninety-minute activity for my group that makes participants live inside this problem rather than hear a lecture about it? I imagine them running a small assembly under a deliberately flawed one-page charter: they govern themselves comfortably for a while, then hit a gap or a self-contradiction in their own procedure, and then must choose how to go on. The discovery should be theirs, not something I announce. I want them to feel that whatever procedure they now reach for is itself unauthorized, that checking the charter does not settle who is entitled to interpret the charter, and that making a decision legitimate is different from finding that the decision was already in the rules. It need not be a point-scoring game; a structured role-play or live simulation would be just as good, so long as they have to act. Please give me the full setup — what the charter should say or what fault it should contain, the scenario that triggers the gap, roles and materials, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto those distinctions.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I can already picture participants asking me to settle it, or papering over the gap by loud agreement — and how I should respond, and be honest about which part of the real problem, with its actual stakes, deadlines, and possibility of coercion, a library meeting cannot reproduce.
