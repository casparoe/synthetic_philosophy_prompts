I'm leading a small church discussion group on whether a translated text can be inspired, and one member wrote a short response to a question I gave. I'm not academically trained in any of this and dont want to lead people wrong, so I'd like you to grade the response for me. Please take on the role of a philosophy professor who has taught on the philosophy of religion, especially questions about Scripture and translation. Work through the answer carefully: first brainstorm the distinct claims its making, evaluate each one, flag anything that's subtly off or doesn't follow, and then assign a letter grade with point-by-point comments explaining the grade.

Here's the question I gave:

"We've been discussing whether inspiration attaches only to the original-language texts. But the New Testament writers quote the Septuagint, a Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, as Scripture, and the early church used it as its Old Testament for centuries. Does that show that a translated text can be inspired in the full sense? And if it does, doesn't that give some support to KJV-onlyism—the view that the King James Version is the inspired, perfect Bible for English speakers?"

Here's the member's answer:

"Yes, I think it shows a translation can be inspired in the full sense. When Paul and the other New Testament writers quote the Septuagint as Scripture, even where its Greek wording departs a bit from the Hebrew text we now have, they are treating the Greek wording itself as divinely authoritative. And if the Greek wording is divinely authoritative, then the translators must have been inspired in the same way the original prophets were; otherwise the differences they introduced would just be errors. The old story that the seventy translators worked independently and all produced identical wording fits this: the early church accepted the Septuagint because the story showed the translation was supernaturally guided. But I dont think this supports KJV-onlyism. The Septuagint was received by the whole early church over centuries, whereas KJV-onlyism is a recent and much smaller movement. Also, the King James was produced by a committee using printed Greek texts rather than original manuscripts, so it cannot claim the same kind of miraculous production. So I'd say a translation can be authoritative for its community only if the translation process itself was supernaturally guided; if it wasn't, then 'inspired' has to be reserved for the original-language texts."

Please give the grade and the point-by-point comments.
