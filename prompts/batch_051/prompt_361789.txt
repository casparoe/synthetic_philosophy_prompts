I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long-form feature on the contemporary rehabilitation of compassion in moral psychology, clinical practice, and even public policy. In the course of researching the piece, I keep running into an older philosophical quarrel that seems to me more than merely historical: Schopenhauer treats compassion, in the section of On the Basis of Morality where he argues for it as the sole genuine moral incentive, as the immediate, unselfish participation in another's suffering — he goes so far as to claim there that boundless compassion for all living beings is the surest guarantee of morally decent conduct. Nietzsche, by contrast, describes pity in Daybreak and elsewhere as something that deepens the suffering it touches, strips the sufferer of dignity, and spreads a kind of depressive contagion. I can see that they are not simply using the same word and disagreeing about its value; the disagreement seems to track a deeper split about what suffering is, what it is to respect a person, and what morality is even for. But I am having trouble stating precisely what that split is, and how much of the quarrel survives once you distinguish the several things both call pity or compassion — for example, the acute pang at another's pain, the disposition to help, the feeling of shared vulnerability, and the subtle condescension that sometimes attends the charitable gaze. Could you explain the real conceptual issue at stake here, and in particular, how much of Nietzsche's critique can be accepted without abandoning Schopenhauer's core claim? I want something I can actually use to frame the empirical findings on empathic distress and compassion fatigue, not a historical survey.
