I'm in a course on infinite regress arguments, and I've been trying to formulate a single test for when a regress is vicious rather than benign. I keep coming back to what I've started calling the "conserved-load criterion." Here is the idea.

Take any regress in which each item is introduced because the previous item was supposed to do some work — justify, explain, ground, instantiate, or otherwise support it. In a vicious regress, the load is conserved: the nth item does that work only if the (n+1)th item does the same kind of work for it, so the original demand is never discharged at any finite point in the series. The homunculus regress seems to fit: the inner person's seeing is introduced to explain my seeing, but the inner person's seeing is exactly the phenomenon that needed explaining, so the explanatory burden is passed along undiminished. The familiar regress of justification also fits when every justifying belief must itself be justified by a further belief: each step reopens the same demand.

In a benign regress, by contrast, either the load is spent — each step discharges the demand it was called in to answer and merely opens a new demand of a different kind — or the load was never really pending, because the further items are entailed by, or supervene on, something already secured rather than being required for it. The regress from a truth to a truth of a truth looks benign for this reason: the further sentence does not make the earlier one true; it simply registers something already settled.

My difficulty is the infinite causal chain. If every event has a cause, the regress of causes appears to conserve the load: what caused C is answered by an earlier cause, which then raises what caused that, in the same sense. Yet many treatments of the causal regress seem not to locate the problem in the mere conservation of the load; the regress is condemned, if at all, on further grounds such as the impossibility of an actually infinite series or the need for a first cause. So I cannot tell whether my criterion wrongly condemns the causal regress, or whether I should hold that in the causal case the load is actually spent — the effect's being caused is fully secured by its proximate cause, and the open question about the cause's cause is a new demand rather than a deferred one.

Can you assess this for me? I mainly want to know whether there is a stable kernel underneath the homemade term, whether the underlying distinction already exists in the literature under a better name, and where the criterion breaks. Give me the strongest objections, especially cases where a regress looks vicious but my criterion calls it benign, or looks benign but my criterion calls it vicious, and tell me whether the causal case is a fatal counterexample or something the criterion can absorb.
