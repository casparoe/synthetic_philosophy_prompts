I run the training-data and feedback pipeline at a mid-sized AI lab, and I am trying to standardise how we choose between process-based and outcome-based supervision when we train our language models. By process-based supervision I mean feedback that scores or critiques each intermediate step a model takes (each line of a derivation, each step of a plan, each edit), and by outcome-based supervision I mean feedback that scores only the final result (the answer, the compiled program, the completed draft). We face this choice for each major training run, roughly four times a year. Each run can consume enormous compute and large quantities of human- or model-generated feedback, and the wrong choice tends to show up late: a model learns to game a weak final metric, or we discover that the output looks right but the reasoning behind it does not generalise. At present the choice is made project by project, and our records show a costly pattern: projects lean on outcome supervision because it looks cheaper, then later pay for process feedback after a model has learned to exploit the final signal.

I would like a one-page decision procedure that a project lead can apply in a single working day, with no special background in reinforcement learning theory, before the proposal goes to the head of alignment and the product owner for sign-off. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and directs us towards process-based or outcome-based supervision, and where judgement cannot be eliminated and a human must make the call. Ground it in whatever is actually known about the trade-off: the density of the learning signal, credit assignment, reward gaming, and the assumptions each kind of supervision makes about the task. Keep in mind that process feedback can sometimes be produced cheaply through automated checkers, rubrics, or model critiques, while final outcomes can themselves be hard to verify; the procedure should be sensitive to that rather than treating process as always expensive and outcome as always cheap. Then run the procedure, step by step, on three proposals: (1) a model that answers multi-step mathematics problems; (2) a model that writes and iteratively revises code against a specification and a test suite; and (3) a model that conducts extended open-ended planning dialogues with a user, where there is no single correct end state. I expect the procedure to produce at least one answer that makes us uncomfortable, and I want you to say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
