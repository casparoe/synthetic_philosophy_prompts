I have been arguing with a friend who designs type, and the argument has pushed me toward a small theory I would like assessed honestly rather than sympathetically. She insists that the counter — the enclosed white space inside a letter — is the real carrier of letter identity, and that everything else is decoration. I think that is too simple. The account I have been building, which I call the skeleton-and-tolerance theory, says that a mark is an 'a' neither by overall resemblance to a prototype nor by a fixed set of necessary and sufficient conditions, but by satisfying a ranked set of constraints. At the top is a skeleton: a coarse stroke topology that records which strokes close into counters, which remain open as apertures, which pass through one another at junctions, and how those forms are placed relative to one another. Next come load-bearing measurements, each with its own tolerance range — whether an enclosed form sits at x-height or rises above it, how tightly a bowl is closed, whether a stem runs straight up or folds back on itself. Below all of that is a free zone of decorative parameters: stroke contrast, serifs, terminals, slope within limits, the precise shape of an arch. On this picture, a typeface is simply a consistent choice of values from the free zone, and style just is variation confined to that zone.

The theory also predicts two ways identity can fail. A letter can be pushed out of its category when a load-bearing parameter crosses a tolerance boundary — the bowl opens and an 'a' slides toward a 'c'-like form — and a mark can sit in the overlap of two skeletons, which is where I would place genuinely ambiguous forms like minimal 'd'/'a' or 'q'/'g' confusions. I am also tempted by a stronger claim: this is why we classify never-before-seen letterforms instantly, and why a slow morph between two letters passes through a region where the form is undecidable even though every small step seems to preserve identity. I already know where my view strains: a single-storey and a double-storey 'a' do not obviously share one skeleton, and my fix — allowing each letter a small licensed repertoire of skeleton variants — feels as though it might be smuggling prototype theory back in. I would like your honest assessment. Is the skeleton doing real work or restating the data? Does the account survive blackletter forms, the fact that the same mark can be a letter in one script and a non-letter in another, and the way readers tolerate severe stylistic distortion without losing the letter? Give me the three or four strongest objections, say which are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable, and tell me whether something close to this already exists under another name and whether my version has anything of its own.
