A friend of mine keeps using the same image to explain why he's relaxed about Britain's trade deficit, and I suspect he got it from Hume, or from a textbook paraphrasing Hume. He says: think of money as water in two connected tanks. If you pour extra water into one tank, its level rises for a bit, but water flows through the pipe until the levels equalise. Likewise, a trade surplus is just a temporarily higher level: the extra money raises our prices, our exports get less competitive, the outflow reverses, and the surplus drains away. So trying to hoard money by running a surplus, or by putting up tariffs to stop it leaving, is like trying to keep water piled up at one end of a tank — you are fighting a self-correcting hydraulic system, which he says is exactly what Hume caught the mercantilists doing. I can already see that water finds its own level, water doesn't create more water, and tanks don't take out loans from each other, but I can't tell wich of those break the argument and which are harmless extras. Could you lay out the full correspondence table (what is the pipe, the water, the level, the flow?), then the places where a real economy lacks the feature of the tanks that the analogy quietly imports, and tell me whether his relaxed conclusion about deficits and tariffs survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether the hydraulic picture was carrying the whole argument. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
