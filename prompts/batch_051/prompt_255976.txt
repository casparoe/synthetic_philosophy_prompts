I am preparing a unit in my philosophy of history seminar on whether large-scale historical change can be quantified, modeled, and predicted, and a colleague has directed me to a widely read long-form profile of Peter Turchin and the research program he calls cliodynamics. The article is written by a competent science journalist rather than an opponent, and it summarizes the view this way.

According to Turchin, all premodern agrarian states, whatever their culture or institutions, pass through secular cycles lasting roughly two to three centuries. A cycle begins with population growth, which gradually fills the land, lowers real incomes, and immiserates ordinary people. At the same time, elite families keep producing more sons than there are top positions, so a surplus of frustrated elite aspirants accumulates. These surplus elites factionalize, compete for a shrinking pool of offices and sinecures, and eventually shatter the state's finances; the society then descends into civil war, rebellion, or foreign defeat, which kills enough people and strips enough elites of their standing to relieve the pressure on land and offices. The cycle then resets and repeats. The article presents this as a demographic clockwork: the real drivers are the ratio of commoners to resources and of elites to positions, while culture, beliefs, individual leaders, and contingent events merely affect the timing and outward form of the collapse, not the fact that it will occur. On the article's account, this is why Turchin was able to forecast, years in advance, the political instability that arrived in the West in the 2010s; it further reports that the model places the United States in a pre-revolutionary phase in the present decade and regards a major crisis in the 2020s as substantially more likely than not.

Before I present this to students or criticize it in my own lectures, I would like an audit of the article's summary. Could you work through it sentence by sentence and say, for each component, whether a careful cliodynamicist would accept it, would call it a defensible simplification, or would reject or heavily qualify it? I am especially interested in whether the article is right to call the cycle a demographic clockwork in which culture and institutions affect only timing and form; whether elite overproduction is being used in the way the theory actually uses it; and whether the inference from the model to the ability to forecast particular episodes of instability, including the unrest of the 2010s and a likely crisis in the 2020s, is actually supported by the accurate parts of the summary. If the article's headline conclusions lean on the distorted parts, tell me what survives the correction.
