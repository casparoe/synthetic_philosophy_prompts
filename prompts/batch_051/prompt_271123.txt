I've been reading about lottocracy lately and I keep getting stuck a concrete case. Here's my working definition of democracy: a system counts as democratic to the extent that (1) the people affected by a decision have an equal say in making it, (2) the decision-makers can be held accountable and replaced by the governed, and (3) decisions emerge from public deliberation rather than force or bare bargaining. Now take this case. A country has an elected lower house that writes most legislation, plus a second chamber of six hundred citizens chosen by lottery each term. They serve four years, get a salary and a small research staff, can amend or block any bill from the lower house, and can be removed only for serious crimes. There's no election for them, no party, no campaign; you can't vote them out because you never voted them in. They are, by design, statistically similar to the public in age, income, education, and region. Is this second chamber democratic under my criteria? I honestly can't tell: it seems to nail equal-say and deliberation, but the accountability condition looks like it fails, unlees you count the lottery itself as a kind of check. Go through it criterion by criterion, say exactly where the definition underdetermines the answer, and tell me whether this case shows my criteria need revision or whether I'm just treating 'accountability' too narrowly.
