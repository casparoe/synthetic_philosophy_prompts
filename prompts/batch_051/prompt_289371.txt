I sit on my university's Claims Review Board, which decides how the costs of campus accidents are allocated among the university's general budget and its outside contractors. We have to issue a written decision in a case that has divided the Board three ways, and I want to circulate a memo before Thursday's meeting. A student was seriously injured in a hit-and-run on the campus ring road shortly after midnight. She can say only that the vehicle was one of the two campus shuttle services' vans, which are visually identical. The shuttle system's automatic counter records every van that enters the ring road; during the incident window, 84 of the 100 logged van movements belonged to Atlas Transit, the larger contractor, and 16 to Halcyon Transit. There is no camera footage, no paint transfer, and no witness who can distinguish the two companies' vehicles. The Board must decide among three courses of action: charge the full settlement and medical costs to Atlas on preponderance reasoning; apportion the costs 84/16 between Atlas and Halcyon under a rarely used clause in our charter that tells the Board, when it cannot identify the particular vehicle, to allocate costs in proportion to the probability that each contractor caused the accident; or decline to charge either contractor because the evidence is undifferentiated statistical evidence that never attaches to any specific van.

I spent much of last night arguing about this with a friend who teaches evidence law. He calls it the naked statistical evidence problem, and he insists that the first option is a mistake even though 84 percent is well above a preponderance threshold, because a bare frequency over a class tells you nothing about the particular event; he leans toward declining to allocate until better evidence turns up. I am not yet convinced that an internal cost-allocation body with no criminal or disciplinary power should reason the way a court would, and I worry that declining to allocate simply lets the larger contractor's risk-pricing off the hook in precisely the cases where the record is thin. Draft the decision memo I will circulate. It should recommend one of the three courses with reasons the Board could actually act on, engage honestly with the strongest argument for each of the two positions not adopted, and say how the principle we adopt would operate in future cases that arrive with the same kind of evidence.
