I run a fortnightly discussion group for graduate students in political science, none of whom has taken a philosophy course. In two weeks I have seventy-five minutes to introduce the moral equality of combatants: the thesis that a soldier who fights for an unjust side and a soldier who fights for a just side can both act permissibly when they kill one another in battle, whatever the justice of the war as a whole. I don't want you to explain the thesis to me; I want a plan for how to explain it to them. Work out the dependency structure: what distinction has to be in place before the thesis can even be stated without sounding like a contradiction, and what has to be in place before that. I expect the central distinction will be something like the split between the justice of a war and the justice of conduct within a war, but I want to know whether the plan needs anything upstream of it — for example, a distinction between the permissibility of an act and the blameworthiness of the person performing it — and what would go wrong if I skipped it. Choose one running example that carries the session; say what features it must have and what features it must conspicuously lack, and justify both. Then map the misconceptions this audience will bring or form — I am fairly sure they will arrive thinking that soldiers on the unjust side are simply murderers, and that if reciprocal killing is permitted then war crimes could not be genuinely wrong, and that the whole view is a dressed-up version of the following-orders excuse — and specify the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, rather than corrected after it has settled. One participant will have encountered the revisionist objection that the doctrine treats soldiers as instruments of the state and severs liability from what an individual actually contributes to an unjust war; plan where that objection should be taken seriously without letting it take over. End with a single check — a variation on the running example, or a question — that would distinguish someone who can restate the thesis from someone who understands why it is attractive and why it is contested. Finally, say explicitly what I should set aside as beyond the session's scope at this level.
