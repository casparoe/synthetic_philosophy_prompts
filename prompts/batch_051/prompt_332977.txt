I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about genuine moral dilemmas — situations in which there is no wrong-free option, or at least every option leaves a moral remainder (a residue of justified guilt, not mere sorrow) — by writing down my reactions to particular cases. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

(1) A runaway trolley will kill five workers; I can throw a switch to divert it onto a sidetrack where it will kill one. Verdict: diverting is right, and the one death is tragic but not something I've done wrong. Refusing to divert is wrong. Not a genuine dilemma.

(2) Two strangers are drowning at equal distances; I can save only one. Verdict: saving either is permissible, and whichever one I leave, I haven't wronged him. I should wish I could have saved both, but the extra death is the world's tragedy, not my wrongdoing. Not a dilemma.

(3) Same as (2), except one of the two is my child. Verdict: I must save my child, and saving the stranger instead would be wrong. That makes it a hard choice with a right answer, not a dilemma. The stranger's death leaves grief, but not the kind of guilt that tracks a wrong I did.

(4) A man with a gun has my two children. He says: point to one, and that one will be killed and the other freed; refuse, and both will be killed. Verdict: pointing is the only thing to do, and refusing would be wrong. But whichever child I point to, I've done wrong by that child — incurred a debt I can never repay — even though I did what I had to do. Genuine dilemma.

(5) I promised to help a friend move, and on the way I come upon a bad car crash; if I stop to help, I'll miss the move, and if I don't, someone is likely to die before an ambulance arrives. Verdict: I must stop. Breaking the promise is not wrong, and I owe my friend an explanation and an apology, but not repair. Not a dilemma.

(6) I promised two friends, long before either date was set, to attend their weddings, and the weddings end up at the same hour on the same day. Verdict: I must break one promise. Attending either wedding is permissible, but whichever friend I stand up has been wronged and is owed real repair, not just an apology. A modest but genuine dilemma.

The pattern I can't make sense of is this. Cases (2) and (6) look structurally alike — two equal, unranked claims and no rule for deciding between them — yet I call one dilemma-free and the other a dilemma. And case (4) becomes a dilemma, where (3) doesn't, merely by replacing a stranger's claim with a second child's claim. The obvious explanations — special ties, how much is at stake, the existence of a decision rule — each handles some cases and fumbles others.

Please extract the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts. State it precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and apply it to the cases I haven't settled: (a) a hospital has one ventilator and two patients with identical prospects, and the only rule is that I must choose; (b) the gunman says "choose which of these two strangers dies, or both will die"; (c) a soldier ordered to take part in an unjust raid knows that if she refuses, her more eager replacement will carry it out and more civilians will die. Say what the principle implies in each, and then tell me honestly which of my six original verdicts it cannot accommodate — and whether you think I should revise the principle or abandon that verdict. Please write your answer in American English.
