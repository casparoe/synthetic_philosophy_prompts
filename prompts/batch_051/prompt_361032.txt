I am trying to work out whether a confident account of petitionary prayer that I was given is actually fair to the tradition, and I would like an accuracy audit rather than a fresh survey. A friend of mine, who has read a good deal of philosophy of religion, summarised the problem and its classic solution like this:

"Petitionary prayer only looks like a problem because God is omniscient and immutable. You cannot tell an omniscient God something he does not already know, and you cannot bend the will of an immutable God, because there is no future change in him for your prayer to bring about. If he has eternally willed to give you something, you will get it whether you pray or not; if he has not, no amount of asking will move him. The classic Thomistic answer is that prayer is not for God's benefit but for ours: its function is to change the one who prays, by reminding her of her dependence and nourishing her confidence in God. Once you accept that, the problem dissolves, because no one ever claimed that petitionary prayer changes the outcome. Asking God for things is, strictly speaking, causally redundant; the petition does no work over and above what meditation or gratitude would do."

Something about this strikes me as too tidy, especially the move from 'prayer changes the one who prays' to 'petition is causally redundant', but I cannot locate exactly where it goes wrong. As an anchor, I have been reading Thomas Aquinas's own discussion in the Summa Theologiae, Second Part of the Second Part, Question 83, Article 2, 'Whether it is becoming to pray', where he writes:

"Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed. Now among other causes human acts are the causes of certain effects. Wherefore it must be that men do certain actions, not that thereby they may change the Divine disposition, but that by those actions they may achieve certain effects according to the order of the Divine disposition: and the same is to be said of natural causes. And so is it with regard to prayer. For we pray not that we may change the Divine disposition, but that we may impetrate that which God has disposed to be fulfilled by our prayers."
