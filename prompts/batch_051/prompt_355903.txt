I am an undergraduate taking a course on science and public policy, and this week we have been working through the familiar argument that public funding for basic science is justified because even the most apparently useless inquiry eventually pays off, alongside the worry that this is often an ex post rationalization rather than an ex ante justification. A guest lecturer presented a thought experiment that I cannot stop thinking about, and I would like a careful analysis of how it would actually play out in practice.

The lecturer proposed that a national research council create a "High-Irrelevance Fund" to replace a portion of its cosmology and fundamental-physics portfolio. Under its rules: (1) a proposal is eligible only if it makes an explicit written case that the proposed work is unlikely to have any practical application for at least thirty years, and the applicant must also list the two or three most plausible routes by which that prediction could turn out wrong; (2) review panels, which would include scientists alongside historians and philosophers of science, are told to score each proposal as the product of scientific significance and the credibility of its inutility case; (3) if a funded project begins to show near-term applications, it is immediately transferred to a separate translational division with a budget top-up, and the original investigators must competitively re-bid to continue; and (4) any commercial licensing of a funded result within thirty years triggers a small levy that partly reimburses the fund and is publicized as a "prediction error."

Please act as an expert in institutional design and the sociology of science, and treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than an abstract idea to evaluate. Begin by brainstorming the different actors and their incentives: investigators, team leaders, panel members, university administrators, industry, and the research council itself. Then reason step by step through the likely dynamics. What sorts of projects would get funded, and what sorts would be quietly killed? How would scientists learn to write the inutility case, and what would gaming look like, for instance strategically underselling one's own work's promise, hiding the most applied implications, or splitting projects across programs? What second-order effects would this have on the culture of cosmology, on early-career researchers, and on how the public comes to understand pure science? What failure modes do you expect in the first five years, and which of them could be patched without gutting the original idea? Where possible, distinguish predictions you find robust from those that depend on contestable assumptions about how academics respond to incentives. Finally, suppose the fund runs for twenty years and, by its own accounting, is a technical success: it funded some scientifically significant but resolutely useless research. Distinguish carefully what that outcome would and would not have shown about the value of knowledge without application.
