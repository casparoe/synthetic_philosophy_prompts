I sit on the constitutional drafting commission of a newly independent state. The draft before us prohibits an official religion and guarantees free exercise, but clause 14 permits the state to collect a modest income surtax from each enrolled member of any recognized religious community and remit the proceeds to that community; a taxpayer may end the obligation at any time simply by deregistering from the community. Proponents defend this as a neutral administrative convenience on the German church-tax model, while opponents call it an establishment by another name. At yesterday's meeting one commissioner circulated the following passage from James Madison's Memorial and Remonstrance against Religious Assessments (1785):

"Who does not see that the same authority which can establish Christianity, in exclusion of all other Religions, may establish with the same ease any particular sect of Christians, in exclusion of all other Sects? that the same authority which can force a citizen to contribute three pence only of his property for the support of any one establishment, may force him to conform to any other establishment in all cases whatsoever?"

She reads it as decisive: the power to compel even a small contribution for a religious establishment is the same power as a more thorough establishment, and the assessment violates equality both by burdening some citizens and by granting others peculiar privileges. A second commissioner replied that Madison was attacking a public fund to support teachers of a general Christian religion, not a scheme in which the state merely handles the mechanics of collecting a member's contribution to the member's own church. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Tuesday's vote: recommend whether clause 14 should be retained, struck, or amended, with a rationale that takes Madison's passage seriously and answers the distinction our colleague drew. State what principle the commission would be adopting under each option, and which reading of the passage better coheres with it.
