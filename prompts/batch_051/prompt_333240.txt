I retired a few years ago from a career in physics, most of it spent on the control of complex dynamical systems, and since then I have been reading my way through the ethics of autonomous systems with a small discussion group. We keep stalling on the dispute over meaningful human control in lethal autonomous weapons, and we have decided to run an adversarial collaboration in the hope of producing a document both sides can sign. The two camps are these. The control-requirement camp holds that a lethal autonomous weapon system may be used against a target only if a human operator exercises meaningful human control over that particular engagement — that is, the operator adequately comprehends the target, the situation, and the likely consequences and makes a deliberate decision to apply lethal force. On this view the per-engagement human role is partly non-instrumental: it survives even in cases where the machine alone would, on the available evidence, be expected to make fewer errors. The compliance-standards camp holds that the fundamental requirement is that the use of the system, given its design, testing, and rules of engagement, can be expected to comply with the principles of distinction and proportionality at least as well as a competent human would in comparable circumstances. On this view meaningful human control is one safeguard among others, neither necessary nor sufficient, and an obligatory human in the loop can in some settings degrade compliance through latency, cognitive overload, or automation bias.

Please draft the document this collaboration should produce. It should begin with a numbered section of claims about autonomous weapons, human performance, and the law of armed conflict that both camps accept, each phrased so that neither side would hedge or add an asterisk. Then include a disagreements table: for each point still in dispute, give a neutral statement of the issue both sides would accept as fair, followed in the same row by each side's preferred formulation of its own position. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side — and state for each whether the dispute is one of principle, of empirical expectation, or is largely verbal. Finally, for each substantive crux, propose a concrete scenario, data-gathering exercise, or argument both sides would regard as probative, and record in advance what each side predicts it would show and what finding would lead that side to revise.

Do not spend space on bibliography or citations; the document should be self-contained. My own suspicion, offered only as seeding, is that the deepest cruxes will concern whether the value of human control is intrinsic or merely instrumental, and how much confidence prospective testing can justify for a system whose target-classification function is learned from data and may behave poorly in conditions unlike those used in testing. But I would rather see that suspicion tested than see it treated as a constraint.
