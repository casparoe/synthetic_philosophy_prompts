Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over. If someone does something cruel and afterwards says 'I did a bad thing,' we read it as moral health — the feeling even gets a name, guilt, and we treat it as almost virtuous, the sign of a conscience doing its job. If the same person says 'I am a bad person,' we call it shame and start worrying he's fragile, self-obsessed, maybe more likely to do something worse next time. Same act, same regret, two first-person sentences a Martian could barely tell apart, and we hand out completely different moral verdicts. Is that asymmetry tracking anything real, or is it a modern Western folk theory with a psychologist's stamp on it? Steelman the distinction as well as you can, then say honestly whether it holds up: is guilt actually the better moral emotion, and should we keep judging the person who feels shame more harshly? Test it on nearby cases: two people make the same mistake; one is tormented by what he did, the other by what he is — should I respect one more then the other? And can a culture deliberately choose to cultivate one of these emotions over the other, or is the choice itself only intelligible from inside one of them?
