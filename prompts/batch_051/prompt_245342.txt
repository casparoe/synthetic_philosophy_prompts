I'm not a philosopher, just a musician who produces records and reads aesthetics on the side, and there's a distinction I keep needing but cant find a name for.

We normally talk as if the recording is one thing and the song is another: a live album is a recording of a performance of a song, and the song itself is whatever all the performances of it share. That picture works fine for jazz records and bootlegs. But a lot of the music I work on doesnt fit it at all. Say I build a track by splicing forty vocal takes together one syllable at a time, reversing the guitar, and layering loops that were never played through as a continuous line. The finished file isn't a recording of any event that ever happened; nobody could perform 'the same thing' live, because part of the thing just is the editing. Yet we treat it as one song: it has a title and a composer credit, and a cover version gets judged against that exact file.

So two quite different animals both go by 'recording'. One is a trace left by a performance; the other is, as far as I can see, the work itself — the recording doesnt document a prior song, it just is the song. My question: does this split have an established name in the philosophy of music, and how should it be drawn exactly? Is it a difference in kind, or do most studio tracks sit somewhere on a spectrum between the two? And when someone plays a 'live version' of a studio-built track, are they performing the same work or making a new work that quotes the first? If there's no established name, I'd want the answer to include a definition with boundary cases rather than just a label.
