I have just taken over the chair of my university's academic accommodations committee, which rules on whether an assistive technology lets a student display their own cognitive ability or instead does the assessed cognitive work for them. I want our decisions to be consistent both with each other and with the picture of cognition we have been using. That picture comes from Andy Clark and David Chalmers, their 1998 paper The Extended Mind. After comparing three mental-rotation cases, they write:

"The kind of case just described is by no means as exotic as it may at first appear. It is not just the presence of advanced external computing resources which raises the issue, but rather the general tendency of human reasoners to lean heavily on environmental supports."

Our recent decisions:

1. We allowed a four-function calculator for a student with documented dyscalculia on a chemistry exam assessing stoichiometric reasoning, because arithmetic was not the capacity being tested.
2. We allowed, with conditions, a screen reader for a blind student on a literature exam, provided the device did nothing but convert printed text to speech.
3. We rejected a step-by-step equation solver in a calculus exam, because constructing the solution path was exactly the assessed capacity.
4. We rejected a real-time translation earpiece in a French oral exam, because understanding spoken French was exactly the assessed capacity.
5. We allowed a grammar checker that corrected subject-verb agreement for a student with dyslexia on an essay exam graded for argument structure rather than formal correctness.
6. We rejected an AI lecture-summarizer in an open-book exam assessing the ability to compress and integrate a semester's material into a short memo, because the tool had performed that compression.

The case now before us: a student with a documented expressive-language impairment asks to use a word-prediction program while typing law exam answers. The program suggests the next word or short phrase; the student accepts the suggestion with a slight movement or overrides it. The process has become so automatic that he describes the tool as a faster, more precise version of his own vocabulary. The exam assesses issue-spotting and the application of legal rules, not prose style. The legal reasoning is his, but many of the word choices and some of the phrasing come from the program.

Before the hearing, I want an account of what our record commits us to in light of the Clark and Chalmers passage. Identify the distinct rules that fit all six prior decisions; for each rule, say what principle we would be adopting and what it predicts in the new case. Then say which single precedent is the closest analog, or whether the new facts fall between two precedents. Explain which interpretation of "lean heavily on environmental supports" should govern, and how much work that passage actually does here. Finally, give your best prediction of the ruling that would make us most consistent with our record, and specify the further case that would best separate whichever rules remain live.
