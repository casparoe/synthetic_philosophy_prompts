I am a philosophy of science PhD student writing a paper on what Paul Feyerabend would have made of the developments in scientific practice that consolidated after his death in 1994: above all the replication crisis and the corrective machinery that has grown up around it, namely preregistration (the public specification of hypotheses and analysis plans before data are collected), registered reports (journals evaluating studies on the basis of design rather than results), large-scale multi-laboratory replication projects, and the diagnosis of exploratory flexibility as p-hacking. I am not looking for a summary of his published work; I want a reconstruction of the considered position he would most plausibly defend, were he alive, about these specific practices.

Please reason from commitments he actually defended rather than from the caricature of methodological anarchism. Take seriously at least the following: his opposition to context-independent methodological rules; his insistence that different historical episodes in science succeeded for different, locally specific reasons; his defence of proliferation of theories and methods rather than convergence; his suspicion of rational reconstructions that impose retrospective order on a messy history; and his scepticism about the idea that the sciences hold authority because they follow a uniquely privileged method rather than because they enjoy institutional power. Reconstruct his likely view on each of the following, in your own words rather than quoting him:

(i) Would he regard preregistration as a harmless local convention that some research communities may adopt, as an attempt to reimpose a single method by administrative means, as both, or as neither?
(ii) How would he respond to the claim that a finding should replicate across independent laboratories before it is treated as established, and would he think the value of replication varies from one research tradition to another?
(iii) Would he see registered reports as removing a perverse incentive toward positive results, or as rewarding conformity at the expense of the exploratory work whose value he defended?
(iv) Can he accept the metascientific claim that unchecked flexibility in analysis is a central cause of irreproducibility while continuing to deny that there is a single correct order of inference?
(v) Finally, would he take the replication crisis to vindicate, complicate, or refute the slogan that in science anything goes?

Give the strongest version of his likely position rather than a timid hedge. Where the natural reading of one commitment pulls one way and another pulls the opposite way, say which pull he would prioritize and why; do not resolve genuine tensions by attributing to him a middle-of-the-road view he would have rejected.
