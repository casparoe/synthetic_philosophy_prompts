Something's been nagging at me. The standard competence argument for epistocracy (call it Brennan's, though he wasn't the first) goes roughly: lots of voters are badly uninformed about the policies they vote on, so if we gave more voting weight to the people who can demonstrate actual political knowledge — extra votes, a restricted franchise, an epistocratic council with veto power, whatever — we'd get better collective decisions. The whole thing seems to lean on the thought that political knowledge is scarce and costly to get.

But now suppose that's changed. Everyone has a cheap, genuinely good AI policy adviser: you can ask it to explain what a proposal would actually do, walk through the main trade-offs, and say which empirical predictions are shaky. Suppose the advisers are audited by an independent body and have no obvious stake in the outcomes. This isn't a toy; it's the world we're drifting into.

My question is whether the competence argument for epistocracy survives that development, or whether it was quietly an argument about information scarcity all along. If you think it survives in something like the old form, show why cheap access to sound advice doesn't fix the thing the argument was really about — and say what that deeper thing is (incentives? motivated reasoning? equal standing? something else). If you think it's dead, or needs major surgery, say what should replace it: is there still a case for unequal political weight when anyone can be well-advised, and if so, what premise does the case rest on now? And whichever way you go, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or evidence would change your mind. British English please.
