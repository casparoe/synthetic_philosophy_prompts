Standard problem cases for the principle of indifference, as far as I know them, are the following three. In Bertrand's chord paradox, the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on whether the chord is selected by randomizing over a radius and a point along it, over endpoints on the circumference, or over the chord's midpoint in the disk. In the water-and-wine paradox, if all one knows is that the water-to-wine ratio lies between 1 and 2, applying indifference to the ratio itself gives probability 1/2 that the ratio is below 3/2, while applying it to the reciprocal ratio gives probability 2/3 for that same proposition. In the cube-factory example, if all one knows is that a cube's side length lies between 0 and 1, then for the proposition that the side length is at most 1/2, indifference applied to side length, face area, and volume gives probabilities 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8, respectively. Give one more problem case of this kind that is not on the list and is not merely a reparameterization, geometric variant, or special case of these. Set it up precisely, derive the incompatible assignments, and then assess whether the case shows a defect in the principle of indifference itself or only in how the setup is parameterized. If you think no further kind exists, defend that conclusion by saying what all three listed cases really have in common.
