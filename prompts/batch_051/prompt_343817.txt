I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, writing mostly about behavioral science and the technology of persuasion, and for the past several months I've been reporting on the debate over what separates manipulation from legitimate persuasion. The specialists I interview keep splitting into two camps that talk past each other, and I want the document that would force them to talk to each other.

The process camp holds that manipulation is defined by the route by which influence travels. On this view, influence that engages a person's capacity to assess evidence and reasons is persuasion, while influence that routes around that capacity — through emotional triggers, framing effects, defaults, salience, or calibrated social pressure — is manipulation, whatever its results and whatever the influencer's aims. The endorsement camp holds that manipulation is defined by the target's reflective relationship to the way they were moved. On this view, influence is legitimate when the target could, on full information and rational reflection, endorse being moved in that way; systematically one-sided or hidden appeals that speak the language of reasons can therefore be manipulative, while an openly affective appeal need not be. Both camps agree that labeling something manipulative is a classification rather than, by itself, a moral verdict, so the dispute is over what the label picks out, not simply over what is permissible.

Please write the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would have produced, structured as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including claims about paradigm cases such as subliminal advertising, gaslighting, and a straightforward evidence-based argument, and about why the distinction matters for political microtargeting, commercial advertising, and informed consent in medicine; these shared claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted. Second, a disagreements table: each row should state a sub-question in neutral wording, then give the process camp's position and the endorsement camp's position, each phrased in wording its own camp would accept. Third, three or four cruxes, meaning sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp; for each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance. The disputed cases should include transparent defaults and framing effects, emotionally vivid appeals, and factually accurate but systematically one-sided argument. Finally, identify any part of the dispute that is on reflection merely verbal, and say what would remain genuinely open if that part were set aside.
