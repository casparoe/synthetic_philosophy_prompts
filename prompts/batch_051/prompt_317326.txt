I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm poking at the way people bring up chaos theory to make claims about history sound more rigorous than they are. At a panel on counterfactual history last week someone wheeled out the old for-want-of-a-nail story — the nail, the shoe, the horse, the rider, the battle, the kingdom — to say that one tiny slip decided everything, and then waved at chaos theory as if that closed the case. Everyone nodded along like its settled, which annoyed me.

I want to do better in my piece than just telling people the cliché is a cliché. Can you lay out exactly five distinct claims that the for-want-of-a-nail proverb could be taken to be making about how history works, from the nearly trivial to the radical or probably false. Number them 1 to 5, each item a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion. For each claim, tell me what the world would have to be like for it to come out true, say whether it's something the relevant historians and philosophers mostly accept, something genuinely contested, or something only a minority hold, and note which of the five readings the panellist's argument actually needed and whether chaos theory gives that reading any real support. Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
