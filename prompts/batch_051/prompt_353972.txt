I'm a journalist who covers ideas, and I've been working on a piece about a deceptively simple question in philosophy of math: is 2 an element of 4? Not whether 2 divides 4, but literally whether the number 2 is a member of the number 4, in the set-theoretic sense. The strange thing is that the answer seems to flip depending on how you code the numbers as sets. In the von Neumann coding, each number is the set of all the previous ones, so 2 = {0,1} and 4 = {0,1,2,3}, which means 2 really is in there. In Zermelo's coding, each number is just the singleton of the one before it, so 4 = {3}, and 2 is not an element of it. Nobody I've interviewed thinks there's a deep fact of the matter about which answer is right, but I keep being handed the same four arguments for that deflationary conclusion. Here they are as I've collected them:

1. The rival-encodings argument. Both codings preserve all the arithmetic, and arithmetic itself gives no reason to favor one over the other. So any membership fact that changes between them is an artifact of a coding choice, not a fact about numbers.

2. The structural-role argument. Numbers are just positions in the natural-number structure — the thing with a first position, a next position for each one, and so on — and the only real facts about a position are its relations to the other positions: successor, less than, addition, multiplication. Membership isn't one of those relations, so asking whether 2 belongs to 4 is a bit like asking whether the shortstop position is made of wood.

3. The invariance argument. Any two standard models of arithmetic are isomorphic, and isomorphic models satisfy exactly the same structural claims. But isomorphism doesn't preserve membership — it can map a model where 2 ∈ 4 comes out true to a model where it comes out false. Anything that changes under isomorphism can't be a property the numbers really have.

4. The ordinary-practice argument. Arithmetic as people actually do it never treats numbers as having elements. The question only becomes well-posed after youve imported a set-theoretic translation that arithmetic never asked for, so it ends up being a question about the translation rather than about 2 and 4 themselves.

I'm fairly sure these are the standard moves, and maybe that's all there is. But I'd love a fifth argument for the conclusion that 2 ∈ 4 has no deep answer — one that's genuinely different from those four, not a rephrasing or a special case or a blend of them, and in particular one that doesnt just assume structuralism at the outset. I don't need a name or a citation for whoever first made it; I need the argument itself. And tell me honestly whether it's any good, and if you think it is, what it adds that the four I already have don't.
