I'm leading a graduate tutorial on the morality of promising, and I want a single thought experiment that forces my students to stop running together two things they have been treating as interchangeable: the predictive expectation a promisee forms, and the social practice of promising itself. In almost every everyday promise the two travel together—you promise, your word is underwritten by a shared practice, and the promisee expects performance—so ordinary cases never put pressure on the distinction. The case I want is built to hold the normative core of promising fixed while deleting expectation and reliance: a promise, made within an established practice of promising and with full uptake by the promisee, that produces no predictive expectation that the promisor will perform, produces no reliance on anyone's part, and yet is binding in the full-blooded way that makes breaking it a wrong against the promisee. To be explicit about the distinction the case must respect: the promisee's normative position—their accepting that the promisor has placed himself under a duty to them—should be fully intact; what should be absent is their predictive state of believing he will actually come through, and anyone else's adjusting their plans in reliance on him.

Constraints: realistic (ordinary people, ordinary stakes), one paragraph, no legal or professional obligations (no contracts, oaths, or promises to a tribunal), no deception about the meaning of the words or the identity of the promisor, and no one should ever learn afterward whether the promise was kept, so that reputation and third-party effects are out. The promisee's lack of predictive expectation must be a stable feature of their psychology, not the product of a misunderstanding of the speech act. And the case should generate its verdict from its details rather than stipulating it; I'll count it a failure if the only reason to call the promise binding is that the case asserts it is.

Begin with a section called "The Case" (about 150 words) presenting the scenario itself. In "Scanlon's View" (about 150 words), say what a Scanlon-style expectation/assurance view must say about this promise—roughly, that a promise obliges because the promisor deliberately leads the promisee to form expectations about his conduct, and breach wrongs the promisee by disappointing the very expectation he created—and explain why the case gives that view trouble, along with any contrived repair it might attempt. In "The Humean View" (about 120 words), explain why a Hume-style convention view has no comparable trouble—roughly, that promising has force only within a social practice whose general reliability is mutually advantageous, so breach wrongs others by free-riding on a practice one benefits from—and say which feature of the case lets the view accommodate the verdict without stretching. In "Shiffrin's Addition" (about 100 words), say what a Shiffrin-style authority-transfer view adds to the diagnosis—roughly, that promising vests the promisee with authority over a slice of the promisor's future conduct, so breach is exercising authority the promisor no longer has. In "The Load-Bearing Feature" (about 80 words), name the single feature of the case doing the work against the expectation view, and say what the verdict would become if that feature were removed. Finally, in "The Scanlonian Reply" (about 150 words), give the strongest available Scanlonian response—by finding a residual expectation, by denying the intuition that the case binds, or by relocating the obligation to a neighboring principle—and assess whether it preserves the distinct wrong of breaking a promise or replaces it with a weaker, different wrong.
