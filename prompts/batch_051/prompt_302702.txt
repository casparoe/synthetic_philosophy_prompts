I am taking a course on philosophy of language, and I have found myself stuck on an analogy that seems to be doing more argumentative work than either side in the debate is willing to admit. Frege's puzzle, as it is standardly introduced, is that a competent speaker can treat 'Hesperus is Hesperus' as trivial while treating 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' as a genuine astronomical discovery, even though both names refer to Venus. The standard explanation invokes distinct senses, or modes of presentation, and it is often illustrated by saying that two different roads can lead to the same city. I have now seen this route analogy aimed in two opposite directions. A Fregean leaning on the analogy says: just as arriving at the same city by a different road can be an informative arrival, the two names give the same referent through different senses, so the informativeness of the identity claim is unproblematic. A friend of mine, however, uses the very same analogy to deflate Frege's problem: two roads to Rome are still just roads to one Rome, nobody includes the roads in what 'Rome' means, and so the informativeness of 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is merely a psychological fact about which route I happened to travel, and not anything that belongs in a theory of meaning or reference.

I would like an audit of the route analogy itself, rather than a defense of either side. What maps onto what when we press the comparison — does the referent correspond to the destination, the sense to the road, and what would the traveler, the map, the signposts, and the act of arriving correspond to, if they have any counterparts at all? Where does the mapping break? The analogy appears to import features of roads that a sense may lack: roads are contingent, external, optional, revisable, and detachable from the destination in a way that modes of presentation may not be. Are those disanalogies the ones that matter for my friend's deflationary conclusion, or are they merely surface differences? Does either use of the analogy — the Fregean or the deflationary — actually survive the points where the mapping fails? And if the road metaphor is too loaded to do the work, is there a better analogy for what Frege needs, or does the search for one reveal that the original distinction was doing something the analogy cannot capture?
