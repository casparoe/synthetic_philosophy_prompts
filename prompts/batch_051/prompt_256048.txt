I have been trying to get clear about what it takes for a disagreement to be “merely verbal” rather than substantive, and the usual gloss — that a verbal dispute is one that would disappear once the parties stopped using the same word for different things — keeps striking me as too simple. I would like a working definition of a merely verbal dispute: one that says what must be true of the parties’ meanings, information, and practical aims for their dispute to be verbal, and what features make a dispute substantive instead. The definition should be precise enough to apply to hard cases without simply stipulating that any dispute about how to use a word counts as verbal.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. (a) Two friends agree about the exact capsaicin content of a curry and about what each of them tastes, but one says the curry is “spicy” and the other denies it; the first uses “spicy” for any detectable chile heat, while the second reserves it for a burning sensation above a threshold they both can report, and once they explain their usages each endorses the other’s underlying description. (b) Two astronomers agree on every orbital, physical, and historical fact about Pluto but disagree about whether Pluto is a planet, and they know they also disagree about how the term “planet” should be used in future public science education. (c) Two labor economists use the same model with the same estimated elasticities, and both agree that raising the minimum wage leaves job destruction among existing workers unchanged while slowing job creation for new entrants; one says the policy “causes unemployment,” while the other denies this because he reserves “causes unemployment” for job destruction and classifies slower creation as a different kind of cost. (d) A cognitive scientist and a clinician examine the same fMRI and behavioral data from a minimally conscious patient; they agree about which regions activate and which commands the patient reliably follows, but they disagree about whether the patient “understands” the commands, because the scientist requires later report or use of the content while the clinician requires only nonaccidental differential responsiveness to meaningful commands. (e) Two jurors agree about all the trial evidence, agree about the legal definition of “guilty beyond a reasonable doubt,” and agree about what the standard requires in the abstract, but disagree about whether this particular defendant meets it.

For each case, I would like the definition to say whether the dispute is merely verbal, substantive, or something else, and to explain why. If no single definition handles all five cases, say which case forces a revision and defend the revised definition.
