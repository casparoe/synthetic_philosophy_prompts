I keep running into the move where someone says Malthus wasn't wrong, just early: the mass starvation he seemed to predict didn't arrive on schedule, but the underlying pressure never went away, so the theory isn't falsified, just running late. Before I accept any version of that move, I want the desiderata. What does a very long-run predictive theory—population, resources, climate, whatever—have to establish before we're allowed to call it 'right but early' rather than false, or untestable, or just vague enough to soak up any outcome?

I want the list of requirements with the argument for each. The one I care most about is the rescue problem: a mechanism being held in check by something the theory itself could have expected or licensed, versus being bailed out by exogenous fixes the theory didn't anticipate—new crops, fossil fuels, nitrogen fixation, sanitation, the demographic transition. Does it matter whether those fixes are themselves responses to scarcity, and therefore count as endogeneous to the pressure the theory named? I suspect that's the crux, but I want it turned into a proper requirement rather than a vibe I keep gesturing at.

For each desideratum, say whether it's non-negotiable or just a taste of a particular camp (economists who want everything microfounded versus historians comfortable with broad mechanisms, that sort of thing). And flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied—that's the bit I'd find most useful. Then, briefly, test how the standard Malthusian story and whichever neo-Malthusian version you take to be the strongest live candidate do against the list, so I can see the checklist actually biting. Keep that last part short; the list is the point.
