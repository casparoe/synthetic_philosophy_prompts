I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into the gender-equality paradox in my inbox: the finding that in countries that score high on gender-equality indices, women and men actually end up in *more* different fields than in less equal countries — e.g. a smaller share of women in engineering degrees, and bigger average sex differences in things like risk-taking or altruism on surveys. Every time I write about it, readers split into camps and talk past each other, so I want a sidebar quiz for a feature article that sorts people by what they actually believe, not by the labels they'd pick for themselves.

Write me a decision tree — or a short branching quiz with a scoring key, whichever works better — that takes a reader from the basic fact of the paradox to one of these positions (they don't have to be called exactly this, but the leaves should capture these camps):

- the "freedom reveals real differences" view: the paradox is mostly genuine, and it shows that when material constraints and discrimination loosen, average intrinsic differences in interests and personality show up more strongly;
- the "artifact" view: the paradox is a statistical mirage — an artifact of bad equality indices, cherry-picked outcome measures, or cross-country comparisons that don't control for the right things;
- the "still culture" view: the pattern is real, but it doesn't show nature winning — egalitarian countries don't remove gender norms, they swap them for subtler ones (essentialist talk about "natural" differences, lingering care-work asymmetries, etc.), and people still sort into careers under social pressure;
- the "so what" view: whatever the descriptive facts, they dont settle what we should do about underrepresentation.

Heres what I want the work to go into. The diagnostic questions should be concrete cases or claims, not requests to self-identify with an -ism. Use at least these three test cases somewhere in the tree: (1) the fact that the headline STEM finding depends a lot on whether you measure the share of STEM graduates who are women versus the share of women who end up in STEM, since the two can move in opposite directions; (2) the objection that gender-equality indices are built partly from outcomes like political representation and wage gaps, so they're not measuring "freedom from gender roles" in the first place; and (3) the thought experiment where you wave a wand and remove all gender-related pressures — no discrimination, no stereotypes, no gendered socialization — and the average interest gap survives. Each node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which camp it puts them in.

Two structural constraints. First, build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of camps: work out the questions and the answer combinations first, then locate the camps in that space, and tell me which cells are empty and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's staked it out. Second, flag honestly what the tree cannot separate. I suspect the "artifact" and "still culture" camps will be hard to pull apart on some questions, because both deny the freedom-reading but for different reasons; and the "so what" view isn't really a leaf on the same tree at all, since it's about what follows normatively and someone can hold it together with any of the other three. If so, say that and tell me how to handle it (maybe a second, separate question at the end, or a footnote).

Keep the whole thing to about six or seven questions max, and write it for a smart general reader who's heard of the paradox but has no background in the academic debate.
