I sit on a five-judge court of last resort in a small common-law jurisdiction that has, over the past twenty years, built up a body of desuetude case law: the doctrine that a long-unenforced penal statute can lose its authority as law. Four of our decisions frame the present problem. First, we dismissed a charge under a 1921 statute that barred barbers from opening on Sundays. The statute had gone unenforced since the 1950s, Sunday barbering was open and nearly universal, and an Attorney-General's opinion had assured barbers decades earlier that it would not be prosecuted. Second, we upheld a prosecution under a 1908 criminal-nonsupport statute that had been enforced only sporadically, stressing that violations were covert, no one had reasonably relied on non-enforcement, and identifiable dependents were left unprotected. Third, we dismissed a charge under an 1899 fortune-telling ordinance that had been openly flouted for seventy years, during which the city licensed fortune-tellers and collected their fees, publicly treating the practice as lawful. Fourth, we upheld a prosecution under a 1927 landlord-heating statute that had very rarely been enforced, emphasizing tenants' repeated complaints, the identifiable class of protected victims, and the absence of any public understanding that unheated apartments were lawful.

We now must rule on a motion to dismiss an indictment under a 1934 statute that makes selling unpasteurized milk a misdemeanor for the protection of consumers. There has been no prosecution under the statute since 1971, though raw milk is sold openly at farmers' markets, advertised without concealment, and bought by thousands; no official has ever affirmatively declared the practice lawful, but no agency has moved against it in more than fifty years. Last month a child was hospitalized with a bacterial infection traced to raw milk from the defendant's farm, and the state charged the defendant. The defendant argues that the statute has lapsed through desuetude.

If we want to be consistent with our own case law, what should we decide? Identify the rule or rules our four decisions can fairly be read to embody, say which precedent is the closest analog under each candidate rule, and explain what principle we would be committing ourselves to under each available outcome.
