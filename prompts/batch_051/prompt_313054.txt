I've been reading Hegel's Philosophy of Right in bits and pieces (im no academic) and one idea keeps nagging at me. As I understand it, he thought long stretches of peace are actually risky for a society: people sink into private comfort and money-making, and the state drifts toward just being a machine for private interests. War, or at least the standing possibility of war and the demand for self-sacrifice, shocks people back into seeing themselves as citizens of a whole, and keeps a state's ethical life from going stagnant. I couldn't help noticing this sounds almost exactly like what forest managers say about fire suppression: if you put out every small fire, dead fuel piles up, the system gets stagnant and samey, and then one catastrophic fire does way more damage than regular low-level burns would have. So both seem to be saying that a system's long-term health depends on recurring destructive stress, and that removing that stress doesn't make you safe, it just stores the damage up. I genuinely can't tell if this is a deep structural point or just two uses of the same "purifying fire" image. Where does the comparison actually break down? And if the ecological side is right, does that lend any real support to Hegel's view, or is the gap between a forest and a political community the whole story? One thing I keep turning over: if the parallel held, you'd predict that societies that completely insulate themselves from war should be the most brittle and prone to some huge collapse. Is that a real implication of Hegel's position, and does it hold up? Also, I'd appreciate if you could seperate what is settled about Hegel's actual view from what's genuinely contested among people who study him, and not make a contested reading sound like the obvious consensus.
