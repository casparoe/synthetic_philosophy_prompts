I'm teaching a small graduate seminar on decision-making under ignorance — the 'no probabilities' case, not textbook risk — and each week half the room wants to defend maximin by citing Rawls's original position, the other half wants Harsanyi's expected-utility response, and the rest like minimax regret because it sounds less pessimistic. Before I let anyone argue for a rule, I want the full spec of what a rule is for. So write the desiderata: what must any adequate rule for choice under complete ignorance get right, assuming a single-shot decision where your choice doesn't influence which state of nature obtains and you either cannot or refuse to assign probabilities to the states? Give me each requirement with the case or consideration that makes it a genuine demand rather than someone's taste, and flag which are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp. Things I've seen treated as obviously compulsory include weak dominance, symmetry or anonymity across states, invariance under relabelling of acts and states, independence of irrelevant alternatives, row and column duplication, and the question of whether the rule should respect only ordinal or only cardinal information about payoffs — and whether requiring cardinal comparisons is already a tacit commitment against maximin — but don't take any of those as fixed; I want to know which survive scrutiny and which are prejudice.

The part I most want to see is this: if some subset of the desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — the way impossibility results crop up in social choice — say so explicitly and show which requirement has to give way first, and whether the choice of which one to drop is itself a tacit commitment to one of the big rules. If there's a single requirement that does most of the work excluding the usual candidates (maximin, minimax regret, the principle of insufficient reason, Hurwicz), name it. Skip the full scorecard; I'll run the candidates against the list in class once I know what the list actually is.
