I'm a retired chemist trying to get into philosophy properly, and I've been reading the preface to Russell's *Mysticism and Logic*. This sentence caught my attention:

"In theoretical Ethics, the position advocated in 'The Free Man's Worship' is not quite identical with that which I hold now: I feel less convinced than I did then of the objectivity of good and evil."

What puzzles me is how far this can be pushed. I sketched the following argument after reading it:

(1) Civil disobedience is morally justified only if there are objective moral principles that the state is violating.
(2) If moral objectivity is in doubt, as Russell suggests, then no one can know whether such principles exist.
(3) So the justification of civil disobedience is, at best, permanently uncertain.
(4) But if a justification is permanently uncertain, it cannot ground the sort of obligation we usually assign to dissidents — for example, that the state ought not to punish them.
(5) Therefore, if Russell is right, our admiration for civil disobedients rests on very shaky ground.

This conclusion seems too strong, and I'd rather keep both (1) and (5) if possible. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I suspect (2) is doing too much work, but I can't see a better reading of Russell.
