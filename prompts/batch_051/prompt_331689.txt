I'm a retired evolutionary biologist who got into moral psychology a few years back — mostly the Haidt and Graham moral foundations work (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and later liberty). The reason I'm writing is that a dinner argument last week turned into a proposal for our dysfunctional city council, and I can't tell whether it's brilliant or a machine for producing hypocrisy.

The idea is this. Any bill must be introduced by two co-sponsors, one from each party. Each co-sponsor writes a one-page justification of the bill using only the foundations that the other side rates substantially higher then their own. Concretely: the progressive co-sponsor has to argue from loyalty, authority, or sanctity and cannot appeal to care or fairness; the conservative co-sponsor has to argue from care and fairness and cannot appeal to loyalty, authority, or sanctity. A staff psychologist reviews both statements and can bounce the thing back if one of them is just the sponsor's home foundations in a wig — say, a progressive's "loyalty" paragraph that's really a fairness argument with the word community sprinkled on top.

I don't want to know whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually behave. Would it train genuine perspective-taking, or just create a market for people with a talent for moral ventriloquism? What kinds of bills would survive — would it select for policies that honestly draw on both sides' foundations, or for mush vague enough to be told in any foundation? How would the sponsors, the parties, the psychologist, and the council leadership each game it, and what second-order effects would follow? And if it fails in the way you'd predict, is there a fixable version or should the whole design be abandoned?
