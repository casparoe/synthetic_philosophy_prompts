A country is considering this arrangement for declared emergencies. The executive may impose border controls, quarantines, or lockdowns immediately, and the measures remain legal for up to ninety days. But every thirty days a citizens' panel of forty, chosen by lot, sits for five days; it receives the same unredacted data as the executive, hears testimony from the relevant agencies, and then votes by majority on a single question: is this measure legitimate? A "no" vote does not make the measure illegal, but it does strip officials and agencies of immunity from civil liability for harms caused by the measure from that point on, and it requires the executive to publish a formal response. Two consecutive "no" votes trigger a mandatory parliamentary debate and vote on whether to continue the measure. Analyze this as a mechanism, not a moral position. What does it incentivize among executives, agencies, panelists, and potential plaintiffs? How would it be gamed—through the timing of declarations, the packaging of measures, the selection or briefing of panels, or strategic litigation? Take a definite position on which failure mode is most serious, argue for that judgment, and say whether it can be fixed without gutting the scheme.
