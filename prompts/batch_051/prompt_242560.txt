I am a PhD student in political science, working at the intersection of security studies and technology policy. Last term I witnessed an argument between two colleagues—one trained in security studies, the other in formal political economy—about whether James Fearon's rationalist explanations for war transfer cleanly to competition over advanced AI systems, and I would like you to adjudicate it point by point. For context: Fearon's framework treats war as a puzzle because fighting is ex post inefficient, so bargains that all sides would prefer to war should normally exist. The reasons bargains nevertheless fail are private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility, the last of which Fearon himself treats with skepticism. I reconstructed the exchange from notes as faithfully as I could; the wording is approximate, but the substance of each move reflects what was said.

(1) The first speaker, P, argued that an AI development race between leading labs or states is exactly the kind of inefficient contest the framework targets, since all parties would prefer the slower, safer trajectory if they could count on others to take it. But the binding constraint, P claimed, is commitment rather than information. Even under full transparency about compute, progress, and safety practice, no actor could credibly promise not to exploit a lead once it has one. An actor that crosses a decisive capability threshold first could deploy, shape the rules and markets that follow, or deter rivals from catching up, before any consortium could coordinate a response. Agreements to slow down near the finish line are unenforceable because the payoff to defecting is enormous, and the defection itself creates the power that makes punishment doubtful.

(2) The second speaker, Q, replied that P had renamed an informational problem rather than eliminating it. If progress were verifiable in the way P's thought experiment assumes, rival actors could write agreements conditional on observed milestones, and a detected violation would trigger immediate acceleration by others or exclusion from shared infrastructure. The reason such conditionality is not available, Q argued, is that research happens in private: each actor observes its own algorithmic insights, talent, and effective compute better than rivals do, and each has incentives to overstate or understate progress depending on its audience. That is exactly private information with incentives to misrepresent.

(3) P granted Q's descriptive point but denied it was fundamental. Commitment problems with a first-move advantage persist under full information: rivals who know one another's arsenals can still face incentives to strike first or fight preventively when the advantage of moving first is decisive. By analogy, labs could observe one another perfectly and still race, because the side that crosses the capability threshold first obtains an advantage that no ex post punishment can reverse. Transparency might not pacify such a race; it might sharpen it, by making clear exactly when defection would become decisive.

(4) Q responded that the first-strike analogy was doing more work than P admitted. A preemptive logic requires a brief window in which the first mover can convert its lead into durable advantage. In AI development that window is not guaranteed: algorithmic ideas diffuse through hiring, publication, and supply chains; compute is increasingly a purchasable input; and an early lead does not automatically translate into lasting dominance. If a first mover cannot consolidate before a second mover arrives, there is no autonomous commitment problem—only the informational problem of knowing when thresholds have actually been crossed. Q added that P's talk of an all-or-nothing prize is an indivisibility claim, and indivisibility is the least persuasive of Fearon's three mechanisms, since side-payments can in principle divide almost any prize.

(5) P answered that Q's side-payment response presupposes the enforceability it is meant to secure. A prize can be divided by side-payments only if some arrangement binds the winner to deliver them, and in a transformative-technology race the winner acquires exactly the capacity to revise the terms. The losing side would be betting on the winner's continuing goodwill. More fundamentally, the relevant good—shaping the ensuing technological and regulatory dispensation—is not the sort of thing one can cash out to a rival. So indivisibility, if it matters, is best read as a face of the commitment problem, not a detachable objection to it.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating, then say where the exchange, taken as a whole, should have gone, and what both speakers together are missing—whether an empirical distinction, a third mechanism, or a defect in the war-to-AI analogy. In particular, are commitment and information competing explanations of AI races, or does one reduce to the other under plausible assumptions? Does the transfer from interstate war to AI development distort any of Fearon's concepts? I would also like you to be explicit about epistemic vulnerability: say which step in your adjudication is least secure, and what empirical or conceptual consideration would most plausibly lead you to a different overall verdict.
