Take the British abolition of the slave trade in 1807 and then of slavery in the empire in 1833 as a case, and use it to test three competing accounts of how entrenched moral practices end: an argument-based account, on which abolitionist moral and religious argument changed enough minds; the interest-based account associated with Eric Williams (the Williams thesis), on which abolition followed a shift in the material interests behind the slave colonies; and Kwame Anthony Appiah's honor-based account, on which such practices die when they stop conferring honor and begin conferring shame. Work out, for each account, what it says the relevant actors were responding to, which features of the episode it handles well, and which it must strain to explain — the mass petition campaigns, the sugar boycotts, the £20 million in compensation paid to slave-owners, the live dispute over whether the trade was still profitable in 1807, and Britain's later pressure on other nations to suppress it. Then run the inference in the other direction: what does the episode reveal about the strengths and limits of each account? In closing, take a position on whether honor is genuinely a third driver of moral change or only the mechanism through which argument and interest operate, and say what that implies for the standard three-way contrast.
