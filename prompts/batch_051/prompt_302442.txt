I find the hindsight-bias and narrative-fallacy account of historical judgment compelling: the same decision is rated more negligent when it ends badly than when it ends harmlessly, outcome knowledge inflates the felt ex ante probability of the outcome, and these effects survive instructions to ignore the outcome and appear even in experts assessing their own domain. The natural conclusion is that most judgments that the warning was clear, the catastrophe foreseeable, or the failure culpable are retrospective artifacts—creeping determinism presented as explanation—and should be distrusted by default. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that some cases escape this treatment: a specific and repeated warning that is ignored through ordinary carelessness seems blameworthy even if, by luck, nothing happens, and the debunking story seems to shield exactly the worst cases of culpable failure from criticism. I am not asking which side to pick. I want to know what would be required for the bias account to debunk the intuition in these hard cases, what would be required for the hard cases to force a principled limit on the account, and whether there is a reading of foreseeable at the time on which both the general debunking reach and the hard-case intuition survive.
