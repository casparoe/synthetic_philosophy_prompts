For my philosophy of science class I want a piece of satire to hand out alongside the Duhem and Quine readings, on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. Write me a deadpan self-help columm in the voice of an upbeat life-coach, titled something like "How to Keep Any Belief, Whatever the Evidence Turns Out to Be." It has to be written from inside the position: the columnist sincerely recommends the technique and never lets on that it's ironic. The jokes should all come from the actual epistemology, and I'd like the column to work through these five moves in order: (1) loading the core belief up front with ceteris paribus clauses or gerrymandered, grue-style categories so no observation ever quite touches it; (2) blaming an auxiliary assumption when a prediction goes wrong; (3) after the auxiliary collapses, swapping in a new one and announcing that the core belief was never really tested; (4) when a rival fits the data equally well, picking whichever one you already held and calling the tiebreaker "simplicity" while never defining it; (5) closing with the reassurance that evidence underdetermines theory anyway, so nobody actually has grounds to prove you wrong. Format the whole thing as exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no sign-off. No citations or bibliography needed — it's for classroom use, not publication.
