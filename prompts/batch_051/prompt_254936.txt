For a piece on how rational agents should handle evidence that does not single out a unique probability, I have been trying to reconstruct an argument against imprecise probabilities that a colleague gave me. The conclusion feels too strong, but I cannot find the exit. Here is the argument as I understand it.

(1) If your total evidence underdetermines your degree of belief in a proposition, rationality requires an imprecise credence: a set of probability functions each compatible with the evidence, such as the interval [0.4, 0.6] for the proposition that a coin is biased toward heads.
(2) A rational agent must nevertheless act, and to act on such a set she needs a decision rule. Any usable rule either selects one distinguished function from the set — say, the midpoint — or processes the whole set, for instance by maximizing the minimum expected utility across all compatible functions.
(3) Whichever rule is chosen, it yields, for any two acts, a verdict about whether one is at least as choiceworthy as the other; otherwise it would not suffice for action.
(4) Once a rule yields such a ranking, standard representation theorems from decision theory recover a single probability function, together with a utility function, that represents the ranking, at least as far as the agent's choices are concerned.
(5) So in all matters that count — choice — an imprecise agent is behaviorally indistinguishable from some precise Bayesian, and the interval is idle.
(6) But the interval was introduced precisely to capture an epistemic state that a point-valued probability misdescribes. If it is behaviorally idle, imprecise probabilities collapse into a notational variant of precise Bayesianism.
(7) That conclusion cannot be right: an agent whose evidence leaves genuine ambiguity differs in kind from one who has settled on a precise value, and a theory of rational belief should be able to say how.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1), since forcing a unique probability onto weak evidence seems to me to be a mistake. But I want to know which premise or step I must give up in order to keep it, and what that concession costs.
