I've been trying to get clear on a cluster of intuitions about who, if anyone, owns a creative work when the original owner has disappeared — and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind my case judgments, or tell me which judgments I need to overturn. I'm deliberately not asking about the law: assume each work below is still under copyright unless I say otherwise, and that all reasonable attempts to locate the owner have already failed. I want an account of the underlying entitlements, not a reading of any statute.

My verdicts, stated as directly as I can:

1. A 1995 adventure game whose studio went bankrupt in 1998 circulated on abandonware sites for years. In 2015, a holding company bought the residual rights at auction, resumed selling the game digitally, and began sending takedowns to those sites. My verdict: the takedowns are legitimate; hosting the game against the company's wishes is not fine.

2. A novella was published in a 1930s literary magazine by an author who died with no identifiable heirs; the magazine folded decades ago and no one has ever claimed the rights. A university library wants to scan and post the novella freely for scholars and the general public. My verdict: fine, and I would resist any demand that it be taken down if a claim were to surface later.

3. A photographer posted a body of striking landscape photographs online in the mid-2000s and then vanished; the account is dormant and every contact route is now dead. A publisher wants to use one of the photographs on a commercial book cover and cannot reach the photographer. My verdict: not fine; without the photographer's permission the publisher should not proceed, and if the photographer resurfaced I would side with them.

4. A 2002 online game's official servers were shut down in 2008; the developer and publisher have since dissolved, nobody sells the game or its server software anymore, and the client can only be installed from old discs. Fans have reverse-engineered the network protocol and run a private server to keep the game playable. My verdict: fine.

The pair that worries me is cases 2 and 3. Both involve an owner who is unlocatable and who has never, to my knowledge, asserted a claim — yet I treat the library as entitled to proceed and the publisher as not. I want to know whether that difference can be made principled, or whether it is just an anti-commercial bias doing the work.

Is there a single principle that captures these four verdicts, or nearly all of them? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Then say what it predicts about a case I deliberately haven't listed: a fan wants to post a full-length translation of a novel published in 1965 whose author is dead and whose estate cannot be found; the novel was never translated into the fan's language and is now out of print. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the only way to keep every verdict is to make the principle suspiciously ad hoc.

I'd like a committed answer: pick a definite principle (or a small set of candidates with a preferred one), defend it, and don't leave me with a menu of options at the end.
