Until this year I would have said I knew exactly what a historical actor is: a person, or a group of people, whose deliberate doings change the course of events. Then my county flooded, and I kept hearing otherwise sensible neighbors speak of the river as if it had made a decision, the drought as an actor in the disaster, and the reinsurance market as the only genuine agent of what happened afterward. That experience pushed me into the debate among historians and geographers about whether volcanoes, pathogens, and climate shifts should count as actors in human history, and about the charge that this way of speaking collapses into environmental determinism.

I would like a working definition of the term historical actor that can responsibly sort the following cases:

- a general whose order begins a war;
- a boulder that falls across a road and reroutes a supply convoy;
- the eruption that produced the Year Without a Summer;
- the plague bacterium during the Black Death;
- a river that slowly moves a national border;
- the gradual cooling of the Little Ice Age;
- a limited-liability corporation that successfully lobbies to weaken building codes.

The definition should do real work: it should not simply equate acting with causing, and it should state what, if anything, intention, responsiveness, or attribution contribute to the distinction.

Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your proposed definition; (2) the cases the definition clearly includes; (3) the cases it clearly excludes; (4) the hard cases where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and why; (5) either a revised definition or a defense of any surprising verdicts the original definition produced.
