I am trying to make my judgments about how consciousness is distributed across the animal kingdom principled, rather than driven by how brainy, vertebrate, or human-like a creature looks. I currently work with four criteria, all of which I take to bear on whether a creature is conscious, though I am unsure which are necessary:

(1) Unity: its sensory streams are integrated into a single global state or a single point of view, so that information from different modalities and different parts of the body is available to one subject at once.

(2) Valence: some of its states feel bad or good to it—they are states there is something it is like to be in—rather than being merely functional signals of damage or reward.

(3) Flexible learning: it can change its behavior on the basis of what has happened to it in a way that is context-sensitive and open-ended, rather than merely habituating or running a hardwired reflex adjustment.

(4) Minimal self: it distinguishes itself from the world in some minimal way, for example by responding differently to self-generated and other-generated stimulation, or by tracking its own internal condition as the thing that is helped or harmed by experience.

Now consider an organism I will describe without claiming that it exists. It is a soft-bodied marine animal, shaped like a flattened tube, with no brain, no head, and no central processing hub. Its nervous system has two parts. A fast-conducting nerve net covers the body and coordinates whole-body withdrawal. Separately, a denser mouth network of roughly thirty thousand neurons receives input from nociceptors, mechanoreceptors, and simple photoreceptors. The mouth network is a genuine learner. If a shadow is repeatedly paired with grit scraped against the animal's tissue, the animal comes to retract its mouth when the shadow alone arrives, and the response persists for weeks. The association forms only when the paired stimulus damages tissue; it does not form when the shadow predicts food. The same network controls crawling: after an injury to one side, the animal biases its crawling away from that side for weeks, and this bias disappears when nociceptor signalling is blocked while the tissue damage remains. The animal is, in that sense, exquisitely sensitive to harm.

But the two subsystems do not seem to share a readout. When several stimuli hit the body at once, there is no single winner: each local region follows the strongest stimulus in its own patch, and nothing about the animal's behavior suggests a unified picture of the whole body. It does not sleep. It does not distinguish one of its own body parts from inanimate food once both touch the mouth. If the connection between the mouth network and the body net is severed, each system keeps learning on its own, and neither shows any sign of needing information the other would have supplied.

Work through my four criteria honestly and say whether the organism satisfies each; then give an overall verdict on whether it is conscious, and say which criteria did the decisive work. Where a criterion is underdetermined by the case as described, say precisely what further fact would decide it. Then tell me what the case reveals about the criteria themselves: are any of them redundant, impossible to apply from the outside, or missing something—in particular, the possibility of valenced harm-sensitivity without a unified subject? Before I use this list to draw the line across the animal kingdom, should I revise it, and if so, how?
