I am giving a paper at a longtermist philosophy seminar next month, and I want to be prepared for the question period rather than surprised by it. The thesis I am defending is that expected value fanaticism should be rejected at the level of modeling practice, not by capping utility and not by falling back into risk aversion. The escape, as I will argue, is that the tiny subjective probabilities on which fanatical wagers depend are only coarsely specified: they are at best fixed to an interval that often spans orders of magnitude, and below a certain evidential resolution such intervals cannot bear the justificatory load that fanatical arguments place on them. Once the uncertainty is represented as a set of probability distributions rather than a single point-valued distribution, the fanatical option loses the unique expected value it needs in order to dominate; the non-fanatical option remains stable across the set, and the defensible conclusion is that choosing the non-fanatical option is at least permitted and plausibly required. I am not importing ambiguity aversion as an extra primitive; I am arguing that point-valued expected utility is an unjustified idealization whenever the numbers it compares are not distinguished by the agent's evidence. I also treat utility as unbounded and linear in value, so the argument cannot be accused of secretly reintroducing a bound or diminishing marginal value.

The audience is a small research seminar of about twenty people, mostly philosophers and decision theorists who work in or near effective altruism, longtermism, and global priorities. They are consequentialists and sophisticated expected utility theorists; they do not need the formalism explained. They are split on fanaticism itself: several are willing to accept, at least in principle, that a sufficiently high-stakes Pascal's-mugging-style wager would have to be taken; several believe fanaticism must be false but are dissatisfied with every existing way of escaping it; and one or two favor bounded utility or similarly decisive rules and will want to say I have arrived near their view without admitting it. What they care most about is that any rejection of fanaticism be principled rather than gerrymandered to dodge an uncomfortable conclusion, that it not collapse into ordinary risk aversion under another name, and that it not undercut the longtermist priorities they work on, which themselves often rely on small probabilities of very large amounts of value.

The format is a forty-minute talk followed by a twenty-five minute Q&A. Prepare me for the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. Phrase each objection the way a member of this audience would phrase it, give me a short reply and a note on the point where that reply is weakest, say whether I should concede and move on or hold the line, and roughly how much of the Q&A each reply is worth. Include the objection I should be most worried someone will ask.
