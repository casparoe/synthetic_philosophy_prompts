I've come around to the view that the Allied prosecutions at Nuremberg and Tokyo were justified despite the retroactivity problem, and that 'victors' justice' is mostly a political talking point rather than a serious jurisprudential objection. But I've never actually stress-tested this. What would have to be true for those trials to have been genuinely illegitimate? Work through the distinct versions of the charge — the nulla poena sine lege problem, the selectivity of who ended up in the dock, the tu quoque issues from Allied bombing and the Soviet alliance, the fact the tribunal's own charter defined the crimes after the fact, and the worry that 'crimes against humanity' and 'crimes against peace' were stretched beyond what their drafters had in mind — and tell me for each whether it's a decisive defeater or just an awkwardness a defender can absorb. I want them ranked from fatal to merely uncomfortable, including the ones you think a committed defender should themselves concede as counting against them. And where is the principled line on retroactive criminal law such that Nuremberg comes out legitimate but show trials and selectively-applied victors' courts don't? Be honest too about whether the standard defense has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's a problem for a justificatory argument of this kind.
