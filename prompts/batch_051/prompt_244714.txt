Hi — I'm in a course on religion and the rise of capitalism, and we're reading Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism. There's a passage that's been stuck in my head:

"Waste of time is thus the first and in principle the deadliest of sins. The span of human life is infinitely short and precious to make sure of one’s own election. Loss of time through sociability, idle talk, luxury, even more sleep than is necessary for health, six to at most eight hours, is worthy of absolute moral condemnation. It does not yet hold, with Franklin, that time is money, but the proposition is true in a certain spiritual sense. It is infinitely valuable because every hour lost is lost to labour for the glory of God."

What's been bugging me is how much this sounds like the productivity/hustle culture I see everywere: time-blocking apps that treat an empty slot in your calendar as a problem, people who describe unplanned hours as "wasted," sleep-optimization types who talk about 5 a.m. routines like a moral achievement, and companies that frame work as a "calling" and treat not being busy as a character flaw. Nobody I know doing this is a Calvinist, obviously. So I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments this practice actually presupposes. Does it still presuppose something like Weber's view — that time is infinitely valuable and idleness is a kind of sin — just with the theology removed? Or can you run the whole practice on a purely secular theory of time, agency, and self-management, where "wasted time" is just opportunity cost? And the part I really want to get at: if we swapped in a different underlying view, say one where leisure and unstructured time are intrinsic goods, what exactly would have to change about the practice — the apps, the office norms, the way people talk about rest — and which parts could stay the same? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, and also be honest about where the practice is underdetermined (i.e., several theories could license it).
