A source I interviewed for a piece on cost–benefit reasoning in public policy offered a defense of utilitarian tradeoffs that I haven't been able to shake. When I raised the familiar worry that aggregating welfare across people ignores the separateness of persons, she replied that society balancing gains and losses across different people is just like a single person balancing gains and losses across the stages of her own life. We unhesitatingly accept that a person can undergo a painful procedure now — surgery, training, years of saving — for the sake of a better future; the utilitarian calculus, she said, is simply that same operation spread across many lives. Audit this analogy rather than dismissing it. Give the correspondence table — what corresponds in the interpersonal case to the earlier self, the later self, the present cost, the future benefit, and the unity of a single life — then identify where the mapping breaks, being explicit about which features of the intrapersonal case the analogy quietly imports (a single subject who receives the compensation, memory and anticipation linking the stages, continuity of concern) and which of those disanalogies actually matter for the point she is trying to make. Finally, say whether the conclusion she wants to license — that tradeoffs across persons require no more justification than tradeoffs within a person — survives once the disanalogies are spelled out, and whether any nearby analogy would do better. End with a single line beginning "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence overall assessment of how far the analogy can carry that conclusion.
