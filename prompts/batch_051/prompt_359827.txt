Draft the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed Schopenhauerian and a committed Nietzschean on the moral status of pity (Mitleid) ought to have produced.

Take the Schopenhauerian position to be as follows. Compassion is the sole genuine moral incentive. In compassion, the agent sees past the apparent separateness of persons — the veil of individuation — recognizes their own inner nature in the sufferer, and acts only to relieve the other's suffering, not for any advantage to themselves. Take the Nietzschean position to be as follows. Pity is not a disinterested moral achievement but an affect embedded in a life-denying, ascetic way of judging existence; it transmits and preserves suffering rather than ending it, binds the sufferer to weakness, supplies the pitier with a covert sense of superiority, and teaches people to assess life by the amount of suffering it contains. The Nietzschean need not deny that pity exists or that it motivates; the dispute concerns what pity is, what it does, and whether it deserves the authority the Schopenhauerian gives it.

The deliverable should be a constructive record of what survives the collaboration, not a compromise. Organize it as follows.

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign together: what is being disputed; what the term Mitleid covers; which psychological and social facts about pity both accept as data; what each side explicitly is not claiming; and any normative or explanatory points on which they converge.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should contain the precise question at issue; the Schopenhauerian's formulation of their own view, worded as a Schopenhauerian would accept; the Nietzschean's formulation of their own view, worded as a Nietzschean would accept; and the condition that would settle that row, if it could be satisfied.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side on a substantive point. For each crux, specify a concrete case, experiment, or argumentative test whose relevance both parties agree on in advance — a described scenario or observation is fine, not a bibliography — and record each side's prediction or reading of that test ahead of time.

4. An assessment of which parts of the dispute, if any, are partly verbal: where pity, compassion, strength, or life-affirmation are being used in different senses, and how much of the disagreement would remain once those senses are fixed.

The statement should be exact enough that neither party can complain that their view has been made easy to defeat, and it should state honestly what each side would count as evidence or argument against their own view.
