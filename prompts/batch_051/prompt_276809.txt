I work in a group that studies how people's preferences shift when they are given information, time, and structured prompts to reflect. Our recurrent dispute is whether a long elicitation process uncovers something that was already there or actively builds it. We have been arguing about a sharp version of the divide: some of us maintain that each person has a stable attractor of "idealized" preferences — roughly what they would want if they knew more, thought longer, and were not moved by transient impulses, what some AI-safety researchers call their coherent extrapolated volition — and that a good procedure recovers this attractor. Others maintain that this is a mere manner of speaking: the procedure itself, including its choice of what information to present and what "reflection" means, helps construct the preferences it then reports.

I would like you to design an experiment that could genuinely pressure these two readings apart, rather than just showing that people change their minds when asked twice. The design should specify the participants or subjects, the manipulation, the measures, and the decision rule: what exact pattern of results would favor the attractor reading, and what pattern would favor the construction reading. It would be reasonable to compare several maximally different elicitation procedures, vary the order and framing of information, measure stability across time and contexts, or probe whether the results predict choices the procedure never rehearsed — but I want the design to be concrete enough that I could hand it to a colleague to run.

Finally, I want the philosophical accounting. Suppose the result comes out in favor of cross-method convergence: several very different procedures, after extensive information and reflection, settle on the same preferences. What would that actually license us to say? Would we be entitled to claim that we have measured the person's idealized preferences, or only that the methods share a particular ideal of what it is to reflect well? And if a residue remains that no experiment could settle, tell me whether that residue is a limitation of our measurement techniques or a defect — a genuine unclarity — in the very idea of a procedure-independent idealized preference.
