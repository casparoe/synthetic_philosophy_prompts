I help run a mathematics and philosophy club at my high school: about fifteen students, one hour, most with no probability background beyond the basics. Design a hands-on activity on Bertrand's paradox that makes participants feel, before it is named, that a randomly chosen chord is not one procedure but several, and that the principle of indifference does not tell us which set of equally possible things is the right one. I want the group to generate chords by three different physical procedures — choosing two endpoints on the circumference; choosing a radius and then a point along it; and choosing a midpoint somewhere inside the disk — and to discover that these give different fractions of chords longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Give precise rules and materials, a timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps each procedure to its probability (roughly one third, one half, and one quarter) and to the partition it silently treats as equally likely. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and give an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: why a defender of the principle of indifference might argue that only one of those partitions reflects the real symmetries of the circle.
