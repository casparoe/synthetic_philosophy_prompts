You are a professional historian of the Holocaust specializing in perpetrator studies. You have been retained as a neutral rapporteur for an adversarial collaboration between two opposing explanatory camps. Treat the historical background supplied below as a joint stipulation; do not append a bibliography, citations, or reading list.

The two parties. First, a situationist in the tradition of Christopher Browning: the participation of ordinary middle-aged German reservists in mass killings is best explained by contextual and group-psychological mechanisms — obedience to authority, conformity and peer pressure, careerism, division of labor, routinization, and gradual brutalization — with antisemitic ideology serving as at most an enabling background rather than the primary driver in each man. Second, an eliminationist in the tradition of Daniel Jonah Goldhagen: the same participation is best explained by the perpetrators' prior culture and character, in particular a widely shared and specifically German eliminationist antisemitism, a deep-seated cognitive and motivational orientation that already included a desire to eliminate Jews, with the wartime situation serving only to release or give opportunity to dispositions the men already had.

Jointly stipulated background. Members of Reserve Police Battalion 101, a unit composed largely of middle-aged reservists not selected for frontline combat, participated in the roundup and mass shooting of the Jewish population of Józefów in the General Government. Before the shooting, the battalion's commander offered the men the chance to refuse or take other duties; only a minority did so at first. The battalion subsequently took part in many further killing actions. The available testimony consists mainly of the men's postwar judicial statements, given long after the events and under legal pressure; both parties accept that this source base must be used critically.

Your task is to draft the joint statement this collaboration would produce, in exactly the following structure.

1. Agreed claims. Present a numbered list of substantive claims that both parties can sign without qualification. Do not merely list events; include at least some claims about what any adequate explanation of the battalion's actions must account for, and state, where the common ground is genuinely thin, that it is thin rather than papering over it.

2. Disagreements table. Provide at least four rows. Each row should give a neutral description of the disagreement, the situationist's own statement of the position in wording the situationist would accept, the eliminationist's own statement in wording the eliminationist would accept, and a label indicating whether the row is primarily empirical, interpretive, or partly verbal.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions whose resolution would actually move at least one party. For each crux, specify the comparison, case, or kind of evidence that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Draw on comparisons such as the minority who took the initial offer, the battalion's later actions when no comparable offer was made, the men's treatment of Jews as against their treatment of non-Jewish Poles, or the difference between units with different composition and standing orders.

Before drafting, think carefully and spend a short brainstorming pass over candidate formulations, especially where the two sides use the same words differently — ordinary, ideological, disposition, situation. At the end, state which parts of the disagreement the collaboration concludes are merely verbal, and give the reformulation, if any, that dissolves them.
