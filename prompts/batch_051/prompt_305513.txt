I have become persuaded by the structural-demographic account of macrohistory, associated with Peter Turchin's work on secular cycles, that certain large-scale political rhythms—above all, waves of state breakdown and civil violence in agrarian societies—are regular enough to be modeled quantitatively and, in principle, predicted. The central mechanism, as I understand it, is this. Population growth increases the supply of labor, which depresses real wages while raising the incomes of landowning elites. At the same time, population growth swells the number of people competing for a roughly fixed number of elite positions. This elite overproduction intensifies competition within the ruling class, which fragments political coalitions, weakens the state's fiscal position, and leaves it unable to contain popular immiseration. Fiscal crisis, intra-elite conflict, and mass grievance then converge in a period of widespread political violence and state collapse, and the resulting demographic contraction resets the cycle.

I want an honest map of what would count against this view, rather than a rehearsal of the standard historian's complaints. Specifically: which empirical findings would be decisive against it, and which merely awkward? What would a single disconfirming case have to look like, and can one case ever kill a theory this probabilistic and tendency-level? Which internal commitments are load-bearing—the wage-population link, the elite-overproduction mechanism, the fiscal-crisis channel, the supposed cyclicality—and which are dispensable? Has the view been qualified in ways that insulate it from refutation, for instance by explaining every apparent exception as a special local condition? And which parts of the story should a defender already concede are not yet supported? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view is testable in the sense that matters for a scientific claim, or whether it is better described as a useful interpretive framework that no particular historical outcome could actually falsify.
