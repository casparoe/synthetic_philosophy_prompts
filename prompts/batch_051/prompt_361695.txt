I work in the budget office of a small European coalition government. The new government agreement commits us to making wellbeing, not just GDP, the test of policy, and the finance ministry has drafted a concrete mechanism for the next budget cycle that my unit has to stress-test before it reaches cabinet. The proposal is this. The country's position in the World Happiness Report becomes a formal whole-of-government performance indicator. Each ministry's discretionary budget for the following year is adjusted by half a percentage point for every place the country moves up or down in the report's ranking, capped at five percentage points in either direction. A new Minister for National Happiness may send back any regulation whose required wellbeing impact statement projects a ranking loss of one or more places. And the national statistics office is barred from commissioning any rival wellbeing survey, so the government cannot improve its standing simply by substituting a friendlier measure. A public scoreboard tracks the ranking and its components.

My working understanding of the ranking, from my briefings, is that the headline figure is not a measure of people's moment-to-moment emotions. It is built mainly from a single survey question on which respondents rate their own lives on a 0-to-10 ladder, averaged across recent years, and the report then explains much of the country-to-country variation with a small set of predictors — income, healthy life expectancy, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceived corruption, broadly speaking. In effect, the proposal pays ministries to move a modelled self-evaluation and its correlates rather than to improve how people actually feel.

I am not asking whether the underlying goal is good; I am asking how this machine would actually run. Work through the incentives it creates for ministers, civil servants, statisticians, survey respondents, interest groups, and the public. Identify the main gaming points — survey translation and sampling, the timing of policies around each new edition, the statistical inputs that feed the ranking, migration, the choice of which groups' wellbeing gets attention — and say where gaming would be indistinguishable from genuinely improving measured wellbeing. Then map the second-order effects: policies that matter for wellbeing but do not move this ranking, and what happens to the indicator's credibility once a government is seen to be chasing it. End by sorting the failure modes into those that could be repaired by amending the mechanism and those that are inherent to adopting any published ranking as a target. Throughout, say how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
