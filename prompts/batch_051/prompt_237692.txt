A colleague defends thought experiments — intuition pumps — as a legitimate source of philosophical evidence by claiming that they are simply experiments carried out in the imagination: the scenario holds the background fixed, the described variation plays the role of a manipulation, and the reader's resulting judgment is the data. He uses the analogy to support a strong conclusion, that thought experiments can confirm or refute a philosophical claim in much the way a bench experiment confirms or refutes a hypothesis. I suspect the analogy is quietly doing most of the argumentative work. I want an audit of the comparison. Spell out the correspondences — what stands in for the apparatus, the test subject, the manipulation, the control condition, and the instrument reading — and then identify the disanalogies that matter. In particular, consider whether the judgment elicited by a case is produced by the very faculty the case is meant to examine, whether the vivid framing of a scenario functions like an uncontrolled confound, and what it means that the measuring instrument cannot be calibrated against anything independent. Then say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy was doing the work, what a more accurate comparison would be.
