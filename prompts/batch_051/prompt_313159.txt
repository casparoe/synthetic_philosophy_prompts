I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I garble this. I'm drafting a near-future novel where two states (and a couple of labs inside them) are racing to build transformative AI, and I've been reading Fearon's rationalist explanations for war so my characters aren't just cartoon villains. The way I understand it: war is puzzling because fighting is costly and risky, so there ought to be some bargain short of war that both sides prefer; Fearon explains actual war through something like private information plus incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. My question is wether this apparatus survives being moved from its original setting—interstate war—to an AI development race.

Here's the changed situation I'm worried about. In my novel the "prize" is being first to AGI: the winner gets a decisive, maybe permanent, strategic advantage, the loser can't realistically catch up, and any deal to share the technology or slow down is (a) hard to verify and (b) worth very little to the loser because a decisive advantage stops being decisive once you share it. War in the usual sense isn't happening yet; instead there's espionage, sabotage, compute-pile destruction, talent poaching, and propaganda. And the race itself is costly in a strange way: both sides might be raising the probability of an AI disaster that hits everyone, which I'm not sure fits the assumption that war is the costly thing and peace is the cheap alternative.

Does Fearon's framework survive this? I want to know, mechanism by mechanism: does private information with incentives to misrepresent do the same work here, does the commitment problem get sharper or just different, and is the indivisibility of "being first" the sort of thing Fearon was talking about or the sort he said wasn't really a separate explanation? Or does an AI race break one of the background conditions the rationalist story needs—like there being a genuine bargaining range, or war being costly enough to make a bargain preferable—so that the framework stops explaining and I'm left writing something more like a tragedy of the commons or a selection effect?

Don't give me plot or character advice. Tell me whether the argument survives the new setting, needs repair, or dies, and flag which premise of Fearon's you're leaning on. Also, please say how confident you are in what you claim and let me know the single point you're least sure about, because I'd rather not build the whole book on a misreading.
