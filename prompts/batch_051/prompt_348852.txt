I teach an upper-level ethics seminar with 14 students, and we've just done the torture vs. dust specks thought experiment. The number of people in the dust speck case is so huge that nobody can actually feel it; they all say "obviously the dust specks" and then move on. I want a structured 80-minute classroom activity that makes them actually run into the aggregation problem themselves, with real (small) stakes. The stakes should be genuinely annoying but trivial and safe: people standing for the rest of class, giving up their phone, cold hands, a loud phone alarm going off at random, that sort of thing. I want the rules or incentives arranged so that they face a sequence of choices between distributing tiny costs over everyone and concentrating a bigger cost on one or two volunteers, and then a debrief that maps what happened onto both the additive intuition and the "don't concentrate harms" intuition. There are alot of ways this could go wrong (people being noble, people being annoyed, the stakes being too small to bite), so tell me what will predictably happen the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the original torture vs. dust specks case a classroom game can't reproduce. I want them to come away seeing that the real force of the puzzle is in the tradeoff structure, not in the big numbers.
