In my reporting on the more longtermist corners of the effective-altruism and rationalist communities, I have repeatedly encountered a conversational move that seems to bring discussion to an abrupt end. I will be asking a researcher about our duties to people a million years from now, or about the expected value of trying to steer the far future of intelligent life, and my interlocutor will say, 'We are almost certainly living in a computer simulation,' as though that single sentence disposed of the whole topic. The suggestion appears to be that whether we are probably simulated has direct implications for how much the far future should matter to us: perhaps there is no genuine far future to be concerned with, perhaps it is not under our control, or perhaps our estimates of its significance are themselves suspect. Before I write about this line of thought, I need to understand exactly what the sentence is being used to claim, because I suspect it is really several different claims travelling under one banner.

I can see at least a few candidates. The sentence might be an empirical forecast about what future technological civilisations will be able and inclined to do, namely run vast numbers of ancestor simulations. It might be a probabilistic claim about the fraction of all conscious observers in reality who are simulated rather than biological. It might be a metaphysical claim about the substrate or causal structure of our own world, or an epistemological claim about how much confidence we may place in our perceptions of scale, time, and value. It might even be shorthand for a trilemma whose proper conclusion is a disjunction rather than an outright assertion, in which case the confident sentence smuggles in a hidden assumption. For each of these readings, I would like to know whether it is close to trivial, substantive and defensible, or probably false, and, crucially, which reading, if any, actually supports using the sentence to dampen concern about the far future. I would also like to know whether the simulation argument, properly understood, really does cut against far-future fanaticism, or whether the slogan is doing more work in these conversations than the underlying argument can bear.
