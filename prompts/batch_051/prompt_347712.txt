A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about the refusal policy built into most large language model assistants, and we ended up unable to say what it actually commits the designers to. The system will flatly decline some requests: it will not help a user plan suicide or produce instructions for self-harm, and it is designed to hold to that refusal even when the user insists. Yet the same system is strongly anti-paternalistic almost everywhere else: it will help the very same user gamble, pursue a visibly disordered diet, quit a job, end a relationship, or script a destructive confrontation, with little more than a generic disclaimer attached. What view of autonomy, competence, or well-being does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, is the asymmetry between acute and chronic self-harm a principled one, or does it quietly depend on a domain-specific view of competence, a particular story about hard versus soft paternalism, or something about the assistant's epistemic position? And what would have to change in the design if the underlying view of autonomy were replaced either by a domain-neutral respect for user authority or by a thoroughgoing welfare-maximizing paternalism?

Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a letter or email.
