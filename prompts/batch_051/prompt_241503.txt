During the COVID-19 pandemic, governments in many countries closed borders, confined residents to their homes, and shut down businesses under emergency powers, often acting by decree and seeking legislative or judicial approval only after the fact. In several of these countries, courts largely accepted the sequencing: they asked whether the emergency was genuine and whether the measures were necessary and proportionate, but they only rarely struck a measure down while the emergency was still running, and some restrictions were renewed again and again or quietly outlived the emergency. The same pattern is written into the standing rules of human rights law. States may derogate from certain guarantees in a public emergency, but only if the emergency is severe enough, the measures are strictly required and limited in time, certain guarantees can never be suspended, and the state must account for itself afterward. At the same time, everyone involved insists that emergency government is exceptional, and that normal legality will return as soon as possible.

I am trying to understand what this practice presupposes about the difference between legality and legitimacy. Does it presuppose that a genuine emergency loosens the normal link between the two, so that a procedurally irregular but genuinely necessary act can be legitimate without being legal? Or does it presuppose the opposite, that legitimacy is just legality applied with a heavier burden of justification and under temporary derogations? And what should we make of the fact that the same institutions insist on temporariness and proportionality while in practice letting emergencies drag on and executives act first? I would like the response to reconstruct the implicit theory or theories of legitimacy at work, to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival views would license it. Finally, please say what would most likely change your mind: which argument, case, or institutional reform would show that the practice rests on different commitments than the ones you reconstruct, and where your interpretation is most likely to fail.
