I was talking with a friend about two colleagues who made opposite career moves, and I noticed an asymmetry in the way people described them that I cannot decide how to assess. Ana left a well-paid corporate practice to become a public-interest lawyer, accepting a steep cut in pay; people described her choice as agonizing and values-driven, and they treated it as a hard choice in which she rightly gave meaningful work more weight than money. Ben left a public-interest office for the same kind of corporate practice, giving up work he found deeply meaningful; people described him as selling out, and almost no one treated his choice as hard — it was seen as a straightforward, faintly discreditable pursuit of income. Structurally, however, the two decisions look symmetrical: each person traded a large quantity of one good for a large quantity of another, and neither good can be straightforwardly measured against the other. On an influential account of hard choices found in recent philosophy, the alternatives in such cases are said not to be incomparable in the sense of being impossible to compare at all, but rather "on a par" — neither one better than the other nor exactly equal — such that a commitment or exercise of agency, rather than the discovery of a preexisting reason that already favored one side, settles the choice. If that is right, it is hard to see why the direction of the trade should matter as much as it evidently does in ordinary judgment. Is there a defensible principle that makes Ana's move a paradigm of a value-guided hard choice and Ben's a transparent mercenary transaction? Or is this asymmetry simply a status bias in favor of certain goods over others, which a clear-eyed account of incommensurable values should lead us to abandon? I would like the strongest candidate justification you can find, tested against nearby cases (for example, Ben may want the salary chiefly to pay for a disabled relative's care, while Ana may be partly moved by the prestige of visible selflessness), and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped.
