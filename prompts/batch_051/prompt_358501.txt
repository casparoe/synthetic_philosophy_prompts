Ok here's the situation. I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and in a couple weeks I'm giving a paper at a law school's public law colloquium. The paper defends the claim that the designated-survivor device should be kept, but with one big change: we should stop keeping the *identity* of the survivor secret and only keep the *location* secret. The current practice is that the president quietly names a cabinet secretary to stay away from the State of the Union, inaugurations, and similar events where the whole line of succession is in one room, on the theory that if the room gets hit someone still survives to be president. My argument is that this is a continuity mechanism that borrows democratic legitimacy it hasn't actually got: the public could end up with a president they never had a chance to evaluate as an emergency successor, and the secrecy makes that worse, not better. The fix I defend is a fixed, publicly known order of succession for the survivor role (obviously not the person's location on the night), with the first stand-in selected through a process with real democratic input rather than presidental discretion. I want the paper to land the point that you can get most of the security benefit without the democratic black box.

The audience is mostly constitutional lawyers -- some originalists, some separation-of-powers and admin law folks -- plus two or three democratic theorists, and no emergency-management or continuity-of-government people. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging, phrased the way they'd actually ask them. For each: a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. And include the question I should be praying nobody asks -- I have a sinking feeling it's the one about wether making the successor public doesn't just make them a target in a way that defeats the whole point.
