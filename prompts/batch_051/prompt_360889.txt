It strikes me as somewhere between a behaviorist and a relativist reading, which sits uneasily with what I know about the normative character of his project, but I can't locate where exactly it goes wrong:

"Brandom's central thesis is that the meaning of an expression is its inferential role: what a speaker is committed and entitled to by using it, and what would commit or entitle someone to use it. To understand 'The apple is red' is to master the network of inferences licensing, for example, the move to 'The apple is colored.' Since inferences like red-to-colored are material rather than formally logical, and since Brandom takes them to be partly constitutive of the concept red, he treats them as semantic rather than as merely collateral world-knowledge. On this view, meaning is nothing over and above the inferential habits a linguistic community actually displays. For the same reason, Brandom is an anti-representationalist: he denies that words refer to objects in any theoretically useful sense, and regards the notion of reference as a ladder we can kick away. An immediate consequence is that if two communities accept different inferences, the sentence just means something different in each, and there is no standard beyond a community's own inferential practice by which its practice could be counted incorrect."
