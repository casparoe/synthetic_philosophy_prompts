Write the final joint statement of a year-long adversarial collaboration between two epistemologists, one a conciliationist and the other a steadfast person, about peer disagreement. Their shared test case is this: two carefully matched peers, Ara and Ben, independently study the same body of evidence for a philosophical thesis P, which is the conclusion of a long armchair argument that both accept as formally valid at each step. Ara ends with high confidence in P; Ben ends with high confidence in not-P. Neither can point to any specific flaw in the other's reasoning, and each rates the other as slightly better than themselves at this kind of step-by-step argument. The conciliationist holds that rationality requires both to reduce confidence substantially; the steadfast person holds that both may rationally retain most of their confidence.

Produce the document in four sections, in this order, with the lengths indicated:

1. Agreed claims — 8 to 10 numbered claims about peerhood, the evidential situation, and what Ara and Ben each rationally could or could not do before learning of the disagreement. Each claim should be one or two sentences, substantive, and worded so both parties sign it without wincing.

2. Residual disagreements — a table of 4 to 6 rows. Column 1: a proposition worded neutrally, so neither side would object to the sentence as a statement of what is at issue. Column 2: the conciliationist's position on it. Column 3: the steadfast position on it. Each cell one or two sentences. If a row turns out to be a merely verbal disagreement, say so and replace it with a better-worded proposition.

3. Cruxes — two or three sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. One crux should concern whether higher-order evidence about one's own disagreement is just more evidence to be weighed by one's own first-order reasoning, or imposes a structural constraint that one's first-order reasoning cannot override. Another should concern whether a thinker may treat their direct acquaintance with their own deliberative process as a legitimate private asymmetry for demoting an apparent peer. For each crux, state what each side would have to concede if the crux were resolved against them.

4. Tests and predictions — for each crux, a concrete diagnostic case or argument both sides agree bears on it. For each test, record in advance what the conciliationist predicts and what the steadfast person predicts about the test's outcome or the correct verdict. Include at least one test that is not simply a reheated version of the opening Ara–Ben case.

Do not cite the literature or name real authors; formulate everything from first principles.
