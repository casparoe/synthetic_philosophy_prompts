I work in reinforcement learning and evolutionary robotics, and I recently came across a taxonomy from philosophy of mind that distinguishes four kinds of creatures. Darwinian creatures have fixed behavioral designs installed by natural selection; the individual does not learn, though its lineage does. Skinnerian creatures add trial-and-error learning: actions followed by reinforcing outcomes become more frequent. Popperian creatures add an internal model in which candidate actions can be tested and rejected before execution, so that bad hypotheses are eliminated without the creature having to pay the real-world cost. Gregorian creatures add tools, especially words and other shared cultural artifacts, which let creatures express, test, and refine behavioral designs in an external medium instead of entirely inside one head.

Restate this taxonomy in the vocabulary I use daily: policies, value functions, credit assignment, model-based versus model-free reinforcement learning, evolutionary search versus gradient-based search, world models, planning, imitation learning, and cumulative cultural evolution. Assume I am fluent in that vocabulary; do not define terms for me.

For each kind of creature, translate the claim honestly, marking what carries over cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary. Then give me a residue report: what did not survive the translation, and is that because my framework lacks something the taxonomy is tracking, or because the taxonomy was partly a vivid metaphor rather than a precise distinction? In particular, I suspect my vocabulary will compress the upper rungs by treating another agent's demonstrated behavior as just additional data for the same credit assignment problem. Is that compression a feature or a loss? Please answer in American English.
