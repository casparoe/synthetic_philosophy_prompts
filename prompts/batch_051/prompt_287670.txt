In a seminar on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, we have been discussing the intentional stance: the idea, associated with Dennett, that we predict and explain a system's behavior by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals. A student in the course has pressed a strong version of the instrumentalist reading. He claims that, for the AI systems we can actually build or reasonably foresee, no observation could ever show that a system literally holds the belief we attribute to it — for instance, that a chess engine believes its position is lost, or that a customer-service model believes the user intends a refund — as opposed to merely behaving in ways that are usefully predicted and explained by that attribution. His argument is that the intentional stance is deliberately indifferent to what is going on inside the system, so all the evidence can do is make our predictions better or worse; it can never rise to a verdict about whether the belief is real. I think he is probably overstating the case, but I cannot specify what a discriminating test would even look like.

Propose the strongest test or observation you can for whether such a system genuinely has the attributed belief rather than merely being well-modeled under the intentional stance. You may idealize the system within reason, and you may appeal to interventions on internal states, out-of-distribution generalization, behavioral experiments, or anything else you can motivate. State clearly which possible result would favor the instrumentalist reading and which would favor the realist reading. Then say exactly what the test would not settle even in principle — in particular, whether a positive result establishes belief or merely establishes the presence of an internal state that does the same predictive work the stance needs, and whether your answer rests on a substantive theory of belief or only on a decision about how to use the word.
