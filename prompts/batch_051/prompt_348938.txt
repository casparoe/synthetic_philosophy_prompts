I'm a software engineer on a platform team, and I've been asked to arbitrate a disagreement over one of our test suites. We maintain a service that generates short, random-looking identifiers for objects in our system. A colleague recently added a test that rejects any identifier containing visually suspicious features—four or more repeated characters, long palindromic substrings, or runs of consecutive byte values. His view is that a genuinely random source would not produce strings with such obvious patterns, so the test is a cheap tripwire for a weak entropy source. Another colleague argues that the test is meaningless, because for any fixed identifier length, each individual string is exactly as improbable as any other, and so rejecting the pattern-laden ones is pure superstition that will occasionally fail in production. I find both arguments too strong, but I cannot articulate what is wrong with either. I have no formal background in philosophy, and I want to make a defensible engineering decision rather than rely on intuition. Can a specific finite output like one of our identifiers ever be called random or non-random on its own, or does that judgment only apply to the process that produced it? If it is a judgment about the process, what exactly is the pattern test testing, and under what conditions would it give real evidence rather than noise?
