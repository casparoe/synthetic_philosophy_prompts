My coworkers got into a lunch debate about whether an oath is really different in kind from an ordinary promise, and it turned into a long thing about the gods. Ana said the ancient Greeks understood something modern contract thinking loses: that some promises are backed by more than just your own character or reputation. She pulled up a passage from Hesiod's Theogony, lines 793–803, as evidence. Here's the passage:

> For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus.

Ana's move was: Hesiod is describing an oath that works because it has a real enforcement mechanism built in. Even the gods need Styx because their ordinary promises can be broken without automatic consequence. Her claim was that an oath is just a promise plus a reliable penalty for breach, and that we still implicitly get this: legal contracts have damages clauses, wedding vows have legal and social consequences. So an oath isn't some sacred mystery, it's just a promise with teeth.

Dave disagreed. He said that's exactly backwards. If the only reason you keep an oath is the penalty, then you haven't understood oaths at all. He said the point of swearing on Styx is that the oath creates a new kind of reason, independent of the penalty. The gods aren't just doing a cost-benefit analysis with a one-year coma and nine years of exile as the cost. If they were, then the moment the penalty became uncertain, nothing would bind. But the oath binds because it's sworn, not because it's enforced. He added that the Hesiod passage actually hurts Ana's case: notice the text says the punishment follows "whoever... is forsworn," so the oath-breaking logically precedes the penalty. The poem assumes the category of forswearing is already intelligible. You can't explain what makes the act wrong by pointing to the punishment, because the punishment is for an act already classified as a violation.

Ana replied that Dave is just asserting a special normativity without explaining what it is. She said: imagine a society where everyone forgets whether Styx water has any effect. On Dave's view, would the gods still be bound? If yes, then the enforcement was never doing any work, and the oath is just a promise with extra steps. If no, then the oath's force is just the enforcement after all. She said Dave can't have it both ways.

Dave came back: the mistake is thinking of the force of an oath as either purely internal (character) or purely external (penalty). The Styx oath is neither. It's a public act of placing yourself under a regime where a failure is met with something. The enforcement isn't a separate consequence added on; it's part of what it is to swear that kind of oath. You can't subtract the consequence and still be left with the same speech act. So saying "if we remove the penalty, is it still binding?" is like asking "if we remove the 'till death' clause from a marriage vow, is it still the same vow?" It's a different act.

I was trying to follow this but they both got heated and I couldn't decide. Please adjudicate move by move: who's right about what, and who is more right overall? Especially: does the Hesiod passage support Ana or Dave, and can you make sense of Dave's "the enforcement is part of the act" idea without it collapsing into Ana's "promise with teeth"?
