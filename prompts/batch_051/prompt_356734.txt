I'm part of a reading group working through the epistemic foundations of game theory, and the phrase "common knowledge of rationality" has started to function as a kind of conversation-stopper, even though I am increasingly convinced that different members mean different things by it. The argument that brought this to a head concerned a one-shot, two-player stag-hunt game with two strict equilibria. One member claimed that because rationality is common knowledge, each player can reconstruct the other's reasoning and the players must therefore choose the payoff-dominant equilibrium where both hunt stag. Another replied that common knowledge of rationality can at best deliver rationalizability — the strategies that survive iterated deletion of never-best-responses — and that in a stag hunt both hunting stag and hunting hare are rationalizable for both players, so no unique action is selected. The first then insisted that the only missing ingredient was a common prior, while a third member objected that a common prior would still not suffice, because Nash equilibrium requires the players' conjectures about one another's actions to be mutually correct, which is a condition on beliefs about strategies, not on rationality alone.

I would like a careful breakdown of the distinct precise principles that "rationality is common knowledge" might be covertly expressing — for example: the infinite iterated hierarchy of "everyone knows that everyone knows... that everyone is rational"; mutual knowledge only up to some finite level; common knowledge of the game's structure as distinct from common knowledge of the players' rationality; common knowledge that everyone maximizes expected utility given their beliefs; common knowledge of the strategy profile; and any other readings worth separating. For each, say how faithful it is to the slogan, whether it is a substantive and defensible assumption or close to tautological, and what exactly it licenses. Then say which reading would actually be needed to make the argument to the payoff-dominant equilibrium go through, and whether adding a common prior is enough to close the gap between rationalizability and that conclusion.
