At work, a colleague defended restrictive immigration and aid policy with a lifeboat analogy: a wealthy country is a lifeboat with fixed carrying capacity, surrounded by swimmers who would like to board; taking everyone aboard exceeds the boat's capacity, and then everyone aboard, including those already there, drowns. He concluded that a country is justified in strictly limiting admissions and in giving its own citizens' welfare priority over aid to outsiders, because the alternative is shared ruin. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and the capacity-limit reasoning feels natural to me, but I suspect the analogy is quietly importing features of a lifeboat that a country does not have. Could you lay out the correspondences between the lifeboat and a wealthy country, identify where the mapping breaks down, distinguish the breakdowns that matter for his conclusion from the merely incidental ones, and say whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table? If the lifeboat is a bad picture here, what would a better analogy be?
