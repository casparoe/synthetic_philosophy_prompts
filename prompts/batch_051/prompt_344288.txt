I've been trying to get my views about economic justice to cohere, and going by my gut on cases has left me with an untidy pile. Start with the old Nozick basketball case about Wilt Chamberlain: a million people each freely pay a quarter to watch him play, and he ends up $250,000 richer while everyone else is just a quarter poorer. I'm fine with that. The final distribution is about as unequal as anything, but everyone moved from a fair starting point voluntarily, so I don't see the injustice. But if the government taxed everyone a quarter and handed the same player $250,000 as an excellence grant, I'd be mad, even though the final spread of money is literally the same. So I apparently think the history of how holdings came about matters, and not just the pattern.

In other cases I am less sure. If someone embezzles $5 from their job, invests it, and over twenty years builds a decent business dealing honestly with everyone, I still think their later partners and heirs don't hold it cleanly; the original wrong feels like a stain that never washes out. But when a fortune traces back to slave labor or land theft generations ago, I cant bring myself to say the present owners are just unjustly holding all of it, the wrong feels too ancient and the chain too tangled. I think gifts are perfectly fine even when they create huge inequality: if I give my whole paycheck to a stranger, the resulting distribution isn't fair but I wouldn't call it unjust. Yet I also think it's fine for the state to make me give a small slice of my paycheck to someone needier, which sounds like a forced transfer I accepted.

A friend says Nozick's entitlement theory explains my first reactions: holdings are just when they come from a just starting point through repeated just transfers, and no patterned principle can survive free exchange. But that seems to conflict with my acceptance of taxation for need, and with my awkwardness about old wrongs. Is there a principle that captures all these verdicts, or do I have to throw some out? What would the principle you propose say about the case I keep going back and forth on: someone inherits a house originally bought with money from a fraudulent loan three generations ago, where every transaction since was an ordinary sale. And which of my verdicts can it honestly not accommodate?
