At a recent AI-governance meeting, one of the organizers repeatedly explained the problem of competitive pressure through an analogy. In his version, Moloch is not a literal deity but a personification of the trap in which each lab, acting on a locally rational calculation, finds that it must spend down its safety margin because otherwise a faster competitor will ship first and capture the market, and the lab that hesitates is selected out of the race. The analogy, he said, shows that voluntary coordination among many labs is structurally unstable: just as no parent in the old story could safely refuse the sacrifice while others were complying, no lab can unilaterally slow down while others accelerate, and therefore the only stable way out is a single enforcement mechanism that can bind all parties at once. I find the analogy persuasive in its broad outline but suspicious in its details. Please give me an audit of how far it holds. In particular: (1) set out the mapping explicitly, item by item, between the Moloch story and the multipolar-race situation; (2) identify the features of the Moloch picture that the analogy quietly imports into the target but that may be absent there, such as a single sacrificial object, a shared altar, symmetric actors, and an external enforcer waiting in the wings; (3) separate the disanalogies that matter for the organizer's specific conclusion from the ones that are incidental; and (4) say whether his conclusion that only a single enforcement mechanism can work survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if this one distorts the available options.
