I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've only been doing philosophy seriously for about two years, so forgive me if I'm mangling Abelard. The position I keep coming back to is his claim that sin sits entirely in consent of the will, not in the outward act. A passing impulse to do wrong isn't yet sin on his telling; what makes it sin is that the will consents to it. The deed itself, and whatever follows from it, adds nothing to the sin. Two people can fully resolve to kill; if one is stopped by pure chance, he's sinned no less than the one who succeeds. I find this elegant: it makes blame depend only on what's under the agent's control, and it gives a tidy decision procedure — look at consent, not outcomes.

But here's the intuition I can't get rid of. Take two agents, equal in malice, equal in resolve. One shoots and kills; the other shoots and, by a fluke, the bullet is stopped by a lighter in the breast pocket, or the victim stumbles at the last second. I want to say the one who killed is more blameworthy — or at least that the completed killing is a worse thing about the agent, not merely a worse thing for the victim. The law certainly treats them differently, and I don't beleive that difference is obviously irrational. So I've got a theory that says the difference is morally irrelevant, and an intuition I find firm, maybe firmer than Abelard's principle.

I know the standard moves are: debunk the intuition as an outcome-bias or a hangover from how we have to organize punishment; amend the theory by distinguishing inner guilt from the objective wrongness of the act and its results; or show the two are compatible, maybe by rethinking what 'consent to the act' includes once the act has actually been done. What I want is not just a verdict. I want to know what would justify each of these moves, and which of them you'd actually take.
