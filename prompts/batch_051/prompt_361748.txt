I have been thinking about the free will debate through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I would like a dialogue that works through it at the level of the actual argumentative moves rather than slogans. The Basic Argument runs roughly as follows: in any situation you act as you do because of the way you are — your beliefs, desires, values, and character; so to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the relevant mental respects. But to be responsible for the way you are, you would have to have chosen to become that way. Yet any such act of self-choosing would itself have to issue from the way you already were, and so the requirement merely reappears one step back; the chain never bottoms out in an act of self-creation. The conclusion is that nobody can be ultimately morally responsible for anything, since every attempt to make oneself responsible for one's own constitution either regresses or terminates in a fact about oneself that was, from one's own point of view, a matter of luck.

Please write a dialogue between two well-informed and evenly matched interlocutors about this. One defends the Basic Argument and sharpens it when challenged; the other is a compatibilist who denies that responsibility requires self-creation and holds that moral responsibility can instead be grounded in something like reflective endorsement of one's motives or reasons-responsiveness. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: the characters should respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past one another, and each should be pushed at least once to refine, grant, or change a claim. The compatibilist should try to show that the regress is an artifact of a questionable demand, while the Strawsonian should try to show that any compatibilist ground of responsibility still leaves the agent hostage to luck in the very same respect. Do not resolve the disagreement artificially; let the dialogue end at a point of maximal clarity about why they still differ.
