In my school philosophy club we were doing a session on dementia and advance directives, and two people got into an argument that none of us could settle. The case was about a woman who wrote an advance directive years ago saying that if she developed severe dementia she would refuse life-saving antibiotics, but now she has dementia, seems genuinely content, and when she caught pneumonia the care home wanted to treat it. The question was whether her earlier directive should still count.

Josh said we should follow the directive. She made it when she was fully competent, and the whole point of an advance directive is that you get to decide now what sort of life you'll except later. If we ignore it, we're turning her into someone she explicitly said she did not want to be. Respecting a person means taking their serious, informed decisions about their own future seriously, even once they can't restate them.

Priya said he was treating the woman as if she's one continuous person, but severe dementia can destroy the memories, values and plans that made the earlier decision hers. The person in the care home isn't the 'her' who signed the form in any sense that matters. Why should a note written by what is basically a stranger control whether this contented person gets to carry on living? Her current wellbeing is the only thing actually in front of us.

Josh came back that if you allow identity to dissolve that easily, no commitment can ever bind: I can't bind my future self to a promise, a marriage, a mortgage or to bringing up children, because by then I'll have changed. The advance directive exists for exactly the situation where you lose the ability to decide, and if it doesn't hold there it's useless.

Priya said promises get renegotiated and marriages can end, but those situations don't involve one version of you medically overriding a later version who is happy right now. She admitted the earlier self's 'critical interests' should count for something, but said that once the person in front of you has settled into a life she seems content with, her present enjoyment matters more than a decision made by a person who no longer exists. The earlier self can't just claim the body from beyond the grave of her old personality.

Nobody would budge, and the rest of us split into camps. Can you go through this exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and what both of them are missing? And overall, who has the stronger case? Please write your answer in British English.
