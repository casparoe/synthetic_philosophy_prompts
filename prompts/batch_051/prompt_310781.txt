Lately I have been trying to pin down a pattern in how people assign value to states of mind, and I suspect it may be a recognized position, or perhaps several positions that I am running together. The clearest case is this: a state with rich, intense pleasure but no consciousness at all seems to have no value, while a state with full consciousness but completely flat feeling seems to contribute nothing either; yet the goodness of a conscious, pleasant state does not look like the sum of its consciousness and its pleasantness. It looks more like one factor multiplying the other — consciousness determines whether the happiness counts at all, while happiness determines what the consciousness is worth. The multiplication shows up elsewhere too: the same modest happiness distributed across a thousand barely-conscious beings seems to amount to less than that happiness realized in one fully conscious being, in a way that simply adding the happiness does not capture. But I have also encountered systems in which goods are straightforwardly added up, and others in which one kind of good simply dominates another, so that no amount of the lower good makes up for losing a little of the higher. What I want to know is whether this multiplicative view — value as roughly consciousness times happiness, or something in that vicinity — has an established name and a clear statement. How is it different from the view that only pleasure matters, and from the view that value just adds up? And what does it commit one to in awkward cases, such as happiness that is only barely conscious, or a very intense experience that is only dimly pleasant? I am less interested in a bare label than in a precise account of what the view is, what it excludes, and where its edge cases are.
