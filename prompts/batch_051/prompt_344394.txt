I will be teaching a 75-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and the philosophy of science fiction; the class has 16 students. I want a full-session classroom game in which the students discover the dark forest hypothesis for themselves before I name it or explain it. By the end, they should see how a cooperative arrangement that everyone can recognize would leave everyone better off can still fail once detection is irreversible and intentions cannot be verified, and they should come to feel, from inside their own choices, why silence and preemptive attack can come to seem rational rather than paranoid.

The hypothesis I want the session to build toward runs roughly as follows. Two axioms: survival is the overriding priority of any civilization, and civilizations expand while the universe's total matter and energy stay fixed. Two further conditions: the chain of suspicion — no civilization can verify another civilization's true intentions, and each knows that its own intentions cannot be verified by others — and the possibility of technological explosion, so that a civilization harmless today may become dangerous too quickly for a delayed response to be safe. The supposed consequence is that any civilization that reveals itself invites destruction, and that the rational response to detection is to stay hidden or strike first, never to make contact.

Design a face-to-face game for the full 16-student group, runnable in roughly 40-45 minutes, followed by a debrief of 25-30 minutes. The rules and payoff structure should do the teaching: participants should run into the problem themselves — the appeal of remaining hidden, the risk of revealing anything, the logic of striking someone who has not threatened you, the way trust decays when intentions cannot be checked — before I name the hypothesis. Use points, not real money. Tell me the materials, the setup, the rules, a payoff table, a phase-by-phase timeline, and the few sentences I as facilitator should say at the start. Give me a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the two axioms and the two conditions, and that handles the likely objection that no real civilization would act this way on a mere suspicion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example, collusion outside the allowed channels, a group too trusting for the dangerous equilibrium to emerge, players treating it as comedy — and how to handle each. Be explicit about which feature of the full hypothesis a classroom game cannot reproduce and will need to be supplied in the debrief.
