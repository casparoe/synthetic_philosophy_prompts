I have been trying to sort out the debate between evidential and causal decision theory, and a long explainer I found while reading about Newcomb's problem introduced me to a position it calls "evidential cooperation in large worlds." The position matters to me because, if its argument works, it would change how I think about voting, donating, and other choices in which an individual's causal impact is small. The article summarizes the view roughly as follows. Evidential decision theory says you should choose the action that would be the best news about the world, not the action that causally produces the best outcome. Evidential cooperation in large worlds is then the further thesis that, since the world contains billions of agents whose choices are correlated with yours through shared genes, shared culture, and shared lines of argument, your choosing to vote, cooperate, or donate is evidence that many similar agents will choose in the same way. The practical upshot, according to the article, is that you should act as if you were deciding for everyone who reasons like you: if a policy would be disastrous when millions of people like you adopted it — for instance, declining to donate on the grounds that your own contribution is negligible — then your adopting it is evidence that they will adopt it too, so what you are really evaluating is the shared adoption rather than your individual act. The article says defenders draw the conclusion that you often should do whatever you would want all like-minded people to do, even when your personal causal impact is tiny. It then adds that critics dismiss the whole position as disguised magical thinking, since if you cannot causally affect those other people, the bare fact that your choice would be correlated with theirs cannot make one option genuinely better than another.

A friend who works on formal epistemology said the article blurs a few distinctions that the relevant people take seriously, but she wanted me to work out which ones myself. I would like an accuracy audit of the article's summary. Which parts would a defender of evidential cooperation in large worlds accept, which parts conflate claims that should be kept apart or overstate a more modest thesis, and is the critics' closing point a genuine refutation or does it simply presuppose the causal theory of decision that evidentialists reject? Would the article's conclusion — that the whole idea can be dismissed — survive if the summary were corrected?
