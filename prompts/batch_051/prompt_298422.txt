In a discussion about whether genuinely altruistic action exists, a classmate kept shutting down counterexamples with the one-liner that people only do what they want to do. When I pressed him, he seemed to move between several distinct claims: that a person always acts on whatever desire is strongest; that every action is ultimately chosen because it gives the agent some satisfaction or avoids some discomfort; and that no one ever aims at another person’s good for that other person’s sake alone. He treated these as if they were equivalent, and from them he concluded that psychological egoism is true and therefore ethical egoism needs no special defense.

I want to be able to separate this slogan into the precise theses it could be expressing, and to say, for each, whether it is a tautology, an empirical claim, or a substantive philosophical thesis, and how plausible it actually is. In particular, lay out the readings as a menu that distinguishes at least the following possible ideas: acting from desire, acting aimed at one’s own felt satisfaction, acting to maximize one’s expected self-interested benefit, and acting without the good of another as an ultimate end. For each reading, say whether it really establishes psychological egoism, and whether it would support treating ethical egoism as the default moral position. Also indicate where, if anywhere, the classic distinction between desires whose *object* is one’s own good and desires whose *satisfaction* merely happens to be one’s own experience becomes relevant, and whether my classmate’s slide between everyday choices and ultimate ends is doing real work in his argument.
