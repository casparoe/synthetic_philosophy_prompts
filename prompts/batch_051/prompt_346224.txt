At my reading group I keep noticing a two-step move I don't have a word for, and it's starting to bug me. Someone argues for a moral principle using a little made-up scenario: a runaway trolley heading for five people, a famous unconscious violinist hooked up to a hospital patient, a machine that lets you live a pleasant life while your real body floats in a tank. The story is loaded with concrete specifics, and those specifics are what make the intuitive verdict feel obvious — you react before any argument is really on the table. Then someone else adjusts one detail: what if the violinist only needs the patient for a week, or the machine lets you log out whenever you want? The first person says that tweak is just clutter; the case is designed to isolate one variable, and adding noise defeats the point. What bothers me is the asymmetry: details that push the intuition in the author's favor are silently doing argumentative work, while details that would push the other way get ruled out as irrelevant decoration. Is there a name for this specific pattern — the asymmetric treatment of concrete detail in philosophical scenarios? I'm not looking for the general label for made-up cases in philosophy; I know that literature exists. I'm after the narrower thing: vivid specifics are load-bearing on the way in, but dismissed as noise on the way back. How does that differ from ordinary idealization, which seems to me like it genuinely strips out complications that don't matter, and from plain framing, where the wording rather than the content does the nudging? And if nobody has named it, what would you call it, what's the tightest definition, and where are the boundary cases — the situations where it's genuinly hard to tell whether someone is cleaning up a case or quietly rigging it?
