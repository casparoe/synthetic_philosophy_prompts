In my seminar on Reformation theology this term, two classmates, Miriam and Daniel, fell into an argument after class about whether the Protestant principle of sola scriptura can actually do the work it is supposed to do, and I have been trying to work out which of them is right. Miriam began by saying that the principle is unstable: if Scripture is the sole infallible authority, then every appeal to the text's plain meaning is still somebody's reading of the text, so either one has quietly installed a second authority — the individual conscience, the interpretive community, or a tradition — or one is left with no way to settle competing readings. Daniel replied that this conflates an authority with the means of access to it: a judge interpreting a statute does not thereby become the law, and fallible readers can still be governed and corrected by a text that they themselves must interpret. He added that the regress objection is not specific to Scripture, because if the church is the authority, then one must interpret the church's rulings, and the objection simply recurs at the next level.

Miriam answered that a living authority can answer back. A fixed text cannot issue a clarification when a new dispute arises, so in practice Protestant controversies are settled by whichever faction's reading happens to prevail institutionally, which is exactly not Scripture alone. Daniel answered that papal and conciliar rulings are themselves disputed texts, so a living voice does not halt the regress but only relocates it; he also said that on the Reformation view Scripture is clear on whatever is necessary for salvation, that disagreement on other matters need not divide the church, and that the point of sola scriptura is not to provide a decision procedure but to refuse infallibility to any human institution. Miriam came back by saying that this merely moves the problem again, since someone must judge which matters are necessary, and whether a given controversy touches them.

They eventually turned to me to referee, and I could not. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the dispute as it actually happened: which of their specific claims are sound, which are overstated, whether either of them escapes the interpretive regress they accuse the other of falling into, what both are missing, and who is more right overall.
