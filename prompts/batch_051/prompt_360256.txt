I keep noticing a split in how intelligent people handle reductive explanations, and I want a principled verdict rather than another map of the landscape. In the physical sciences, nothing-but explanations pass without objection: a rainbow is nothing but refracted and dispersed sunlight; heat is nothing but mean molecular kinetic energy; the hardness of a diamond is nothing but the configuration of its lattice. No one thinks these explanations explain the rainbow, the heat, or the diamond away. But change the subject and the same people stiffen. Tell them that romantic attachment is nothing but pair-bonding machinery shaped by natural selection, that moral conviction is nothing but conditioned affect plus social sanction, or that religious ritual is nothing but costly signaling for group coordination, and they insist the account leaves out something essential — that it is reductive in a bad way — even though the form of the explanation, X is nothing but Y, looks identical across the two cases. I want to know whether this asymmetry can be justified or whether it is a flattering kind of special pleading.

Give me the strongest principle you can find that separates the reductions people welcome from the reductions they resist. Candidate resources you might test include: the idea that some higher-level patterns are real without being identical to their lower-level realizers; the contrast between a crane, a bottom-up mechanism that does incremental explanatory work, and a skyhook, a top-down stop that merely renames the phenomenon; and the charge that a particular reduction is a greedy reduction, right about the parts but wrong about the whole. But do not let those labels do the arguing for you. Press whatever principle you favor against nearby cases — temperature, money, a legal verdict, love, a moral conviction — and then say plainly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
