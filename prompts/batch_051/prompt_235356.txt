I serve on a regional water board, and we must decide this year how much flood protection to build, using climate projections for our county. The projections come from models developed at different institutions, and the staff report treats a projection as robust whenever most models in the ensemble agree. I have started applying my own rule instead: I trust an agreed projection only to the extent that the agreeing models are genuinely independent of one another. When I learn that models share code, physical approximations, or calibration datasets, I discount their agreement; when much of the ensemble shares ancestry, I treat even a wide consensus as unresolved. My worry is that agreement among near-copies of one model is worth little more than one model's say-so. But I cannot make the rule precise. How much should shared ancestry reduce the evidential weight of two models compared with one? Is there a principled way to set that discount, or is my rule only a rough heuristic? And can a whole ensemble still err in the same direction despite institutional independence, because all models inherit a common structural assumption? Tell me whether the rule is defensible, where it breaks down, and what the best defensible version would be for a decision we cannot postpone. End with \answer{...} giving your overall verdict as one of: 'keep as stated', 'modify', or 'abandon'.
