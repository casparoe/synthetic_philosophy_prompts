I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of law. At a workshop last term, two of my fellow students fell into an argument about desuetude—the condition of a penal statute that remains on the books but has not been enforced for decades, is openly violated, and is treated by officials and the public alike as obsolete. One of them, Reimer, defended the view that such a statute remains valid law; the other, Okafor, defended the view that its persistent non-enforcement changes its legal standing. Neither was arguing from the doctrine of any particular jurisdiction, only from first principles about what law and legality are. The exchange ran roughly as follows, and I could not settle it.

Reimer opened with a sources-based argument. The statute was enacted through the ordinary legislative procedure and has never been repealed, he said, and in a constitutional order built on the separation of powers, legislative silence is not repeal. If prosecutors could extinguish a criminal prohibition merely by declining to enforce it, the executive would acquire a de facto veto over the criminal law. Whether a statute is widely ignored or negligently unenforced is a fact about how the system is failing to administer the law, not a fact about whether the law exists.

Okafor replied that this confuses a sentence in a code with a legal norm. Legal systems, on her account, are normative practices: a rule counts as law only insofar as it figures in the way officials and citizens actually guide, evaluate, and answer for conduct. When a prohibition has gone unenforced for generations and is flouted in public without consequence, she argued, calling it law is like calling a language spoken when no one has used it for generations. It has become a dead letter, and a dead letter imposes no obligation.

Reimer answered that even on a practice-based view, the decisive practice is the officials' practice of recognition, not citizens' compliance or prosecutors' charging priorities. The courts still treat the statute as part of the code whenever anyone invokes it; the legislature can still amend or repeal it; and nothing in the system's practice of recognition makes desuetude a validity-defeating condition. If Okafor wants to say that widespread disregard changes the law, she has conflated efficacy with validity. A speed limit is no less law because everyone speeds.

Okafor responded that a practice of recognition is shown by what officials do, not by what they would say if asked to catalogue the code. For as long as anyone can remember, the responsible agencies have not charged the conduct, and if they suddenly began to do so, the natural description would be a change in enforcement policy, not the belated discovery of a pre-existing obligation. Here she claimed the rule of law cut in her favour. A citizen trying to know where she stands is badly served by being told that the state may ignore a prohibition for a generation and then, without any legislative act, deploy it selectively against someone it now has reason to target. A fully valid but unenforced statute, she said, is a dormant power available for arbitrary use, and guarding against arbitrary power is exactly what legality is for.

Reimer granted that selective revival would be an abuse but insisted that the remedy is to discipline the revival directly—through fair-notice limits or a doctrine against arbitrary enforcement—not to announce that the statute has died by neglect. Suppose, he said, the unenforced statute criminalises conduct that is genuinely harmful and that the state has failed to prosecute out of negligence or bias. Okafor's position would reward that official failure by writing the conduct out of the criminal law without any democratic decision, and it gives the state a standing incentive to abandon inconvenient laws rather than seek repeal. Official neglect, he argued, should not be allowed to do the work of legislation.

Okafor's last move was to press a dilemma. If a court will not permit the statute to be applied against anyone, she said, then for practical purposes it is no longer law, and the only real question is whether doctrine admits that or hides it in standing and equitable exceptions. If instead a court will sometimes permit it to be applied, then the citizen's protection against arbitrary enforcement is illusory. Reimer's view, she concluded, cannot both keep the statute fully valid and deliver the freedom from arbitrary enforcement he himself concedes is required.

I found myself unable to say where the disagreement really turned. Please go through their exchange point by point: identify which claims are sound, which are overstated or mistaken, and which disagreements are substantive rather than merely verbal. Then give a verdict on who has the better overall position, and whether a defensible account of desuetude lies elsewhere.
