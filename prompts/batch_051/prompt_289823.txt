I have been trying to understand why population ethics has been in the same holding pattern since Derek Parfit introduced the repugnant conclusion in Reasons and Persons. The data points of the debate are not in dispute: nearly everyone grants that, on a total welfarist picture, a world containing an enormous population of lives barely worth living would outrank a much smaller world of flourishing lives, because sufficiently many small positive surpluses add up; and nearly everyone finds that conclusion repugnant while nevertheless feeling the pull of each step that leads to it, including the mere addition of a happy person to an otherwise unchanged population. The disagreement is about which principle to give up. Average utilitarianism avoids the conclusion, but then denies that adding a happy person whose welfare is just below the average makes the world better; critical-level views either allow the aggregative logic back in at a higher level or refuse to count lives worth living as good additions; and person-affecting restrictions, which make value depend on gains and losses to particular people, have trouble even stating how to compare a world in which one person is brought into existence with a world in which a different, better-off person is brought into existence instead.

That the options keep sorting into the same dead ends makes me suspect the impasse is not a first-order moral disagreement that someone might eventually win, but a disagreement about what it is for one outcome to be better than another when the outcomes differ in both the number and the identities of the people they contain. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are reasoning from different standpoints: an impersonal standpoint, from which a world is assessed as a whole, and a person-affecting standpoint, from which all value must register in some person's life; and that neither camp can translate its fundamental commitments into the other's idiom without already assuming what it needs to show. Is that the right diagnosis, or is the dispute substantive and merely hard, a hidden formal or empirical matter, or a sign that the question which of these worlds is better is simply ill-posed when the comparison changes who exists? I would like a diagnosis that engages the actual moves made by totalists, averagists, critical-level theorists, and person-affecting theorists, followed by an honest account of what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress: a formal result, a new distinction, a change of question, or a reason to expect none.
