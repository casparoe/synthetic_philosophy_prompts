I spent my working life in the biological sciences, and since retiring I have been reading my way through metaethics. One distinction I keep returning to concerns reasons for attitudes rather than reasons for action. A standard case: an evil demon threatens to harm you unless you admire him. You plainly have a reason of some kind to admire him, yet it would be strange to conclude that he is admirable or that admiration is the fitting attitude to take toward him. The threat seems to be the wrong kind of consideration to settle the fittingness question, even though it is a genuine practical reason to bring the attitude about. I want to approach this problem the way I used to approach a new instrument or protocol: specify what a solution must do before evaluating any candidate.

So here is my request. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction for reasons for attitudes must meet. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. In particular, I want you to take a stand on whether the account must generalize beyond admiration to belief, desire, and the other emotions; whether it must explain in what sense both kinds of consideration count as reasons; whether it must avoid collapsing into either a crude evidentialism or a crude pragmatism about when attitudes are justified; and whether the distinction has to be drawable by someone who cannot already identify the evaluative properties in question. Then flag any subsets of desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to eliminate otherwise plausible accounts, saying which camp it favors and which it hurts. Please tell me how confident you are in the resulting list, and single out the point you are least sure of.
