In my philosophy of cognitive science course we have hit the same impasse several times over computationalism, and I would like a diagnosis of why the debate does not move rather than a survey of the positions. One side, in the Marr–Fodor tradition, holds that the brain is literally a computer in the only sense that matters: mental processes are operations over representational vehicles, and an explanation of a cognitive capacity just is a specification of the algorithm. Another side, associated with van Gelder and the dynamical systems tradition, replies that cognition is better described as the evolution of coupled continuous variables, so calling the brain a computer is at best a loose metaphor. A third camp shrugs that everything from stomachs to planetary orbits can be said to compute under some mapping, so the claim is trivial unless one imposes constraints on which mappings count as implementations, and nobody agrees on those constraints. What frustrates me is that all three camps appeal to the same empirical findings — single-cell tuning curves, population codes, recurrence, oscillations — as evidence for their own view, so the disagreement does not look like an ordinary empirical one. My working hypothesis is that this is really a disagreement about what the word 'computation' has to mean before 'the brain computes' is explanatory rather than mere redescription, together with a clash about what a successful explanation in cognitive science has to deliver. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, a hidden factual question, or are the sides answering different questions under one heading? Given the diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress — a formal result about implementation, a distinction, an experiment, or a better-posed question. Please write in British English.
