Question: What is epiphenomenalism, and why do some philosophers think it faces a paradox of phenomenal judgment? Does this paradox give us good reason to reject epiphenomenalism? Explain.

Student's answer:

Epiphenomenalism is the view that mental states are non-physical properties that are caused by physical states in the brain but have no causal powers of their own. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is that, if epiphenomenalism is true, then when I judge 'I am in pain,' the pain itself doesn't cause my judgment; my judgment is caused entirely by physical processes. But then my judgment would be false, because a judgment can only be about a mental state if that mental state caused it. So epiphenomenalism would make all our reports about our own conscious experience false. Since we obviously have true beliefs about our own experiences, epiphenomenalism must be rejected. This is a decisive refutation, and it is why Chalmers eventually abandoned epiphenomenalism in favor of interactionism.
