A popular-audience book on risk I'm reading has a paragraph about the St. Petersburg paradox that's been nagging me. Paraphrasing from memory, the passage runs like this:

The St. Petersburg game is a lottery where you flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if that happens on the nth flip you get 2^n dollars. The expected payout is (1/2)(2) + (1/4)(4) + (1/8)(8) + ..., which is 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., i.e. infinite. So standard decision theory says a rational person should be willing to pay any finite price, even a billion dollars, for a single ticket. Since no actual person would pay that, people must be systematically irrational. Daniel Bernoulli solved the paradox by pointing out that people care about utility, not money, and that the utility of money grows only logarithmically; with log utility the game's expected utility is finite and the fair price works out to about four dollars. So the paradox is defused — the infinite price was just an artifact of treating dollars as if their value were linear.

I trust most of this, but two things bug me. First, the jump from "infinite expected dollars" to "decision theory says pay any finite amount" feels sleight-of-handy — isn't that only forced if you've already assumed dollars are the same thing as utility? Second, a friend said Bernoulli's fix just postpones the problem: if the original game pays 2^n dollars, make a game that pays 2^(2^n) dollars, and then even a log-utility agent has infinite expected utility. I can't tell wether that's a real rejoinder or my friend overcomplicating things. I also once heard someone say the paradox vanishes once you notice that no real casino could actually pay the promised sums, so the payout should be capped at the house's bankroll.

So my question is: how fair is the book's paragraph, sentence by sentence? And seperately, do the later objections — the log-utility blow-up variant and the finite-bankroll point — actually undercut the "Bernoulli solved it" conclusion?
