Ok so I cover science and ideas for a living and I'm working on a piece about what you can and can't infer about what a system "wants" from what it does — from people, from markets, from AI. In gathering my notes I've realized I hold a pile of views on this that I've never actually checked against each other, and I'd like an audit before I commit any of it to print. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Behavior underdetermines objectives. Any finite record of choices can be rationalized by many different reward functions plus different beliefs and search spaces, so the objective isn't recoverable from behavior alone.
2. But I also think the interesting work in inverse reinforcement learning is never really the reward function; it's the assumptions about what the agent was searching over and how it searched. Once you fix a search space and a search procedure, the set of objectives consistent with the behavior shrinks a lot, and I think people under-sell how much of the inference is riding on those assumptions.
3. I think revealed preference theory in economics is basically the same move, done more honestly: it's not a claim about a person's real inner utilities, just an as-if rationalization of choice under constraints. So I don't find the usual "revealed preference is underdetermined" objection devestating — it only looks devestating if you thought it was metaphysics.
4. And yet I do find that underdetermination devestating when someone reads a person's real values off their purchases or career choices, because without knowing what alternatives they considered, or could have considered, the same behavior is consistent with too many different valuations.
5. I'm suspicious of goal-talk and "wants" language for LLMs. What looks like a coherent objective is, I suspect, mostly the training loss plus the details of the sampling and selection procedure, and I don't think bolting a reward model on top changes that.
6. But I also think the only practical way to check whether a model is aligned is to watch what it does across lots of situations, including adversarial ones, and that this behavioral evidence can license pretty strong conclusions — strong enough to ship or not ship.

Go through them and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are. Rank the tensions by severity, say what would have to give for each, and what the cheapest fix is. And if the whole task is mis-posed because I'm quietly using "objective" in two different senses, or because "aligned" is doing something different in (5) and (6), just say that plainly instead of humoring the assignment.
