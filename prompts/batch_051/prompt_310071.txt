I'm a novelist, mid-revisions on a book where a lot of the story turns on the gap between what people say and what they mean, so I've been reading craft books on dialogue. One of them — I won't name it — has a whole section on Grice, of all people, and it gives this summary:

"Grice drew a line between two kinds of meaning: what a sentence literally says, and what a speaker is getting at by saying it. The second kind he called implicature, and it's basically the same thing novelists mean by subtext — the message that hovers between the lines. His account of how we recover it is this: conversation runs on four rules — be informative but not more than needed, say what you believe to be true, be relevant, and be clear. When someone obviously breaks one of these rules, we don't just think they're confused; we assume they still mean to communicate and we look for a hidden meaning that restores the sense. So implicature only works because both parties are following the rules at a deeper level. And Grice's rules double as a formula for writing dialogue: to give a line subtext, make its literal meaning break one of the rules in a way that points the reader toward what the character actually means."

Ive used that last bit as a revision tool and it's been weirdly productive, which makes me nervous — productive tricks are exactly the ones I never fact-check. The part that smells wrong to me is "both parties are following the rules at a deeper level," because the dialogue I love most is full of people being evasive or uncooperative, and Grice must have noticed that. So my question is: how fair is this summary overall? I want to know which pieces are accurate, which are subtly off, and whether the writing advice actually follows from Grice's real view, or whether Ive just been getting better at writing dialogue for a seperate reason.
