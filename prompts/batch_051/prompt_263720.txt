I keep going back and forth on something I ran into reading the Confessions (Book Eleven, the time part) and I think it matters for how I think about writing. Augustine's way out of the riddle—the past is no longer, the future is not yet—is that past and future only have being in the mind: memory for the past, expectation for the future, attention for the now, so time is a stretching of the mind rather than something out there in the world. And honestly this tracks almost everything I do as a novelist. A novel is all memory and anticipation on paper: a three-page stretch can cover a decade (summary), a ten-minute scene can take forty pages, backstory only works because the reader is pulling the past into the present, suspense only works because the reader is already leaning into a not-yet. I can even write a whole book in present tense and the now never feels like it's located anywhere outside the sentence. So the theory has real grip on me.

But I also cant shake the feeling that what Augustine is describing is the felt time, the representation, not the thing itself. My characters get older. If I sit staring at a blank page, an hour passes whether I'm paying attention to it or not, and the deadline doesn't care about my attention. The universe had a before and after long before there were minds to stretch anything. That seems like more than a hunch; it seems like the thing I'm trying to get the novel to catch.

So here's the question. I don't want to be told that Augustine was prescientific or that current physics favors the block universe—I already know those outs. I want to know, given the pull of both, which one should give: should I treat the mind-independent sense of time as something to debunk, should I shrink Augustine's claim until its only a story about how time feels and stop pretending it says anything about what time is, or is there a reading where the threefold present and objective time don't actually collide, and my sense that they do is the real confusion? And what would I be giving up with each move—not just which side wins, but what would make it right to let one of them go?
