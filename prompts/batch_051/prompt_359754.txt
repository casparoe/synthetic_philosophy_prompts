I keep seeing arguments whose load-bearing step is an independence assumption treated as a free default — two witnesses, two diagnostic tests, two "unlikely coincidences" that are supposed to compound, two features assumed uncorrelated in a model — and the whole inference holds only if that assumption was licensed rather than merely convenient. Before I accept or reject the next one, I want the specification of the thing itself: what must an adequate account of the presumption of independence get right? Write the desiderata, with the case or argument behind each. I want the distinctions an account cannot collapse — probabilistic independence as a property of a distribution versus evidential independence as the absence of a common cause; default independence versus conditional independence; independence relative to one partition of an outcome space versus independence relative to another — and the failure modes it has to address: unobserved common causes, selection effects that correlate evidence gathered after the fact, and the incoherence of assuming independence under every redescription of the outcomes. Mark which requirements are negotiable and which are not, flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement knocks out the most popular naive views. Then a short scoreboard for the obvious contenders — the bare Bayesian "independence until the data complains" line, the causal-graph approach, and the working heuristic that you assume independence and watch for failure — is enough; keep the weight on the list and its motivation, not the scoring.
