In my government class we got onto local zoning fights and my teacher showed us this proposal from a city out west (I forget which one) that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Before any public project can get approved — a road widening, a cell tower, a new subdivision on the edge of town — any resident could file something called a "sanctity objection." The objection wouldn't have to claim anyone gets physically or financially hurt; it would just have to say that the site has some kind of sacred or spiritually significant character for that person or their community. A panel of five randomly selected residents would then hear the objection and vote on a single question: would the project violate the sacred character of the place? If a majority says yes, the project gets blocked unless the developer pays a set amount into a community fund that can only be spent preserving or restoring other sites residents consider sacred. So it isn't a flat veto, more of a "pay to build on sacred ground" fee.

I think the theory behind it is that disgust and sanctity are a real kind of value and shouldn't just be dismissed as excuses for NIMBYism, and I actually find that interesting. But when I try to picture this running for ten years I get confused. Would it mostly protect actual burial grounds and old churches, or would every unpopular project suddenly become "sacred" to someone? Do the random panelists end up trying to judge how sincere a belief is, and can you even do that fairly? And wouldn't developers just start treating the fund as one more permit fee and build anyway, except now the city gets a cut?

Could you lay out how this would actually play out as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion? I want it to cover what it incentivizes people to do, where it gets gamed first, what happens to who objects, and whether the failure modes could be fixed without gutting the whole idea.
