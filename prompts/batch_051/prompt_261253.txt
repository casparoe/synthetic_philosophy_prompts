In a seminar on the epistemology of history, we have been using a working account of what makes a narrative text usable as evidence about the remote past. On this account, a text is a historical source to the extent that it meets several conditions: temporal proximity to the events narrated; access, meaning the author drew on earlier written records, documents, or a reliably transmitted oral tradition rather than sheer invention; independence from other surviving accounts; internal coherence, including a plausible chronology and consistent geography of persons and places; external fit with material evidence, inscriptions, or unrelated texts; and a traceable line of transmission from the time of composition to the manuscripts now available.

Now consider a deliberately constructed but realistic case. A monastic chronicle from a small inland kingdom was compiled in the mid-ninth century and narrates the kingdom's founding in the fifth and sixth centuries. The compiler says he drew on a set of now-lost royal annals, on genealogies recited at royal inaugurations, and on a body of praise-poems that he quotes at length; the language of the quoted poems suggests they are considerably older than the ninth-century prose around them. The chronicle is internally consistent and supplies a detailed king-list with reign lengths. A single surviving foreign inscription from the seventh century names two of the kings and places them in the same order as the chronicle. Yet the chronicle also includes accounts of miracles, derives the dynasty from a god, and survives only in two later manuscripts whose minor divergences suggest some editorial reworking. No contemporary text from the fifth or sixth century survives, and archaeological work has so far neither confirmed nor undercut the chronicle's claims about early settlement.

Using the working account above, does this chronicle qualify as a historical source, and if so, for what kinds of claims—political chronology, institutional practices, religious conceptions, or something else? Work through the conditions one by one; where they underdetermine the verdict, say so explicitly; and say whether the case suggests that any of the conditions should be revised or weighted differently.
