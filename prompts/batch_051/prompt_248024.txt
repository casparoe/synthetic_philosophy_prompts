I run an advanced undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of social norms — about sixteen students, one seventy-five-minute session. I'd like an in-class game that makes students feel the difference between a mere convention and a full-blown social norm: specifically, the moment at which a coordination regularity that could easily have been otherwise starts to feel obligatory, so that violating it invites not just surprise or mild frustration but something closer to censure. I don't want to lecture first, and the students shouldn't hear the words convention and norm in their technical sense until the debrief.

The game should move through three stages. Stage one should be a pure coordination problem with several equally good equilibria — a shared side, a meeting place, a price, a signal — with small point payoffs and no real money; students play repeated rounds without talking, with outcomes announced publicly so that a regularity can emerge from precedent and common knowledge rather than from instruction. Stage two should keep the same underlying coordination problem but introduce pressures that make deviation tempting: some players earn more by breaking the emergent regularity, or an outside option pays better when few others take it. Stage three should add sanctioning that is cheap but not free — for instance, spending a point to deduct several from a deviator, or publicly marking a defector in a way that shapes later rounds — so that students face the second-order question of whether to pay to punish someone who violated a rule no one ever explicitly agreed to. The aim is for them to feel the shift from "everyone happens to do X" to "doing Y is out of bounds," and to feel how precarious that shift is when the punishers themselves are not being policed.

The debrief should map what happened in each stage onto Lewis's account of a convention — a regularity everyone conforms to, expects everyone else to conform to, and prefers to conform to given those expectations, in a situation where at least one alternative regularity would have served just as well — and then onto the further machinery that seems needed for a norm rather than a mere convention: normative expectations, felt entitlement, and willingness to punish violations even when punishing is costly. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect students will converge on the first obvious equilibrium, or that nobody will bother to sanction in stage three because it is individually irrational — and how I should handle each failure. And be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: the participants will know the whole thing is artificial and time-limited, so the sense of obligation will be thin, and the facilitator will have to supply the historical depth of how an arbitrary regularity comes to be taught to newcomers as binding, which no single session can simulate.
