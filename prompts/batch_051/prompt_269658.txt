Look, I'm a novelist — I've spent way more time thinking about point of view, tense, and what a narrator can and can't know about a character over time than I have about metaphysics, so treat me as fluent in narrative craft and only semi-literate in philosophy. But I keep bumping into the endurantism vs perdurantism debate and I want it translated into my actual working vocabulary.

Here is how I understand the two sides, correct me if I've got this wrong: endurantists think a persisting thing is wholly present at every moment it exists, like the very same object moves through time; perdurantists think what persists is a four-dimensional whole and that at any given moment only a temporal part, a slice, is present.

So do this: restate each side in the vocabulary of how a novel manages a character across a book. I mean stable names and pronouns, past vs present tense narration, a fixed focalization through one character vs an omnisicent narrator who can apparently see the whole of a life at once, scene and summary, flashback and flashforward, free indirect discourse, and the way a reader tracks "the same person" through enormous changes. Go position by position. For each one, tell me what carries over cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no expression in the narrative vocabulary at all.

Then the part I actually care about: say whether what doesn't survive translation is a limitation of how fiction talks about people in time — something narrative just isn't built to express — or whether it's a sign that the metaphysics is doing less work than it looks like. I honestly cant tell if perdurantism is just the omniscient narrator's atemporal view of a character, dressed up as a theory of what there is, or if it's claiming something genuinely extra. Make me see the difference, or make me see that there isnt one.
