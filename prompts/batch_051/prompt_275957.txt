I'm preparing a single guest session for first-year undergraduates with no background in ancient philosophy, on Parmenides' argument that plurality and change are unreal. I want a plan for the session's explanatory arc rather than the lecture itself. The central pedagogical problem is that the ban on thinking or speaking what is not feels like a merely verbal rule until students see it doing real work; the plan should therefore force them to use that ban to derive each later conclusion themselves, so the conclusions feel earned rather than absurd. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one—in particular, the distinction between the way of truth and the way of mortal opinion, and the impossibility of coming-to-be and passing-away, should be in place before the denial of plurality is introduced. I also want the one example or case that can carry the whole argument, and what it must have and what it must lack; the misconceptions these students will predictably bring or form and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; the check that would show they have understood the argument rather than merely finding it strange; and what I should deliberately leave out at this level and say so. Give the plan as exactly five numbered stages in the order of presentation, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
