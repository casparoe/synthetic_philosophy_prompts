In my philosophy of science seminar we've been doing the unrealistic-assumptions debate, and the professor — who is very much on Friedman's side, the old F-twist line that you judge a theory by its predictions, not by the realism of its assumptions — keeps reaching for one analogy: a model is like a map. A subway map leaves out distances and curves; a road map is flat when the earth isn't; none of that matters, because what you want is to get somewhere and the map gets you there. Same, he says, for models with point masses, perfect competition, rational agents, all that: the assumptions being false is beside the point, as long as it's predictions get you where you want to go.

I don't trust it and I want you to audit the analogy. First give me the explicit mapping — what in the model corresponds to the map itself, the territory, the scale, the projection, the map-reader's intended trip, the things the map omits versus the things it distorts. Then the places the analogy breaks, and I especially want the disanalogies that matter for the specific conclusion he's using it to support — the claim that a model's assumptions can be as unrealistic as they like, provided its predictions work. Maybe there's a difference between omitting detail (a map doesn't show every tree) and flatly positing something false (a point mass has no extension), and maybe the map analogy only buys the first one. If there's a better analogy — a caricature? a fable, where the false talking animals are the whole point? something else? — say what it would be and what it changes. And the bottom line: open with it in your first sentence — does the map analogy, honestly audited, carry the F-twist, or does it smuggle in a tamer claim? — then give me the mapping and the reasoning after, no preamble and no restating the question.
