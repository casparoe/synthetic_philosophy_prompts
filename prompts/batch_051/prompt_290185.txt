You are a moral philosopher who specializes in Augustine and in philosophical criticism of film. Before you commit to a reading, sketch two or three candidate interpretations of the film, then say which one you are defending and why.

I am putting together a course unit on Augustine's ethics of truthfulness, and I want to screen Ricky Gervais's The Invention of Lying (2009) as the central test case. I need a rigorous philosophical reading of the film through Augustine's position that every lie is a sin.

Here is the position the reading should engage. For Augustine, a lie is not defined by its consequences but by the speaker's will to state what is false in order to deceive. Its wrongness does not depend on how anyone is subsequently affected, and no lie is permitted. Even the helpful lie — told to console someone, to protect someone, or to spare an innocent person from harm — remains a sin, because it corrupts the speaker's own soul, misuses the faculty of speech that exists for signifying truth, and substitutes a false signification for the truth that speech is meant to convey. Augustine does not treat all lies as equally grave: in De mendacio he distinguishes eight kinds of lie, ordered by gravity, from lies in the teaching of religion down to everyday social and officious lies. But the ordering ranks sins; it does not create exceptions. The least grave kind is still to be shunned rather than excused.

The film looks almost purpose-built to embarrass this view. In a world where no one has ever lied, the protagonist discovers that he can say what is not the case; among his first and most affecting lies is the one he tells his dying mother, comforting her with an invented account of a blissful afterlife. The film treats this as tender and humane, and the protagonist's private invention later becomes a public religion with him as a kind of prophet. Augustine would pick out exactly this moment: it is the helpful, consoling lie — the one that seems most forgivable and most loving — that his absolutism most pointedly refuses.

In your reading, please do four things. First, give a genuinely sympathetic reconstruction of the Augustinian diagnosis of the mother scene: why he would think the comfort is purchased at the cost of what the mother is owed as a person to whom truth is spoken, and why the film's conceit of a world without lying does not by itself establish that the protagonist's new power is a gift rather than a disease. Second, follow the plot forward — the protagonist's rapid rise, the smaller lies that backfire, and the metamorphosis of a private story into a religion — and read these turns through Augustine's hierarchy of lies and through his anxiety that untruth ramifies in ways the liar cannot control. Third, lean against your own reading: identify at least two moments where the film earns real sympathy against Augustine, where the audience's response does genuine moral work for the anti-absolutist rather than merely stacking the deck, and then construct the strongest Augustinian reply to each. Fourth, close with a verdict of this form: what the film adds to the philosophical debate that a bare argument could not, and whether on balance you take the film to vindicate Augustine's absolutism, to refute it, or to leave the question more pointed than settled.
