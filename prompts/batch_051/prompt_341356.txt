I help run a small research collective — about forty people, mostly computational biologists and ML engineers — working on generative models for protein design, and we have been stuck for months on a governance problem. Several of our members independently have the standing to release trained models, publish protocols, or push datasets live, and any one of them could take a step that permanently changes the field's trajectory: a dual-use enzyme design, a dataset that makes misuse easier, a preprint that triggers a regulatory crackdown. We had a close call last year when one member almost released a model he was convinced was safe, while most of the group would have objected but was never asked. The obvious advice is to deliberate more, but the group is busy and no one wants to be a permanent bottleneck. The structural feature that worries me is this: when any single member can set the group's course, the most optimistic member effectively decides for everyone.

The rule I have been pushing, which we call the notice-and-bond protocol, works as follows. Before taking any action that could plausibly have irreversible consequences for the collective or the wider public — with the proposer making the initial judgment about whether an action falls under the protocol — a member must post a notice to the whole group at least ten days in advance and lodge a bond worth one week of their pay. During the notice window, any other member may file a written objection by lodging a smaller bond equal to one day of their pay; each member may file at most one objection per proposal, and each objection extends the window by five days. If the number of standing objections reaches five, the proposal goes to a secret ballot of all members and requires a two-thirds majority to proceed. If the window closes with fewer than five objections, the proposer is free to act and the bond is returned. If a ballot rejects the proposal, the proposer forfeits the bond to the group's common fund, while objectors always get their bonds back when the matter resolves. I want this analyzed as a machine rather than evaluated as a moral position. What does it incentivize among proposers, among would-be objectors, among members who never file anything, and among outsiders who know the rule exists? How would it be gamed, and what second-order effects would it have on honesty, risk-taking, and the culture of the group? And which failure modes could not be patched by adjusting the bond sizes, the thresholds, or the windows?
