I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so please don't just wave me off with jargon—but also don't dumb it down. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on ethics and social epistemology, especially transparency, privacy, and what we owe each other as knowers. Think carefully before you respond, and maybe start by brainstorming the two or three strongest objections to what I'm about to lay out, then tell me whether my essay idea survives them.

I'm sketching an essay, working title "Against Legibility," or maybe "The Case for Being a Little Unreadable." It grew out of something I keep noticing in my characters: the ones I respect most refuse to explain themselves, and readers tend to hate that at first and then, if I do my job, come to see the refusal as a kind of gift. I want to turn that into a philosophical claim, but I'm not sure it holds up.

The core idea: there's a background assumption in a lot of modern life—therapy talk, workplace communication, relationship advice—that being legible is a moral achievement, that you owe it to people to be readable: to state your feelings and intentions clearly, to make yourself interpretable, not to be a puzzle. I want to argue this gets something backwards. Opacity isn't just a privacy right you have against other people; in some cases it's a way of respecting the other person. When you make yourself completely legible, you pre-empt their act of interpretation. You turn what could have been a joint, open-ended practice of trying to understand someone into a transfer of information. So refusing to explain yourself can be a way of leaving the other person room to know you in their own way, or to be surprised by you. Its not evasion; it's an invitation to a different kind of attention.

Second strand: the demand for legibility is asymmetrical in a way that tracks power. If I'm the boss, the platform, the more secure partner, I can demand "be transparent with me" while my own opacity stays protected. So transparency-talk often functions as an audit tool for the powerful. But I don't want to settle for the cynical point that legibility demands are just power moves. I want the stronger point: treating legibility as a general duty misdescribes what it is to understand another person. It treats the other mind like a text with a single correct reading, rather than something partly constituted by how it's read.

Third, the novelist's bit: a character who is fully legible is a bad character, and a novel in which everyone explains their motives forecloses the reader's activity. Maybe fiction is one of the few places we train ourselves to tolerate and even value opacity. If that's right, the current cultural push for total legibility isn't just a moral mistake; it's an aesthetic one too, and maybe the two are connected.

What I'd like from you:

1. Is the central distinction—between opacity as hiding and opacity as respecting the interpreter—actually doing work, or have I just relabeled evasion? Be blunt.

2. Where does a philosopher attack this hardest? I vaguely worry about cases where opacity is used to gaslight someone, or where the "gift" of unreadability is just a way of dodging accountability. Is there a way to draw the line without collapsing the whole essay?

3. Is the idea interesting enough, or is it a warmed-over version of something I haven't read? If it overlaps with existing work—privacy, the right to opacity, epistemic injustice, whatever—tell me where and what I'd need to add to make it mine.

4. Any suggestions for how a novelist could make the philosophical move convincing to people who aren't philosophers? I want the essay to feel like an essay, not a lecture.

Please don't just praise the idea; I'd rather you find the weak spots. And if some of my terms are sloppy, flag that to—I'd like to fix the sloppiness without losing the felt sense of the thing.
