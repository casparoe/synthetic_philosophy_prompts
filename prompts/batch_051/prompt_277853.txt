I've been drafting a short paper on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I've reached the point where I can't tell whether my central move is a real contribution or just a translation of the familiar debate into new vocabulary. The familiar debate, as I understand it, turns on the agglomeration principle and the ought-implies-can principle: roughly, if two obligations each hold, their conjunction must hold, but if the two acts cannot both be performed, that would require the impossible, so one of the alleged obligations must give way. I suspect this framing has stalled, because both sides can hold their ground by adjusting how they treat conflicting requirements. I want to recast the question instead in terms of the formal structure of an agent's choiceworthiness ranking.

Here is the argument I have in mind, in compressed form. (1) Distinguish three ways practical conflict can fail to yield a single best option: ties, where two options are equally choiceworthy; incomparability, where neither is ranked above or equal to the other; and cycles, where A outranks B, B outranks C, and C outranks A through a chain of pairwise comparisons the agent endorses. (2) Ties and incomparability give us maximal options—options not beaten by anything—and therefore make each option permissible, though the choice remains hard; they do not by themselves give us a dilemma in the strict sense. (3) A genuine dilemma requires that each available option be dominated by a standard that still speaks to the agent, so that the agent is criticizable whichever way she goes; that is exactly the structure of a cycle. (4) The most promising source of such cycles is role conflict: an agent deliberates from the standpoint of several irreducibly distinct roles at once, and each role supplies its own ranking of options. When pairwise comparisons are settled by whichever role is salient for that comparison, the aggregated ranking can be cyclic in much the way majority rule over several voters can be cyclic. (5) The upshot would be that genuine dilemmas are possible but rare and local: they arise only where an agent's normative sources aggregate in a path-dependent way, and accepting them does not force us to abandon the ought-implies-can principle if we read it as saying that each obligation can be satisfied taken singly rather than that all obligations can be satisfied jointly.

I would be grateful for comments aimed at whether this is worth developing and where it is weakest. In particular: Is the claim that ties and incomparability merely yield permissibility, while only cycles yield a genuine dilemma, sound, or does it beg the question against someone who thinks an undischarged obligation already constitutes the dilemma? Does the analogy between intra-personal role conflict and multi-voter cycles do real work, or is it decorative? And if you were supervising this paper, what would you require it to establish before you would count the taxonomy of ties, incomparability, and cycles as a genuine advance rather than a relabeling of existing positions?
