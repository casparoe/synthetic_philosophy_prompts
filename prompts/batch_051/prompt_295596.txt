In defamation law, a false factual claim — "the firm defaulted on a loan last quarter" — can ground liability when it damages someone, yet a credit rating or business forecast that conveys the same information and does the same damage — "we rate the firm an unacceptable credit risk," "we expect it to be insolvent within a year" — tends to be protected as non-factual opinion, even where the speaker has no factual basis for it. Is that asymmetry justified? Address the strongest defenses: that only factual claims can be proved false, that audiences discount evaluations and predictions, and that protecting ratings and forecasts serves information markets. Then test them against the hard cases at each end of the line: a rating agency that knowingly issues a false downgrade, and a defamer who launders a factual allegation by simply adding "we predict." If you would keep a line between protected and actionable speech, say exactly where it falls and why the relabeling problem does not collapse it; if you would abandon it, say what principle should replace it.
