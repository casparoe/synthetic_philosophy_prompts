I am a PhD student in neuroethics, and I keep noticing a harm in consumer neural-interface cases for which I have no name. In one case, a headband streams the wearer a continuous score of her own attention or drowsiness; nothing beyond low-level states is decoded, and no third party sees the data. Yet users report that the display changes how they inhabit their own minds: they begin steering their mental states to keep the score in a good range, and the difference between being tired and noticing that one's fatigue score is rising starts to blur. A meditation device that grades focus in real time produces the same effect, so that the practice becomes performing focus for the sensor. This is not a privacy violation, since nothing is disclosed; it is not interference, since nobody else acts on the information; and it is not obviously a loss of agency, since users are, if anything, more self-observing and deliberate. Nearby concepts — cognitive liberty, mental integrity, some form of self-constitution — each seems tailored to other cases. Is there an established term for this specific phenomenon? If not, how should it be defined, what are its boundary cases, and what distinguishes it from ordinary self-monitoring such as a heart-rate reading?
