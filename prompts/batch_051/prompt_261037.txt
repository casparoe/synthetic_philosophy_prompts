I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mathematics, choosing a dissertation topic around the question whether the real numbers—and the continuum more generally—are discovered or defined. The received way of setting up the debate looks like a standoff to me. The "defined" story starts from the nineteenth-century arithmetization of analysis: Dedekind cuts and Cauchy-completion build a complete ordered field from the rationals, and one can show that any two complete ordered fields are isomorphic, so there is a sense in which the construction pins down its target exactly. The "discovered" story replies that this only pushes the puzzle back, because the construction succeeds precisely by imposing the second-order completeness condition that already characterizes the intended continuum; the categoricity result then looks less like evidence that the object is a free stipulation and more like evidence that the construction is tracking an antecedent structure it did not create. Cohen's forcing result complicates the picture further: assuming ZFC is consistent, ZFC neither proves nor refutes the continuum hypothesis, so the standard foundational framework underdetermines the cardinality of the continuum. I cannot yet tell whether that result deepens the discovered/defined question or shows that it was misposed from the start.

I have roughly three years and want the dissertation to clarify the discovered/defined debate conceptually rather than contribute new set-theoretic consistency proofs. My question is: which two or three open questions in this area are most worth prioritizing, and in what order? I am asking for a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list. For each question you recommend, say why it is upstream of the other candidate questions, how tractable it is for someone working with philosophical tools rather than technical set theory, and what would count as recognizable progress on it. Please also state the criteria behind the ranking—importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much downstream work depends on it—and indicate what development over the next few years would move each item up or down.

Among the candidates I have been turning over: whether categoricity in second-order logic is enough to underwrite realism about R despite the freedom in first-order set-theoretic reconstruction; whether the multiplicity of constructions of R (cuts, Cauchy sequences, decimal expansions, Eudoxus reals) shows that what gets defined is only a presentation of something presentation-independent; whether the independence of CH shows that "the continuum" is really a family of related structures rather than a single object; and whether the discovered/defined dichotomy is itself too coarse, such that the better question concerns which sense of "definition" is at issue—historical, epistemic, or semantic. I would also like your judgment on whether the set-theoretic multiverse and pluralism about the continuum should be treated as central to this question or as a plausible-looking distraction, and whether the independence of CH should be taken to reframe the discovered/defined contrast, leave it untouched, or dissolve the question around which I have been organizing the project.
