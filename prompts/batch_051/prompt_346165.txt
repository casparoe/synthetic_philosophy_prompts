I've been writing a novel where one character insists envy is always a vice, and another keeps pointing to cases where it seems like the only sane reaction to a rigged world. I went back to Rawls's A Theory of Justice, section 80, the part on envy, and now I'm not sure he's consistent, which is bugging me because I'd wanted to lean on him for the moral psychology.

In one place he describes envy as a vice, something a well-ordered society would discourage, and he says it's not a moral feeling; it's generally to be avoided because it makes the envious person worse off and doesn't aim at any good. But then, later in the same section, he introduces what he calls excusable envy: when inequalities are so great that people in the worse-off position can't reasonably be expected not to feel it, he says we shouldn't blame them for it, and he seems to treat it as a symptom that the society has failed by its own standards. I'm paraphrasing both bits because I don't have the text in front of me, but I'm fairly sure that's the shape of it.

So here's what I want to work out. Are these two claims in genuine tension, or can they be reconciled at little cost? Is it that the first is about envy as a settled character trait and the second about an occurrent reaction to particular circumstances? Or that the vice is acting on envy, whereas the excusable part is the feeling itself? Or is excusable envy just an affective signal of injustice that even a good theory of justice should make room for without changing the verdict on envy generally? I can think of several readings, but I'd like to know which of them is honest and which amounts to special pleading, and whether any reading lets him keep both the vice claim and the excusable-envy claim without smuggling in a distinction he never actually draws. Also, if they can't be reconciled, which one does the overall argument of the book actually need?
