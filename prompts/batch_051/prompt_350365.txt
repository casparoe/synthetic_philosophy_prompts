I keep encountering heritability estimates in arguments about education, social mobility, and policy, and I want to be sure I understand the concept well enough to audit the claims that get made with it. A popular book on genetics that I read recently summarizes the idea as follows:

"Heritability is a measure of how much of a trait is genetic. It is estimated mainly by comparing identical twins, who share all their genes, with fraternal twins, who share only half. If identical twins resemble each other more than fraternal twins do on some trait, the excess similarity is attributed to genes, and that gives the trait's heritability. A heritability of roughly 0.8 for adult height means that 80 percent of an individual's height comes from their genes, with only about 20 percent coming from the environment. It follows that environmental interventions such as better nutrition or reduced childhood illness can have only modest effects on height within a population, because environment accounts for so little of the variation. By the same reasoning, when one population is on average shorter than another, the gap is likely to be largely genetic: if genes explain most of the variation among individuals, they probably explain most of the difference between groups as well. Finally, because the heritability of IQ rises from modest levels in early childhood to substantially higher levels in adulthood, differences in early family environment must have little lasting effect on adult intelligence."

I have a vague sense, from reading debates online, that some of these moves are too quick — I keep seeing the slogan that high heritability does not imply immutability, and the claim that heritability within a group says nothing about differences between groups. But the author is a professional, and I don't know enough behavioral genetics to say which sentences are defensible, which are subtle misstatements, and whether the author's conclusions follow from the defensible parts. Could you go through the passage roughly claim by claim and tell me what a careful behavioral geneticist would accept, what they would correct, and what the corrected version would and would not imply?
