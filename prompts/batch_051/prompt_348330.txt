I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, and in a few weeks I will present a chapter from my dissertation at my department's political theory workshop. The chapter defends a two-chamber mixed lottocracy: a citizens' assembly chosen by stratified random sampling from the whole adult population, charged with setting the legislative agenda, assigning bills to committees, and returning bills to the floor with amendments that the elected chamber may override only by a three-fifths vote; the elected chamber then deliberates under that agenda and takes the final vote on each bill. My central claim is that this division of labor captures what sortition is distinctively good at—breaking the agenda-setting cartel of party leaders and organized donors and forcing issues onto the floor that a professional political class prefers to bury—without surrendering the retrospective accountability that elections supply. I treat full lottocracy, with a randomly selected chamber replacing elections entirely, as a strawman, and I argue that the mixed design is the version that should be taken seriously as a constitutional proposal rather than a deliberative experiment.

The workshop will include several deliberative democrats who have organized or studied citizens' assemblies and are sympathetic to sortition in principle but doubt it can be scaled into a standing institution with real agenda control; two formal theorists who will press on incentives, information, and accountability chains; and a senior comparative politics scholar who thinks I have ignored how real legislatures aggregate information and manage coalitional bargaining. Please prepare me for the question period as a committed preparation sheet, not a survey of possibilities: the objections I will actually receive, in roughly the order of how likely and how damaging each is, phrased in the terms this audience would use, each with a definite reply I can give in about two minutes and a frank note on where that reply is soft, together with what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to concede. Include the question I most hope nobody asks. Write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a written briefing, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
