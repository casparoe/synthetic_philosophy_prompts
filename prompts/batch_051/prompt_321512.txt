I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, writing a chapter on the ethics of airpower and civilian targeting, and I keep going in circles over Anscombe's "Mr. Truman's Degree." The pull toward her view is real. The doctrine of double effect gives me something I find hard to get anywhere else: a principled line between choosing civilian deaths as one's means, which is murder no matter what it buys, and letting civilian deaths happen as a foreseen side-effect of hitting a military target, which then just has to pass a proportionality test. And her reading of the war as a slide from a norm the great powers accepted to a norm nobody accepted seems almost exactly right. She opens with this:

"In 1939, on the outbreak of war, the President of the United States asked for assurances from the belligerent nations that civil populations would not be attacked.

In 1945, when the Japanese enemy was known by him to have made two attempts toward a negotiated peace, the President of the United States gave the order for dropping an atom bomb on a Japanese city; three days later a second bomb, of a different type, was dropped on another city. No ultimatum was delivered before the second bomb was dropped."

That contrast is what makes me want to just submit to the theory and let it run. But then I notice that I do not, in my gut, think Truman was a murderer. Its not that I want the bombing to have been right; I'd much rather keep the clean, exceptionless view. It's more like the intuition is in me against my will. Maybe it's the projected invasion-casualty numbers, maybe it's the thought that at least the first bomb was aimed at ending the war, maybe it's that I can't imagine myself, sitting in his chair in August 1945, doing otherwise. Whatever the source, the intuition is firm: whatever else should be said about Hiroshima, calling it the kind of deliberate killing the theory calls it seems wrong. And that is exactly the reaction Anscombe is trying to disqualify.
