I'm a retired wildlife biologist, and after years of rolling my eyes at the humanities I've fallen into Hobbes. (The bit that stuck: a promise without something to enforce it is only words. I'm paraphrasing, I know.) I've now been put on the board of our little land trust, mostly because no one else wanted it.

We do a lot of what the board calls handshake conservation agreements — a landowner promises to leave a wetland alone or keep cows out of a creek, and in return we do some maintenance, split some costs, sometimes make a one-time payment. Some of these hold for two decades; others die the first time the property changes hands, or whenever the landowner gets a better offer from a developer. We can't force anyone to do anything; there is no sheriff behind us, and we can't afford a lawyer for every deal.

So I'd like you to turn Hobbes's logic of the state of nature and the social contract into a decision procedure we can run in about an hour, before we commit time or money to an agreement. The procedure should help us ask when a covenant is likely to be stable without a common power over both parties, and when we are just paying for a peace that won't last.

Please give:
- the questions to ask, in order;
- what counts as a good enough answer to each;
- where the procedure stops with a no;
- where it hands the decision to a person because the theory doesn't settle it.

Then run the procedure on three made-up cases: (1) a retired couple who wants to donate a restrictive covenant on their own 40 acres; (2) a dairy farmer who will only agree to a riparian buffer if we fully compensate lost production each year; (3) a developer who will grant a conservation easement in exchange for a tax break and a density bonus from the township. Make sure at least one case comes out "don't do it" even though the landowner is friendly and the board is enthusiastic. And be straight about what the procedure can't tell us — including, I suspect, whether a deal is actually just, rather than merely stable.
