Background: I'm a software engineer and I keep thinking about seccession rules the way I think about API design — the constitutional clause is the interface, and politicians are adversarial callers who'll exploit any undefined behavior. No philosophy training, so forgive the vocabulary.

Here's a mechanism I've been stress-testing. Imagine a country adopts this as a constitutional clause available to any historical region (roughly, a territory with its own legislature that's been part of the country for at least fifty years):

1. A petition signed by 20% of the region's registered voters triggers a binding independence referendum, but the signatures expire after two years.
2. The vote is yes/no, with a 60% turnout threshold, and independence carries only at 55% or more of votes cast.
3. If it passes, nothing changes immediately. There is a five-year transition: the region stays in the currency union, must keep freedom of movement with the rest of the country, and pays a share of the national debt set by arbitration, capped at the region's population share times 1.3.
4. If it fails, there's a twelve-year cooldown before another petition, but if a pro-independence party then wins an absolute majority of regional seats on a single-issue platform, the cooldown drops to four years.
5. The central government cannot veto the vote, but it writes the ballot question, subject to review by a constitutional court for neutrality. During the petition phase, the centre may also put a second, pre-negotiated option on the ballot: stay, but with devo-max (full tax and legal autonomy, no foreign policy or its own currency).

I want someone to walk me through how this actually plays out, not whether it's right. What does it incentivize for regional separatist parties, unionist parties, the central government, neighboring regions that don't get a vote, and ordinary people in the region (businesses, pensioners, young people thinking about moving)? Where does it get gamed first — timing referenda to wreck the centre's bargaining position, massaging the definition of the historical region, pumping turnout through fear-mongering, dragging out the debt arbitration, or the centre using the devo-max option to split the yes vote? Would Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec-style movements behave differently under this, and what does that reveal about what the clause can't control? And which failure modes could be patched without defeating the whole point of giving secession a legal path?
