I've slipped into an argumentative habit and I want to know if its actually defensible. Whenever a debate seems to turn on how a word or category gets used — whether a hot dog is a sandwich, whether viruses are alive, whether streaming a song counts as owning it — I write it off as a merely verbal dispute: everyone agrees on the descriptive facts, and the rest is just different people preferring different definitions, so there's nothing left to be right or wrong about. Lately I've been applying this to bigger, more abstract questions too. If someone asks whether numbers exist, or whether a statue is the same object as the lump of clay it was shaped from, I say each side is just using 'exist' or 'same object' differently and the disagreement disappears once you pin down the words. My roommate says this is a cop-out, that I'm treating substantive questions as dictionary squabbles because I don't want to take a stand. I genuinely can't see what would remain to argue about after the non-linguistic facts are fixed. So is this policy of mine defensible? If it is, what separates the disputes I should dismiss as verbal from the ones I should treat as substantive, and have I overreached by extending the move to existence questions?
