There's a asymmetry in how I value effort that I can't sort out. Two people write equally good novels. One bangs theirs out over six relaxed months; the other takes ten years, wrecks a marriage, nearly goes broke, and still only ends up with the same quality of book. People are far more impressed by the second book—not just by the person, but the achievement feels bigger. Yet if you'd offered the second writer, before any of it, a guarantee of the identical book with none of the suffering, nobody would call them irrational for taking it; we'd probably think they where mad to turn it down. Same pattern in moral cases: the person who saves a child while terrified gets more admiration than the one who saves the child on instinct, even though, again, any of us would choose to be the instinctual one. And effort with nothing to show for it—a decade of failed novels—impresses nobody. So backward-looking praise treats effort as if it adds value to the success, while forward-looking choice treats it as a pure cost, and failed effort gets almost no credit at all. Is there a principle that makes these three judgments consistent, or is our admiration for effort just a way of making outcomes feel earned? If you had to, would you keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give up on effort having any value beyond its results?
