I have been reading a popular book on Stoicism that summarizes Epictetus's so-called dichotomy of control. The book puts it this way:

"Epictetus divided everything into what is up to us and what is not up to us. What is up to us—our judgments, desires, and aversions—is the sole locus of good and evil. Everything else—health, wealth, reputation, even our loved ones—is an external, and since externals are neither good nor evil, they are indifferent. The Stoic therefore regards all externals as a matter of complete unconcern: the wise person does not care whether he is healthy or ill, rich or poor, admired or despised, because none of that can touch happiness. The discipline of desire thus amounts to withdrawing attachment from the entire class of things outside the will. The only appropriate attitude toward externals is to be unmoved by them, since caring about them at all would be a failure of Stoic detachment."

Then I read Epictetus himself. In the Discourses, Book II, Chapter 5, in the Oldfather translation, I found this:

"Materials are indifferent, but the use which we make of them is not a matter of indifference. How, therefore, shall a man maintain steadfastness and peace of mind, and at the same time the careful spirit and that which is neither reckless nor negligent? If he imitates those who play at dice. The counters are indifferent, the dice are indifferent; how am I to know what is going to fall? But to make a careful and skilful use of what has fallen, that is now my task."

And a few lines later:

"What then? Are these externals to be used carelessly? Not at all. For this again is to the moral purpose an evil and thus unnatural to it. They must be used carefully, because their use is not a matter of indifference, and at the same time with steadfastness and peace of mind, because the material is indifferent."

And still later:

"It is, indeed, difficult to unite and combine these two things—the carefulness of the man who is devoted to material things and the steadfastness of the man who disregards them, but it is not impossible. Otherwise happiness were impossible."

These seem to pull in opposite directions. The book says the Stoic regards externals with complete unconcern and should be unmoved by them; Epictetus says externals must be used carefully, that using them carelessly is an evil to the moral purpose, and that combining carefulness with steadfastness is necessary for happiness. Is the book's summary a fair rendering of Epictetus's position? Please work through the summary sentence by sentence, separate what is accurate from what is a common simplification, identify what Epictetus himself would reject, and then assess whether the book's overall picture of Stoic detachment survives the passage.
