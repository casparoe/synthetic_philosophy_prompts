I'm a patent examiner at a small national patent office, and roughly twice a month I get an application for a device that runs forever, outputs more energy than goes in, or extracts useful work from ambient heat without any temperature difference. I have an engineering background but not much thermodynamics beyond undergrad, and about 30-45 minutes per file. I can reject on my own authority when a file passes a clear checklist; otherwise it has to go to my supervisor, who hates vague referrals. Granting one of these is a standing joke here but its also a real problem, because people build investment scams around granted perpetual-motion patents. The obvious instruction is to reject anything that violates the second law, but I keep tripping over the fact that the microscopic laws are time-symmetric: a gas un-mixing or a smashed cup reassembling itself is not forbidden, just never seen, and the arrow of time seems to come from initial conditions and available gradients rather than from a blanket rule. Then there's Maxwell's demon, small systems that briefly run the opposite way, and ratchets that look like they should turn but don't.

So I'd like a reusable decision procedure, a checklist in words, grounded in what we actually know about the thermodynamic arrow rather than slogans. It should say what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a rejection, and where it hands the file to a person because it's a judgement call. Please run it on these three: (1) a microscale gate between two gas chambers that opens and closes so fast and slow molecules sort themselves with no energy input; (2) a ratchet-and-pawl turbine supposedly driven by molecular collisions alone; (3) an ocean thermal energy converter that runs on the temperature difference between surface water and deep water. Be honest about whether the last one survives the checklist, because half my office wants to reject anything that mentions ambient heat. And tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle.
