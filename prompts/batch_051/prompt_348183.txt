An incongruity-resolution theorist and a benign-violation theorist of humour, each tired of talking past the other, have agreed to draft an adversarial-collaboration statement rather than exchange further papers. Write the document they would both sign.

For the purposes of the document, the two positions should be understood as follows. The incongruity-resolution camp holds that humour consists in the recognition of an incongruity together with its resolution: a person notices that two frames or scripts clash, and then finds some way — however partial, local, or retrospective — of making the clash intelligible; without such a resolution the response is puzzlement, anxiety, or aesthetic interest rather than mirth. The benign-violation camp holds that humour occurs when a situation is simultaneously appraised as a violation of some norm — moral, social, linguistic, logical, practical, or physical — and as benign, acceptable, or safe; no separate resolution stage is required, and cases in which a resolution is reported are simply cases in which the resolution is one way of making the violation feel benign.

The statement should contain the following sections.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign without gritting their teeth. These should be non-vacuous and should cover, at minimum: whether all humour involves some kind of incongruity, violation, or both; why the same content can be funny in one context and offensive or merely puzzling in another; which effects or functions of humour both sides accept; and how individual differences in threat sensitivity bear on the threshold for amusement.

2. Disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give four columns: the precise question; the incongruity-resolution answer, worded as its proponent would accept; the benign-violation answer, worded as its proponent would accept; and a final column recording whether, after negotiation, the two sides regard the disagreement as substantive or partly verbal. Pay particular attention to whether "benign appraisal" is merely "resolution" under another name, or whether each notion does work the other cannot.

3. Cruxes. Three to five sub-questions such that, were one side's answer to a crux established, at least one camp would revise its position. Each crux should be stated as a question both sides agree is load-bearing, with a sentence explaining why it matters.

4. Tests and predictions. For each crux, a concrete case or comparison that both sides accept bears on it, specified in enough detail that the two sides' predictions come apart, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Draw on at least two of the following, chosen because the two theories tell systematically different stories about them: unresolved absurdist humour (nonsense verse, Dada, anti-jokes); the fate of a joke on repeated exposure; content that violates a norm at different psychological distances; and grave or threatening violations that become funny only when distance or framing changes.

5. Closing paragraph. A summary each side could endorse in front of its own colleagues: what has genuinely been agreed, what remains open, and which purported disagreement the collaboration revealed to be a matter of wording.

Do the difficult work in the drafting: formulations must be precise enough that the two accounts make different predictions, yet neutral enough that neither side would object to how its own view is recorded. If, on some large class of cases, the two positions turn out to be one view with different emphases, say so and identify the residue.
