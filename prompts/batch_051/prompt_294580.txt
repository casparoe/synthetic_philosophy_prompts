I've been thinking through a puzzle on my own — I later learned it has a name, the toxin puzzle — and a line of reasoning keeps pushing me toward a conclusion that feels too strong. I'd like you to go through the steps and tell me whether the argument actually works.

Here is the situation. An entirely reliable person makes me an offer. Tonight at midnight, I must genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make me ill for a day and then leave no trace. Tomorrow morning, if at midnight I had that intention, I'll be paid one million dollars; if I didn't, I'll be paid nothing. The payment is fixed by my intention at midnight alone; whether I actually drink tomorrow afternoon changes nothing. I care about the money and about avoiding the illness, and nothing else about drinking matters to me one way or the other. I also have no way to change my later incentives or manufacture the intention from outside — no side bets, no hypnosis, no precommitment devices. The only thing in play is whether I can form the intention.

My reasoning runs like this:

1. At the moment tomorrow when I would actually drink, the money has already been paid or not paid. Drinking earns me nothing and only costs me a day of illness.
2. A fully informed, rational person facing those payoffs will not drink the toxin when the moment comes.
3. So at midnight, if I am that person, I know I will not drink tomorrow afternoon.
4. But I cannot genuinely intend at midnight to drink if I already know I will not drink. I can want to have the intention, or wish I were the sort of person who would drink, or regret that I cannot form it — but none of that is the same as intending to drink.
5. The offer requires the intention itself, not the wanting-to-have-it.
6. Therefore a fully informed, rational person cannot form the required intention, and the million dollars is unreachable for such a person.

That conclusion seems absurd to me: it says rationality locks me out of a fortune that depends only on a state of my own mind. But perhaps it isn't absurd, or perhaps the argument buys the conclusion too cheaply. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and on what reading of its key terms? If the argument is valid, say what I am missing about why the conclusion is less paradoxical than it first sounds, or what the setup quietly assumes. I half-suspect step 4 is doing more work than I can see, but I cannot say exactly what.

One request. If the question is badly put, or the way I've set things up slides in an assumption that already decides the answer, tell me that plainly rather than patiently working around it. I'd rather be told that the maze is built wrong than be led through a maze that was built wrong.
