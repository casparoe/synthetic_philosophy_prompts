I started my PhD in a lab that does active inference stuff last fall, and I keep running into the same argument about the free energy principle that never seems to get anywhere. The skeptics in the department say the principle is basically empty: once you grant that an organism persists over time and has a boundary between itself and the outside (a Markov blanket), saying it 'minimizes free energy' or 'minimizes surprise' is just a fancy mathematical way of saying it doesn't dissolve or die, so there's nothing that could count against it. Our lab folks say this is exactly the lazy reading — the principle only looks empty if you refuse to commit to an actual generative model of the creature's environment, and once you do, it predicts concrete things about perception, action, and even how attention gets allocated. They also point out that nobody says predictive coding is unfalsifiable, and the free energy principle is just the larger framework that makes sense of it. Everyone has heard the other side's points a hundred times and nobody's moved; some of the grad students have started wondering if the two camps are even talking about the same thing.

My own suspicion is that one side is treating the free energy principle as an empirical claim about what brains actually do, while the other is treating it as a kind of normative principle about how any adaptive system has to be modelled, and those aren't the kind of claim that can directly contradict eachother. But I'm not confident about that. What I'd like is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, about standards of evidence, about what counts as a scientific explanation, or about two different questions living under one name — and, given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress. A formal result? A concrete experiment? Or is the argument just a sign that the whole question is ill-posed?
