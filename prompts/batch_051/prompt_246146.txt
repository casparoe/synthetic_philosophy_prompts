My paper for next month's economic history workshop argues that the familiar debunking of path dependence is aimed at the wrong target. The room will be mostly empirical economists and microeconomic theorists, with a couple of economic historians—people who treat market selection for efficiency as the default hypothesis and who arrive inclined to think path dependence has been overclaimed. The standard debunking move is to show that an allegedly locked-in outcome was not actually inefficient: QWERTY had defensible technical advantages, Betamax did not lose on pure merit, later re-examinations undercut the classic horror stories. My claim is that even if all of that is right, the interesting version of path dependence remains untouched. The interesting version is not that a worse outcome persisted because history mattered; it is that, early on, among several adequate alternatives, a choice that was not obviously better ex ante settled which practices, standards, and beneficiaries would persist, and the resulting equilibrium is efficient but not unique. What is locked in is which efficient equilibrium got selected and who is stuck bearing the adjustment costs of living in it. On my view, a convincing demonstration that QWERTY was good enough does not refute lock-in; it is closer to confirming it, because it shows that the selection among adequate alternatives did its work without any efficiency pressure to reverse it.
