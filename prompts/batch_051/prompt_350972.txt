Give me your actual credence that there is at least one genuine moral dilemma, where a genuine dilemma means that one and the same agent, in a single situation, all-things-considered ought to do each of two mutually exclusive things—not merely that two pro tanto obligations pull in opposite directions and leave a moral remainder. Decompose the number into at least these terms: (a) the probability that ought-claims are truth-apt and stand in logical relations, rather than being merely prescriptive or expressive; (b) conditional on (a), the probability that the correct deontic logic permits conflicts at the level of all-things-considered ought—say, by rejecting agglomeration or restricting ought-implies-can—instead of ruling them out; (c) conditional on (b), the probability that the stock examples (a student forced to choose between joining the resistance and caring for his mother; a parent forced to choose which of two children to save; three promises that cannot all be kept) are genuine instances of the permitted conflict, rather than merely tragic cases of incompatible pro tanto obligations. Give each sub-question a probability and a one-sentence justification, then state how the parts combine and which one is doing the most work. My own credence is about 0.35 and a colleague's is about 0.7; say where our disagreement most likely lives. Finally, say what evidence or argument would move your number the most. Write your answer as exactly six numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your overall credence; (2)–(4) the three sub-probabilities and their justifications; (5) the combination and the most load-bearing term; (6) where my 0.35 and my colleague's 0.7 most likely diverge, and what would move you most.
