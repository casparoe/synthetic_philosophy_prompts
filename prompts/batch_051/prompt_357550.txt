I'm a philosophy of mind professor preparing a one-page proposal for a six-month fellowship, and I want it built around an idea I haven't been able to sharpen on my own. Over the past year our department has run a joint reading group with a computational neuroscience lab, and I've repeatedly noticed the two sides talking past each other about whether the brain is a computer: the neuroscientists defend the claim that their models explain neural data, while the philosophers attack the claim that neural processes literally are computations, and each side leaves convinced the other has missed the point. I suspect the sentence "the brain is a computer" covers at least three claims with potentially different answers — an explanatory claim (computational models explain neural phenomena), a metaphysical claim (neural processes implement computations in a robust, non-vacuous sense), and a methodological claim (a computational level of description is indispensable to doing neuroscience at all). The question I want funded is whether the deadlock in the computationalism debate is largely an artifact of this conflation, and whether the controversy would decompose into smaller, tractable disputes, some favoring each side, once the claims are separated.

Please write the proposal so that it does the following in order: states the question precisely; explains why the existing positions on both sides leave it open; describes the approach honestly — I imagine a rational reconstruction of the canonical moves (multiple realizability, the triviality-or-pancomputationalism objection, the semantic-content objection) in which each move is mapped onto the three claims to see which one it actually supports or undermines — and says why this approach is preferable to the obvious alternatives of defending one side or surveying the literature; and states what a result and a failure would look like. I would count the project a success if the taxonomy shows that at least two stock arguments are not genuinely opposing each other while still locating one remaining disagreement that is substantive rather than merely verbal; I would count it a failure if every substantive dispute survives the separation intact. Include an honest assessment of whether six months is a realistic scope. Don't include citations or a reading list; I will add those myself.

One further instruction: if the framing rests on a confusion — for example, if the explanatory or methodological claims collapse into the metaphysical claim once they are made precise — say so plainly and recast the question, rather than writing a polished proposal for a non-question. As for format, give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the question, the gap, the approach and why it beats the alternatives, the criteria for success and failure, and the feasibility in six months.
