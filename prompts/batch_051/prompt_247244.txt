I have been trying to settle a running dispute about how seriously to take a certain implication of expected-value reasoning, and I would like your help. Expected-value reasoning instructs us to multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability and choose the action with the highest sum. That rule has a notorious implication: if utilities are unbounded and probabilities are taken literally, then any action with a nonzero probability of producing an astronomically large amount of value — such as preventing an existential catastrophe or generating an enormous number of future lives — can dominate all competing actions that produce merely large amounts of value with high confidence, provided the astronomical payoff is large enough. A philanthropist choosing between a well-evidenced malaria-prevention program and a wildly speculative one-in-a-billion intervention that might prevent human extinction seems forced, on straightforward expected-value grounds, to fund the speculative intervention. Call this the fanaticism problem.

Below are eight responses that have been defended or floated as ways of resisting the fanaticism problem. For each I give a brief gloss; do not treat my gloss as authoritative, and say so if you think a gloss mischaracterizes the response.

A. Bounded utility: place an upper limit on how much value any outcome can contribute, so that no single payoff, however large, can swamp finite high-probability goods.
B. Probability thresholding: treat sufficiently small probabilities as zero for purposes of decision, on the grounds that they fall below any meaningful resolution.
C. Diminishing marginal value of utility itself: apply a concave transformation to utilities so that enormous payoffs contribute less than their face value.
D. Rejecting expected-value maximization under moral uncertainty: replace it with a more conservative decision rule, such as maximin or a rule requiring a robustly positive expected value across a set of plausible models.
E. Biting the bullet: accept fanaticism as a correct, if uncomfortable, consequence of taking stakes and probabilities seriously.
F. Imprecise probabilities: represent deep uncertainty about the speculative intervention's probability and payoff with sets of probabilities or intervals, and refuse to let a single point estimate dominate.
G. Cluelessness-based skepticism: hold that for many speculative interventions our uncertainty about the sign, let alone the magnitude, of the expected value is so severe that the calculation is not action-guiding at all.
H. Domain restriction: treat expected-value maximization as appropriate for ordinary prudential choice but not for moral choices involving astronomically large stakes, where different norms apply.

Your task is to rank these eight responses from most to least defensible as a solution to the fanaticism problem. By defensible I mean that the response genuinely blocks the fanatical conclusion where that conclusion is implausible, while preserving as much as possible of what makes expected-value reasoning attractive: sensitivity to both probability and stakes, transitivity, and practical usability. First state, in a sentence or two, the criteria you are using. Then rank all eight responses, with a short paragraph for each explaining why it lands where it does; where two responses are close, say what consideration would move one above the other. Please write in American English. End your answer with a line of the form \answer{...} containing your ranking as a comma-separated list of the labels from most to least defensible, for example \answer{E, A, F, ...}.
