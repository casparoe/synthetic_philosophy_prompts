Here is a claim I keep encountering in discussions of whole-brain emulation: that behavioral indistinguishability, if it is sustained long enough and across a wide enough range of circumstances, is all the evidence we could have that an emulation is the same mind as the original — and therefore all the evidence we should want. I suspect that "same mind" is doing more work than any third-person test can reach, but I do not want to dismiss the claim by fiat; I want to see it pushed as far as it can go.

Design the strongest concrete test you can of the proposition that a whole-brain emulation preserves the original's mind, rather than merely reproducing the original's observable behavior. Be explicit about the protocol, the comparison condition, and the result that would count as passing. As a starting point, consider a contrast between two protocols: (1) gradual replacement, in which a subject's neurons are replaced one at a time by functionally equivalent emulated units over an extended period while the subject remains conscious and self-reporting; and (2) scan-and-copy, in which the original is destroyed and an emulation is launched from the scan. In both cases, suppose the resulting system is behaviorally indistinguishable from the original baseline. Either develop that contrast into a genuine test or replace it with something better.

Then state precisely what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, separate the layers of functional fidelity, psychological continuity, and whatever residue — qualitative consciousness, the importance of causal continuity versus pattern identity, the possibility that there is no fact of the matter — cannot be reached by any third-person test. If your best test cannot separate two positions that people routinely distinguish in this debate, flag that rather than pretending the evidence forces a choice.
