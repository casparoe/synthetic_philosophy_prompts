Design a runnable 90-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar of fourteen students, a mix of philosophy and linguistics majors, on the question of whether words have meanings of a fixed, context-independent kind. The idea you are trying to make participants feel is a qualified negative answer: for many ordinary words, competent application shifts with the purpose of the exchange, so that two speakers can know all the same non-linguistic facts and still, without empirical or linguistic error on either side, take opposite views about whether a word applies to the very same thing.

To fix the target, the activity should be built around a starter case in the spirit of the following, which you should paraphrase in your own words as part of the facilitator's setup; no attribution or citation is needed, and you should not treat it as a quotation from a text. A Japanese maple has leaves whose natural color is a reddish-purple, but a gardener sprays the leaves green for a party. A botanist who is selecting specimens for a display of natural foliage color is asked whether the plant has green leaves and says no. A set decorator who needs a green backdrop is asked whether the same plant has green leaves and says yes. Each can pass the other's tests perfectly: the botanist can see the green pigment, and the decorator can see the underlying red. Neither has misperceived the leaves. What differs is what the word is being used to do in each exchange.

The activity must make participants run into this phenomenon before it is named. Rules, incentives, or roles should produce the collision themselves rather than the facilitator announcing it. The design should include all of the following:

1. Roles or rules. Divide the room into teams or assign individual roles whose practical goals pull in different directions on one or two carefully chosen words. The word or words should be ordinary (for example, green, empty, safe, clean, food, book, or a stronger choice of your own), but the edge cases should be genuine: items for which ordinary competent application flips as interests change. Give each team a concrete task with real but small stakes, such as points, a mock client contract, or a race to build a shared inventory, where success depends on classifying edge cases according to the team's assigned purpose.
2. A timed sequence. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 90 minutes, including setup, a first classification round, a twist, and the debrief. The twist should prevent teams from treating their classifications as private: for example, teams with different purposes may be forced to merge their classifications into one shared definition, negotiate a single policy, or arbitrate a public dispute, with a penalty for papering over the disagreement with vague wording.
3. A built-in trap. Arrange the material so that at least one team will confidently classify an item one way with a correct justification of its own, and then discover that another team classified the same item the opposite way with an equally correct justification, without either side being able to point to a factual or definitional mistake in the other. Choose at least one edge case where this collision is likely rather than merely possible.
4. A debrief. The debrief should not be a lecture summary. It should map specific moments in the activity onto the target idea: the moment classifications collided, the moment someone tried to save a fixed definition and what it cost them, and any moment someone wanted to say the other team was simply using the word badly. Name the view the activity supports, the view it puts pressure on, and why the Japanese-maple style case is not best handled as mere ambiguity plus a list of senses. Include what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, and how to handle each failure mode (for example, teams may insist the other side has made a mistake, or may demand that the facilitator settle the issue).
5. An honest limitation note. Explain which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by reading or discussion afterward, such as the fact that the same pressure accumulates across a whole linguistic community over time, or the gap between this phenomenon and any single decisive counterexample to fixed meaning.

After the activity design, add a short self-assessment: state how confident you are that the activity will produce the intended insight rather than confusion, and identify the single point in the design you are least sure will work as intended.
