Write a critical essay reading Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day through Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith. I am preparing a philosophy-through-literature unit and want the reading to be faithful in both directions. On one side, Stevens the butler-narrator is a natural candidate for bad faith: he presents his life as a sequence of requirements laid down by service, dignity, and loyalty, treating his own decisions as if they were not decisions at all. On Sartre's view that is exactly the structure of bad faith—denying one's freedom by identifying with a fixed role and letting values or duties pretend to settle what one must do. On the other side, the novel is a first-person retrospective, and Stevens's self-deception includes genuine forgetting, selective memory, and delayed recognition in ways that sit awkwardly with Sartre's insistence that bad faith is a project in which the truth is not simply absent to the deceiver. Take a definite position: is Stevens best understood as engaged in Sartrean bad faith, or does the novel expose a limit in Sartre's concept? Identify the scenes or narrative moments that most support the reading and those that most strain it, and conclude by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical debate about bad faith that a standard treatment of the concept would miss. Do not end on a hedge.
