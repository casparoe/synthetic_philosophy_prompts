A study-group friend of mine has argued himself into a corner and now I cant talk him out of it. His claim is that respect for persons cannot be both an attitude and something owed to everyone, so one of those ideas has to go. Heres the argument as he laid it out.

(1) To respect someone is to hold them in some kind of positive regard — to think well of them.
(2) Respect for persons is owed to every person just because they are a person.
(3) If something is owed to every person just because they are a person, then it is owed equally and unconditionally: it doesn't depend on their particular qualities, and it can't be earned, forfeited, or graded.
(4) But positive regard is a quality-tracking attitude: it comes in degrees, it can be earned and lost, and it would be strange to feel the very same positive regard toward a close friend, a stranger, and someone who has wronged you.
(5) So no attitude of positive regard could be owed equally and unconditionally to all persons.
(6) Therefore either respect for persons is not an attitude at all — just shorthand for respectful actions like not interfering or insulting people — or it is not really something we owe.
(7) Both horns look unacceptable. We criticize people for contempt itself, not only for contemptuous behavior, and we treat respecting persons as non-negotiable.
(8) So the idea of respect for persons, understood as an owed attitude, is incoherent.

Before I could respond, he anticipated the obvious reply. He said: sure, maybe respect in (1) means esteem, and there is another sense of respect where you just take seriously that someone is a person with moral standing. But he thinks that other sense cant save the idea of owed respect as an attitude, because taking seriously that someone has moral standing is just a belief — believing they have a status, the way you believe a stranger has a heart. And a belief is not an attitude that can be owed in the way respect is supposed to be: you can't make yourself feel something just by registering a status. So, he says, the dilemma stands: the thing that is clearly an attitude (esteem) isn't owed equally, and the thing that is owed equally (the belief in the person's standing) isn't really an attitude.

I'd most like to keep claims (2) and (7). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost — what do I have to say about what attitudes are, and about owing them, to make the position hold together?
