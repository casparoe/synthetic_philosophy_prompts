A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether the alignment tax in models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the same shape as a familiar objection to the ideal of the moral saint, and I left the argument unable to tell which of us was right. The RLHF side: a model is optimized against a single learned reward model that compresses many qualities users care about—accuracy, willingness to challenge a user's false premise, calibrated confidence, dry humor—into one scalar, and as optimization pressure rises, real but under-measured qualities get drained away even when raters would reward them if they appeared. The moral-saint side: the familiar objection to moral perfection says a person who treats moral reasons as always decisive and tries to be maximally good will starve non-moral excellences such as wit, aesthetic absorption, and adventurousness, not because those are wrong, but because a single dominating standard pushes every good it does not encode toward the minimum. The mechanism looks identical: one objective, maximized, bleeds out the values it omits. My friend objected that this is where the analogy breaks: the RLHF tax is a fixable defect in a measuring instrument, whereas the moral saint's narrowing would persist even with perfect knowledge of what morality requires, since it follows from the commitment to treat moral considerations as always decisive. I cannot decide whether that difference is the point where the structures come apart or merely a restatement of the resemblance. Is the parallel deep, superficial, or somewhere in between? State the mapping as precisely as you can, say what would have to be true of both sides for conclusions to transfer, and name at least one prediction the analogy would license that you take to be false.
