I am the chief actuary at a mid-sized life insurer. My underwriters price applicants by assigning each one to a reference class — the age table, the smoker table, the occupation table — but different underwriters pick different classes for the same applicant, and the same applicant can come out with materially different premiums depending on the choice. We write thousands of these policies a quarter, and a class choice that is either discriminatory or actuarially unsound can produce complaints, lawsuits, or losses across an entire block of business.

I want a single written decision procedure, usable by an underwriter with no background in probability theory or philosophy and executable in about fifteen minutes per file, for deciding which reference class or combination of classes to use when pricing an individual applicant.

I am aware that this runs into the reference class problem: an applicant belongs to indefinitely many classes at once — a 52-year-old non-smoking male with treated hypertension, a private pilot's license, an occupation, a postal code, a family history — and the classes carry different observed mortality rates, while there may be no fact of the matter about which class gives the applicant's true risk. The procedure must not pretend that problem dissolves; it should impose a defensible, repeatable discipline on a choice that is partly statistical and partly judgment.

The procedure should specify:

- the questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- when to prefer a single class, when to pool or average classes, and when to refuse to quantify risk on the available data;
- what to do when the narrowest class with usable data is very small, or when two well-supported classes point in different directions;
- where the procedure stops and hands the file to an underwriting manager or compliance counsel because the question is one of values, law, or policy rather than statistics — in particular, when the most predictive available class is one regulators or courts will treat as prohibited or suspect;
- how the final choice is to be explained to a regulator or defended in court.

Then run the procedure on three applicants:

1. A 52-year-old with treated hypertension who smokes occasionally and flies small planes for recreation; the age-sex table, the hypertension table, and the general-aviation table point in different directions.
2. An applicant in an occupation with a sharp elevated mortality signal, but the occupation class contains only a few hundred people in the available data and the signal is unstable year to year.
3. An applicant whose most predictive available class is a small geographic unit that functions, in practice, as a proxy for race or ethnicity.

Make sure at least one worked example is a case where the procedure gives an answer the company will not like — for instance, forbidding use of a class that would clearly raise or lower the premium — and end by saying explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which step of assigning a single-case probability cannot be mechanized, and why admitting that is better for an insurer than pretending the procedure has eliminated it.
