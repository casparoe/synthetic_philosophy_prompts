I'm a PhD student in historical linguistics and I've been asked to run a one-off fifty-minute section for first-year linguistics students on writing systems. I dont want to do the usual "tour of the world's scripts" thing. What I want is for them to leave with one specific idea: a writing system is a theory of what matters in a language, not a recording device. The sharp version for alphabets is that no real alphabet is purely phonemic — even the most "phonetic" alphabets keep morphology, etymology, or word identity in the spelling, and the one-letter-one-sound ideal is a useful fiction that actual orthographies keep violating on purpose.

I need a plan for teaching that, not an explanation of it for me. What has to be in place before the claim even makes sense — for instance, do they need the grapheme-versus-phoneme distinction first, or can I let that distinction fall out of the example? I want one carrying example to build the whole session around. The candidates I keep going back and forth on are the English plural spelled <s> but pronounced several ways while the spelling stays visually constant; sign/signature; or inventing a toy alphabet in class and letting them notice where it leaks. Which one should carry it, and what does the example need to have and not have so they don't walk away thinking "English spelling is broken" but instead notice the script is doing non-phonemic work on purpose? Then map the misconceptions I should expect — Chinese characters are pictures of ideas, alphabets are pure sound notation, phonemic spelling is just better, silent letters are useless leftovers — and say where in the sequence each one should be headed off, not just that they exist. Give me one question at the end I can use to check they got the actual point rather than just agreeing that English is weird. And tell me what to openly refuse to explain at this level; I'm worried someone will ask about the Great Vowel Shift and I'll lose the whole room for ten minutes.

Write the answer in American English.
