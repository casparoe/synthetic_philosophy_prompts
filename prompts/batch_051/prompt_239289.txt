I'm giving a talk at a workshop next month defending the claim that 'all-things-considered better than' can fail to be transitive. My line is that the right response to the familiar spectrum arguments ist to deny transitivity rather than to insist that one of the adjacent comparisons in the chain was mistaken. The argument I lean on runs a series of outcomes from, say, a shorter but very intense pain to a much longer but very mild pain, where each adjacent pair seems like an improvement when you tweak duration and intensity a little at a time, but the endpoints look ordered the opposite way. I claim the cost of saving transitivity is that you have to reject something that felt obviously right about an adjacent pair, and that the cost of nontransitivity is more manageable than people think.

The audience is mostly decision theorists and formal epistemologists, plus two welfare economists. They basically all think rational preference has to be transitive and that any 'better than' relation worth using must be representable by a utility function. Theyre the kind of people who will immediately say 'money pump' or 'just sharpen your predicates.' And include the one question I should be praying nobody asks.
