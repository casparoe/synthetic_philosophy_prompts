A recurring move in moral philosophy is to test an act by asking what would happen if everyone did that—understood more carefully as the question whether the agent could coherently will, or prescribe for all, the performance of the act by everyone in relevantly similar circumstances. The criticisms of this move which I already have on file, and which therefore should not be offered as my new item, are the following:

1. The aggregation problem. A maxim can be harmless in the single case and harmful in the aggregate, and the test provides no principled account of the population, threshold, or time-index at which the aggregate effect becomes decisive; it seems to condemn innocent acts whenever their universal performance would be collectively ruinous.

2. The maxim-tailoring problem. Since the test evaluates a maxim rather than a bare act, and a maxim's description can be made arbitrarily fine-grained, the verdict can often be reversed by gerrymandering the description—so the test appears to lack a stable object.

3. The recalcitrant-willer problem. Kant's contradiction-in-will branch convicts a maxim only when its universalization conflicts with ends the agent actually wills; a sufficiently self-sufficient or despairing agent can therefore will a universal law of non-beneficence, or of never developing his talents, without facing the relevant contradiction.

4. The coordination problem. Some practices—driving on a given side of the road, using a given currency—are right or wrong only relative to what others actually do, and neither their performance nor their prohibition is ruled out by any self-contained universal law; the test is silent where a convention has to be selected.

5. The empty-formalism charge. Universalizability is compatible with monstrous universal policies, such as a wholehearted ruthless egoism, so long as the agent is prepared to will them universally; consistency in willing seems not to supply moral content.

I am looking for a further objection to universalizability tests—whether aimed at Kant's categorical imperative, Hare's universal prescriptivism, or Marcus G. Singer's generalization argument—that is not on this list and not a mere rewording, special case, or combination of the items above. It may be an objection you regard as standard and think I have simply omitted, or one you construct yourself. Then say honestly whether the objection is any good: does it genuinely damage the test, or is there a plausible reply for a careful defender? If a defender would dismiss your objection as just another face of one of the five, explain why you think that dismissal is mistaken.
