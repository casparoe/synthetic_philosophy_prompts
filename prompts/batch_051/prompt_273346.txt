I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy writing a dissertation on medical decision-making, and I serve as the trainee member of our university hospital's clinical ethics committee. A case has been put on the agenda for next Tuesday, and the chair has asked me to draft the memo that will be circulated to members beforehand. The patient, a thirty-seven-year-old woman with end-stage amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, has signed an advance directive instructing that, immediately after she is declared legally dead, her body be transferred to a cryonics organization for low-temperature preservation. She arrived at the decision recently but after extended discussion with her family and a counselor; she is competent, understands that revival is not guaranteed, and frames the choice as a wager: while the probability of any future revival may be very small, the value to her of a restored life is so large that she considers the expected value worth pursuing. Her spouse and parents support her. The cryonics organization would supply a bedside standby team during her final hours and cover every cost; the hospital would bear no financial burden. Her attending physician is sympathetic but uncertain whether the standby protocol might compromise palliative comfort care, and is also concerned about exposing the hospital to criticism for cooperating with an unproven intervention. A palliative-care nurse has filed a written objection contending that the institution should not lend its facilities and staff time to a practice with no demonstrated chance of success, and that doing so would encourage the family to postpone grief. The committee must decide two things: first, whether to authorize the bedside standby and rapid post-mortem release for this patient, and under what conditions; second, whether to adopt a general position on cryonics transfer requests rather than deciding case by case. Please draft the memo. It should give a clear recommendation with a concrete time frame and safeguards, address the nurse's objection honestly, and engage the deeper question the case raises: whether a very small probability of revival is the sort of consideration that an institution should weigh at all, or whether institutional duties should be settled on other grounds.
