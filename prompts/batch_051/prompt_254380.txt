Until fairly recently I would have said I had a settled view about punishment. That changed after my brother was badly hurt by a repeat drunk driver; I followed the sentencing closely, and then served on a jury in an unrelated theft case. The two experiences left me less confident than I expected, and I would like help extracting whatever principle, if any, is behind my own reactions. I realize my verdicts may not cohere, and part of what I want is to learn which of them I should revise rather than defend.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, reported as honestly as I can.

1. A nineteen-year-old with no record takes a car for a short joyride and returns it undamaged, then confesses before anyone has identified him. I think a stint of community service is about right, and that prison would be clearly wrong.

2. A man in his early seventies confesses to a murder he committed decades ago. He has lived quietly and lawfully since, has no pattern of violence, and is now in the early stages of dementia; he is very unlikely to harm anyone again. I still think he must be sentenced for the murder, but I cannot say what the punishment is for, and I am less sure than I expected that it should include prison.

3. A first-time drunk driver with no prior record causes a crash that kills a pedestrian. He is genuinely devastated and will probably never drive again, let alone reoffend. I still think a prison sentence of several years is appropriate.

4. A parent steals infant formula for a hungry child. I would suspend the sentence or impose at most a nominal penalty, and I find the argument that leniency encourages shoplifting unpersuasive.

5. Two people attempt to murder someone, with equally deliberate intent; one misses and the other succeeds. I feel that the one who succeeded deserves the harsher sentence, but I cannot defend that difference in terms of what either intended or how dangerous either now is, and that troubles me.

6. An informant with a minor offense of her own offers to testify against a dangerous drug operation, but only on the condition that her own charge is dropped. I would accept the deal, even though it means letting a guilty person go unpunished, because the testimony is likely to prevent serious harm to others.

My working rule has been that punishment is justified only for the guilty, and that the sentence should be set by what the offender deserves, with consequences serving merely as a side-constraint against pointless or excessive suffering. But when I test that rule against what I have just listed, I seem to treat consequences as far more than a side-constraint in some cases and almost ignore them in others, which makes me suspect I am rationalizing whichever conclusion I already favored.

Could you tell me what principle, or what small set of competing principles, best captures the pattern of these verdicts? Where no single defensible principle captures all of them, say which verdicts it would force me to abandon and why. Please also apply the account you think best to two cases I have not settled: a terminally ill prisoner who seeks compassionate release after serving most of a long sentence, and a corporation that self-reports a regulatory violation after profiting from it for years. Finally, I would ask you to be self-critical: indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and say which argument or consideration would most plausibly lead you to reverse the principal verdict in your answer.
