I am a high school student taking a philosophy elective on ethical theory, and for the past few weeks we have been discussing loyalty, impartiality, and the obligations we have to particular people. I have been trying to pin down my own position, but I have ended up with a set of commitments that I circulate among without being able to tell whether they form a coherent package. I would like an audit of the set before I present it: which commitments genuinely pull against one another, which only seem to, and what the cheapest repairs would be. Here is the package I have so far.

(1) I believe in special obligations. The fact that someone is my friend or family member gives me a stronger reason to help that person than I have to help a stranger, even when the stranger's need is the same. If I can use an evening either to help a close friend through a crisis or to do an equal amount of good for several strangers, I do not think the friend's claim on me is simply canceled by the numbers; the relationship itself carries moral weight.

(2) I also believe that the moral point of view is impartial. When I assess whether a rule, practice, or policy is fair, I should be able to justify it from a standpoint that gives equal weight to everyone affected and does not quietly assign extra importance to me and mine just because they are mine.

(3) I believe friendship itself is necessarily partial. A person who gave my well-being no more weight than a stranger's would not be a friend in any meaningful sense, and I do not think impartiality toward a friend should be described as an ethical ideal.

(4) I regard loyalty to large and mostly anonymous groups—a nationality, a race, a school clique, a fan base—as bias rather than virtue. People often treat such loyalties as duties, but the line that picks out the group seems morally arbitrary in a way that a particular history of care with a particular person does not.

(5) I believe favoritism within institutional roles is wrong even when it is motivated by genuine love. A coach who gives their own child playing time over a clearly better player, or a teacher who quietly rounds up the grade of a friend's child, is not being a good parent or friend; they are corrupting a role that belongs, in some sense, to everyone.

(6) I do not want the view to rest on a bare split between private life and institutions. I should be able to explain, in one vocabulary, why the same underlying phenomenon—letting who-you-love matter more than what a stranger would be owed—is a virtue in one setting and a vice in another. If that explanation cannot be given, I am prepared to hear that one of (1) through (5) has to go.

Please go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ranking the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the conflict consists in, what the least costly revision would be, and what that revision would give up. Finally, tell me which single revision across the whole set would do the most to make the package coherent. I would rather have the fault lines identified precisely than receive a general survey of the loyalty-versus-impartiality debate. Please write the response in American English.
