I have been thinking about this issue since I recently served on a jury and could not stop wondering whether the defendant, given the life he had led, could ever have been anyone other than who he was. After the trial, a friend in my discussion group summarized Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for me this way:

"Strawson shows that no one can ever be truly morally responsible for what they do. You act as you do because of the way you are—your dispositions, values, and capacities—and you did not create the way you are. To be responsible for your action, you would have to be responsible for the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are, you would have to have intentionally brought about the way you are, which would require bringing about an earlier version of yourself, and so on without end. Moral responsibility therefore collapses for everyone in every case. It follows that praise, blame, and punishment are never genuinely deserved; they are an illusion we maintain only because society could not function without them."

I suspect the final step—the conclusion about blame and punishment—is stronger than the argument's premises warrant, but I cannot locate exactly where the summary goes wrong. Is this a fair statement of the Basic Argument? Please separate what is accurate, what is a simplification or overstatement, and whether the final claim about praise, blame, and punishment follows from the argument as summarized.
