I can't figure out what principle I actually hold about how a nation should remember its defeats, so here are my gut verdicts, and you tell me what principle is doing the work. A small town in Alabama keeps a Confederate monument put up in the early 1900s, with a plaque honoring the soldiers as heroes of a lost but righteous cause; I think it should come down. For a long time Israeli soldiers were sworn in at Masada, where Jewish rebels chose mass suicide over capture by the Romans; I can't work up the same objection, even though this is also a defeated people turning their defeat into a founding story. Serbian nationalists treat the 1389 Battle of Kosovo as a holy defeat that defines Serbian identity; I want to say this is bad, but it can't be bad for the same reason as Alabama, since the Serbs were the ones being invaded. Britain commemorates Dunkirk as an almost triumphant escape from disaster; I think this is mostly fine, even good. Is there one principle that gets all four of those, or close? What would it say about two cases I haven't settled: a museum in the American South that presents Reconstruction as a tyranny imposed by northern occupiers, and a Japanese war memorial that honors the war dead including men convicted of war crimes? And be honest: which of my four verdicts should I give up if I can't keep them all, or am I stuck with a messy principle?
