I spent my career in experimental biology, mostly endocrinology and later neuroendocrinology, and since I retired I've been working through philosophy of science and philosophy of psychiatry. Szasz's argument that mental illness is a myth is the first thing in ages that has me genuinely split down the middle. Put him at his strongest: disease ought to be a natural-kind term, tied to an identifiable lesion or at least a real pathophysiological process. If you call a pattern of thought and behavior an illness without that, you have made a category mistake and wrapped it in medical authority, and the word looses whatever diagnostic or explanatory work it was doing. On that view, the right thing to say about someone with severe schizophrenia — the voices, the fixed conviction that the neighbors are replacing his organs — is that he is not a person with a disease but a person caught in a terrible difficulty of living, and that if we ever find the lesion, the condition leaves psychiatry and becomes neurology. I have to admit the conceptual hygiene is exactly what I was trained to demand.

But I cannot make myself believe that conclusion about people I have known. The intuition that severe schizophrenia is an illness in something like the way rheumatoid arthritis is an illness is among the firmest I have, and I do not want to give it up just because I cannot point to a lesion. In endocrinology we treated plenty of disorders as real, unified diseases long before we had their mechanism; the missing mechanism made them mysterious, not mythical. Szasz would say those were physiological all along, so the cases are not parallel. Maybe my intuition is just conflating the judgment that this person needs care and may not be fully responsible with the judgment that this person has a disease. That is possible, but it does not feel like what is going on.

I am not asking for a verdict. I want you to tell me what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing I should drop, what would have to be true for the theory to be the thing I should revise, and whether there is a third route on which both survive — for instance, splitting the disease concept into its different jobs, such as picking out cases, explaining them, and licensing treatment, or treating illness as a functional notion rather than a lesional one. And I want some way of telling a genuine amendment apart from special pleading by someone who simply does not like where Szasz's argument leads. Show me what would make each concession reasonable, and what a mixed verdict would actually commit me to.
