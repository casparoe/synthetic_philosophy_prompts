I was rereading Descartes' Discourse on Method Part V and the passage where he gives his two tests for whether a machine built to look like a person would give itself away. Paraphrasing since I don't have the text in front of me: no machine could use words or other signs to respond to the sense of what's said the way even a dull human does, and even if one did some single task as well as or better than a human, it would fail at many others, which would show it acts from the arrangement of its organs rather than universal reason; he then extends this to animals via the magpie/parrot.

The parallel that's nagging me is that this is basically an early distribution-shift argument. Test one is compositional, context-sensitive language; test two is generalization across many tasks rather than a narrow benchmark. The modern stochastic-parrot critique of LLMs says they pass one and not the other, so there doing organ-disposition not mind. But Descartes' inference from would-fail-the-tests to lacks-mind seems to need a hidden premise that having mind guarantees passing both, which is exactly the premise the modern argument needs too. Is this parallel deep, in the sense that the same inference from failed generalization to absent mind does the work in both cases, or superficial because Descartes is making a metaphysics-of-causation claim about soul versus matter while the modern argument is about statistical generalization? What would have to be true for the mapping to transfer, and what does the parallel predict that turns out false?
