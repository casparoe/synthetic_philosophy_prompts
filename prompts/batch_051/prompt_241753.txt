I have no training in economics, but I have been thinking on my own for a long time about a question that will not leave me alone, and I would like to know whether there is a single principle behind the judgments I have formed. The question is this: when new money is created — printed by a government, typed into an account by a bank, or spent into circulation — under what conditions does it create real wealth, and when does it merely create claims on wealth that already exists or on wealth that never gets produced?

I have judgments about a set of concrete cases, and I cannot tell whether one principle captures all of them or whether some of my judgments must be revised.

Here are the cases as concretely as I can state them.

1. A government in a deep slump, with millions of unemployed workers and factories standing idle, mails every adult a sizable check and pays for it with newly printed money. People spend the money at local businesses; the businesses hire workers and increase production; goods and services are produced that would not otherwise have existed. My judgment: real wealth was created.

2. The same government mails the same checks, but this time the economy is running near full capacity, with almost no unemployed workers and no idle machinery. My judgment: no real wealth is created. The volume of real goods and services is unchanged; the new money mostly bids prices upward.

3. A central bank creates new money and uses it to buy government bonds that private banks are holding. The banks end up holding newly created central-bank money instead of bonds. My judgment: by itself this creates no new wealth. One paper asset has been swapped for another. What happens next depends on whether the banks lend and whether that lending finances production that would not otherwise occur.

4. A bank makes a loan to a builder to put up an apartment building on an empty lot. The money for the loan is not drawn from anyone's savings; it is newly created when the loan is made. The building is constructed and occupied. My judgment: wealth was created. The new deposit is a claim, but the building is a real addition to the world, and the loan brought it into being.

5. A bank makes a loan of the same size, again with newly created money, but this time to a purchaser of an existing house in a rapidly rising market. No building takes place; the main effect is that house prices in the area rise and existing homeowners feel richer. My judgment: no real wealth was created. The same houses now carry larger price tags, and the new deposit is a claim on that unchanged housing stock.

6. A skilled counterfeiter prints currency indistinguishable from the government's and spends it over the course of a year. My judgment: no wealth is created. Whatever the counterfeiter can buy is transferred from everyone else through a broad rise in prices; it is a disguised tax collected by a private party.

7. A government prints money and uses it to pay off bonds that have reached maturity and are held by the public. The bondholders receive cash in place of the promise of future cash. My judgment: no new wealth is created. One claim is exchanged for another; the economy's real productive capacity and the government's future taxing power are unchanged.

8. A government prints money to pay for an extra year of schooling and job training for a large number of adults, and their measured productivity clearly rises afterward. My judgment: real wealth is eventually created, because the economy can now produce more than it otherwise could have.

I can see the rough shape of a principle behind these judgments. Something like: newly created money adds to real wealth only to the extent that it draws idle resources into production or creates new productive capacity, and it adds nothing when it only changes the form, ownership, or size of claims on an unchanged real economy. But I worry that the principle does not survive its own logic.

Case 6 looks mechanically similar to case 1: in both, money appears from nowhere and is spent on real goods and services, and in a slump the counterfeiter's spending could give work to idle hands just as the government's checks could. If I follow the principle honestly, must I accept that counterfeiting during a slump creates real wealth, even though that judgment feels wrong to me? And in case 5, the seller of the house walks away with a new deposit that can purchase real goods; have I too quickly denied that any wealth was created? I also worry that I have been using the word wealth loosely — sometimes for a stock of assets, sometimes for the flow of goods and services people actually get — and I would like the account to say which sense is doing the work in each case, or how the two fit together. I would rather be told which of my judgments give way than have a principle bent to save them all.

I would like your answer to do the following. First, state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my judgments, in as precise language as you can manage without retreating to jargon. Second, run each candidate through all eight cases, showing where it agrees with me, where it contradicts me, and where it forces me to choose between the principle and the judgment. Third, tell me plainly which of my judgments I would have to surrender if I accept the simplest honest version of the principle, and what extra clause or distinction would be required to keep each vulnerable judgment. Fourth, apply the account to at least two cases I did not list — for example, a bank loan that finances a factory that is later abandoned, and a government that prints money to buy shares of companies from private owners — and say what your account predicts and why. Please write your answer in American English.
