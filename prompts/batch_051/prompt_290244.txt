I am a retired chemist who came to philosophy late, and I have been reading recent work on what, if anything, adult children owe their parents. A common line of thought treats grown children's obligations on the model of friendship: they depend on the present quality of a voluntary relationship rather than on what happened decades ago. I would like the strongest case you can make for the following position: the friendship model misses a residual kind of obligation, because the parent–child relationship is the developmental context in which a person first acquires the very capacities—trust, reciprocity, self-restraint—that make adult friendship and its mutual obligations possible. Please argue for that position without appealing to debt, gratitude, or repayment, and address the objection that an adult child who has been treated badly owes nothing merely because of the bare fact of having been raised.
