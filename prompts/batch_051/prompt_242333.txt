I find myself treating punctuality as a moral issue rather than a mere matter of etiquette or workplace convention, and I want to know whether that reaction is defensible. A friend, knowing this, summarized a book on the ethics of small promises for me, and I would like an accuracy audit of her summary.

She summarized the book's argument as follows. Because every appointment is an implicit promise, chronic lateness is not just rudeness but a moral failure. The book's core claim is that the wrong of breaking a promise, whether large or small, lies entirely in raising another person's expectations and then disappointing them. On this view, being twenty minutes late to a casual coffee is wrong in exactly the same way, and to exactly the same degree, as breaking a serious promise, because both wrongs work through the same mechanism of expectation and disappointment. The only legitimate excuse for lateness is a genuine emergency that the person waiting would accept as releasing you from the promise. The author also claims that the size of a promise is morally irrelevant, since if small promises did not bind, the whole practice of promising would collapse.

I am inclined to accept the idea that punctuality can be a moral matter and that breaking a small promise is still a wrong of some kind, but the claimed equivalence with serious promises and the emergency-only rule strike me as too strong. Which parts of my friend's summary are accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations or overstatements, what would a careful defender of the book's position say in response, and which of the conclusions I am tempted to draw survive the correction?
