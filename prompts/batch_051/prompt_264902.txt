For a graduate seminar I teach on the epistemology of expertise, I would like a decision tree that sorts positions on the question why fields with clear, fast feedback (software engineering, surgery) appear to accumulate knowledge faster than fields with slow or contested feedback (macroeconomics, much of philosophy). The tree should start from the commitment that this difference in apparent progress is real and worth explaining, and it should end in named positions. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: first the branching claims and the combinations of answers they allow, then the positions located in that space. The distinctions that matter most for my purposes are whether the faster progress is real or an artifact of how progress is measured; whether the advantage lies in the practitioners or in the ideas and practices; and whether feedback works mainly by killing false claims or by making problems modular and coordinated. The leaves must include at least these five, with room for one or two more only if the space demands them: the falsificationist/error-correction view (feedback makes false claims fail visibly and cheaply); the problem-decomposition view (feedback lets a field split into subproblems that can be settled independently); the consensus/convergence view (feedback forces shared results, making work cumulative); the selection/ecology view (the people get filtered, not the propositions); and the metric-artifact deflationist view (the apparent speed is an artifact of defining progress by a field's own feedback metrics). Each branch node should be a concrete claim or case a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and at least one node should work through a specific field contrast. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, note whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and identify any two leaves the tree cannot reliably separate. Aim for six or seven leaves and no more than three levels of branching. End with a brief note on where the tree is most fragile: which split you would be least willing to defend, and what argument or evidence would most likely lead you to redraw the taxonomy.
