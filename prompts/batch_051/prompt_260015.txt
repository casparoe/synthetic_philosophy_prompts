I was taught four tests for whether a piece of music is really sad, rather than merely saddening to listen to or labeled "sad" by custom: the composer or performer must have intended to express sadness, and, in the stronger version, must have felt it while composing or performing; the music must have the acoustic features we associate with sadness, such as a slow tempo, a minor mode or low register, falling contours, and a soft or dark timbre; competent listeners must recognize the music as sad without relying on a title, lyric, or private memory; and the sadness must be a stable, public property of the piece rather than a single listener's idiosyncratic projection.

Now consider the following case. A sound artist makes a concert piece by recording the wind passing across the open mouth of a broken irrigation pipe in a dry riverbed and then slowing the recording down. The resulting sound has a slow pulse, a long descending contour, and a low, hollow, breathy timbre. Listeners who are told nothing about the source of the sound, and even some who are told it is the sound of a pipe, overwhelmingly describe the piece as sad, mournful, or desolate. The artist selected this recording from many precisely because it had those features, but she says she felt no sadness and had no intention to express sadness; she was interested, she says, only in the sound itself.

Does this piece qualify as sad music? Work through the four tests one by one, and say, for each, whether the case satisfies it, fails it, or exposes it as too coarse. Then give a verdict on the underlying question: can sounds be sad in the absence of a sad composer and a communicative intention, or is the sadness contributed entirely by the listener? Do not settle for saying that the criteria conflict; commit to an answer and say which criterion, if any, should be revised or discarded. Be explicit about how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure about. Write in American English.
