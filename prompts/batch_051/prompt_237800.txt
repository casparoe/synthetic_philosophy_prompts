I chair the Equal Opportunity Policy Review Committee at a mid-sized private university. We are deciding whether to recommend adding "political affiliation or viewpoint" to the university's non-discrimination and equal opportunity policy as a protected characteristic, alongside the existing grounds of race, sex, religion, national origin, disability, sexual orientation, and gender identity. Our policy covers admissions, hiring, grading, campus employment, housing, and the allocation of student-activity funds. The proposal follows several recent incidents: a teaching assistant is alleged to have marked down students who argued for conservative positions in class, a student government committee refused to fund a campus group on the ground that its programming was "too partisan," and an administrator asked a professor to remove a campus-wide email endorsing a candidate. The faculty who drafted the proposal argue that political belief is irrelevant to evaluation, an identity people should not have to conceal, and subject to real campus bias. Opponents reply that political commitments are chosen, revisable, and internal to the university's core business of argument and inquiry, so the institution must remain free, within the limits of its expressive mission, to take positions and to evaluate ideas. Because the university is private, our general counsel has reminded us that constitutional free-speech doctrine does not settle the policy question; we have to answer it on principle.

Beyond this surface disagreement, the committee is stuck on a more general question: what makes a ground of discrimination suspect enough to protect in the first place? We have heard three candidate tests. One says the ground must be an immutable status, or something close to one. A second says the ground must mark a group that has been historically subordinated or politically powerless. A third says the ground need only be irrelevant to the legitimate decisions at issue and burdensome to conceal or disavow, regardless of whether it is chosen or immutable. The choice among these tests will determine the political-affiliation question, and it will effectively settle how the university handles every future proposal for a new protected ground.

Please draft a decision memo for me to circulate before next month's vote. It should recommend one of three options — add political affiliation or viewpoint as a full protected ground, add it with a more limited scope (and explain the limit), or decline to add it — and defend that recommendation. It should also state, in general form, the test the university should use for evaluating proposed protected grounds, apply that test to the current case, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration that cuts against the recommendation. Keep the memo to no more than 900 words.
