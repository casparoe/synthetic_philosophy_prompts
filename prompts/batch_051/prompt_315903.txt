In my reporting on science and ideas, I keep encountering a pattern in the way certain values are defended, and I want to know whether it has an established name—and, either way, how to carve it precisely. A research lab insists that truth-seeking is its non-negotiable norm, yet under budget pressure its members defend truthfulness almost entirely by its downstream effects: reproducibility, catching errors, public trust. When someone proposes a study whose findings would change no decision, they still treat truth-seeking as binding and fall back on the assertion that the truth matters in itself. A community justifies a yearly ritual by social cohesion and risk reduction; when researchers find the ritual no longer delivers either, the community keeps it, and the defense becomes the claim that this is simply who they are. A dietary rule is justified by contamination risk; once refrigeration and testing remove the risk, the rule survives and tightens. The common shape is this: a value is defended as a means; the means-based case is undercut; the value is reasserted as an end; then, under pressure, defenders drift back to means-talk. I know the intrinsic/instrumental distinction and the notion of sacred or protected values, but neither seems to capture the oscillation itself—the way the two framings are swapped to deflect challenges. Does this configuration have a name? If nothing established fits, I would rather have a sharp definition, boundary cases, and an account of what the phenomenon is not than a bare label.
