I am a retired geophysicist who has lately been reading philosophy of science, particularly debates about whether knowledge is worth pursuing for its own sake. I also sit on the grants board of a private foundation, the Lowenthal Trust, whose charter commits it to funding scientific research that serves the public good. We are deadlocked on an application, and I have agreed to draft the short decision memo for Thursday's meeting.

The application is from a cosmology consortium proposing to build a sparse array of low-frequency radio receivers in a radio-quiet valley, in order to map 21-centimeter emission from neutral hydrogen at high redshift and determine whether the dark-energy equation-of-state parameter w has changed over cosmic history. The external reviewers judge the science exceptional and the methods sound, but they also concede, with unusual frankness, that there is no foreseeable application: no clinical or industrial instrument, no commercializable software, no practical contribution to energy, climate, or health. The sole deliverable would be a sharper or revised account of how the universe has expanded. A competing proposal would deploy a network of inexpensive groundwater sensors to help municipalities manage aquifers through drought; its science is ordinary, but its usefulness is immediate. One faction on the board reads the charter's reference to the public good narrowly and says that in a tight budget year we should fund the sensors. Another faction replies that a foundation devoted to science, not engineering, exists precisely to underwrite work whose only immediate product is understanding, and that yesterday's useless cosmology sometimes becomes tomorrow's indispensable technology, though no one can specify how it would happen in this case. The argument has become one about the purpose of the institution, not the merits of the two teams.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and it should offer a criterion the board can actually apply to the next frontier-science proposal rather than a one-off judgment. Begin with the memo itself, with the recommendation in the first sentence and no preface or commentary, and do not restate the question. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against; a memo that caricatures the losing side will not move anyone.
