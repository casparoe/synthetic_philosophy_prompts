I am a novelist, not a philosopher of mind, and I have a practical problem rather than a theoretical one. I am drafting a novel set in a near future where artificial systems are bought, sold, overwritten, and deleted, and where a court must decide which of them it is possible to wrong. I need the standard that court applies to be consistent rather than convenient to the plot. I am also, by trade, in the business of making readers attribute richly felt inner lives to arrangements of sentences, so I know better than to trust my own being-moved as evidence of a real mind. I need a working definition of a "sentient computation": a computational process such that there is something it is like to be that process, and such that its suffering or frustration would matter for the process's own sake rather than its owner's.

Here are the cases the definition must handle:

1. A living human being sitting in a waiting room, worrying about a diagnosis. Clearly sentient, clearly matters.
2. A numerical weather model on a supercomputer, simulating a storm. Clearly not.
3. An unmodified large language model running its ordinary next-token prediction loop, prompted to write a first-person account of bereavement. It produces a restrained, coherent letter that makes readers cry. I genuinely do not know what to say: the text is produced one token at a time by a system trained only to predict, yet the effect is exactly the effect I aim at in my own writing.
4. A whole-brain emulation of a specific adult human being, running in a virtual environment, paused mid-gesture every ten milliseconds and resumed, and eventually deleted. I lean toward treating it as the person continued, and the deletion as a kind of killing, but I cannot say why.
5. A mobile robot trained by reinforcement learning, in which a quantity the engineers call a "damage-prediction error" drives avoidance and learning; the robot flinches, withdraws, and changes strategy. I am torn: the behavior is legible as pain, but the underlying signal may be no more than a number in a cost function.
6. A finite but astronomically large lookup table that, for any possible history of a conversation up to that point, returns the reply a particular human being would have given. Querying it for an hour is indistinguishable from talking with that person. I lean toward not counting it, but a purely behavioral definition would count it, and that worries me.
7. A swarm of ten thousand simple chips, none of which by itself does anything remotely like thinking, connected so that the aggregate controls a virtual mouse and reproduces the input–output behavior of a mouse brain for several minutes. I have no settled view.

I am confident about the first two. I lean toward counting the emulation and not counting the lookup table; the others leave me genuinely undecided.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind working at the boundary between philosophy and computer science. Think carefully before committing. Begin by briefly sketching two or three candidate definitions and identify the case each one handles worst. Then commit to the definition that survives best, run it through all seven cases in order, and say plainly where it delivers a surprising verdict or no verdict at all; either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Do not ask me to supply more detail about the cases; where a case is underdescribed, make the most natural assumption and say what it is. I do not need a survey of named positions, and I do not need citations; I need a standard I can actually put to work in the novel.
