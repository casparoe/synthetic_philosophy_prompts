I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist-adjacent blogs, and I tend to think about institutional design in terms of incentives, Goodhart-style distortions, and second-order gaming rather than whether the institution expresses the right principle.

A research funding body I follow is seriously considering the following scheme, which its board describes as implementing satisficing in grant review. Each applicant submits a short proposal, which is scored 0–100 against a public rubric by a single randomly assigned reviewer. There is no committee, no discussion, and no comparison between proposals. The board publishes an acceptance threshold before each funding cycle, initially 85. Every proposal scoring at or above the threshold is funded in full, in the order the scores are returned, until the cycle's budget is exhausted; any proposal below the threshold may be revised and resubmitted in the next cycle without restriction. The board may adjust the threshold exactly once a year, and the board itself is evaluated every three years on only two numbers: the proportion of funded projects that produce at least one publication in a selective venue within three years, and the total number of projects funded.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analysed, not as a question about the philosophy of grant review. I want to know how it would actually behave. What does it incentivise among applicants, among the reviewers, and among the board? Where does it get gamed first, and what do the candidate failure modes look like — resubmission of trivial variants until a generous reviewer is drawn, threshold-drift by the board to protect its two metrics, a portfolio of projects clustering just above the threshold, or something else? What happens when the budget runs out mid-cycle? Does the scheme genuinely inherit the properties of satisficing as a search procedure with an aspiration level, or does it quietly reintroduce optimisation through the threshold-setting process? Which failure modes are fixable without reintroducing explicit ranking?
