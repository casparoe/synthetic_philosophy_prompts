Consider the following proposed criteria for something to count as a formal geological epoch, that is, a unit in the official geological time scale:

(1) It must be defined at a single, physically located boundary point — a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, or "golden spike" — in a named stratigraphic section, using a primary marker plus supporting secondary markers.
(2) The boundary signal must be globally correlatable: it must be detectable in many independent archives (ice, lake and marine sediments, soils, biota) and be synchronous or nearly synchronous worldwide.
(3) The signal must be durable and distinctive, so that a geologist in the far future could recognize it in the rock record; it cannot be a transient perturbation that will blur away.
(4) The unit must be adopted through the formal governance of stratigraphy — proposal, vote in the relevant subcommission, and ratification by the International Commission on Stratigraphy and the International Union of Geological Sciences.

Now consider this case. A proposal defines the Anthropocene as a formal epoch beginning in the mid-twentieth century, with its primary boundary marker the global appearance of plutonium-239 and other artificial radionuclides from atmospheric nuclear weapons testing, preserved in ice cores, lake sediments, and soils. Secondary markers include the roughly synchronous upturn in aluminum, concrete, plastics, and other "technofossils" of the Great Acceleration. A candidate boundary section is a seasonally laminated lake deposit in eastern North America. A version of this proposal was in fact advanced by the Anthropocene Working Group and has so far not been ratified by the governing stratigraphic bodies.

Critics concede that the signal is sharp, global, and easily detected, but make three objections. First, a boundary less than a century old is stratigraphically premature: the relevant sediments are mostly unconsolidated, and how the signal will survive into deep time is a projection rather than an observation. Second, anchoring the epoch to the nuclear age privileges a particular cluster of human events and thereby smuggles a narrative about modernity, militarism, and technology into what is supposed to be a neutral reading of the rock record. Third, the Anthropocene is better treated as a diffuse, diachronous event or as a historical concept than as a formal epoch with a single start date.

Does the mid-twentieth-century proposal qualify under the four criteria? Go through the criteria one by one, and say explicitly where they underdetermine the verdict. Then address this further question: do the critics' objections show that the real problem is not applying the criteria but the criteria themselves — especially criterion (4)'s assumption that the question belongs to stratigraphic bodies? Who, if anyone, should decide when the Anthropocene begins, and should a geological decision bind historians, or are the geological and historical questions best kept apart?
