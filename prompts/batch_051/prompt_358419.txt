So I'm in a reading group on the ethics of life extension, and we've been having the same fight for six months, and I'm starting to think the blockage is structural rather than just us being slow. We mostly agree on the science — it's all decades away and uncertain — so feasibility isn't doing the work. The pro-extension side treats death as a medical problem that takes everything you would otherwise have had, full stop, and says the burden is on anyone who wants to leave that harm in place. The other side says a life without a finite horizon would lose its narrative shape and urgency, that things matter partly because you won't get to do them forever, and that wanting centuries is greedy or even a bit childish. What's odd is that whenever the meaning side gets pushed — no, they can't say whether an extra fifty healthy years would also ruin meaning, and they can't give a cutoff between a good lifespan and a ruinously long one — they retreat to something vaguer about acceptance and wisdom. The pro side has its own tic: it won't hear 'I might not want to live for centuries' as anything but internalized deathism, where deathism is our habit of rationalizing aging and death as good because we can't fix them yet — sour grapes with a philosophy degree. The pro side also keeps pointing out that even a cured-aging world would still have accidents and a finite life expectancy, so 'infinite boredom' is kind of a strawman. I have a hypothesis: both sides are doing motivated cognition — the pro side can't sit with loss, the anti side can't sit with hope — but I don't want to psychologize the whole question away. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? A factual disagreement about what very long but still finite lives would feel like, a values disagreement about which lives count as worth living, a merely verbal disagreement about the word 'meaning', or a disagreement about which intuitions count as evidence in the first place? And given that diagnosis, what would actually move the needle — a distinction, a thought experiment, an empirical question we could answer later — or is this the kind of dispute that just runs on temperment?
