I'm a PhD student working mostly on moral responsibility, and I've been trying to sort out my own verdicts about when resentment is appropriate — all the Strawson stuff about reactive attitudes, the participant stance, the objective stance. I've noticed my gut pulls in different directions depending on the case, and I'd like to know whether there's actually one principle behind it or I need to loose some of these verdicts.

Here are the cases.

1. A friend cancels lunch because he's absorbed in a new relationship and just forgot. I'm mildly annoyed. That feels appropriate.

2. Same friend cancels because he's had a crushing week and got three hours of sleep. No resentment — not even a flicker. I just feel bad for him.

3. A woman with advanced dementia says something brutal to her daughter. The daughter says she feels hurt but not resentful, like the person she's reacting too isn't really her mother anymore. I think that's right.

4. A man's personality changes after a brain tumor; he becomes cold and verbally cruel. His family say they can't find it in themselves to resent him, even though his behavior is exactly like their abusive father's was. I share that: no resentment.

But here's where it gets weird for me:

5. A person is cold and cruel, and the entire explanation is a brutal upbringing. Same deterministic-looking causal story as the tumor, but I do resent him when he mistreats people. I don't exempt him, even if I get why he's like that.

6. A diagnosed psychopath who "can't" grasp other people's feelings manipulates someone out of their savings. I think blame is still in order, but the feeling is different — I move toward something colder, more like managing a hazard than resenting a fellow person. The blame doesn't vanish, though.

7. A normally decent person whose kid is in the hospital snaps at a cashier. I don't think he should be blamed in any deep sense, and yet I'd still have a flash of "that was a shitty way to talk to her." The flash doesn't seem wrong exactly, just beside the point.

8. This one is artificial but it's what first got me stuck. Suppose a neuroscientist has secretly implanted a device in someone; whenever the person starts to control their anger, the device fires and they lash out. Before I learn about the device, I resent them. After I learn, most of the resentment drains away — but not all of it, and what's left seems to point at the neuroscientist rather than at the person. Is that second feeling resentment too, or something else?

9. Someone raised in an honor culture kills a person over an insult, sincerely believing it's required. I blame them less than I'd blame a cold-blooded killer doing the same thing for advantage, but I don't treat them like the dementia case either. Somewhere in the middle.

10. And the self-directed version: I still feel guilt for something I did at nineteen under coercive pressure from an older partner. If a stranger told me the same story, I'd excuse them completely. But my own guilt persists anyway.

The tentative principle I've been toying with is something like: you're a proper target of resentment exactly when the action expressed your own evaluative standpoint — your cares, values, character — rather than some process that bypassed it. That seems to handle 1, 2, 7, 8, and maybe 4. But it looks like it should also lead me to exempt the person in 5, since his evaluative standpoint was itself made by the abuse, and I don't. And it's unclear what it says about 6, where the person's evaluative standpoint is precisely the problem.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or most of them? If you have to knock a verdict or two out, which ones go, and why those rather than others? And what should the principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — someone whose values were deliberately rebuilt by a cult over years and who then hurts people, but who has since been deprogrammed and feels terrible. Do I resent the person for what they did while inside, resent the cult leader, or neither — and does it change if the person genuinely can't recover their old values, only perform regret?
