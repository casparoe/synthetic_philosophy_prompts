I've been making my way through Saussure's Course in General Linguistics (I'm not an academic, just trying to get clear on something). At one point he says, in the translation I have, that a language and its writing are two separate systems of signs and that the only reason for the writing system to exist is to represent the spoken language; then he immediately turns around and says writing has been given an importance that doesn't belong to it, because people come to think the written form is what matters. I can't shake the thought that this is exactly the tension people live with all the time.

Here's the asymmetry. If you point out that English spelling is awful, someone will say "spelling is just a way of writing down the sounds; it's not the language itself." So letters are supposed to be judged by how well they match speech. But the same person will treat "their" for "there" or "would of" for "would have" as real mistakes, even when the pronunciation is identical or the meaning is clear. And if you propose respelling "night" as "nite" to make it work better, the same person says no, spelling is conventional, it doesn't need to track sound. So which is it? Is there a principle that justifies using speech as the standard in one breath and denying it in the next, or do we just have two different games going on under one word "spelling"? I'd like the strongest principle you can find for treating writing as both an encoding of speech and a system with its own rules, and an honest verdict on whether that principle works or whether we should give up one of the two attitudes.
