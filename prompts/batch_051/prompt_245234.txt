Write a dialogue between two epistemologists who disagree about what to make of the dogmatism paradox, usually credited to Kripke and discussed by Harman. One of them, the dogmatist, accepts the core of the paradox's reasoning: if you know that p, and you know that any evidence against p would be misleading, then you are in a position to dismiss apparent counterevidence without lowering your confidence, so the interesting questions are about how knowledge is lost or defeated rather than about whether you should update. The other, a Bayesian fallibilist, treats the paradox as a reductio of treating flat-out knowledge, closed under known entailment, as the central guide to rational belief: he holds that a rational agent never assigns probability one or zero to empirical claims, that Cromwell's rule blocks total immunity to revision, and that surprising evidence should always move a careful thinker at least a little. Let them argue over a concrete case: a person knows some proposition through a highly reliable method and then receives competent but not infallible testimony that appears to contradict it. The dialogue should work through the closure inference, the sense in which the counterevidence is misleading, the difference between evidence that is merely misleading and evidence that undermines one's basis for knowledge, the possibility that receiving the counterevidence destroys the very knowledge that would license disregarding it, and the question of whether the dogmatist can ever change his mind without incoherence. Both characters should be equally sharp and charitable, and each should respond to the other's actual points rather than talk past him.
