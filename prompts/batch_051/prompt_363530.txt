I've always been puzzled by a weird double standard in how legal systems handle ignorance of the law. On the one hand, the maxim that ignorance of the law doesn't excuse you is applied pretty ruthlessly: people get convicted for violating rules they had no actual idea existed, even when the rule is buried in a regulation or changed last week. On the other hand, the same system acts like laws have to be knowable before they can bind anyone. Statutes don't take effect until they're published, secretly enacted laws are treated as invalid, courts strike down vague criminal statutes because ordinary people couldn't tell what they forbid, ambiguous criminal laws get read narrowly in the defendant's favor (the rule of lenity), and some serious white-collar crimes require willfulness, which courts often gloss as knowing the legal duty you're accused of violating. So the law seems to both assume you know everything and assume you can't be expected to know much. Which is it? I want you to reconstruct what view of legal obligation, fairness, or the rule of law this whole pattern presupposes. Be careful to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with. Then tell me where the pattern is underdetermined: could someone with a genuinely different theory of law endorse the same surface behavior? Finally, work through what would have to change if you replaced the presupposition with either (a) a strict rule that nobody may be punished without actual knowledge of the rule, or (b) the rule that anything published counts as known, no matter how obscure, technical, or scattered it is. Also tell me which replacement is less disruptive to the existing practice and why. Please write the answer in American English.
