I'm compiling a list of objections to the therapy/enhancement distinction for a handbook chapter, and I suspect I already have the standard moves. The list thus far: (i) the distinction inherits the contested character of 'health' and 'disease', since therapy is defined against a conception of normal functioning that is both scientifically underdetermined and normatively loaded; (ii) the boundary is historically and culturally mobile—conditions migrate across it as classifications shift—so it cannot supply a stable natural line; (iii) many interventions fall on both sides at once (immunisation enhances an immune response yet is classed as preventive medicine; corrective lenses can be prescribed for better-than-species-typical acuity), so the distinction is not exhaustive; (iv) even where a line can be drawn, moral permissibility does not track it, since some enhancements are banal and certain therapies are gravely risky; (v) the baseline against which enhancement is measured, species-typical functioning, is itself morally arbitrary, since there is no obvious reason why the typical or the average should set the norm against which departures count as enhancement. Give me one further objection with a genuinely different structure—not a rewording, special case, or combination of these. It should attack the distinction at a point the five above leave untouched. Then say what the new objection shows that the existing list does not, and whether the objection is any good.
