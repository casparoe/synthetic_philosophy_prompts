I am a student taking a course on classical Chinese philosophy, and a debate from that course has been colliding in my mind with something I learned in a course on law and society. The Confucians we read defend the view that care and obligation should be graded: one owes most to parents and children, less to more distant relatives, less again to acquaintances, and least to strangers, and they treat this hierarchy not as selfish favoritism but as the soil in which moral development grows. Mozi attacks just this ordering. He argues that such partiality is what produces conflict, and that we should instead practice impartial care toward everyone, treating a stranger's welfare as a direct and serious object of moral concern rather than something one reaches only after kin have been served. I want to understand a concrete institution in light of that dispute.

The institution is family-based immigration policy. In the country where I am studying, the immigration system gives an enormous priority to family reunification: a citizen or permanent resident can sponsor a spouse, child, or parent, and in some categories an adult sibling, while a foreigner with scarce skills or a pressing humanitarian claim may wait for years or have no comparable path. The sponsoring relative must also sign a legal undertaking promising to support the sponsored family member financially so that the newcomer does not become dependent on public funds.

My question is what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. On its face the visa preference looks like the state institutionalizing something close to Confucian graded love, since it treats a relation to kin as a powerful reason that strangers cannot claim. But I am not sure the practice strictly presupposes that theory, because an impartialist such as Mozi might defend family preferences instrumentally, as an efficient way to transfer care obligations to private networks. I would like to know what implicit view of the family, moral partiality, and the force of special relationships the institution is committed to, whether its two parts—the kin preference and the sponsor's financial undertaking—presuppose the same view or point in different directions, and what would have to change in immigration law if the underlying view were replaced by Mozi's impartial care rather than merely tempered by it.
