I recently had a long argument with a friend about Brandom’s inferentialism—roughly, the view that the meaning of a sentence is its inferential role: what one commits oneself to and what one entitles oneself to by asserting it, together with what it rules out. My friend defended a strong version of this view as the proper alternative to representationalist or truth-conditional semantics. We ended the evening with a stack of examples and no agreed principle. Since then I have written down my honest, pre-theoretic verdicts. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures them, which of my verdicts I should revise or reject, and what the resulting principle says about two further cases I still cannot settle.

Here are my cases.

1. A speaker asserts that some liquid is an acid but refuses to commit to the claim that it will turn litmus paper red under normal conditions. My verdict: this is a factual or empirical deficiency, not a semantic one; the person can fully understand the word “acid” and simply be wrong about a test.

2. A speaker asserts that Bob is a bachelor but refuses to commit to the claim that Bob is unmarried. My verdict: this is a semantic failure; the person has not mastered the word “bachelor.”

3. A speaker asserts that the same surface is red all over and green all over. My verdict: this is a semantic failure, because these claims stand in a relation of material incompatibility that anyone competent with ordinary color terms should recognize.

4. A speaker asserts that there is water in the glass but refuses to commit to the claim that there is H₂O in the glass. My verdict: this is not a semantic failure; it is the same kind of empirical ignorance as the acid case.

5. A speaker asserts that they know the door is locked but refuses to commit to the claim that the door is locked. My verdict: this is a semantic failure involving “know,” not merely a pragmatic oddity.

6. A speaker asserts that a person is a mother but refuses to commit to the claim that the person has at least one child. My verdict: if the speaker is using “mother” in its ordinary sense, this is a semantic failure; if they are introducing a stipulated social or legal category, it is not. I am unsure whether my acid and water verdicts should get the same qualification.

The further cases I keep going back and forth on:

A. A speaker asserts that a ring is made of gold but refuses to commit to the claim that it is made of the element with atomic number 79. Is this closer to the water case or to the bachelor case?

B. A speaker says “I promise to come” and then denies any commitment to coming. Is that a semantic failure with the word “promise,” a moral failure, or both?

Is there a single principle behind my verdicts, and if not, which verdicts should I give up?
