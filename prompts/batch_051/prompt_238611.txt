I am building a seminar around Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" as a way of pressing on philosophical theories of intellectual property, and I want to make sure the story is doing real argumentative work rather than serving as decoration. Read the story through the standard accounts of why creators should own their works — the labor-desert view, the personality/personhood view, and the incentive view — and use them to adjudicate the narrator's central claim that Menard's word-for-word identical pages are nevertheless a distinct and greater work. On each theory, does Menard produce something he can be said to own, and does any verbal overlap with Cervantes threaten that ownership? Then turn the story back on the theories: wherever they have trouble saying what makes Menard's "new" text a different object of ownership, locate the exact pressure the story is putting on the idea that what one owns is a sequence of words rather than an act of meaning in a historical situation. Conclude by saying what the fiction adds to the philosophical debate that the theories alone leave out.
