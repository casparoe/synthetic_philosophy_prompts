I am an undergraduate in a seminar on global justice, and I sit on my university's Trademark Licensing and Labor Committee. The committee meets monthly to review garment factories in our licensed-apparel supply chain — usually ten to twenty per meeting — and decides whether to continue a license, continue it only with specified conditions and a timeline for remediation, or terminate it. I am one of three student members; the other members are administrators and one faculty member. None of us is a labor lawyer or an economist, each factory gets about thirty minutes of deliberation, and the committee's vote becomes a recommendation to the university president, who has so far never rejected one. Whatever procedure you give me will therefore have consequences for the committee's decisions and for workers who depend on these jobs.

The seminar has been working through the dispute about whether an exchange can be exploitative even when both sides benefit relative to their next-best alternative. One camp argues that a transaction can still be wrongfully exploitative when the workers are better off taking the offer than refusing it, because what matters is the fairness of the terms, the share of value the workers receive, or the absence of a meaningful option to refuse. Another camp argues that if workers are made better off than under any realistic alternative and choose the work, the charge of exploitation is much harder to sustain, and that severing the relationship can harm precisely the people a critic wants to protect. I want a procedure that the committee can use without first having to settle this dispute.

Please write a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off recommendation for a single factory. It should be a sequence of questions the committee asks in order, with the answer to each question determining the next step, where the procedure stops, and which action follows: continue, condition, terminate, or escalate. It must be runnable in thirty minutes per factory by non-specialists, it must keep factual findings separate from value judgments, and it must mark each step that is a judgment call rather than a question of fact. It should explicitly address the baseline problem: whether we compare a factory to what workers would otherwise have, to an independent standard such as a living-wage benchmark, or to both, and what the procedure should do when those two comparisons pull in opposite directions. Write the procedure as if the committee will vote to adopt it as standing policy.

Then run the procedure on these three factories.

- Factory A pays above both the legal minimum and the local agricultural wage but below our living-wage benchmark; hours are long but overtime is chosen by the workers; the workforce is mostly young women who intend to leave within two years; the university has very little leverage over the vendor.
- Factory B employs migrant workers who paid large broker fees and signed contracts in a language they do not read; conditions formally meet the university's code of conduct, but the committee cannot determine the workers' realistic alternatives and has real doubts about whether their consent was informed. Terminating the relationship may leave them with debts and no evident way home.
- Factory C pays good wages and maintains safe conditions but has no independent worker representation and a pattern of dismissing organizers; output per worker is high and the wage share is low.

At least one of these worked applications should arrive at a recommendation the committee will not like, and you should say plainly why the procedure produces it and what the committee should do with that discomfort.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will misclassify, the ways a factory could pass through it while remaining morally troubling, and the exact point at which the committee should stop pretending the disagreement is about facts and instead take a vote on values.

Structure your response in these five sections, in this order, with the named headings: Purpose and scope (about 150 words); The procedure, step by step (about half of the response); Worked applications (about a third); Limits and irreducible judgment (roughly 250 words); What I should tell the committee (about 150 words).
