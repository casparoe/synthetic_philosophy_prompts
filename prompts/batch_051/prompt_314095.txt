I am an undergraduate serving on my university's student conduct board, and I am currently taking a philosophy course on the epistemology of digital media. In that course we have discussed the idea that audio and video recordings function as an epistemic backstop for testimony: much of what we accept on another person's say-so is in principle checkable against such recordings, and we tend to treat a recording as closer to perception than to hearsay. We have also discussed how readily fabricated synthetic media erodes that backstop.

Our board now has a case in which this stops being an abstraction. A student, K., is charged with making a contemptuous and demeaning remark about the complainant's ethnic background during an off-campus party in September. The only evidence is an eleven-second video posted anonymously to a campus forum. In the clip, K. appears to say exactly the words alleged. K. does not deny being at the party, does not deny being one of the people shown, and does not deny having been near the complainant; K. denies only having uttered the words. K.'s defense is that the clip, or its audio track, has been fabricated or altered. The points K. makes are not easy to dismiss: the incriminating speech lasts about four seconds; the original file has never been produced; the posting account cannot be traced to any witness; our IT services office says that, from the posted copy alone, it can neither authenticate the clip nor rule out manipulation; and no other partygoer has agreed to go on the record about what was said.

The code of conduct instructs the board to find responsibility on a preponderance of the evidence, but it says nothing about what to do when the authenticity of the decisive evidence is disputed. I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft it. The opinion should recommend a concrete decision about whether K. should be found responsible on the record now before us; it should set out a rule or standard the board should apply to contested video or audio evidence in future cases; and it should present in its strongest form, and then answer, the argument for the opposite decision, including the worry that a deepfake defense, if accepted too readily, may allow any student to escape responsibility simply by pointing at any recording and calling it fabricated.
