I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, and after thirty years of isotope ratios and plume models Ive started reading philosophy properly — action theory mostly, with a side of military ethics. Last year I got drafted onto the technical advisory panel of a small institute that reviews proposed autonomous weapons systems, most of them defence-funded. The panel is me, a retired Air Force wing commander, two roboticists, and a human-rights lawyer; none of us are philosophers. Every quarter we get one or two proposals and have to write a formal assessment saying whether the system retains 'meaningful human control.' At the moment this phrase does no work for us: we argue, someone says 'a human can always hit the stop button,' someone else says 'that's not control, that's regret,' and we end up voting on vibes. I'd like to fix that.

My working hypothesis, from my reading, is that what matters isn't whether some human is nominally in or on the loop. It's something like: for any individual action the system takes — a target it selects, a moment it commits to engagement — a human's earlier specification of the task has to be a genuine part of the explanation of why that action happened, not just a boundary condition. If the target selection is explained mostly by the optimizer's objective function, the human is not controlling it, no matter how many buttons are on the console. But I dont know how to turn that into something an engineer can check on a Tuesday afternoon.

Please write me the procedure. I want a checklist or decision procedure the panel can actually run on a proposal in about three hours: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is a values question rather than a technical one, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three worked examples so I can see it in action: (i) a loitering munition that autonomously identifies and engages vehicles inside a geographic box a human operator designated earlier that morning; (ii) a ship-defense system that has to respond to incoming anti-ship missiles in under two seconds and can only follow pre-authorized rules of engagement; (iii) a system where a human has a veto but the vendor's own test data show the operator almost never manages to use it in time. Make at least one of these come out as a no the panel will not like, and say honestly what the procedure can't settle. Please don't tell me to go read the ethics literature — I need the working tool, not a reading list.
