A colleague in my philosophy of history seminar has led me into the following argument, and I cannot find the way out. I would rather believe that the multi-cause explanations historians give of major events are often genuine than give up the counterfactual test for a cause, but the argument seems to force me to choose. Tell me which step to reject; if you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise it is least costly to give up. Either way, say why the rejected step looked right and what accepting the diagnosis commits me to, including whether I can keep a modified version of the counterfactual criterion. Here is the argument. (1) A historical event is explained by a factor only if the event would not have occurred without that factor. (2) An event is overdetermined when two or more actual factors are present such that each, together with the existing background conditions, would have been sufficient for the event even if the others had been absent. (3) In an overdetermined event, no individual factor satisfies the condition in (1): the event would still have occurred without any single one of them. (4) Therefore no individual factor explains an overdetermined event. (5) Nor can the historian repair this by citing several causes at once, because a conjunction of items none of which explains an event does not itself explain that event. (6) Yet the events historians most want to explain, such as the outbreak of a war or the collapse of a state, are overdetermined in the sense of (2), and historians routinely explain them by citing several causes together. (7) Therefore those multi-cause explanations are no better than lists of individually inert factors, and historians are systematically mistaken about their own discipline's central product. I find (7) absurd. Please write your diagnosis as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
