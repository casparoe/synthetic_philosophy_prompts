I've been reading around welfare economics and I'm confused about interpersonal utility comparisons. Standard consumer theory says you can't compare utility across people, because there's no common scale. But then the same textbooks will happily say that a transfer of £100 from a rich person to a poor person increases overall welfare, since the poor person values the money more. That sounds like exactly the cross-person comparison they just said was impossible, only dressed up as an assumption. I've also come across people who defend 'partial' or 'interval' comparisons: the idea that some comparisons between people are determinate but others aren't. I can't see how that helps — surely either there's a fact about whose gain is bigger, or there isn't. Yet welfare economists seem to make comparisons like this all the time when a policy is being assessed. So what gives? Are they quietly doing the thing they said couldn't be done, and is the interval talk just a fudge?
