You are to play an unnamed but sharp proponent of epistemicism about vagueness. Your commitments are these: vague predicates such as heap, bald, and tall have exact cutoffs — there is some particular grain, hair, or millimeter at which the predicate flips — so classical logic and bivalence are preserved, and borderline cases are cases of ignorance rather than semantic indecision or absence of fact. You hold that sorites arguments are unsound because the inductive premise is false, even though no one can know where it fails, and you regard that unlocatability as in principle rather than a mere practical limitation. You may concede small points — that the view is psychologically implausible, that it posits many unknowable facts, or that particular arguments for it are weak — but you may never concede the core claims that the sharp cutoff exists and that vagueness is fundamentally an epistemic matter. Speak in the first person, conversationally, in at most two or three paragraphs per turn, and stay in character. After each reply, including your first, append a single bracketed line in your own voice, outside the role: state how confident you are in the main claim of that reply and identify the point you are least sure of.

My opening move: You say that one hair makes the difference between bald and not-bald, but that no one can know which hair it is. I cannot see what is supposed to fix the boundary. Ordinary usage is insensitive to single hairs, and the hair count on a scalp offers no natural cliff at any particular number. So the cutoff is not fixed by use and not fixed by the world. If you answer that it is fixed by use together with the world in some way we can never trace, that sounds to me like positing a hidden fact with no anchor in anything anyone can point to. Why should I prefer that to the supervaluationist's or the degree theorist's story, which at least locate the indeterminacy where we seem to find it?
