I am a formal-verification engineer, and my working vocabulary is model-theoretic: Kripke structures, states, execution traces, path quantifiers, and the satisfaction relation. I reason in linear temporal logic when a formula is to be evaluated along a single trace, and in branching-time logics such as CTL when I need to distinguish what holds on every trace from what holds on some trace.

I would like you to restate the philosophical debate about future contingents entirely in that vocabulary, and then tell me what does not survive the translation. The debate runs roughly as follows. An ancient argument begins from the claim that, if the sentence there will be a sea battle tomorrow is already true or already false, then the outcome is now unavoidable, which seems incompatible with its being genuinely contingent. Two standard responses have developed: the Peircean, which treats a future-tensed statement as true only if the event occurs in every branch of the future and accordingly leaves contingent future statements neither true nor false until the relevant branch is realized, and the Ockhamist, which posits a distinguished actual history through the tree relative to which ordinary future-tensed claims can already be true even while other histories diverge.

Give me a systematic translation of each of the following into linear- or branching-time semantics: the bare statement that there will be a sea battle; the fatalist step from its truth to now-necessity; the Peircean suspension of truth values for contingent futures; the Ockhamist thin red line; and the claim that the future is open. Be precise about what is a path formula and what is a state formula, and flag any point at which a gappy or three-valued assignment has to be squeezed into the two-valued semantics of a Kripke structure.

Then provide the residue report. Which elements translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in the LTL or CTL setting at all? In particular, say whether the Peircean–Ockhamist dispute reduces to a choice between evaluating path formulas over an anonymous tree or over a tree with a designated trace, and whether that choice is a modelling convenience or the philosophical dispute itself. Is any step of the fatalist argument genuinely clarified, rather than merely restyled, once expressed as model checking over a fixed trace? Where the translation strains, say where the defect lies: in the verification vocabulary, or in the philosophical positions.
