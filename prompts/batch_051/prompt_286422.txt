Machiavelli is resurrected and given a job as a columnist for a business magazine. His first asignment is to explain the rise and fall of a founder — say, the CEO of a crypto exchange who built a huge fortune by being agressive, opaque, and quick, kept winning while the market went up, and then lost almost everything in a few weeks when the market turned and regulators closed in. The editor told him to use his own categories of fortuna and virtù the way he actually developed them — not the pop-culture Machiavellianism about the ends justifying the means. Write the column he would produce. In particular, where would he credit virtù and where would he blame fortuna in this story? Would he treat the collapse as the kind of flood you could have prepared for with dikes and channels, or as one of those moments where fortune leaves nothing a person can do? And deal with the tension in his own view: he seems to say both that a prince succeeds by adjusting how he acts to the times, and that bold, impetuous action usually beats caution. Which of the two applies to the founder, and does Machiavelli resolve the mismatch or just walk right into it?
