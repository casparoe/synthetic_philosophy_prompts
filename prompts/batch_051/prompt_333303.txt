For the past two years I have been half-embedded in a complex-systems institute and half-embedded in my university's history department, and next month I present to the department's research seminar. My talk defends a quantified version of the "secular cycles" program associated with Peter Turchin: the claim that many large agrarian states, over the last two or three millennia, undergo recurring multi-century swings between political integration and fragmentation, driven by an interaction among population growth, elite reproduction and competition for scarce offices, state fiscal strain, and popular immiseration. My thesis is stronger than the narrative version. I argue that these cycles are a real dynamical phenomenon that can be captured with a small system of difference equations fitted to historical proxies, and that the resulting model should predict coarse turning points — on the order of which half-century a polity comes apart — rather than specific wars, dynasties, or dates.

The audience is mostly social, economic, and environmental historians whose standing worry is that quantitative macro-history smooths over exactly the local mechanisms and documentary texture they study. There will also be one cliometrician who is comfortable with time series but suspicious of "universal cycles," and one historical sociologist sympathetic to comparative work but allergic to anything that smells of determinism. I know in broad terms what they will say: my proxies are crude and anachronistic; my cases are selected on the dependent variable; the same coarse outcomes are compatible with many different causal stories; the model is overfit; and real history is path-dependent in ways a four-variable system cannot respect. What I need is not a generic list of complaints but a Q&A prep sheet for this room: the eight or so objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased as these particular people would phrase them, each with a short reply — about two minutes — that says what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is soft. Include the one question I am praying nobody asks, and tell me why it is so dangerous. Leave out citations and reading suggestions; I will add the footnotes myself. Start with the prep sheet itself, with no preamble and no restating of my situation, and open with the objection that could do the most damage.
