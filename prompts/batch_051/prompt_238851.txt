I keep running into this move in arguments about evolution and about optimizers more generally, where somebody says natural selection has a rationale for a trait without any agent actually having that rationale in mind — the "free-floating rationales" idea. I've been turning over a cleaner version of it and I can't tell if its airtight or if there's a bait-and-switch hiding somewhere. Here's the argument:

(1) When natural selection explains a trait like the heart, it cites the effect that made the trait spread (pumping blood), not just any old side effect of the trait.

(2) Citing that effect as what made the trait spread is explaining why the trait is there by citing a respect in which it outperformed the alternatives actually present in the population.

(3) A respect in which one candidate outperforms the alternatives is a reason for it, at least in the minimal sense that it can answer "why this one rather than the alternatives?"

(4) Natural selection can do all this without any organism, or anyone else, representing the outperformance.

(5) So there are reasons for traits that no one actually has or represents — reasons without reasoners.

I think the weak point has to be (3), but every time I try to reject it I end up saying "reasons" just means "an agent's represented considerations", which makes (5) false by stipulation instead of showing where the argument actually goes wrong. So: is the argument valid? If it is, what does it actually buy — does it show anything about normative reasons (what should be the case), or only about explanatory ones (what in fact explains why the trait is there)? If it's invalid, where exactly does it fail? And if (3) is the problem, give the strongest reading of "a reason" on which (3) is true, and check whether (1), (2), and (4) all still go through on that same reading.
