Something's been bugging me about asylum systems and I want to know what philosophical picture they're actually committed to. To be recognized as a refugee you basically have to show you'd be singled out on account of who you are — race, religion, nationality, political opinion, membership of a particular social group — and not just that your country is falling apart or that a gang runs your neighborhood. Yet once you clear that, your claim can still fail if an official decides you could of just moved to a different part of the same country, or if you passed through another country that has a safe-third-country deal with the host state, so who must take you in ends up getting settled by itinerary rather than by anything about the danger itself. So: what view of the state, of persecution, and of who owes shelter to whom does this practice presuppose? Does the first test quietly assume that generalized suffering is no state's problem unless there's a discriminating persecutor? And do the internal-relocation and safe-third-country rules rest on the same picture as the definition, or on a different one where geography and arrival order do the moral work? I'm after the implicit commitments, plus the spots where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and several theories would all license it.
