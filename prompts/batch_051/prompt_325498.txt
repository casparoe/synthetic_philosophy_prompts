Act as an expert in political philosophy and in the design of classroom simulations, and think carefully before you settle on a design. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy with about twenty students, and I need a single ninety-minute session that makes them feel the force of an idea rather than merely receive it as a lecture. The topic has recently become personally urgent for me: a close relative has become involved in a seasteading project and now talks as though the state is an optional service provider one can simply decline. I want a way to put both the appeal of that thought and its structural difficulty in front of my students without stacking the deck.

Substantively, the session sits inside the debate about private cities, charter cities, and seasteading. Conceptually, I want students to run up against the following dilemma themselves: a serious attempt to opt out of the state must either silently rely on a host state's legal order and coercive enforcement, or rebuild a state-like authority under another name. By the end they should be able to articulate that tension in their own words, not as a definition I handed them. The activity should not announce this. The rules, roles, and choices should lead participants into it.

Please design a runnable ninety-minute role-play or structured deliberation for four groups of five. At least one group should be founding a seastead that its members claim lies outside any state's territory; at least one should be negotiating a charter city with a host government; the remaining groups may be private residential cities, special economic zones, or similar arrangements. Include exact rules, printable role cards or briefing sheets, a timetable, and a token or payoff scheme that gives the choices real stakes without using money. Specify what the instructor says at each stage and what each group must actually settle: how contracts are enforced, how disputes are adjudicated, how shared infrastructure and public goods are funded, and how outsiders are excluded or handled.

The session should end with a debrief of at least fifteen minutes. In it, map particular moments in the activity onto the underlying dilemma—for example, the moment a group without any monopoly on force discovers that enforcement must be bought, borrowed, or improvised. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—free-riding, groups collapsing into farce or petty tyranny, confusion about the tokens, one group refusing to play along—and how I should handle each. Be candid about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply afterwards.

Begin by brainstorming at least three candidate structures, say briefly why you reject the ones you do, and then present the one you refine in full. Please write the entire answer in British English.
