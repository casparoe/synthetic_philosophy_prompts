A friend and I spent an evening arguing about algorithmic fairness after he read a popular piece on risk-scoring systems. When we finished, he sent me his summary of the article:

"The fairness literature has shown that fair risk prediction is mathematically impossible. There is a theorem that no scoring algorithm can simultaneously be well calibrated—within every group, the outcome rate among people assigned a given risk score equals that score—and satisfy two balance conditions, equal false positive and equal false negative rates across groups. The only exceptions are trivial: groups with effectively identical base rates, or a perfect predictor. So every real-world score used in lending, hiring, or recidivism must be unfair in at least one of these ways, and anyone who says otherwise is doing public relations."

I suspect this overstates something, but I don't know the literature well enough to say exactly where. Which parts would a careful treatment of the impossibility results accept, which parts conflate or overstate, and does the final conclusion actually follow from the theorems as stated?
