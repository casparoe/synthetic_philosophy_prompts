I’m taking a class on the history of economics and we read Hume’s essay Of Money, and I keep arguing with a friend about what he actually committed himself to. At one point Hume says, roughly, that money is not itself one of the subjects of commerce, just the instrument people have agreed on to make exchange easier; it is not the wheels of trade but the oil that makes the wheels move more smoothly. A bit later he says doubling all the money in a country would simply double prices and leave the real quantity of commodities unchanged, so the amount of money doesn’t matter in the long run. But then in the same essay he also seems to say that an increase in money, before prices fully adjust, can rouse industry: people holding the new money spend more, and a wise state should keep money gradually increasing to keep that spirit of industry alive. These look like two different answers to the question of what adding money creates—nothing real on one page, genuine activity on the next. Are they actually in conflict? If they can be reconciled, what exactly is the mechanism of the interval between new money and rising prices, and does that mechanism force Hume to say a sustained policy of gradual monetary expansion creates real wealth? Or is the long-run neutrality doing all the work and the short-run story just an observation he doesn’t fully integrate? I want a reading that doesn’t dissolve the tension by insisting one passage is a mere rule of thumb unless there’s textual pressure for that.
