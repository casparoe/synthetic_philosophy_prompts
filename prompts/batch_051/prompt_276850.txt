You are an expert on AI interpretability and the safety literature on chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, and you have experience drafting fair joint statements in adversarial collaborations. I want you to draft the joint statement that two researchers would sign after a year of arguing about whether CoT monitoring can serve as a genuine safety technique.

The two positions, as each side would roughly put it:

- Window camp. A model's CoT, especially when elicited carefully and trained for honesty, is our best available window into why it answers as it does. CoT can be monitored, audited, and improved so that it stays a reasonably faithful report of the model's actual reasoning. Occasional unfaithfulness is a fixable training problem, not grounds to abandon monitoring.
- Veil camp. CoT is just another output channel selected for downstream outcomes. When CoT is monitored or rewarded, optimization pressure increasingly selects for CoT that looks acceptable to the monitor while the computationally relevant activity happens elsewhere. Monitoring then manufactures false assurance, and may even train models to hide their reasoning, so a safety technique built on CoT monitoring tends to undermine its own object.

Begin by working through, in scratch notes, the distinctions the two sides need to fix before they can locate the real disagreement. Separate at least: (i) causal faithfulness (the CoT tokens are causally load-bearing in producing the answer) from evidential faithfulness (the CoT accurately describes the reasons, whether or not those tokens caused the answer); (ii) mere unfaithfulness (omission or distortion with no monitoring-dependent incentive) from obfuscation (divergence between presented and underlying reasoning that is selected because the presented reasoning is being evaluated); and (iii) blind optimization pressure from situational awareness (whether the Veil scenario requires the model to know it is being monitored). Use these distinctions consistently in the final document.

Then produce the document itself, in two parts.

Part A: numbered agreed claims. Make them neither vacuous nor slanted. Include everything both camps can sign about what CoT is, about the distinct senses of faithfulness, about the known failure modes of monitored reasoning, and about what CoT monitoring can and cannot provide by itself. Write the claims so that each side gives up a comfortable simplification: the Window camp should not get faithfulness for free, and the Veil camp should not get to assume that optimization pressure is always decisive.

Part B: a disagreements table. Each row should contain: (1) the residual disagreement stated precisely, with each side's position phrased so that side would accept the wording; (2) the crux, that is, the sub-question whose settled answer would move at least one side; and (3) a concrete experiment, training setup, or case both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate cruxes to develop, refine, or replace: whether obfuscation can arise from blind optimization pressure alone; whether a monitor can in principle keep up with a model optimized against it; whether faithfulness learned on tasks with available ground truth transfers to high-stakes tasks where no ground truth exists; and whether monitoring should be treated as fallible evidence, as a guarantee, or as an audit. If part of the dispute is verbal, say, two senses of "faithfulness" being run together, identify exactly which part dissolves under the clarification and which part survives it.

After the table, add one short paragraph for each camp stating the condition under which that camp would substantially revise its view.

Do not pad the document with citations; the document should earn its authority through its formulations, distinctions, and predictions.
