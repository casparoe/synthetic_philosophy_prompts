A colleague of mine works on what some call the tightness or looseness of cultures: how much rule-following a society or community is entitled to demand of its members, and when enforced conformity is justified rather than mere coercion. She keeps a running file of her verdicts on proposals to introduce or retain enforceable rules, reconstructed here from my notes:

- A coastal town, repeatedly struck by hurricanes, proposed mandatory annual evacuation drills with fines for households that failed to participate. She approved enforcement.
- A city facing a measles outbreak proposed conditioning school enrollment on proof of vaccination. She approved enforcement.
- A mining region proposed requiring all underground workers to wear helmets and check-in tags, with penalties for noncompliance. She approved enforcement.
- A city with a row of late-night bars proposed a 2 a.m. closing rule, backed by fines, after a rise in assaults and noise complaints. She approved enforcement.
- A university proposed a demerit system for students who failed to address faculty by title and stand when a professor entered the room. She opposed enforcement.
- A homeowners' association proposed fining residents whose fences departed from the approved style and who displayed yard signs. She opposed enforcement.
- A religious community proposed expelling members who shared meals with outsiders during a harvest festival. She opposed enforcement.

Next week she must evaluate a new proposal. A dense commercial district badly damaged by a flood wants to require every shop to fly a standardized green banner throughout a six-month rebuilding period and to close for one hour each Wednesday morning for a street-wide cleanup assembly, with fines for noncompliance. The district's stated rationale is not direct safety: the visible, uniform participation would reassure outside insurers and aid donors that the district is committed to rebuilding, and would deter shopkeepers from free-riding on a recovery effort carried out by others.

Predict her verdict on the new proposal. Then identify the general rule or rules about when enforced rule-following is justified that best fit her earlier verdicts, state what each candidate rule predicts about the banner-and-assembly proposal, and give your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with reasons. If her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so: identify the closest analog among her earlier cases, and specify a further case—real or invented—that would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules.
