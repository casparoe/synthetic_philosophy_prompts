I'm a math PhD student and I've been TAing our first proof-based course for a couple of years now. The way we grade proofs has started to look philosophically weird to me, and I can't sort out weather the practice is coherent. We don't just check that every step is valid. If a student hands in a fully valid derivation but can't tell you what the key idea is, or why that particular epsilon was chosen, or what would break if a hypothesis were removed, they get marked down hard. The department's line is that "the steps check out" doesn't yet mean you understand the proof. But in research math the norm seems almost inverted: a proof is accepted once the steps are correct and the gaps are small enough for a referee to fill, and nobody claims the theorem isn't proved just because the author gives no illuminating explanation. Technically correct but unilluminating papers get grumbled about, but their still proofs; a referee can basically say they don't see why it works but the calculations verify it, and that's an acceptable report.

I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit view of mathematical understanding that the grading norm presupposes — not a list of views it's merely compatible with, but what it actually commits us to. Is that view coherent, and is it consistent with how the research world treats the proved/understood distinction, or are we running two rival epistemologies in parallel? And suppose someone convinced me that understanding a proof is nothing over and above being able to check and expand each step, the way a machine-verification system does. What would have to change in how we teach and grade proof-based courses under that flat, procedural view, and what would it fail to capture about actual mathematical practice if it's wrong?
