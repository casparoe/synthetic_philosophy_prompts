I have been defending a broadly Bayesian picture of rationality in which the same norms govern all of one's beliefs: a rational agent has degrees of belief over every proposition she can entertain, updates them by conditionalization on evidence, and uses them to guide action. On this picture, my own future actions are just events in the world among others, so I should be able to assign probabilities to them — that I will report the misconduct, keep the resolution, leave the job. The view has real virtues: no ad hoc carve-outs, one unified account of belief and decision, and a single procedure that applies as cleanly to myself as to the weather.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake, captured by the idea that deliberation crowds out prediction. When I am actually choosing — say, whether to report a colleague's misconduct, knowing that doing so will cost me — there is something wrong with my saying "I am about 65 percent sure I will report." If that credence is meant literally, it seems to turn me into a spectator of my own will; if it is high enough, it seems to constitute having already decided; if it is intermediate, it seems to treat my own choice as a coin flip or as a fact about me that deliberation can only discover rather than settle. And if I allow the credence into the deliberation itself, a strange loop opens up: I revise my estimate of what I will do in response to noticing what I estimate, so the self-description begins to drive the thing described. Put aside the metaphysical question whether the future is fixed; my worry is about the norms of belief and decision, not determinism.

Present this clash at full strength. Defend the Bayesian theory as well as you can, and defend the intuition as well as you can. Then give me a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked as a confusion, and if so, which confusion exactly; should the Bayesian picture be amended so that credences about one's own choices are suspended or exempted during deliberation, and at what cost to its simplicity; or is there a reading on which both are correct — perhaps by distinguishing the first-person deliberative stance from a third-person predictive stance, or by reinterpreting what the probability is a probability of? I want the response to say what would justify each move, not simply choose a side. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning about this you think failure is most likely.
