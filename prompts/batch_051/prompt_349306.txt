i'm a psych phd student working on how people judge randomness and detect patterns, which makes this especially annoying to me. last spring, on the one-year anniversary of my dad's death, i was in a city i'd only been to once before, years ago, and i randomly thought of a childhood friend i hadn't spoken to in maybe fifteen years. not just thought of him: i had a vivid memory of a specific dumb joke he told in sixth grade, word for word. an hour later i walked into a coffee shop and he was behind the counter. same guy, same weird haircut he'd had as a kid, almost. he'd moved there three weeks earlier.

the part of me that does statistics knows exactly what to say. i'm commiting the birthday-paradox error. yes, the probability of that exact thing happening to that exact person in that exact hour is tiny, but the probability that some emotionally salient coincidence of roughly this shape happens to someone somewhere is enormous, especially once you allow "an hour later," "a different city i happened to be visiting," "almost the same haircut." under a loose enough description, coincidences like this are basically guaranteed. the feeling of significance is a post-hoc selection effect: i never count the thousands of unmatched thoughts, so the one hit looks miraculous.

but i cannot get the intuition to shut up. it wasn't just any person, it was this person, on that day, one hour after a memory with that much specificity. and the day matters. the anniversary doesn't feel like a random feature i'm allowed to fold into "some coincidence or other"; it's part of what makes the event the event. if i had actually pre-registered it — "today, around this time, i'll think of james and then run into him in a city neither of us lives in" — the probability would be absurdly small. the standard line only works by retreating to a vaguer event after the fact, and retreating to a vaguer event feels like changing the question rather than answering it.

i don't want to be told which side wins. i want a worked account of what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes (or at least to need amending), and whether there's a reading on which both survive. for example: the theory is right that i shouldn't infer some hidden meaning or mechanism, but the intuition is right that post-hoc broadening is doing real epistemic work and i'm allowed to insist on the narrower, anniversary-indexed event. is there a defensible way to say the narrower description is the relevant one, or is that just the bias talking? and does the fact that i would have pre-registered the specific version count as evidence about the event's probability, or only as evidence about my state of mind?
