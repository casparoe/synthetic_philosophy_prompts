So I've been working through objective Bayesianism lately, and I keep flip-flopping. The maximum entropy principle is the first thing I've seen that actually makes the problem of priors feel tractable: you write down the constraints you actually have — say, I know a quantity's mean and nothing else — and entropy picks the unique distribution that satisfies them and adds no further information. It has the virtues I usually want from a theory: it's simple, it gives a definite answer, and it feels principled rather than arbitrary. It also seems right that if two people share the same background information, they shouldn't be able to rationally choose wildly different priors, and max ent looks like a way to say which one they must choose.

But my intuition keeps rebelling on this one point: whatever prior counts as "ignorance" shouldn't depend on how I decide to write the problem down. Its a redescription-invariance intuition, I guess. If I know nothing except that a thing lies somewhere between 0 and 1, a flat prior over the thing says one thing, a flat prior over its square or its odds says another, and they're not the same distribution after I transform back. The needle-dropping case makes it vivid for me: max ent over one set of variables gives different probabilities for the same event than max ent over another equally natural set of variables. So the supposedly unique rational prior ends up looking like an artifact of the parametrization I happened to pick. That feels like a real problem rather than a technicality, because "ignorance" ought to be about the world, not about my variables.

I'm not looking for a canned "max ent is hopeless" or "just bite the bullet." I want the strongest version of each side. What exactly does the objective Bayesian have to say to the redescription complaint — is the reply that ignorance is always relative to a question, and you shouldn't expect invariance across different questions? And how far can that reply go before it stops being objective Bayesianism and becomes "the prior is just whatever symmetries you stipulate"? On the other side, if the intuition wins, what do I do about the fact that without max ent I'm back to feeling like any prior is arbitrary? I want to know what it would take for me to justifiably drop the intuition, what it would take for me to give up or amend the principle, and whether there's a version of "there are rational priors" where both can survive.
