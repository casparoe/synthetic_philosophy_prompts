I am a teaching assistant for a philosophy of mind course. I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether the global workspace theory explains access consciousness, phenomenal consciousness, or both, and I want to make sure my grading of one student's answer is not skewed by that argument. Below are the exam question and the student's answer.

Exam question:

Distinguish phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness. Does the global workspace theory aim to explain phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, or both? State the theory clearly and evaluate what it can and cannot account for.

Student answer:

The distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness is this: a mental state is phenomenally conscious if there is something it is like for the subject to be in it; it has a subjective, qualitative character. A state is access-conscious if its content is poised for use by consuming systems—available for verbal report, reasoning, and the rational control of behavior—without its having any qualitative feel in itself. As concepts, the two can come apart: a state can be access-conscious without being phenomenally conscious, and a state can be phenomenally conscious without being fully access-conscious, since reportability is a further functional matter.

Global workspace theory says that a representation becomes conscious when a coalition of processors wins a competition and its content is broadcast through a global workspace to a wide range of specialist systems, making it available for report, memory, planning, and other control processes. That description is plainly a description of access: it is an account of functional availability, not of what the state feels like. GWT therefore explains access consciousness. It says why some contents, rather than others, are poised for widespread use, and it fits evidence from masking, attention, and reportability.

However, I do not think it is right to say GWT explains only access. Once a content is globally broadcast, the subject is thereby aware of it, and there is something it is like to have that content active in this way. The global broadcast is not merely a precondition for report; it is the neural realization of phenomenal consciousness itself. Since verbal report is our only route to knowing about another person's phenomenal states, the fact that GWT explains reportability means it explains everything we could strictly mean by phenomenal consciousness. To insist on a residue of unreportable feel is unscientific. Thus GWT is best read as a theory of both: it explains access, and what it explains about access simply is the explanation of phenomenal consciousness.
