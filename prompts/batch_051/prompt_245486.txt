For most of my professional life I ran an analytical chemistry laboratory of the kind where, at the outset, nothing important was opaque. A careful colleague could sit down with the raw chromatograms or spectra, follow the baseline corrections and integration choices, redo the error propagation, and arrive at our published numbers; indeed, several members of the lab could and did do exactly that when a result looked suspicious. Over three decades this stopped being true, and it stopped being true without anyone deciding that it should. Routines were layered on routines: automated baseline fitting, deconvolution, iterative Monte Carlo error models, and then machine-learning classifiers for peak assignment. Each step was individually reasonable, usually validated against data, and documented. Yet by the time I retired, there was no single person in the lab who could reconstruct the entire chain from raw output to final figure in a way that did not itself depend on the very software whose behavior was in question. Nor, so far as I could tell, could the lab as a group.

What I found striking was that no one treated this as a crisis, and confidence in fact went up, not down. Error-checking did not disappear; it migrated. We checked that the software was an industry standard, that other laboratories obtained compatible results with different instruments, that the vendors had reputations to protect, and that the findings hung together with the rest of the field. The possibility of catching a subtle systematic error by independently redoing the analysis from first principles simply vanished from the set of things anyone expected a competent scientist to be able to do.

I have been reading philosophy of science in retirement and I keep wanting a term for the shift itself: the moment, or the process, by which a scientific community passes from "there is someone who can independently redo this" to "this has been collectively accepted as checked," where the checking is now a property of the social network rather than of any individual act of understanding. It resembles deskilling, and it resembles what I see discussed as automation bias, but neither captures the collective and almost demographic character of what I watched. It was not that particular people trusted a machine too much on particular occasions, and not merely that individual skills atrophied. It was that the community's capacity to detect a certain class of error relocated from persons to institutions, and nobody registered that relocation as a loss.

Is there an established name for this transition, or for the community on the far side of it? If none fits exactly, I would be grateful for a proposed term, a definition, and a clear statement of how the phenomenon differs from deskilling, from automation bias, and from any nearby concept I have not named. A bare label would not satisfy me; what I want is the carving, including what would have to be true for my worry to be misplaced.
