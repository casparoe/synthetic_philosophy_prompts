I work as a legislative aide for a city councilmember in a mid-sized US city, and I've somehow become the person who triages all the exemption requests that come into the office: variances from the sign code, a block party that wants to run past the noise cutoff, a small landlord who wants out of the short-term rental cap because she only rents her basement once in a while, a restaurant that wants to pay a fee instead of installing the required bike racks. It's a couple dozen a week, and my boss keeps saying "just apply the what-if-everyone-did-that test," which is not exactly a procedure. So I'd like a genuine decision proceedure my team could actually run on each request in about 45 minutes, using the one-page staff summaries, before it goes to the councilmember. The stakes vary from trivial to full-on neighborhood drama, and the councilmember signs off on all of them.

I've read enough to know the test is not as obvious as it sounds. There's the old problem that how you describe the act changes the answer: what if everyone whose great-aunt is visiting next Tuesday parked in the loading zone is not the right question, but neither is just what if everyone parked there. There's Hare's point that universalizability is about whether you can consistently prescribe the action for anyone in your situation, which by itself doesn't settle what would happen if the thing were actually adopted by everyone. And there's the generalization argument (I think it's usually attributed to Marcus Singer) — roughly, if the consequences of everyone's doing x would be bad, nobody may do x — which has its own known trouble with thresholds and aggregate harms, where the bad outcome is caused by everyone doing it at once and not by any one person. Please turn this into a checklist or ordered questions that keeps those distinctions, but in a form a non-philosopher staffer can actually use: what questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it kicks the decision to the councilmember because it's a values call rather than a mechanical test.

Then run the procedure on three requests: (1) a well-liked corner grocery that wants to stay open 24 hours in an otherwise residential neighborhood, (2) an exception for one block association's annual street fair from the city's amplified-sound rules, and (3) a small nonprofit asking to be exempted from the city's new event-permit fee because its events are free and tiny. Make sure at least one of the three comes out the other way from what the councilmember's gut says — I want to see the procedure bite, not just confirm vibes. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do: the places where what-if-everyone-did-that runs out and an actual decision has to be made some other way.
