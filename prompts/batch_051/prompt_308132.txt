I recently started working on a project that runs an imitation-game-style evaluation of conversational systems, with human judges exchanging typed messages with a mix of human and machine participants. In a design meeting about which channels of information have to be controlled, a colleague made an offhand reference to Alan Turing having worried about telepathy in his original imitation-game paper. I went back and reread the relevant passage, and I now find it more philosophically interesting than the charming oddity I had remembered. Turing appears to treat the statistical evidence for telepathy as substantial, and suggests that if one accepts that evidence, the test procedure itself needs to be altered so that participants cannot influence each other through any such channel.

I am trying to understand how this passage fits into the paper's overall strategy. The paper is famous for setting aside the question of whether machines can think as too ill-defined, in favor of a restricted behavioral test. But the extrasensory-perception discussion seems to do the opposite: it takes seriously a claim that looks far less respectable than the original question, and draws a concrete design consequence from it. Could you explain the dialectical role of that discussion? In particular, I want to understand why the imitation game, once adopted as an operational replacement for a vague question, commits its defender to worrying about confounding causal channels such as telepathy rather than dismissing them out of hand, and what this reveals about what the test is and is not supposed to establish. Please keep your explanation to no more than 450 words.
