At a Fourth of July cookout my sister and her husband got into an argument about rationality after someone brought up a lottery hypothetical, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Here's the setup they agreed on. Marcus described two choices. In the first, you pick between (A) a guaranteed $1 million and (B) a lottery that gives an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. In the second, you pick between (C) an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing, and (D) a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing. Marcus said most people pick A over B and also D over C, and that this is inconsistent: the second pair is just the first pair with the 89% chance of $1 million that's common to A and B swapped out for an 89% chance of nothing. By the independence axiom, if you prefer A to B you are committed to preferring C to D. So the popular pattern is irrational.

Elise said the popular pattern isn't irrational because the two "nothing" outcomes aren't the same thing. In B, losing means knowing you walked away from a sure million, and that regret is a real part of the outcome; in D there's no sure million to give up in the first place, so the experience of losing is different. Marcus said this is just relabeling the violation: independence says the common branch is irrelevent, and if you let imagined regret about the branch you didn't pick do the work, you can rationalize any preference whatever by adding feelings to outcomes after the fact. Elise answered that feelings aren't after the fact; they're part of the outcome from the start, and expected utility theory is only as good as its outcome descriptions. Marcus came back that if that's the game, the axiom is unfalsifiable and "rationality" means nothing, because any alleged inconsistency can be hidden inside a richer outcome description. Elise said the axiom isn't supposed to be a psychological litmus test anyway; it's a coherence condition on preferences over carefully described options, and the Allais example just shows how hard it is to describe options well. At that point someone needed more ice and the arguement sort of died.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I'd like to know, for each claim in their exchange, whether it's right, wrong, or somewhere in between, and who has the stronger overall position. In particular, I can't decide whether Elise's move is a legitimate defense of expected utility theory or a way of making the whole framework vacuous.
