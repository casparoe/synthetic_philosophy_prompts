I am a retired natural scientist — chemistry by training — who came to moral philosophy late, and I have been working through the universalizability tradition: Kant's formula of universal law, Hare's prescriptivism, and the what-if-everyone-did-that style of generalization argument one finds in Singer and in everyday moral reasoning. I am compiling a list of objections to the idea that acting rightly is a matter of being able to will one's maxim, or one's prescription, as a universal law, and I want one more.

So far I have five.

(1) The maxim-description problem: the test's verdict depends on how the act is described. The same token lie can be brought under a maxim about saving a life that passes, or under a maxim about convenient lying that fails, and the formula supplies no canonical level of description.

(2) The empty-formalism problem: maxims of ruthless self-advantage or universal nonbeneficence can be conceived as universal laws without the strict contradiction the test needs; Kant's fallback, that such a world cannot be willed, trades on empirical needs or moral assumptions that the egoist may simply refuse.

(3) The rigorism problem: applied in earnest the test seems to forbid what it should require. In the standard case of lying to a would-be murderer at the door, Kant's own treatment condemns even the lie that would save an innocent life, a result nearly everyone finds perverse; here the test is not too vague but all too definite, and the definite answer looks morally wrong.

(4) The fanaticism problem, pressed hardest by Hare: a bigot who is genuinely prepared to prescribe that he himself be treated as he treats his victims if he were in their position satisfies universalizability while prescribing something monstrous, so the logical requirement does not by itself exclude evil.

(5) The frequency problem for the naive generalization argument: if everyone stayed home from work the economy would collapse, and if everyone refused to become doctors no one would be cared for, but nothing follows about my one sick day or my choice of profession. The badness depends on how many do the thing, not on each one doing it.

I would like a sixth objection that is not on this list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of any of these — one with a genuinely different structure, and one a serious defender of the universalizability test would have to answer rather than wave away. After you give it, say exactly how it differs from each of the five. Then take a committed view: state plainly whether the objection ultimately succeeds, or whether the best version of the test can absorb it, and defend that verdict rather than laying out both sides and leaving me to choose.
