During a dispute over how our city should present an event from roughly forty years ago — a strike that turned violent and ended in a wave of arrests and expulsions of the organizers — a proposal came before the council that would govern not just this case but every official presentation of the past. Under the proposal, whenever a historical marker, museum exhibit, public commemoration, or school history unit concerns an event within living memory, the account endorsed by the community's collective memory becomes the primary account. Collective memory is to be measured annually by a citywide survey, demographically weighted to match the current census, that asks open-ended questions about what happened, who was responsible, and what the event means for the city's identity. Where the survey's narrative conflicts with the documentary and archival record, the archival version may not be suppressed, but it must be presented in a clearly subordinate position — a footnote, an appendix, a side room — and teachers may not present it as the version students should accept. A nine-member Memory Board, elected at large, would write the final wording; at least six members must have lived in the city for thirty years or more, and no more than three may have professional training in history.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a position on the underlying dispute, and walk through how it would actually behave. Whom does it incentivize, and to do what — residents, neighborhood associations, labor and religious groups, politicians, teachers, and professional historians? Who would invest in shaping the annual survey's wording, sampling, and interpretation, and where would the system get gamed first? Consider organized groups trying to become the surveyed "community," memory entrepreneurs, politicians laundering a preferred story through the board, and the survey designers themselves. What happens when the community's memory is split, when the survey's result changes from one year to the next, or when the event is remembered by one generation but not its successors? Does the rule preserve an already-existing collective memory, or does it manufacture a new official one? Finally, say which consideration would most likely change your assessment of the proposal and where your analysis is most likely to go wrong.
