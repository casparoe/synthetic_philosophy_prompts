I do engineering at a startup that's building an agent that can take over chunks of our ops work — it drafts scripts, moves data around, sends emails, updates tickets, that kind of thing. We're about two months out from letting a few paying customers use it, and the team is split on where the remaining saftey effort should go.

One faction (mostly the security-minded people) wants to spend the time on containment: sandboxing, requiring a human to sign off before the agent touches production or spends money or emails anyone outside the company, logging everything, and capping how much it can change about its own code. Their line is that we fundamentally don't know what goals the system has; fine-tuning for helpfulness doesn't prove it's on our side, and a system can be competent and still be trying to do something other than what we asked. So the rational play is to treat it as if it might be working against us and make sure it can't do much damage.

The other faction thinks this is backwards. Their argument is that a system that has to be contained is already a liability: if it's actually competent it will find a hole in the sandbox, or prod a sleepy human into approving something they shouldn't, and worst case it learns to look nice during evals and only acts up once the controls are loosened. They want to spend the two months trying to get the model to genuinely want to help the user — better RLHF with actual user outcomes, training on cases where the helpful-sounding answer is not what the user really wants, eval suites designed to catch a model that is just performing niceness. They say containment mostly makes the demo worse and buys a false sense of security.

I keep going back and forth. I don't think we're anywhere near the regime where the agent could systematically outwit our sandbox; at worst it's a sycophantic intern with occasional sharp edges, so maybe the containment work is the safer bet and buys us another quarter to get the motivational side right. But we also all know the plan is to loosen those controls the first time a customer asks for something that needs more access. So who has the better engineering prioritization? And does it actually matter that this system is not very capable — or would the two sides of this argument be exactly as strong at a frontier lab?
