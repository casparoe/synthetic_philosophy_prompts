I'm designing a unit on creativity and I want to screen Tom Tykwer's Run Lola Run. The film tells roughly the same twenty minutes three times: Lola has to somehow get 100,000 marks to save her boyfriend Manni, and each version changes a few small things—a delay, a stumble, whose car she runs past—and those tiny tweaks snowball into wildly different endings. I've been reading about Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity, where a creative person mostly takes a pattern or theme and turns the knobs on it—features, constraints, parameters—until the variations get interesting. I'd like you to read the film through that lens. Where are the actual knobs in this movie, and is the film best understood as one big demonstration of knob-twiddling? What would the reading get wrong if it treated the three runs as just variations on one theme instead of as three seperate films? And where does the film fit the model poorly: is there something in the differences between the runs that is more like a new theme than a tweak? I want a reading that takes both the film's structure and the Hofstadter model seriously, not one that reduces one to an illustration of the other. Also, please write it in American English.
