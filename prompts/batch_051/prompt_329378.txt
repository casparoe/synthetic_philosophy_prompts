I'm taking a machine-learning-ethics-ish class this semester (the syllabus calls it Fairness and Algorithmic Decision-Making), and for the final project I want to build one of those "which statistical fairness criterion are you?" quizzes — like a personality quiz but for how people actually want a classifier to treat different groups. I've got the rough idea but I keep writing questions that collapse into each other, or that basilcy ask "do you like calibration?" which defeats the point.

Could you help me design it? I'd like eight to ten concrete cases — a COMPAS-style pretrial risk score, a loan model, a medical triage score, a university admissions predictor, maybe a hiring screen — each with two or three answer options that don't use any of the fairness terms. The options should force a real choice between: same predicted score means same probability of the outcome across groups (calibration); false positive and false negative rates are the same across groups (equalized odds); selection rates are the same across groups (demographic parity); or positive and negative predictive value are the same across groups (predictive parity). In at least half the cases the two groups should have different base rates, otherwise the real tensions never show up, and each option should have an actual cost attached (extra false positives, a selection-rate gap, worse calibration somewhere) so it's not obvious which one is "nicer." The scoring key should map answer patterns onto those four criteria, plus a leaf for someone who turns out to have no consistant statistical criterion, and a leaf for someone who ends up thinking no single criterion can be the whole story. Since we spent weeks on the impossibility results, I'd also want the key to flag answer patterns that combine criteria which are mathematically in tension when base rates differ — like calibration plus equalized odds unless the groups have equal base rates or the predictor is basically perfect, and equalized odds plus demographic parity when base rates differ and the predictor is doing any actual work — and for each impossible combo, one sentence on what you'd have to give up to insist on it anyway. Also flag any two leaves the quiz can't really separate (I suspect calibration and predictive parity will smear into each other) rather than pretending the questions decide between them. The audience is undergrads who have taken one ML class but haven't memorized the fairness definitions, and the whole point is that the cases do the sorting, not the labels.
