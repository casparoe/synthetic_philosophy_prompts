In my research ethics seminar, we have been discussing clinical equipoise, the standard requirement that a randomized controlled trial may proceed only when the relevant expert medical community is genuinely uncertain about which arm is superior, so that no participant is knowingly assigned to a treatment believed inferior. I am interested in what would happen if an institutional review board replaced this substantive judgment with a procedural test. The proposed rule is this: for each proposed two-arm trial, the IRB opens a public prediction market before enrollment, in which any physician or patient may buy or sell contracts that pay out one dollar if the experimental arm beats the control by at least the sponsor's prespecified primary-outcome margin and nothing otherwise; the trial may commence only if the market's implied probability of such an advantage remains strictly inside a prespecified indifference band centered on one half for six consecutive weeks, with the band widened for trials sponsored by non-profit institutions. Please analyze how this mechanism would actually operate: what it would incentivize among sponsors, investigators, clinicians, patients, and other market participants; how it would most likely be gamed; where it would diverge from the ethical concern that motivates equipoise; and whether its failure modes could be repaired by adjusting the trading rules or are intrinsic to using a market as a test of ethical permission.
