I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether any of our beliefs are rationally unrevisable. He kept insisting that even our most basic commitments—say, that the Earth has existed for a long time or that our memories are broadly reliable—must in principle be open to evidence, since otherwise we have abandoned fallibilism. I wanted to push back using the image in Wittgenstein's On Certainty, around remarks 341 to 343, where he describes how the questions we raise and the doubts we entertain depend on certain propositions being exempt from doubt, much as hinges on which a door turns. My friend replied that this metaphor actually cuts the other way: hinges can be replaced one at a time while the door keeps swinging, so it suggests piecemeal revisability rather than unrevisability. I am not sure he is wrong, and that is what I would like help thinking through.

I would like you to set an agenda for the philosophical work here. Suppose a graduate student with three years to work in epistemology asked you which open questions in hinge epistemology are most worth pursuing. Which would you rank highest, and why? I am less interested in a survey than in the reasoning: for each question, what makes it upstream of other debates—about skepticism, deep disagreement, epistemic injustice, or the rationality of conversion, for example—how tractable it is for someone without a background in Wittgenstein scholarship, and what would count as progress or a changed mind. Also, please say which supposed implications of hinge epistemology, such as the claim that some disagreements cannot be rationally resolved or that certain worldviews are wholly beyond epistemic criticism, you think are overrated or do not follow from the hinge picture.
