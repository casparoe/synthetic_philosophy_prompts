Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Only sentient beings matter for their own sake; species membership, being alive, or having certain genes adds nothing directly.
(2) I put the probability that insects are sentient pretty low, maybe 2 to 10 percent depending on the day, and I lean toward explaining most insect behavior with nociception, reflexes, and simple associative learning rather than felt pain.
(3) I accept expected value reasoning under uncertainty: when you don't know whether something matters, you multiply the probability that it matters by how much it would matter, and act on whichever option comes out highest.
(4) I therefore buy the scale argument: with something like 10^18 or 10^19 insects alive at any time, even a low single-digit probability of sentience means the expected number of sentient insects dwarfs the expected number of sentient vertebrates, so insect welfare should be near the top of any global priority list.
(5) I also think Pascal's wager, Pascal's mugging, and more generally the move of letting a small probability of an enormous payoff capture all of your decision-making is fanatical and should be rejected.
(6) I don't think this commits me to caring about plants or bacteria, because there I assign a negligible or effectively zero probability of sentience, whereas insects have centralized nervous systems and enough behavioral flexibility that sentience is a live empirical question in a way it just isn't for plants.
(7) If I became convinced insects are not sentient, I would also think there are no direct duties to individual insects, though there could still be indirect duties through ecosystem effects or the sensibilities of sentient beings.
