In the epistemology of testimony, the reductionist tradition associated with Hume holds that a hearer is justified in accepting a report only if she has positive, non-testimonial evidence for the reliability of testimony in general or of this source in particular. The anti-reductionist tradition associated with Reid denies this: testimony, like perception or memory, is a basic source of justification, and absent specific grounds for doubt a hearer is entitled to accept what she is told. One standard way of motivating the anti-reductionist position is to observe that the practice of testimony can have this default status because speakers are normally answerable agents: they have some competence about what they report, and they ordinarily have something at stake in being found reliable, so it is rational to treat their say-so as innocent until shown guilty.

Consider now a specific development. Much of what ordinary people now encounter as testimony-like text—fluent, informative, confident—is produced by large language models at near-zero marginal cost and presented without any identifiable speaker. There is no agent who vouches for the content, no stable character against which an inductive track record could be built, and often no mechanism by which the source's standing is damaged by being wrong. The social conditions of ordinary testimony—being answerable to one's audience and to shared standards of evidence—are absent.

Does the anti-reductionist argument survive this development? I want a precise diagnosis, not a policy recommendation.

In particular: (1) Which premise is genuinely under pressure—the premise that the source is an intentional agent with something at stake, the premise that the source has competence worth trusting, or the premise that the practice of testimony is generally reliable enough for defaults to be reasonable? (2) A natural first reply is that machine output is not testimony at all, since nothing is attested by anyone, so the anti-reductionist can simply restrict the default entitlement to persons. Is that restriction principled or ad hoc? What must be true of a source for the default to attach, and does that condition already smuggle in what made the default reasonable? (3) If anti-reductionism can be repaired, give the strongest repaired version—for example, one that grounds default acceptance in a source's occupying a role within an institution that monitors and sanctions its outputs. If it cannot be repaired without losing what made it distinctive, say so, and say whether the resulting view is simply reductionism for machine-generated text. (4) Does the reductionist inherit the field by default? Or does the reductionist face an equally serious problem here, since the inductive track record that reductionism demands may itself be unavailable, manipulated, or uninformative for such sources?

Conclude by stating the clearest defensible updated position, and be explicit about what it gives up compared with the original anti-reductionist view.
