The topic is punctuality and whether a small promise is really broken. I want a case where the expectation view of promising and the practice view come apart. The expectation view says the wrong of breaking a promise is that you disappoint the expectations you led another person to form. The practice view says the wrong is in breaking the rules of the social practice you invoke when you make the promise, whatever the person privately expected.

So construct a one-paragraph scenario from ordinary life — a dinner, a lift, a phone call, that kind of thing — where someone is late in a way that counts as a broken promise on the practice view but not, or much less so, on the expectation view. The late person shouldn't be a villain or a obligation-phobe, and this shouldn't be a loophole about saying "I'll try" instead of "I promise." The promisee's private expectations should be the feature that does the work, and you can stipulate the psychology as long as it stays believable.

Then walk through the reasoning for each view: what exactly each says the wrong consists in, and why they verdict the case differently. After that, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and how a defender of the expectation view would push back on using it. End with whether removing that feature makes the two views collapse into agreement, or whether some other feature takes over.
