Write a ten-question sorting quiz for a philosophy blog, under a working title such as "Which view of loyalty and impartiality do you actually hold?" The quiz should classify respondents on the question whether partiality toward kin, friends, and fellow group members is a moral requirement, a justified permission, a virtue, or a bias. Sort respondents by what they say about concrete cases, never by which label they like: no question may ask them to classify themselves or to endorse a philosophical term.

The cases. Write ten vignettes with two or three answer options each. Distribute them across close family, friendship, professional and institutional roles, and group or national membership. Include both low-stakes cases (a hiring decision, a recommendation, a small grant, a queue) and high-stakes cases (rescue, whistleblowing, concealing wrongdoing, allocating a scarce medical resource). At least one case must involve partiality toward a group or institution rather than an individual person. At least one case must be written so that the loyal act and the impartial act coincide, to catch respondents who reject partiality even when no impartial interest is set back. At least one case must vary the size of the impartial cost, from one stranger to very many strangers, or from a small overpayment to a serious harm. In every case, the options should differ in what the respondent would do or judge, not in vocabulary.

Build the key from logical space rather than from a list of labels. Before writing the questions, specify the two underlying axes that will organize the key: a deontic axis (for a partial act that sets back impartial interests: wrong, permissible but not required, required, or supererogatory) and an explanatory axis (whether the act is justified by the relationship itself or by impartial considerations such as trust, the efficient division of epistemic or moral labor, or privacy). State the allowed values for each axis and the combinations that are taken to be possible. Then locate these five positions in that space and give a scoring rule that maps each question's answer options to axis values and maps aggregate profiles to one of the five positions:

1. Impartialism: partial acts are never justified except as heuristics or as role-duties that impartial rules would themselves endorse.
2. Constrained-permission impartialism: impartiality supplies the default decision procedure and side-constraints, but partial acts are permitted inside those constraints.
3. Partiality as a virtue: partial acts are justified by the relationship and by the agent's judgment, not by impartial accounting; they are neither universally required nor merely tolerated, because loyalty is one excellence among others and can be misapplied.
4. Special-obligations partialism: some partial acts are required outright, and such requirements can outweigh impartial duties even at serious cost to outsiders.
5. Role-graded partialism: partiality is required, its strength is graduated by the proximity and kind of relationship, and a residue of impartial respect is owed at every level.

For every axis combination that no listed position occupies, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, empty because it is unattractive, or empty because it is merely unexplored.

Flag separability limits. Where two of the five positions answer the vignettes identically, say so, explain which underlying difference the cases fail to elicit, and either add a question that would separate them or specify how the key should report respondents who fall in that region. Also specify how to score mixed profiles that do not match any single position, rather than forcing them into the nearest leaf.

The quiz is for readers with no formal philosophy background. Answer options may be plainspoken, but the scoring key and rationale should use the philosophical vocabulary precisely; the instrument should stand on its own, without attributing any view or answer option to named historical figures.
