A friend and I have spent much of the past month arguing in circles about the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, and last night convinced me that the argument is stuck for structural reasons rather than because either of us is missing a fact. The setup is simple. An urn contains 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. Offer 1 is a choice between a bet paying $100 if red is drawn and a bet paying $100 if black is drawn; offer 2 is a choice between a bet paying $100 if black or yellow is drawn and a bet paying $100 if red or yellow is drawn. The familiar pattern is to prefer the red bet in offer 1 and the black-or-yellow bet in offer 2. That pair of preferences cannot be represented by any single assignment of probabilities to red, black, and yellow, so it cannot be ordinary expected utility maximization. My friend takes that as decisive: the pair violates a separability condition of the same family as Savage's sure-thing principle, the idea that a ranking of two options should not depend on outcomes in states where the options agree, so a rational agent should correct the preferences once the structure is plain. I keep resisting, but I cannot state precisely what my resistance is. The same standoff appears in the decades-long literature on ambiguity aversion: one side argues that a rational agent facing partially unknown probabilities is entitled to be cautious, while the other side counters that ambiguity-averse preferences can be Dutch-booked (led by a series of individually attractive choices to a sure loss) or otherwise behave inconsistently across time. Both sides have produced sophisticated competing formal models rather than convergence. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict. Is this a substantive normative disagreement dressed up as a debate about axioms, a technical disagreement about which formal conditions are genuinely binding, a disagreement about whether the standard Ellsberg pattern should be treated as data at all, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about what rationality demands under ignorance? My working hypothesis is that the two sides implicitly mean different things by "rational," but I am not sure that hypothesis survives contact with the formal work. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a reformulated question, or an argument that convergence should not be expected?
