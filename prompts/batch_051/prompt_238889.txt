Longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums here, with a question about the role of aesthetic considerations in theory choice.

I recently came across a general-audience book on the philosophy of physics whose author summarizes the view that elegance is evidence for a theory's truth in roughly the following way:

Physicists have long treated elegance as a sign that a theory is on the right track. The reasoning starts from the record: the theories that survived — Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell's electrodynamics, special and general relativity, Dirac's equation — were strikingly economical, symmetric, and unified. Elegance is not mere decoration; it tracks compression. A theory that derives a wide range of phenomena from a small number of independent principles has fewer adjustable parts, and a theory with fewer adjustable parts is more difficult to fit to coincidence. So when a compressed theory keeps producing novel predictions that come out correct, those successes are strong evidence that the theory has latched onto something real. That is why, all else equal, the more elegant of two competing theories is more likely to be true, and why a sufficiently ugly or patchwork theory is very likely false. Recognizing elegance does not require any special training or expertise; it is the same feeling of neatness anyone gets from a tidy explanation.

Something about that last stretch bothers me. I can see how the compression-and-overfitting part might be a reasonable gloss on a Bayesian idea about priors, but I cannot articulate why the step from "successful theories have tended to be elegant" to "elegance makes a theory more likely to be true" feels too strong, and the line about needing no training seems suspicious in a way I cannot pin down.
