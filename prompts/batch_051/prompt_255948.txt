I spent thirty years teaching chemistry and then intro physics at a state university, and only after I retired did I start reading philosophy of science. It's made me think back to a colleague in the physics department, Dana, who had a particular way of sorting student errors. She'd say some wrong answers were real theories and others were just slogans or slips. She wasnt being generous or harsh — she genuinely thought some misconceptions had the structure of a theory and some didn't. I kept a mental list of her calls.

A student said a thrown ball keeps moving because "the throw puts force inside it, and the force gets used up as it goes." The student used this to explain why the ball slows, why a harder throw goes farther, and predicted a heavier ball would hold its force longer. Dana: real theory.

A student said heat is a fluid that flows from hot things to cold things, used it to explain why a mug cools and why a spoon in tea gets hot, and predicted that touching a hot pan lets some of the fluid flow into your hand. Dana: real theory.

A student said the current gets "used up" by a bulb, and consistently predicted that in a series circuit the second bulb gets less current, and that a battery eventually runs out of current. Dana: real theory.

A student said heavy things fall faster than light things, but when pushed — feather and hammer on the moon, two balls of different weights tied together, a heavy ball dropped next to a light one — just repeated the slogan or gave answers that contradicted each other. Dana: not a theory, just a slogan.

A student once said a feather and a hammer dropped in air would hit at the same time, then immediately corrected himself. Dana: not a theory, just a slip.

Now I'm in a reading group and we got into what makes a student error theory-like, and I remembered a student Dana never got to classify. This student said that cold is a fluid that flows into the room when you open the fridge, and used it to explain why the floor near the fridge gets cold and why closing the door stops it. But a week later the same student said cold is just the absence of heat, it isnt a thing, and when asked to redo the fridge case just shrugged. What would Dana have said — real theory or not? I can see at least two principles that fit her old calls and they point differnt ways here. What are those principles, which one should I bet on, and what further student case would settle it?
