Im a statistician, and Ive got a rule I use in my own modeling and when I referee papers: if a model needs a prior and all I actually know is a constraint—say a mean, bounds, maybe a variance—I use the maximum entropy distribution that satisfies the constraint, and I treat any other prior as unjustified unless the person can point to real information I dont have. Concretely, if I know X is in [0,1] with mean .7, I use the truncated exponential with that mean, and I'll reject a Beta(7,3) out of hand. I think of maxent as the only prior that doesn't pretend to know more than I do, and Ive gotten genuinely annoyed in arguments where people say its just one convention among others. I know about the reparameterization problem—that the maxent prior for X and for, say, logit(X) can disagree—but I usually wave it off by saying I parameterize by the quantity I care about. Heres my question: is this rule rationally defensible, or have I dressed up a bias as rigor? What exactly does maximum entropy get you, and when does "least informative" stop being a fact and become a modeling choice? Is there a version of the rule I can keep that's actually defensible, or should I drop the mandatory part and treat maxent as just a default I have to argue for case by case?
