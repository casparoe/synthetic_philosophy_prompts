I work as a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I keep hitting a tension in how we design hiring, review, and promotion processes, and I want a committed verdict on it rather than a survey of options.

Here is the tension. In ordinary life I hold something like this: favoring the people you know and care about is not just permissible but often good. A mother who spends her weekend helping her own daughter with algebra rather than volunteering at a tutoring program is being a good mother, not committing a bias. An engineer who uses a slow Friday to unravel a mess in a friend's code base instead of taking a ticket that would help more of the organization is being a decent colleague. In these cases I would describe the same underlying disposition—weighting the interests of people close to you more heavily than the interests of strangers—as loyalty, maybe even duty.

But when the setting changes only slightly, I call that very disposition bias. A reviewer who waves through a friend's sloppy pull request has, in my view, failed at the job and should not be reviewing that code. A manager who gives a friend a generous performance rating that the work does not support has corrupted the process. A promotion committee member with a close personal stake in a candidate is expected to recuse, and we would be angry if they did not. Same disposition, same kind of favoritism; one verdict is deeply positive, the other deeply negative.

A second asymmetry feels related. If an engineer finds that a stranger has been padding expense reports and reports it, that is straightforwardly the right thing to do. If an engineer finds that her own teammate has been doing the same thing and reports it, many of us—me included, if I am honest—feel some residue of unease, as if the act were now disloyal even if it was still correct. I want to say the external report is simply good and the internal one is somehow morally mixed. I do not know whether that residue is a defensible judgment or just an instinct I should ignore.

What I want from you is the strongest defensible principle that separates the cases where partiality is a virtue from the cases where it is a bias, applied honestly. State the principle precisely. Run it against all the cases above, including my uneasy reaction to reporting a teammate. Then test it on at least two further cases that make the line hardest for it—for example a judge ruling in a friend's divorce, a doctor pulling strings to get a relative seen sooner, a nation favoring its own citizens in immigration, or any harder case you think of. Tell me explicitly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to drop or revise. Take a definite position and defend it.
