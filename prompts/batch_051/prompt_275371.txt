I have been arguing with two friends about the standard proportionalist objection to the doctrine of hell, and I keep getting stuck on what the objection is actually targeting. The objection says, roughly, that any just punishment must be proportionate in severity to the offence; that the offences of finite creatures are finite in gravity (setting aside the reply that sin against an infinite God is infinitely grave); but that eternal conscious torment imposes a punishment of infinite duration and, on many versions, of maximal severity. So the traditional view cannot be just, and one of the alternatives — eventual annihilation, a finite period of suffering, or universal salvation — must be right. One friend thinks this argument is sound and pushes straightforwardly to annihilation. Another thinks it collapses the moment you modify the punishment at all, which he takes to show that the objectors are merely reacting to the word "eternal."

To settle this, consider two precise variants. In the first, the punished person suffers forever, but the intensity decays geometrically: each year is half as intense as the one before, so the total suffering summed over all eternity is a finite quantity, and after a finite number of years each remaining year is arbitrarily close to painless. In the second, each year is as severe as a year of ordinary torment and never decays, but at the end of each year the punishment ends with independent probability one percent, so the expected total duration is a finite hundred years, although with positive probability it lasts for any given finite number of years, however large.

Work through what a careful proportionalist should say about each variant, and how that changes our assessment of the original argument against eternal torment. If neither variant escapes the objection, then the force of the argument cannot really come from the literal infinity of the duration: say what does the work instead — infinite aggregate suffering, infinite expected suffering, merely unbounded possible suffering, or the absence of any actual end. If one of the variants does escape, say exactly which feature of unmodified eternal torment the objection depends on. Finally, state which of the three positions — eternal torment, annihilation, universal salvation — is best supported by the strongest surviving version of the argument, and whether that version, once made precise, would also condemn some punishments that a finite human legal system could impose.
