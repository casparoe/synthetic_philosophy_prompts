I have been reading Gilbert Ryle’s book The Concept of Mind, especially the chapter called Knowing How and Knowing That. There is a point in that chapter where he argues, if I have understood him, that considering a proposition is not the hidden cause of skill but is itself a kind of performance—something a person can do intelligently or clumsily. That idea struck me as important, because it seems to suggest that we cannot explain all know-how by pointing to an inner piece of know-that. But when I try to sort out my own reactions to particular cases, I find myself pulled in different directions. I would like you to tell me whether there is one principle behind my verdicts, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

Here are my cases. First, a man who can recite complete instructions for solving a Rubik’s cube and can talk a friend through every turn, explaining what each move accomplishes, but who has never actually handled a cube himself because of a severe hand tremor. I find myself saying he does know how to solve the cube, at least in a meaningful sense. Second, a woman who can solve the cube very quickly but cannot explain or teach any of what she does, even to herself; when asked why she made a certain move, she says it just looked right. I have no hesitation saying she knows how to solve it.

Third, a beginning driver who has memorized the whole parallel-parking section of the manual and can give a crisp rule about when to turn the wheel, but on the street she cannot park without hitting the curb. I do not feel she knows how to parallel park. Fourth, an experienced driver who parks perfectly every time but gives a completely wrong account of what he does with the wheel when asked; I still say he knows how to parallel park.

Fifth, a native speaker of Spanish who uses the subjunctive correctly in fluent speech but, when asked to explain the rule she is following, gives a false one. I say she knows how to speak Spanish even though she does not know the rule. Sixth, a chess novice who has read several books and can state the principles of central control and piece activity, but who regularly loses to children. I find it hard to say he knows how to play chess.

I can see that my second, fourth, and fifth cases point away from requiring explicit knowledge. But my first and third cases seem to point in opposite directions: the cube-coach with no hands-on practice seems to know how, while the driving student with a manual but no practice seems not to know how. What could explain that difference? Is it that solving a cube is more like following a finite recipe, while driving requires continuous adjustment that no verbal rule can fully supply? Or am I just being inconsistent, and one of those two verdicts has to go?

I want a principle, stated plainly, that sorts these cases the way I do, or as close as possible. Tell me which of my verdicts the best principle would force me to abandon, and give me the hardest case you can think of for the principle you end up with. Also, as you present the principle, please say what argument or counterexample would lead you to abandon it, or where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail. And does Ryle’s point about the activity of considering propositions actually support the principle you propose, or did I read too much into that passage?
