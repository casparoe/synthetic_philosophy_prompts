In my ethics teaching I often encounter a version of the following position: people who ordinarily abstain from animal products on moral grounds will nevertheless eat eggs from rescued hens or meat and dairy recovered from waste, on the grounds that doing so creates no demand and leaves no animal worse off than it would otherwise have been. I want to understand what philosophical commitments such an exception carries. Reconstruct the theory of what makes eating animal products wrong, if anything, that the practice presupposes. In particular, does the exception strictly presuppose that the wrongness consists entirely in causing avoidable harm or supporting a harmful system, or is it also compatible with the view that consuming animal products is wrong in itself, with a complicity-based view, or with a view on which eating has expressive or symbolic significance? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely coheres well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me whether the position remains coherent when extended to neighboring cases such as roadkill, leather salvaged from discarded goods, or eggs found in the wild, and what a proponent would have to change if she came to hold that the wrongness resides in the act of consumption itself rather than in its consequences. Write your answer in continuous prose, as you would a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
