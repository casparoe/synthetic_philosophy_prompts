A guy I sometimes interview for pieces about math and AI cornered me after a talk and walked me through something he says is the underrated lesson of Gödel: not that there are unprovable truths, but that nobody is actually justified in trusting arithmetic. His chain goes like this.

(1) Gödel's second incompleteness theorem: take any consistent formal system with an effectively specifiable set of rules that contains enough arithmetic — the kind you could actually program — and that system cannot prove its own consistency.

(2) Every time you trust a calculation or a proof, you're implicitly trusting that the background system is consistent. In an inconsistent system anything follows, so a derivation of 2+2=4 would buy you exactly nothing.

(3) So being justified in trusting arithmetic requires having a proof that arithmetic is consistent.

(4) But by (1), arithmetic can't supply that proof itself. Any consistency proof for arithmetic would have to live in some stronger system — set theory, say.

(5) And then by (2), trusting that stronger system requires a proof that it's consistent, which by the same reasoning lives in a still stronger system — and off you go on an infinite regress.

(6) Taking some system's consistency on faith isn't justification, and an infinite regress isn't justification either. So no one is justified in believing arithmetic is consistent.

(7) And if you're not justified in believing arithmetic is consistent, then you're not justified in believing 2+2=4, or any mathematical claim you've ever used to build a bridge or balance the books.

When I pushed back that mathematicians do prove consistency by constructing models, he said sure, but a model-theoretic proof just moves the game to set theory, which is stronger and still can't prove its own consistency, so step (5) just re-engages. He also says this is the real, boring lesson of why Hilbert's program collapsed. I keep wanting to say the trouble is somewhere around the leap from (2) to (3) — between "trusting a system commits you to its consistency" and "you need a separate proof of consistency to be justified" — but whenever I try I end up sounding like I'm just insisting arithmetic is fine because it obviously works. Which step would you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
