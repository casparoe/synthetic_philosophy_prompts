I am a PhD student working on the measurement of well-being, and I have been trying to pin down how two dimensions of an experience combine into its value: how conscious, lucid, or richly aware it is on the one hand, and how pleasant or unpleasant it feels (its valence) on the other. I keep gravitating toward the thought that the value of an experience is simply the product of the two — consciousness times valence, as it were — but my case-by-case verdicts do not all behave like multiplication, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they actually express. Assume throughout that all experiences compared last the same length of time, say an hour, and that no further differences matter.

Here are the verdicts. (1) A dreamlessly sleeping person who has no experience at all gets zero welfare value for that hour, even if we stipulate that the underlying state would feel wonderful if it became conscious; valence without consciousness counts for nothing. (2) A fully lucid hour of severe pain is far worse than a heavily sedated, foggy hour of equally intense pain; the same felt hurt seems to count for more the more conscious it is. (3) A fully lucid hour of mild contentment seems to me at least as good as, and probably better than, an hour of intense pleasure in a half-asleep haze, even though the latter has much higher valence. (4) Yet when a pleasant experience is dimmed but not close to unconsciousness — say, the warm feeling of dozing in the sun while only half aware — I value it almost as much as a fully lucid episode of the same felt pleasantness, not half as much, which also sits awkwardly with straightforward multiplication. (5) A lucid, vivid hour of simply noticing things, with neither pleasure nor pain, feels to me like it has no value in itself; I would not choose it for its own sake. (6) Still, I cannot bring myself to say that a barely-there flicker of mild pleasure in a heavily drugged patient is worth almost nothing; it seems to count for more than the product of its dimness and its mildness suggests. (7) Finally, I would never trade an hour of mild pain for any number of hours of bliss in a sufficiently dim haze: felt pain seems to have a kind of veto that felt pleasure lacks, whatever the consciousness arithmetic says.

Is there one explicit combination rule — additive, multiplicative, lexical, or something in between — that captures most of these verdicts? Where no simple rule can capture all of them, which of my verdicts should I revise, and why? Please also say what your favored principle predicts about a case I have not listed, such as one hour of moderately lucid but strongly unpleasant experience versus three hours of barely conscious mild pleasure. In presenting the principle, indicate how confident you are in it and single out the point you are least sure of.
