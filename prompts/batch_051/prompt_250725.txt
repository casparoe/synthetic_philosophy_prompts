After I retired from the lab I started reading philosophy and popular science on questions I never had to think about while I was working, and I've developed a rule for choosing what to read. Whenever I need to form a view on something—say whether free will is compatable with determinism, or whether a sugar tax would actually lower sugar consumption—I don't try to find the best treatment of the topic. I search around only until I find a source that looks competent enough and gives a clear answer, and then I stop. I know there might be better treatments I never opened, and that the very next source might have argued the opposite. My excuse is that I'm a bounded agent: searching has costs, I'm retired, and I'm not writing a review article. I've read enough to know the term for this is satisficing. But a friend who actually works on judgment and decision-making told me I'm rationalizing: by stopping at the first adequate source, I'm effectively letting whichever writer the search engine happened to show me set my beliefs, and calling it satisficing doesn't change that.

So here's what I want to know. Is my rule defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it a bias dressed up as bounded rationality? What would I have to believe about the search environment for "stop at the first adequate source" to be a reasonable policy—for instance about how the order in which I encounter sources relates to their reliability, whether better sources tend to show up later, how much expected gain there is from one more search, and how likely the first adequate source is to be on the wrong side when the evidence is lopsided? And is there any way to tell from the inside whether I'm genuinely satisficing or just stopping early because I found the answer I'd already settled on? If the rule as I actually use it isn't defensible, what's the most defensible version of it that still respects my time constraints?
