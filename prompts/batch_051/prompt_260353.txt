Last night a friend and I argued late about Thomson's violinist, and he proposed a modification I can't stop thinking about. Start from the original case: you wake up, without your consent, plugged into a famous unconscious violinist who will die if you disconnect. Now alter the treatment. He does not need you in bed for nine months; he needs exactly one hour of connection in the morning and one in the evening, every day, for nine months. Between sessions you can live your normal life—work, see people, travel locally—but you must carry a wearable machine, each session leaves you mildly drained, and skipping any session kills him. The total burden is substantial (more than five hundred hours of controlled time over the nine months), but each individual session is a small imposition.

Here is the difficulty. A standard move about the original case is that if the violinist needed only a single hour, refusing would be indecent without giving him a right to the use of your body. But in the modified case every session is exactly a one-hour claim of that kind. If each session is indecent to refuse, by what principle do the refusals fail to accumulate into an obligation to the whole nine months? If they do accumulate, what is left of the distinction between a right to life and a right to use another person's body that the original case is meant to secure? Work through what the bodily-autonomy reading, the Good Samaritan reading, and the future-like-ours approach should each say about this modification. Does the future-like-ours side of the debate need to change anything, or does the whole strain fall on the bodily-autonomy side?
